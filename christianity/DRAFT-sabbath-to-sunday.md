# DRAFT — The Sabbath and the Lord's Day: How the Day of Rest Moved

## What the evidence shows (the short version)

1. **The red text has Jesus keeping the Sabbath and arguing about how to keep it, never ending it or moving it.** His disputes are about what is lawful *on* the seventh day (Mark 2:27, 3:4).
2. **Christians met on the first day of the week very early.** Acts 20:7 and Didache 14 are both first- or early-second-century, and all four gospels put the resurrection on the first day. The Sunday gathering is not a fourth-century invention, and we shouldn't say it is.
3. **What no early text does is transfer the Sabbath commandment to Sunday.** The early witnesses describe a *gathering* on the first day, not a *rest* on it. The first-day gathering and the seventh-day rest sit side by side for centuries.
4. **Jewish-Christian groups kept the seventh-day Sabbath**, and Epiphanius, writing in the 370s, names that as one of the main things that set the Nazoraeans and Ebionites apart from the church he belonged to.
5. **Sunday as a legal day of rest is fourth-century**: Constantine's law of 321. **A church rule against resting on the Sabbath is also fourth-century**: Laodicea canon 29. Those are the identifiable moments where Sunday took on the Sabbath's rest and the seventh-day rest was discouraged.

So the narrow claim is this: **the first-day assembly is early; Sunday as the Christian day of *rest*, replacing the seventh day, is late, and it came by imperial law and church canon, not by any text in the New Testament.** That fits core thesis point 5 without needing the stronger version ("Sunday worship was invented by Constantine"), which the early evidence doesn't support.

## 1. The commandment and the red text

**The commandment.** The Sabbath is the seventh day, grounded in creation:

> "But the seventh day is the sabbath of the LORD thy God: in it thou shalt not do any work" — Exodus 20:10 (KJV)
> "For in six days the LORD made heaven and earth, the sea, and all that in them is, and rested the seventh day: wherefore the LORD blessed the sabbath day, and hallowed it." — Exodus 20:11 (KJV)

Deuteronomy grounds it in the Exodus as well ("remember that thou wast a servant in the land of Egypt", Deuteronomy 5:15, KJV). Exodus 31:16-17 calls it "a perpetual covenant" and "a sign between me and the children of Israel for ever" (KJV); Ezekiel 20:12 repeats the sign language. Isaiah extends it to outsiders: "the sons of the stranger, that join themselves to the LORD... every one that keepeth the sabbath from polluting it" (Isaiah 56:6, KJV).

**Jesus's practice.** Luke presents synagogue attendance on the Sabbath as his habit: "as his custom was, he went into the synagogue on the sabbath day, and stood up for to read" (Luke 4:16, KJV). Mark 1:21 and 6:2 have the same setting.

**Jesus's arguments.** Every Sabbath controversy in the gospels is about *how* to keep it:

> "The sabbath was made for man, and not man for the sabbath: Therefore the Son of man is Lord also of the sabbath." — Mark 2:27-28 (KJV)
> "Is it lawful to do good on the sabbath days, or to do evil? to save life, or to kill?" — Mark 3:4 (KJV)
> "Wherefore it is lawful to do well on the sabbath days." — Matthew 12:12 (KJV)
> "Thou hypocrite, doth not each one of you on the sabbath loose his ox or his ass from the stall, and lead him away to watering?" — Luke 13:15 (KJV)

These are arguments from inside the Law, the same pattern `questions/jesus-and-the-torah.md` (§4, the Sabbath controversies) lays out. Healing on the Sabbath is defended as fulfilling the day, the way circumcision on the eighth day is allowed to fall on it (John 7:23). In John 5:17 he answers the charge with "My Father worketh hitherto, and I work" (KJV), and John 9:16 reports some Pharisees saying he "keepeth not the sabbath day": that is his opponents' charge, not the narrator's verdict.

**The mainstream reading to acknowledge.** Many Christian readers take "Lord also of the sabbath" as authority to reshape or supersede it. That is a reasonable reading of the words. It is still a reading: the saying claims authority over the Sabbath, and the example he gives with it (Mark 2:23-28, the grain fields) is about what may be done on the day, not which day it is.

**Two red-text details often cited on the Sabbatarian side**, given with their limits:
- "pray ye that your flight be not in the winter, neither on the sabbath day" (Matthew 24:20, KJV). This assumes the Sabbath still matters to Matthew's audience. It is Matthew only (Mark 13:18 has winter without the Sabbath), so it tells us about Matthew's community at least as much as about Jesus.
- After the crucifixion the women "rested the sabbath day according to the commandment" (Luke 23:56, KJV). That is narration, not teaching, and it is Luke's description of Jewish women before the resurrection.

**Thomas.** "If you do not observe the Sabbath as a Sabbath, you will not see the father." — Gospel of Thomas 27 (Lambdin). The Greek fragment (P. Oxy. 1) reads more literally "sabbatize the Sabbath" (reference level; not held). Readers divide over whether this means literal observance or a whole life made into a Sabbath. Either way, it does not point to another day.

**The resurrection day.** All four gospels put the empty tomb on the first day of the week (Matthew 28:1, Mark 16:2, Luke 24:1, John 20:1, KJV). John adds an appearance to the gathered disciples "the same day at evening, being the first day of the week" (John 20:19, KJV) and another "after eight days" (John 20:26, KJV). This is the root the later "Lord's day" grows from, and it is in the red-text gospels themselves. (Mark 16:9, "risen early the first day of the week," is in the longer ending, which the KJV prints and the cards flag.)

## 2. The apostolic writings

**Acts: the Sabbath continues as the setting.** Paul goes to synagogues on the Sabbath (Acts 13:14, 17:2, 18:4: "he reasoned in the synagogue every sabbath, and persuaded the Jews and the Greeks," KJV), and Gentiles ask to hear more "the next sabbath" (13:42). Acts 15:21 notes that Moses is "read in the synagogues every sabbath day," and Acts 21:20 describes thousands of Jewish believers "all zealous of the law" (KJV). Acts treats the synagogue Sabbath as a mission setting; it doesn't tell Gentile believers to keep it or to stop.

**The first-day texts.** Three are always cited for Sunday:

> "And upon the first day of the week, when the disciples came together to break bread, Paul preached unto them, ready to depart on the morrow; and continued his speech until midnight." — Acts 20:7 (KJV)

The strongest of the three: a gathering, on the first day, to break bread. Limits: it is a single occasion, at Troas, on the eve of a departure; on Jewish reckoning the first day begins at sundown, so this may be Saturday evening (scholars differ); and nothing in it says the day was set apart for rest.

> "Upon the first day of the week let every one of you lay by him in store, as God hath prospered him, that there be no gatherings when I come." — 1 Corinthians 16:2 (KJV)

An instruction to set money aside, weekly, on the first day. The KJV's "lay by him" reads as at home; it doesn't describe a service. It does show Paul's churches tracking the Jewish week and treating the first day as a marked day.

> "I was in the Spirit on the Lord’s day, and heard behind me a great voice, as of a trumpet," — Revelation 1:10 (KJV)

The first use of "the Lord's day" (Greek *kyriakē hēmera*). The text doesn't say which day it means. Most scholars read it as Sunday, given the second-century usage below; a minority read it as the eschatological "day of the Lord" or as the Sabbath. Honest position: probably Sunday, not demonstrably so.

**Paul on days.** Three passages relativize sacred days:

> "Let no man therefore judge you in meat, or in drink, or in respect of an holyday, or of the new moon, or of the sabbath days: Which are a shadow of things to come; but the body is of Christ." — Colossians 2:16-17 (KJV)
> "One man esteemeth one day above another: another esteemeth every day alike. Let every man be fully persuaded in his own mind." — Romans 14:5 (KJV)
> "Ye observe days, and months, and times, and years." — Galatians 4:10 (KJV)

The mainstream reading: Paul frees Gentile believers from the obligation of the weekly Sabbath. Sabbatarian readers argue Colossians 2:16 means the ceremonial sabbaths of the festival calendar, not the weekly one. Our position follows the source hierarchy: **these are what Paul taught, not what Jesus taught**, and they belong in the Paul stack. Two things are worth saying about them even so. First, none of them moves the Sabbath to Sunday; they make days a matter of conscience ("every day alike"). Second, Colossians's authorship is disputed in mainstream scholarship. If anything, these passages are the strongest textual basis for *not keeping any day as binding*, not for keeping Sunday instead.

Hebrews 4:9 ("There remaineth therefore a rest to the people of God," KJV) uses a rarer Greek word, *sabbatismos*, a Sabbath-keeping (reference level; no lexicon held). It is read both as a continuing Sabbath and as a spiritual rest fulfilled in Christ. Footnote material, not a load-bearing argument for either side.

## 3. The earliest church orders and letters (c. 100-160)

**The Didache (held).** The clearest early text in the repo:

> "On every Lord's Day—his special day—come together and break bread and give thanks, first confessing your sins so that your sacrifice may be pure." — Didache 14 (Richardson)

Richardson's "his special day" renders a doubled Greek phrase (roughly "according to the Lord's [day] of the Lord," reference level). Most scholars read it as Sunday. The point to concede: **a weekly gathering on "the Lord's Day" is attested in a church manual usually dated somewhere between the late first and early second century.** The points that keep it narrow: the chapter is about assembling, confessing and breaking bread, not about rest; and the same manual defines its week against the Jewish one in a different way: "They fast on Mondays and Thursdays; but you should fast on Wednesdays and Fridays" (Didache 8, Richardson). The Didache community is keeping a Jewish-structured week and differentiating itself within it. It says nothing for or against keeping the seventh day.

**Not held, cited at reference level** (these are the second-century texts that carry most of the mainstream case; we should add them before relying on them):

- **Ignatius of Antioch, *To the Magnesians* 9** (c. 110). Contrasts those who had lived in the old ways and now no longer keep the Sabbath but live according to the Lord's day. Two cautions: the Greek manuscripts differ (some read "the Lord's life"), and Ignatius is arguing against Judaizing, so this is a polemic, not a neutral description.
- **Epistle of Barnabas 15** (c. 70-135). Argues that the true Sabbath is eschatological and that Christians keep the eighth day with joy, the day Jesus rose.
- **Pliny the Younger, *Letters* 10.96** (c. 112). A Roman governor reports Christians meeting on a fixed day before dawn. He doesn't name the day.
- **Justin Martyr, *First Apology* 67** (c. 155). The first description of a Sunday service: Christians gather on the day of the Sun, read the apostles and prophets, pray, and share the Eucharist; Justin gives creation and resurrection as the reasons. In the *Dialogue with Trypho* (c. 160), Justin says Christians do not keep the Sabbath, and he also knows Jewish believers in Jesus who still keep the Law and says some Christians would not share fellowship with them (*Dialogue* 47).
- **Gospel of Peter 35, 50** (second century). Uses "the Lord's day" for the resurrection day.

**What this section adds up to.** By the mid-second century, at least some Gentile churches (Rome under Justin, Asia Minor under Ignatius) met on Sunday and some of their writers argued against keeping the Sabbath. None of these texts calls Sunday a day of rest, and none cites a command of Jesus or the apostles to change the day. The reasons given are the resurrection and, in Barnabas, an allegory of the eighth day.

**The Clementine Recognitions (held)** use the term in passing. At the close of the work, after Faustinianus's conversion, Peter "proclaimed a fast to all the people, and on the next Lord's day he baptized him" (Recognitions 10.72, Smith). Two limits: the Recognitions are usually dated c. 220-300, and the Greek is lost, so "Lord's day" is Rufinus's Latin (c. 400), which may or may not mirror the original. Even so, it is notable: a work drawing on Jewish-Christian tradition places a baptism on the Lord's day without comment. Elsewhere it names the seventh-day rest as a custom Jews keep "wherever they may be" (Recognitions 9.28, Smith), as an example of law, not fate, governing a whole people.

## 4. Jewish-Christian groups who kept the Sabbath

Epiphanius (*Panarion*, c. 375-377) is hostile to these groups, which makes his testimony about their practice more useful, not less: he has no reason to invent the Sabbath-keeping he is attacking.

**The Nazoraeans:**

> "In this alone they differ from Jews and Christians: they do not agree with the Jews, because they have believed in Christ; and they are not of one mind with Christians, because they are still bound by the law, by circumcision, the sabbath and the rest." — *Panarion* 29,7,5 (machine translation from Holl's Greek, checked by Craig Wescoe)

The same chapter says they "proclaim one God and his child Jesus Christ" (29,7,3). On Epiphanius's own account, the main things separating them from the church of his day are practice, and the Sabbath is named.

**The Ebionites:**

> "...he held the same views as the others and differed in this alone, that he adhered to the law of Judaism in sabbath-keeping, in circumcision and in all the other observances practised among Jews and Samaritans." — *Panarion* 30,2,2 (Wescoe)

and later groups who "have kept Ebion’s circumcision, sabbath and customs" (30,17,5, Wescoe). Epiphanius also reports the Ebionite story about Paul, that after failing to marry a priest's daughter "he grew angry and wrote against circumcision, the sabbath and the law" (30,16,9b, Wescoe). That is the Ebionites' polemic as Epiphanius reports it, not history; it is evidence that they connected the abandonment of the Sabbath with Paul.

**Epiphanius's answer** is the mainstream fourth-century position in its own words: Christ "is the great and eternal sabbath, of which the small and temporary sabbath was a type, serving until his coming, ordained by him according to the law, and in him loosed and fulfilled in the gospel" (30,32,9b, Wescoe). He argues it from the gospel healings (30,32,1a-2).

**Not held, at reference level:** Eusebius, *Church History* 3.27 (early fourth century), says one group of Ebionites kept the Sabbath and the rest of Jewish discipline and *also* celebrated the Lord's days, as the church did, in memory of the resurrection. If that holds up when we add the text, it is the cleanest early picture of both days kept side by side.

**Narrow claim:** the groups with the best claim to continuity with the Jerusalem community (see the Essene-Nazarene-Ebionite cards) kept the seventh day, and by the fourth century that was a mark of separation from the church. We can't show from these texts that they *refused* a first-day gathering; Eusebius suggests some did not.

## 5. The fourth-century steps: law and canon

**Constantine's Sunday law, 321 (not held).** Two rescripts of that year: one (preserved in the *Codex Justinianus* 3.12.2, dated 7 March 321) directs judges, city people and craftsmen to rest on the venerable day of the Sun, while allowing farm work to go on; the other (*Codex Theodosianus* 2.8.1, July 321) allows the freeing of slaves on that day. The law is civil, uses the name "day of the Sun," and does not mention Christ, the Sabbath, or the resurrection. It did not forbid Sabbath-keeping. **This is the first time Sunday became a day of rest by law.** That claim is solid and narrow; "Constantine invented Sunday worship" is not, since the gatherings were already two centuries old.

**Council of Laodicea, canon 29 (not held).** A regional synod in Phrygia, usually dated c. 363-364 (estimates run from the 340s to the 380s). Canon 29, as commonly numbered, says Christians must not Judaize by resting on the Sabbath but must work on it, honouring the Lord's day by resting if they can, and puts anyone found Judaizing under anathema. **This is the first church rule we know of that discourages the seventh-day rest and assigns rest to Sunday.** It is regional, not ecumenical. The same council's canons (16 and 49, as commonly numbered) still treat the Sabbath as a liturgical day, with Gospel readings and the Eucharist in Lent, which shows the Sabbath had not disappeared from church practice: it was being emptied of rest, not of worship.

**Epiphanius's own church (held), c. 377.** His summary of catholic practice describes the week without a Sabbath rest:

> "Assemblies have been appointed by the apostles to be held on the fourth day of the week, on the day before the sabbath, and on the Lord’s day." — *De Fide* 22,1a (Wescoe)
> "Every Lord’s day this holy catholic church counts a day of delight, and holds assemblies from dawn, and does not fast; for it is inconsistent to fast on the Lord’s day." — *De Fide* 22,8 (Wescoe)

He attributes the pattern to the apostles; that is his claim, not evidence of first-century practice. He also cites an "Ordinance of the Apostles," saying "He who afflicts his soul on the Lord’s day is accursed before God" (*Panarion* 70,11,4d, Wescoe).

**The Ethiopic Didascalia (held, OCR-damaged).** The Ethiopic version of the Apostolic Constitutions (the Greek is late fourth century) keeps both days. One chapter heading reads "THAT WE OUGHT TO REST ON THE SABBATH DAYS AND HEARKEN TO THE WORDS OF THE HOLY SCRIPTURES" (Ethiopic Didascalia ch. 38, Harden 1920; corresponding to *Apostolic Constitutions* 7.36). The prayer under it gives the Sabbath rest as commanded because the Lord rested on it, then calls the resurrection day, which Harden renders "the Christian Sabbath," greater. Other chapters (corresponding to AC 5.14 and 5.20) forbid fasting on the Sabbath except on the Sabbath of the Passion. The OCR drops the letter "t" throughout, so only the heading is quoted here; the prayer should be checked against the scan before anyone quotes it. **This is the counterweight to Laodicea:** in the same century, a major church order still told Christians to rest on the seventh day and honour the first.

**Not held, at reference level:** Socrates Scholasticus (*Church History* 5.22, c. 440) says nearly all churches celebrated the mysteries on the Sabbath every week, except Alexandria and Rome; Sozomen (7.19) says much the same. In the fifth century, the seventh day was still kept as a day of worship across much of the church.

## What we can and can't say

**We can say:**
- Jesus kept the seventh-day Sabbath and disputed only how to keep it (Mark 2:27-28, 3:4; Luke 4:16, KJV).
- No New Testament text transfers the Sabbath commandment to the first day, or calls the first day a day of rest.
- Christians gathered on the first day early: Acts 20:7 (KJV) and Didache 14 (Richardson), with the resurrection on the first day in all four gospels.
- Jewish-Christian groups kept the seventh day, and by the 370s Epiphanius named it as a mark that separated them from the church (*Panarion* 29,7,5; 30,2,2, Wescoe).
- Sunday became a legal day of rest under Constantine in 321, and the earliest canon we know of against resting on the Sabbath is Laodicea 29 (c. 363). Both at reference level.
- In the same century, the Apostolic Constitutions tradition (Ethiopic Didascalia, held) still told Christians to rest on the Sabbath, so the change was neither sudden nor uniform.

**We can't say:**
- That Sunday worship began with Constantine. The gatherings are two centuries older.
- That the early church as a whole kept the seventh day and switched in the fourth century. Ignatius and Justin (not held) argue against Sabbath-keeping in the second century.
- That Revelation 1:10 or Didache 14 certainly mean Sunday. Probably, on the mainstream reading; not demonstrably.
- That Paul's letters forbid the Sabbath. They make days a matter of conscience, and they are Paul, not Jesus.
- Anything in the words of Veith's denominational writers until the originals are in the repo.

**Prefer the narrow claim:** the first-day gathering is early and honours the resurrection; the first-day *rest*, standing in for the seventh day, comes by law and canon in the fourth century, and no gospel text asks for it.
