# Matthew 26:28 — Textual Layers of "Blood of the New Covenant"

A deep dive on the single most-quoted blood-atonement verse at the Last Supper: *"This is my blood of the new covenant, which is shed for many for the remission of sins."* When you compare this verse against its synoptic parallels, against Paul, against John (who lacks the institution narrative entirely), and against the Didache (the earliest non-canonical Christian liturgical document), the verse turns out to be at least three layers of theological development compressed into one utterance. The phrase that does the explicit atonement work — *"for the forgiveness of sins"* — is unique to Matthew. It appears in no other early source, including Matthew's own Markan source.

This document reconstructs the layers from the manuscript and synoptic evidence, and shows why the standard atonement reading requires treating Matthean editorial work as historical Jesus.

---

## The Short Answer

Yes, in the canonical gospels and in Paul's 1 Corinthians, Jesus is recorded saying blood-of-the-covenant language at the Last Supper. The most quoted version is from Matthew:

> "This is my blood of the new covenant, which is shed for many for the remission of sins." (Matt 26:28)

But when you compare the four gospels side by side with each other, with Paul, and with the *earliest* non-canonical Christian document we have (the Didache), the picture gets a lot more complicated. The specific atonement framing — *blood shed for the forgiveness of sins* — appears to be a later theological layer that wasn't in the earliest tradition.

This isn't a fringe position. It's where the manuscript evidence actually points.

---

## The Textual Problem: Comparing the Sources

If Jesus instituted the Eucharist as a blood-atonement memorial, you'd expect the earliest sources to say so consistently. They don't.

| Source | Date | What it says about the cup | "For forgiveness of sins"? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mark 14:24** (earliest gospel) | ~65-70 CE | "This is my blood of the covenant, poured out for many." | **No.** |
| **Matthew 26:28** | ~80-90 CE | "This is my blood of the covenant, poured out for many **for the forgiveness of sins**." | **Yes.** |
| **Luke 22:20** | ~80-90 CE | "This cup is the new covenant in my blood." | **No.** |
| **1 Corinthians 11:25** (Paul) | ~53-54 CE | "This cup is the new covenant in my blood." | **No.** |
| **John** | ~90-100 CE | *No Last Supper institution at all — just foot washing.* | **N/A.** |
| **Didache 9-10** | ~50-120 CE | Thanksgiving for "the holy vine of David" and "life and knowledge." | **No (no blood/sacrifice language at all).** |

A few things jump out.

**Matthew is the only source that adds "for the forgiveness of sins" to the cup saying.** Not his own source (Mark). Not Luke. Not Paul. Not John. Not the Didache. *Matthew alone.*

**And you can see where Matthew got the phrase.** In Mark it belongs to John the Baptist: "John did baptize in the wilderness, and preach the baptism of repentance for the remission of sins" (Mark 1:4, KJV). Matthew, copying that scene, drops it — his John says only "Repent ye: for the kingdom of heaven is at hand" (Matthew 3:2, KJV) — and "remission" appears once in all of KJV Matthew, at the cup (26:28). Forgiveness is lifted off John's baptism of repentance and set on Jesus's blood. Davies and Allison, Luz, and Meier read this as deliberate Matthean editing. The words are secure in Matthew's manuscripts, so this is a claim about the evangelist, not a copyist. (Florez sheet, B14; Mark 1:4 and Matthew 3:2 checked against the held KJV, `audits/2026-10-26-florez-red-letters-citation-check.md`.)

**John's gospel has no Last Supper institution narrative at all.** The latest and most theologically developed gospel — written by a community deep into Jesus's divinity — leaves out the eucharistic institution entirely. If "this is my blood, shed for the forgiveness of sins" was a central teaching of Jesus, John's omission is hard to explain.

**The earliest Christian liturgical document — the Didache, "Teaching of the Twelve Apostles" — has no blood theology in its communion liturgy at all.** Its Eucharist is a thanksgiving meal celebrating what Jesus *revealed*, not what he *died for*. Zero body, blood, sacrifice, death, or "remembrance of my death" language.

---

## What "Blood of the Covenant" Actually Meant

Even taking the earlier Markan version — "this is my blood of the covenant" — that phrase is not atonement language in its original Jewish context. It's a direct echo of **Exodus 24:8**:

> "And Moses took the blood and threw it on the people and said, 'Behold the blood of the covenant that the Lord has made with you...'"

This is the covenant-ratification ceremony at Sinai. Moses sprinkles blood on the people to seal Israel's agreement with God to live by the Torah. **The Sinai blood does not pay for sins. It seals an agreement.**

When Jesus echoes Exodus 24 at a Passover meal, the cultural-religious referent is *covenant ratification*, not atonement. He's saying "we are entering a covenant relationship" — possibly echoing Jeremiah 31:31's prophecy of a new covenant written on hearts (which is about internalized Torah, not blood paying for sins).

It's only Matthew's added phrase — "for the forgiveness of sins" — that pulls the saying into Levitical sin-offering territory. Without that addition, it reads as a covenant-sealing echo. With it, it reads as a sacrificial death memorial.

---

## Why This Matters: It Contradicts Jesus's Own Teaching on Forgiveness

The forgiveness mechanism Jesus *actually* teaches throughout the gospels is the exact opposite of blood atonement:

| Reference | What Jesus says |
|---|---|
| **Matthew 6:12** (Lord's Prayer) | "Forgive us our debts, **as we forgive** our debtors." |
| **Matthew 6:14-15** (right after) | "**If you forgive** others their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you. But **if you do not forgive** others their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses." |
| **Mark 11:25** | "Whenever you stand praying, forgive, if you have anything against anyone, so that your Father also may forgive you." |
| **Luke 6:37** | "Forgive, and you will be forgiven." |
| **Matthew 18:21-35** (Parable of the Unforgiving Servant) | King forgives servant a massive debt. Servant refuses to forgive a small debt owed to him. King *revokes* the forgiveness. |

Every single time, the mechanism is: **YOU forgive others → God forgives you.** It's conditional. It's active. It's a practice you participate in. The parable of the Unforgiving Servant even has God *revoke* forgiveness when the human doesn't forgive in turn — which is incompatible with "it is finished" / "paid in full" atonement theology, where you can't lose what Jesus already bought.

Side by side:

| | Jesus's teaching on forgiveness | Blood atonement |
|---|---|---|
| **Who does the work?** | You (forgive others) | Jesus (dies for you) |
| **When?** | Continuously | Once, on the cross |
| **What activates it?** | Your active forgiveness | Your belief / acceptance |
| **Can it be revoked?** | Yes (Unforgiving Servant) | No (it's a finished transaction) |
| **Model** | Relational / ethical | Transactional / legal-sacrificial |

These are two different mechanisms answering the same question. They can't both be how forgiveness actually works.

## "I Desire Mercy, Not Sacrifice"

Jesus quotes **Hosea 6:6** twice in Matthew alone (Matt 9:13, 12:7): "I desire mercy, not sacrifice." That's part of a long Hebrew prophetic stream rejecting sacrificial atonement:

- **Psalm 51:16-17** — "You do not delight in sacrifice... the sacrifices of God are a broken spirit."
- **Micah 6:6-8** — "What does the Lord require? To act justly, love mercy, walk humbly. (Not: accept a blood sacrifice.)"
- **Isaiah 1:11-17** — "I have had enough of burnt offerings... cease to do evil, learn to do good."
- **Jeremiah 7:22-23** — "I did not speak to your fathers concerning burnt offerings or sacrifices. But this command I gave them: obey my voice."

So you have a coherent stream: **The prophets reject sacrifice. Jesus quotes the prophets twice. Jesus teaches forgiveness as relational practice. The earliest Christian community (Didache) preserves it that way.** Then Paul shows up, claims private revelation ("I received from the Lord" — 1 Cor 11:23, Galatians 1:11-12, neither traditioned through the Twelve), and reintroduces the very sacrificial framework Jesus and the prophets had spent centuries dismantling.

---

## So What Probably Happened

Matt 26:28 isn't one teaching from Jesus. It's at least three layers of theological development compressed into one verse:

1. **Earliest layer (possibly Jesus):** "Blood of the covenant" as an Exodus 24 echo at a Passover meal — covenant ratification language.
2. **Mark, ~65-70 CE:** Records the saying with "poured out for many."
3. **Matthew, ~80-90 CE:** Adds "for the forgiveness of sins" — explicit atonement. *Not in his source.* This is Matthew's editorial layer.
4. **Later scribes:** Add "new" to "covenant" in many manuscripts, harmonizing with Paul. The KJV shows this layer: "my blood of the new testament" at both Matthew 26:28 and Mark 14:24 (KJV). "New" is missing from Sinaiticus and Vaticanus in both gospels (and from P37, Regius, Θ and 33 in Matthew; from Ephraemi, the Greek of Bezae, Regius and Bobiensis in Mark), and modern Bibles (NRSV, NIV, ESV, NABRE) read "the covenant." It comes in from Luke 22:20 and 1 Corinthians 11:25, the only places Jesus is reported speaking of a *new* covenant. (Florez sheet, A24. Say "new covenant," not just "new": he says "new" elsewhere, e.g. "drink it new," Mark 14:25.)

The verse most English Bibles print today is Layer 4. The verse Jesus may have actually spoken is closer to Layer 1.

The historical context matters. Matthew was written around 80-90 CE — *after* Paul's letters had been circulating for 30+ years and *after* the Temple was destroyed in 70 CE. With the sacrificial system literally gone, communities were scrambling to explain what replaced it. Reading Jesus's death as the replacement sacrifice was the dominant interpretive move by then. Matthew almost certainly believed what he was writing. But what he believed had been shaped by 50 years of Pauline interpretation and the trauma of 70 CE — not by the historical Jesus.

---

## Bottom Line

Reading Matt 26:28 the way most Christians do — as Jesus declaring his blood pays for the forgiveness of sins — requires:

- Treating Matthew's editorial addition as straight from Jesus's mouth
- Ignoring that no other early Christian source has that phrase
- Ignoring that the *earliest* community (Didache) celebrated communion with zero blood-atonement language
- Ignoring that this contradicts Jesus's own teaching on forgiveness (which is active, conditional, and relational)
- Ignoring that Jesus quoted the prophets *twice* rejecting sacrifice as the path to forgiveness

The instinct that this verse feels out of place is correct. It IS out of place. It's a later theological layer applied to an earlier non-atoning saying — preserved alongside, but not coherent with, the rest of Jesus's actual teaching.

The simpler reading: Jesus taught that you find forgiveness by *practicing* forgiveness. That's the Lord's Prayer, the Unforgiving Servant, Matthew 6:14-15. It doesn't need a sacrifice. The blood-atonement framing was added later, by people working from a different theological framework than Jesus himself worked from.
