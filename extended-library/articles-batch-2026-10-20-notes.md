## 1. Manfred Davidmann, "Origin of Christianity and Judaism: What Actually Happened, What Jesus Actually Taught and Later Changes" (© 1994, 2nd ed., ISBN 0 85192 051 9; ~14,000 words; the copy held is a 2016 print of justgivemethetruth.com, which mirrors solbaram.org)

### What it argues

Davidmann's thesis is that Jesus taught the social laws of the Torah: the Ten Commandments read as a charter of freedom, plus the Pentateuch's system of interest-free loans, seventh-year release of debts, the Sabbath as equal rest for servant and master, and the kingship laws of Deuteronomy 17 restraining rulers. The first Christians were Jews who kept these laws. Paul, in his reading, preached against them on behalf of the ruling establishment: Romans 13:1-7 makes rulers God's appointees, and Romans 13:8-10 reduces the law to the last five commandments plus "love your neighbour," which Davidmann takes as a device for setting aside the first five and the social legislation. He reads the Rich Young Man (Matthew 19:16-30) as Matthew's direct answer to Paul: Jesus lists the same commandments Paul listed, the young man asks what he still lacks, and "sell what you possess and give to the poor" is the social law Paul dropped. The Labourers in the Vineyard (Matthew 20:1-16), sharing the tag line "many that are first will be last," is read as a criticism of unequal pay, confirming the lesson.

The article's distinctive move is to place Matthew first among the gospels and to read Mark and Luke as successive edits that soften Matthew's point: Mark's "Why do you call me good?" replacing Matthew's "Why do you ask me about what is good?", Mark's dropping of "love your neighbour as yourself" from the list, Luke's turning the young man into a "ruler," both later gospels omitting the Labourers parable. A second strand argues that the Talmud records the same struggle under other names: Hillel's rulings (the golden rule in the negative, Shabbat 31a; the ruling that let the rich collect debts past the seventh year) are Paul's teaching, so that in Davidmann's reading Hillel stands for Paul and Shammai for Jesus. A third strand, drawn from Baigent and Leigh's *The Dead Sea Scrolls Deception* (1991) and through them from Eisenman, identifies the Teacher of Righteousness with Jesus and the scrolls' "Liar" with Paul, and says the Early Church and the Qumran community were one movement. An appendix gives a plain-English paraphrase of the Ten Commandments and a generation-by-generation account of the Hasmonean dynasty from Simon to Herod, with the Talmud's "pairs" (zugot) mapped onto the five generations after Simon.

The tone is that of a social-justice reading rather than a theological one. "Love" without definition is, for Davidmann, "vague and abstract and quite meaningless," and the point of the whole is that Christianity and rabbinic Judaism were both re-shaped in the same period, by the same kind of establishment pressure, to drop the economic laws of the Torah.

### Sources it relies on

- Bible: RSV (1952/1971) and KJV, named in note 4, which also shows that Romans 13:9 differs between the two (the KJV includes "bear false witness"; the RSV does not). Passages: Exodus 20 and 32:16; Deuteronomy 5 and 17:14-20; Romans 4:15 and 13:1-10; Matthew 19:16-30 and 20:1-16; Mark 10:17-31; Luke 13:30 and 18:18-30.
- Talmud: Shabbat 31a (Hillel and the proselyte on one foot), Sanhedrin 96b (descendants of Sisera, Sennacherib and Haman studying Torah), Avot 1-2 (Simeon the Just, Antigonus of Socho, the "evil eye" saying), the Bene Bathyra episode (Pesachim 66a, not cited by tractate), the prozbul ruling (Shevi'it 10:3, Gittin 36a, not cited by tractate). Most Talmud references are given secondhand to his own four-volume series *Freedom Now, Freedom for Ever* (solbaram.org).
- Dead Sea Scrolls: entirely through Baigent and Leigh (1991) and Hugh Schonfield, *The Essene Odyssey* (1984); Eisenman is named as their source. No scroll is quoted directly.
- No church fathers, no Thomas, no Didache, no Clementines.

### Claims to check

1. **Matthew was written first, then Mark, then Luke, and the later two edited Matthew to favour Paul.** The whole "censored" section depends on this order. The majority view (Markan priority, two-source) runs the other way and is the order the repo's own cards assume. Augustine held Matthean priority; the Griesbach hypothesis (Matthew, Luke, Mark) is the modern minority position; "Matthew, Mark, Luke" with Mark knowing Matthew is Davidmann's own ordering. Any use of his gospel comparisons has to say which order it assumes.
2. **Hillel in the Talmud is a cover name for Paul, and Shammai for Jesus.** Hillel's traditional dates (d. c. 10 CE) precede Paul's activity (30s-60s CE); Shammai likewise predates Jesus's ministry. The article treats the names as later cryptonyms, which is an interpretive claim with no external support offered. Check the name etymologies he gives ("Hillel" = "made bright by God," "Shammai" = "laid waste by God"); Hillel is usually derived from *hallel*, "praise."
3. **"The law never did follow Hillel."** Eruvin 13b records a heavenly voice declaring that the halakha follows the House of Hillel. Davidmann's reading is the reverse of the standard one; check what Mishnah text he has in mind (his volume 3, p. 32-35, is the only reference).
4. **Only two rulings in the whole Mishnah are attributed to Hillel, and both favour the rich.** The prozbul (Shevi'it 10:3) is one. Count the Mishnah's Hillel attributions before repeating; Avot 1:12-14 and 2:4-7 alone attribute several sayings to him, so "rulings" has to be defined narrowly.
5. **Joshua ben Hananiah in Avot 2:11 is a coded reference to Jesus.** Joshua ben Hananiah is a well-attested tanna of the generation after 70 CE (a student of Johanan ben Zakkai); "happy is she that bore him" (Avot 2:8) is said of him by his teacher. The decoding rests on the name alone.
6. **Antigonus of Socho's saying (Avot 1:3) is the slogan of an oppressive establishment.** The usual reading is a counsel of disinterested piety. Interpretive, not factual; flag as his reading.
7. **The Bene Bathyra are "the sons of those doing the cutting," i.e. the circumcised Jewish Christians.** Bathyra is a place-name (Josephus, *Antiquities* 17.2.1-3, the Babylonian Jewish colony Herod settled in Batanea); the Bene Bathyra of Pesachim 66a are a family of that name. The etymology offered is unsupported.
8. **The Talmud was compiled "contemporaneously with the gospels," from the first century.** The Mishnah was redacted c. 200 CE, the Jerusalem Talmud c. 400, the Babylonian c. 500-600. The four-hundred-year parallel with the Christian canon (Carthage 397) is approximate at best.
9. **The Teacher of Righteousness is Jesus; the Liar is Paul; the Early Church and the Qumran community "were one and the same."** This is Baigent and Leigh's popularization of Eisenman. The repo's DSS overview records the majority dating of the Teacher a century and more before Jesus and names Eisenman's view as a minority position; the "Liar" (*matif ha-kazav*, CD 8:13, 1QpHab) and the Wicked Priest are placed by most scholars in the Hasmonean period. Cite at reference level, as Eisenman's hypothesis, never as what the scrolls say.
10. **"Essene" derives from *'Osei ha-Torah*, "Doers of the Law."** Eisenman's etymology; others derive it from Aramaic *hasya* ("pious") or Hebrew *hasidim*. Unsettled.
11. **Nazareth did not exist in Jesus's time** (note 11, quoting Baigent and Leigh, 1991). Excavations since then (the 2009 house excavation by Yardenna Alexandre, the Nazareth Village Farm survey) have produced first-century remains. The 1991 statement is out of date.
12. **Paul's Romans 13:9 list "pointedly left out" the first five commandments.** The list is accurate (adultery, murder, theft, [false witness], coveting), and Davidmann's note 4 correctly records the RSV/KJV difference. Whether the omission is pointed is interpretation; Matthew 19:18-19 (KJV held) also omits coveting and adds honouring parents, and Mark 10:19 adds "defraud not," which Davidmann does not mention.
13. **Mark and Luke both omit the Labourers in the Vineyard; Luke moves "the first shall be last" to another story.** Matthew 20:1-16 is Matthew-only; Mark 10:31 keeps the saying; Luke 13:30 has it in a different setting and Luke 18:18-30 does not. The textual facts hold; the motive assigned is his.
14. **The Talmud citations themselves** (Shabbat 31a, Sanhedrin 96b, Avot 1:3, 2:11). The first three are accurately reported as far as the wording given; Sanhedrin 96b does list the descendants of Sisera, Sennacherib and Haman as Torah students (Shemaiah and Avtalyon as Sennacherib's descendants). His conclusion that this is a coded condemnation of Shemaiah and Avtalyon is the reverse of the usual reading, which presents the passage as praise of repentance. No Talmud is held in this repo; cite at reference level.

## 2. Walter J. Veith, "Denominational Testimony" (amazingdiscoveries.org, published 2009-09-02; adapted from his *Truth Matters*; ~1,100 words, most of it quotation)

### What it argues

The article is a catena: short quotations from writers in eight Protestant traditions (Anglican, Baptist, Congregationalist, Disciples of Christ, Episcopalian, Lutheran, Methodist, Presbyterian, Southern Baptist), each conceding that the New Testament contains no command to transfer the Sabbath from the seventh day to the first. Veith's own text is one sentence: members of many denominations recognize that Sunday observance has no scriptural basis. The rest is the sources speaking. It is one page in a series on the Sabbath (the neighbouring pages are "Constantine and the Sabbath" and "Boasts of the Roman Church about Sunday"), on a site whose teaching is Seventh-day Adventist in orientation, and the series' larger argument, that the change was made by the church on its own authority and later inherited by Protestantism, is carried here by the quotations rather than stated.

### Claims to check

1. **The Hiscox quotation.** This is the most-circulated item on the page and the most disputed: Hiscox's paper was printed in the *New York Examiner* (16 Nov. 1893), and Hiscox later objected in print to its use by Sabbatarian writers, saying he did not conclude that Christians should keep Saturday. Locate the *Examiner* text and quote from it, with his later disclaimer, or not at all. The sentence about Sunday being "christened with the name of the sungod" is the part most often questioned.
2. **"Canon Eyton" filed under Presbyterian.** Robert Eyton was an Anglican, Canon of Westminster; *The Ten Commandments* (1894) is a set of Anglican sermons. The quotation may be accurate; the label is not.
3. **"Amos Binney, *The Methodist Book Concern*."** The Methodist Book Concern is a publisher, not a title. The work is Binney's *Theological Compend* (editions from 1875; the 1902 printing is presumably what is meant). The quotation on infant baptism and the first day appears in Sabbatarian compilations; find the page.
4. **Secondhand quotations with no primary locator**: Bishop Seymour (George Franklin Seymour, Episcopal Bishop of Springfield, d. 1906, is presumably meant), the *Manual of Christian Doctrine*, and *The Sunday Problem* (1923) are all given only through Morgan (2002). They cannot be cited until the originals are found.
5. **Neander (1848), p. 186.** A genuine and widely reproduced passage; check that the Rose translation's wording matches ("far from them, and from the early apostolic Church, to transfer the laws of the Sabbath to Sunday") and that the page is right for the 1848 New York printing rather than the London edition.
6. **The series' implied history** (the day was changed by the Roman church and inherited by Protestants). The catena establishes only that the quoted writers found no New Testament command. The dating of first-day observance has its own early witnesses not addressed here: Didache 14 (held), Ignatius, *Magnesians* 9, *Barnabas* 15, Justin, *1 Apology* 67 (all second century, none held). The two questions, "is there a command?" and "when did the practice begin?", are different, and the page answers only the first.
7. **Spelling and attribution slips** ("Lymen Abbot" for Lyman Abbott, editor of the *Christian Union*) suggest the page was assembled from earlier compilations rather than the originals. Treat every citation as a lead, not a confirmed reference.

## 3. "Where did the term 'Christian' come from?" (sabbathcovenant.com, "newdoctrine" section; author not named; the copy held is a 2019 print; ~3,500 words, including an excerpt from the book *Torah Zone* and two Wikipedia excerpts; refers readers to fossilizedcustoms.com for more)

### What it argues

The article argues that "Christian" was never the name of Jesus's followers and should be abandoned in favour of "Nazarenes" (Acts 24:5), which it renders "Natsarim." Its case has four parts. First, etymology: *Christos* is said to be a native Greek word, not a translation of *Mashiach*, originally a medical term for an ointment (with the Chi-Rho offered as the origin of the pharmacists' "Rx"), and "Chrestos" ("good") is said to have been a title of Mithras, Helios, Osiris and Serapis, so that "Christians" existed as pagans before Jesus; Justin Martyr and Clement of Alexandria are cited for calling believers *chrestoi*. Second, Hebrew priority: the disciples spoke and wrote Hebrew or Aramaic, "Mashiachim" may have been Luke's original word at Acts 11:26, and the Greek was a translation layer preferred by Hellenized translators. Third, scorn: *christianos* is said to have been a derisive label, linked through the French *crétin* (from Swiss French *crestin*, "Christian") to the sense "idiot," with Hebrews 5:12 read as describing believers "retarded in growth" in Torah. Fourth, syncretism: Constantine is said to have created Christianity by blending the empire's pagan religions, altering and burning the manuscripts, and enforcing the result through "the Inquisition" and the Dark Ages (dated to the 5th-10th centuries); Easter is derived from Ishtar, "I.H.S." from Isis/Horus/Seb, and the name "Jesus" from Hesus/Horus/Krishna.

Throughout, the divine name is written "Yahuah" and the Messiah's "Yahusha," the usage of the Sacred Name movement. The article's practical conclusion is that the reader's faith belongs with "the Hebrew origins," which "one of them hinders and clouds," and the other "is essential to it."

### Sources it relies on

- Scripture: Acts 11:26, 24:5-16 (quoted at length from a modern translation with bracketed glosses), 28:22; Hebrews 5:12-14 (with glosses); Ephesians 2:11-13.
- Epiphanius on the "Natsarim" keeping the commandments (no book or section given; *Panarion* 29 is the passage meant).
- Justin Martyr and Clement of Alexandria on *chrestoi* (no works cited; the passages are *1 Apology* 4 and *Stromata* 2.4).
- *The American Heritage Dictionary* (4th ed., 2009) and an *American Heritage Illustrated Encyclopedic Dictionary* entry for "cretin."
- Two Wikipedia articles, "Hellenistic religion" and "Hellenization," quoted (the latter for Bultmann's view).
- An unnamed Vatican relief said to read "CHRESTOS MITHRAS."
- The author's own books, *The Mystery Religion of Babylon*, *Christianity – The Great Deception*, and *Abstract*, and the book *Torah Zone*; fossilizedcustoms.com (Lew White) for the cretin argument.

This article is almost certainly the kind of page those on-air claims came from, and it adds the "Chrestos" spelling, the Vatican relief, Serapis, and the cretin etymology, none of which the hosts used. Items 26 (Constantine's Sunday law and Laodicea), 45 (burned and rewritten manuscripts; Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44), 63 (Ishtar/Easter/Semiramis), and 130 (what Epiphanius says of the Nazoraeans versus the Ebionites) cover the article's other main claims.

### Claims to check

1. **"Chrestos Mithras" on a Vatican relief; Mithras, Helios, Osiris and Serapis worshippers were called "Christians."** No such inscription is catalogued, and the Shabbat Studies notes already say the title is unattested. What *is* attested is confusion between the two words in Roman ears: Suetonius, *Claudius* 25.4 ("Chrestus"); the Mediceus manuscript of Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44 ("Chrestianos," corrected); Tertullian, *Apology* 3 (pagans mispronounce the name). The Serapis claim comes from a letter attributed to Hadrian in the *Historia Augusta* (*Saturninus* 8), a fourth-century text of doubtful reliability about the second century CE, not "as early as 200 BCE."
2. **Justin and Clement called believers *chrestoi*.** True as wordplay: *1 Apology* 4 argues that the name Christian (*Christianos*) sounds like *chrēstos*, "good," so it is unjust to hate it; *Stromata* 2.4 makes the same pun. Neither says the name came from the pagan title. Neither text is held; cite at reference level.
3. ***Christos* is a Greek medical term, not a translation of *Mashiach*; "Rx" comes from Chi-Rho.** *Chriō* does mean to smear or anoint, and *chrisma* an unguent; but the Septuagint uses *christos* for *mashiach* (the references in the Shabbat notes, item 1), which settles the translation question. "Rx" is the Latin *recipe* ("take"), ℞, with no connection to the Chi-Rho monogram.
4. **"Krishna is Sanskrit for Christ."** *Kṛṣṇa* means "dark" or "black." No relation.
5. **The disciples were called "cretins"; *christianos* "is from the root word cretin."** The *American Heritage* etymology quoted in the article runs the other way: *crétin* comes from a Swiss-French dialect form of *chrétien* ("Christian," hence "poor fellow"), attested from the eighteenth century. A first-century Greek word cannot derive from an eighteenth-century French one. The article also says "Christian" is used three times in the New Testament and then "twice ... as a device of scorn"; three is right (Acts 11:26, 26:28; 1 Peter 4:16, all KJV held).
6. **Acts 28:22 names the Nazarenes.** It does not; the KJV reads "this sect." Acts 24:5 does.
7. **"Mashiachim may have been the original word used by Luke at Acts 11:26."** Acts is a Greek composition with no Hebrew or Aramaic manuscript tradition; the article offers no evidence. The repo's one Hebrew-original argument is for Matthew (`christianity/hebrew-gospel-of-matthew.md`), not Luke-Acts.
8. **Nicaea was "headed by Constantine and his son Crispus (325-326 CE)"; Constantine was "a follower of Mithra."** Nicaea met in 325; Crispus was executed in 326 and had no role at the council. Constantine's pre-conversion devotion was to Sol Invictus; Mithraism was a separate, private cult. The Shabbat notes, item 26, cover the related Sunday-law claims.
9. **Constantine "literally altered the original manuscripts," "had the originals burned," enforced the religion "by threat of death" through "the Inquisition," and "the Dark Ages fell upon humanity in the 5th Century."** No source has Constantine burning New Testament manuscripts (the great scripture-burning was Diocletian's, 303); the book-burning attested under Constantine is of Arius's writings; the medieval Inquisition begins in the thirteenth century; the Shabbat notes, item 45, already record that the Greek text survives in thousands of copies. The claim that "Constantine created Christianity" is contradicted by every pre-Constantinian text the repo holds (Didache, Thomas, Hermas, the Clementines, Epistula Apostolorum).
10. **Easter from Ishtar; I.H.S. from Isis/Horus/Seb; "Jesus" from Hesus/Horus/Krishna.** These descend from Alexander Hislop, *The Two Babylons* (1853). "Easter" is Old English (Bede, *De temporum ratione* 15, on Eostre); most languages use a form of *Pascha*; IHS is the Greek abbreviation of ΙΗΣΟΥΣ; "Jesus" is the Latin form of Greek *Iēsous*, from Hebrew *Yeshua*. Item 63 in the Shabbat notes flags the same cluster.
11. **"Yahuah" and "Yahusha."** These vocalizations are particular to the Sacred Name movement and are not the forms used by scholars (Yahweh; Yeshua/Yehoshua). Note where the usage comes from if quoting.
12. **"The Greeks conquered Jerusalem, destroyed the Temple of Yahuah and took the original Hebrew texts and altered them."** The Seleucids desecrated the Temple in 167 BCE (1 Maccabees 1, KJV Apocrypha, held) and did not destroy it; the Septuagint was made by Jewish translators in Alexandria, not by conquerors. The Babylonians (586 BCE) and Romans (70 CE) destroyed the Temple.
13. **"It's a promotion of the Jesuits" that the disciples wrote in Greek; "all the first Protestants were Catholics ... indoctrinated with the Jesuit teachings."** The Society of Jesus was founded in 1540; Erasmus's Greek New Testament (1516) and Luther's break (1517) both precede it.
14. **Epiphanius on the "Natsarim."** Accurate for *Panarion* 29.7.5 (held), with two cautions from the Shabbat notes, item 130: do not merge the Nazoraeans of *Panarion* 29 with the Ebionites of *Panarion* 30, and the spelling "Natsarim" is the article's, not Epiphanius's (Greek *Nazōraioi*).
