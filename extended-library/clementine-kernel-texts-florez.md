# The Kernel Texts of the Clementine Literature

*As of October 27, 2026*

### The short version

The Clementine Homilies and Recognitions are fourth-century books, but they are built on top of older material. Underneath both sits a lost early-third-century novel, the *Circuits of Peter* (c. 220–250). Underneath that, scholars identify two older kernels: a cycle of Peter's sermons and letters usually called the *Kerygmata Petrou*, and a Hebrew-believer history embedded in Recognitions 1.27–71 that is conventionally called the *Ascents of James*.

Those kernels are generally dated to the second half of the second century (roughly 150–200). That puts them about a generation before the Circuits and roughly 150–200 years before the Homilies and Recognitions as we have them. They preserve the voice of a Torah-keeping, James-centered, anti-sacrifice, anti-Paul community that the later editors were already softening.

One naming note before going further. "Circuits of Peter" (*Periodoi Petrou*) is the name most scholars give to the lost base novel itself, not to the oldest kernel. The oldest Peter material inside it is the *Kerygmata* ("Preachings") layer. The dating argument therefore runs through three tiers rather than two, laid out in the next section.

### The layers, oldest at the bottom

The "Clementine literature" is a novel about Clement of Rome traveling with Peter, watching Peter debate Simon Magus, and being reunited with his lost family. It survives in two versions that overlap heavily, often word for word. Since neither copied the other, both must descend from a common lost original ([NASSCAL e-Clavis](https://www.nasscal.com/e-clavis-christian-apocrypha/pseudo-clementines/)).

1. **Top layer, 4th century.** The *Homilies* (20 books, Greek, with three prefatory letters) and the *Recognitions* (10 books). The Greek Recognitions is lost; it survives in Rufinus' Latin translation of about 406–407, plus a Syriac translation preserved in a manuscript dated 411 ([NASSCAL](https://www.nasscal.com/?p=410)).
1. **Middle layer, early 3rd century.** The lost common source, called the *Basic Writing* (German *Grundschrift*) and most likely titled *Periodoi Petrou*, the *Circuits of Peter*. NASSCAL places its origin among the Torah-keeping Hebrew believers of Syria in the early third century ([NASSCAL](https://www.nasscal.com/?p=410)). Strecker placed it in Coele-Syria around the middle of the third century ([Early Christian Writings, Homilies intro](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/clementinehomilies.html)).
1. **Bottom layer, 2nd century.** Older documents the Circuits author absorbed. The two that matter here are the *Kerygmata Petrou* (Peter's sermons, the Letter of Peter to James, and the oath called the *Contestatio*) and the source behind Recognitions 1.27–71, the so-called *Ascents of James*.

The core observation is that the kernels do not fit the novel they sit in. Their theology, their tone, and their concerns are sharper and older than the Hellenistic, apologetic romance wrapped around them.

### Kernel 1: The Preachings of Peter (Kerygmata Petrou)

The Kerygmata is a reconstructed source: a set of Peter's discourses that Peter supposedly sent to James in Jerusalem, with strict instructions on who may receive them. It is known only from inside the Clementine literature and is not the same work as the *Preaching of Peter* quoted by Clement of Alexandria ([Olson, BYU ScholarsArchive](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/studentpub/114)).

**How it is identified.** Georg Strecker reconstructed it starting from the two documents placed in front of the Homilies, the *Epistula Petri* (Letter of Peter to James) and the *Contestatio* (the oath). He then pulled out passages elsewhere in the Homilies and Recognitions that share their outlook ([Early Christian Writings, Kerygmata Petrou](https://earlychristianwritings.com/kerygmatapetrou.html)). In the frame story, Peter sends his preachings to "bishop" James and asks for great care in handing them on, so they will not be falsified by followers of "the hostile man" ([Strecker, quoted at Early Christian Writings](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/clementinehomilies.html)).

**What it teaches.** The material is distinctive and does not match the rest of the novel.

- **James is the authority.** Peter reports to James, not the reverse. Teachers are to be accepted only if they carry credentials from James (Homilies 11.35; Recognitions 4.35).
- **The True Prophet.** Yeshua is the True Prophet who has appeared again and again since Adam, and the Torah is still binding.
- **Against sacrifice.** The True Prophet is said to quench the fire of the altars ([Kerygmata excerpts, H 11.19](https://mail.earlychristianwritings.com/text/kerygmatapetrou.html)), and another excerpt says he hates sacrifices, bloodshed and libations ([Kerygmata excerpts, H 3.26](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/kerygmatapetrou.html)).
- **False pericopes.** Scripture contains inserted falsehoods that must be sifted out by the True Prophet's teaching (Homilies 2.38, 3.47–50).
- **Syzygies (pairs).** Every true figure is preceded by a false one, darkness before light (Homilies 2.15–18). Simon comes before Peter the way Cain came before Abel.
- **Paul behind the mask of Simon.** The Letter of Peter complains that some Gentiles rejected his lawful preaching for the lawless teaching of "the man who is my enemy" (Ep. Petri 2.3). In Homilies 17.13–19, Peter tells Simon that visions cannot make someone an apostle, asks whether Yeshua ever spent a full year teaching him, and accuses him of standing against Peter and calling him "condemned." That is a direct echo of Galatians 2:11. NASSCAL notes that the Simon of these texts closely resembles Paul and reflects early hostility toward him ([NASSCAL](https://www.nasscal.com/?p=410)).

**Its date.** Strecker put it around 200, with no evidence pushing it deep into the second century ([Early Christian Writings](https://earlychristianwritings.com/kerygmatapetrou.html)). Others, such as Hans-Joachim Schoeps, argued for an Ebionite source in the mid-second century. Either way it sits before the Circuits.

### Kernel 2: The Ascents of James (Recognitions 1.27–71)

In the middle of Recognitions Book 1, the novel stops and a different document takes over. It is a sacred history running from creation to the early Jerusalem assembly, and it reads nothing like the chapters around it. Scholars have long treated it as an embedded source ([Wikipedia, Ascents of James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascents_of_James)).

**What it says.**

- **Sacrifice was a concession.** Moses allowed sacrifice only temporarily because the people were attached to it from Egypt. The Prophet like Moses (Deuteronomy 18:15) would come to end it and replace it with washing in water (Recognitions 1.36–39, 1.54).
- **The Temple's fall is the penalty for continuing to sacrifice.** Those who believe are kept safe in a fortified place in the country while war destroys the rest (Recognitions 1.37, 1.39), widely read as a memory of the flight to Pella.
- **Debate on the Temple steps.** The apostles argue with the Sadducees, Samaritans, scribes, Pharisees and followers of John (Recognitions 1.53–65).
- **James nearly wins Israel.** James, as head of the Jerusalem assembly, goes up the Temple steps and argues for seven days until the people and the high priest are ready to be immersed (Recognitions 1.66–69).
- **The enemy man.** A "certain hostile man" storms in, incites the crowd, and throws James headlong from the top of the steps, leaving him for dead. The next chapter says this man then received a commission from Caiaphas to arrest believers in Damascus (Recognitions 1.70–71). That is Saul. Paul is portrayed as the man who stopped James from converting the Hebrew people ([Wikipedia, Ascents of James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascents_of_James)).

**The link to Epiphanius.** Around 375, Epiphanius says the Ebionites used a book called the *Anabathmoi Iakobou*, the *Ascents of James* (Panarion 30.16.6–9). It portrayed James speaking against the Temple, the sacrifices and the altar fire. It also claimed Paul was a Greek who became a proselyte to marry the high priest's daughter and, when rejected, wrote against circumcision, the Sabbath and the Law ([Encyclopedia of the Bible, Ascents of James](https://www.biblicaltraining.org/library/ascents-of-james)). The title is usually explained as James' "ascents" up the Temple stairs to address the people, which is exactly the scene in Recognitions 1.66–70 ([M. R. James, The Apocryphal New Testament (1924)](https://kwx.liehendr.com/content/wikisource_en_all_maxi_2022-09/A/Page%3AThe_Apocryphal_New_Testament_(1924).djvu/58)).

**The link to Hegesippus.** Lightfoot suggested that Hegesippus' account of James being thrown from the Temple, preserved in Eusebius, was the grand finale of these "ascents" ([Encyclopedia of the Bible](https://www.biblicaltraining.org/library/ascents-of-james)). Current scholarship holds that Hegesippus and Recognitions 1 both draw on an earlier shared tradition rather than one copying the other ([Frizzell review of Jones, Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/9857)).

**Its date.** Robert Van Voorst dates the source to the second half of the second century. F. Stanley Jones, in the standard edition, dates it to about 200 and places it in Judea, possibly Jerusalem ([NASSCAL e-Clavis](https://www.nasscal.com/e-clavis-christian-apocrypha/pseudo-clementines/) describes it as written around the end of the second century).

### Why the kernels are older than the books around them

The case rests on five lines of evidence. Each is independent, and they point the same direction.

1. **External dating fixes the middle layer.** The Circuits author used Bardaisan's *Book of the Laws of Countries* in Recognitions 9.19–29, and Bardaisan died in 222. Strecker treats that as the earliest possible date for the Basic Writing ([Early Christian Writings](https://earlychristianwritings.com/kerygmatapetrou.html)). Origen, writing in the 230s, already quotes the astrology section of Recognitions 10 as Clement's work in the *Circuits* (Philocalia 23.22). So the base novel exists by about 230, and anything it absorbed is older.
1. **The kernels break the novel's style and frame.** Recognitions 1.27–71 interrupts the Clement story with a self-contained history that has its own narrator and its own goals. Fred Lapham and others note it differs in both form and content from the rest of the work ([Clementine literature overview](https://kaweah.freedombox.rocks/kiwix/wikipedia_en_all_maxi_2023-10/Pseudo-Clement)). The Kerygmata letters are likewise a separate frame, a Peter-to-James transmission that the Clement romance does not need.
1. **The theology is unassimilated.** The novel is a polished philosophical romance. The kernels are blunt. They demand Torah observance, abolish sacrifice by washing, put James above Peter, and attack Paul. The later editors visibly tone this down. The anti-Paul edge is stronger in the Homilies than in the Recognitions ([Clementine literature overview](https://www.doc.surf/wiki/clementine-literature)), and Rufinus softened further in Latin.
1. **The community it assumes is a second-century one.** The Ascents still argues with Sadducees, Pharisees, Samaritans and followers of John as live opponents. It knows the flight from Jerusalem and the Temple's destruction and treats them as the vindication of the anti-sacrifice message. Its scenes on the Temple steps and its James tradition run parallel to Hegesippus (c. 180) through a shared older tradition ([Frizzell review of Jones](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/9857)).
1. **Epiphanius saw the free-standing works.** In the 370s, Epiphanius describes Ebionite books called the *Circuits of Peter* and the *Ascents of James* as separate titles in use ([Catholic Encyclopedia, Ebionites](https://www.catholic.com/encyclopedia/ebionites)). The content he reports, James against the Temple and the sacrifices and fierce hostility to Paul, matches what is embedded in Recognitions 1. A source circulating independently and also embedded in the novel is exactly what you expect from an older text that the novel absorbed.

### What is settled and what is disputed

The Ascents source is the stronger of the two kernels. The Kerygmata is real material but a weaker case for a single, separate document.

| Claim | Standing | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The Homilies and Recognitions depend on a lost earlier base novel | Near-consensus | The overlap is too close to be anything else; almost all specialists accept it |
| Recognitions 1.27–71 is an older embedded source | Broadly accepted | Jones, Van Voorst and Bauckham all treat it as a distinct second-century source |
| That source dates to c. 150–200 | Broadly accepted | Van Voorst: second half of the 2nd century; Jones: about 200 |
| It is the same book as Epiphanius' Ascents of James | Disputed | Van Voorst and Bauckham use the label; Jones rejects the identification, and the relationship remains uncertain ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascents_of_James)). Epiphanius' Greek-proselyte story about Paul is not in Recognitions 1 |
| The "enemy man" in Recognitions 1.70 is Paul | Strong | The Caiaphas commission to Damascus in 1.71 makes the identification hard to deny |
| Simon Magus is a mask for Paul in the Peter material | Strong for key passages | Homilies 17.19 echoes Galatians 2:11 directly; the letter of Peter attacks "the hostile man" |
| The Kerygmata Petrou was one distinct document | Contested | It is reconstructed from parallels, starting from the prefatory letters; Strecker himself admits there is no evidence for it outside the base novel ([Early Christian Writings](https://earlychristianwritings.com/kerygmatapetrou.html)). Jones and others doubt it can be isolated as a single work |

**The calibrated statement.** The Peter material preserves traditions older than the novel, and the anti-Paul polemic predates it. Whether those traditions once formed a single second-century book called the Kerygmata is unproven. For Recognitions 1.27–71, "a source dated c. 150–200 embedded in a fourth-century text" is the position the evidence supports. Its identity with the *Ascents of James* that Epiphanius saw is probable, not certain, and should be stated that way.

**One limit on "early."** The Ascents source already knows and rewrites the opening of Acts, so it is later than Acts. Its value is that it is the earliest surviving counter-history to Acts from the James side, not that it predates the New Testament writings.

### Dating at a glance

| Layer | Approx. date | Where it survives |
|---|---|---|
| Ascents of James source (Recognitions 1.27–71) | c. 150–200 | Embedded in Recognitions Book 1 |
| Kerygmata Petrou material | c. 150–200 (Strecker: around 200) | Prefatory letters to the Homilies, plus passages in both versions |
| Circuits of Peter (Basic Writing) | c. 220–250 | Lost; reconstructed from the overlap of H and R |
| Homilies (Greek) | 4th century | Greek manuscripts with the Peter and Clement letters |
| Recognitions (Greek original) | 4th century | Rufinus' Latin (c. 406–407) and Syriac (manuscript dated 411) |
| Epiphanius' report of the free-standing books | c. 375 | Panarion 30.15–16 |
