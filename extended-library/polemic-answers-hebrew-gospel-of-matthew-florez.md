# Polemic Answers, The Hebrew Gospel of Matthew, One Book Under Three Names, and Every Recorded Saying

> **What this sheet is for.** In the first centuries, the believers who stayed closest to Yeshua's own people, the Nazarenes and the Ebionites, used a Gospel written in Hebrew that the church fathers said was the original Gospel of Matthew. That book is lost. What survives is a few dozen quotations and descriptions scattered through the writings of the fathers who saw it, from Papias in the early second century to Jerome at the end of the fourth, plus marginal notes in a family of Greek Bible manuscripts. This sheet does three things. It introduces every ancient witness who says the book existed. It lays out why the book the fathers call the Gospel according to Matthew in Hebrew, the Gospel according to the Hebrews, and the book modern scholars call the Gospel of the Ebionites are one and the same document, following the argument of James R. Edwards. And it collects every recorded saying and reading of that Gospel that survives, each with its source and a link. It is written for someone meeting this material for the first time.

> **A note on terms.** This sheet says "Hebrew" where most books say "Jewish," except when quoting a label an ancient manuscript or a modern scholar actually uses. It uses "Yeshua" for Jesus. Scripture references use a period, so Matthew 3.15 is chapter three, verse fifteen. Each witness is tiered. **Tier 1** means a mainstream father reporting matter of factly, or a quotation he says he copied from the book. **Tier 2** means a real witness with a known problem, such as a hostile bishop or a late manuscript note. **Tier 3** means late, medieval or disputed, worth knowing but not to be leaned on.

> **Words used in this sheet.** A **father** is a Christian writer of the first five centuries whose works the church preserved. A **fragment** is a quotation from a lost book preserved inside someone else's book. A **recension** is an edition of the same book that differs in wording or contents, the way a director's cut differs from a theatrical cut. A **Semitism** is a turn of phrase in Greek that only makes sense as a translation of Hebrew or Aramaic. A **variant** is a place where manuscripts of the same book disagree. The **Synoptic Gospels** are Matthew, Mark and Luke, called synoptic because they can be laid side by side.

### THE SHAPE OF THE ARGUMENT

1. **The fathers say Matthew wrote first, and wrote in Hebrew.** Papias, Irenaeus, Pantaenus, Origen, Eusebius, Epiphanius and Jerome all say it, across three centuries and three continents. None of them says otherwise. That is Part A.
1. **The book survived among the Nazarenes and Ebionites.** Irenaeus says the Ebionites used only Matthew. Eusebius says the Ebionites used only the Gospel according to the Hebrews. Epiphanius says the Nazarenes had Matthew complete in Hebrew and that the Ebionites called their Matthew "the Hebrew Gospel." Jerome says the Nazarenes and the Ebionites used one Gospel, which most people called the authentic Matthew. That is Part B.
1. **No ancient writer ever speaks of more than one Hebrew Gospel.** The names "Gospel of the Ebionites" and "Gospel of the Nazarenes" do not exist in any ancient source. They were coined by modern scholars, the first in 1689 and the three-way split in the twentieth century. That is Part C.
1. **The fragments are what one book looks like after three centuries of copying and translating.** The differences scholars use to split it into three are differences of language, edition and the interests of whoever quoted it, the same differences that exist among manuscripts of every Gospel. That is Part C and Part J.
1. **What the book says matters.** Its Yeshua comes to abolish the sacrifices, refuses the flesh of the Passover, keeps the law and the prophets, and appears first to his brother James after rising. Its John the Baptist eats no locusts. Its Spirit begets Yeshua at the Jordan. And the apostle whose name it bears, according to Clement of Alexandria, ate seeds, nuts and vegetables without flesh (*The Instructor* 2.1.16). That is Parts D through G.
1. **Luke appears to have used it.** Edwards argues that the Hebrew-flavored material found only in Luke comes from this Gospel, which would make it older than our Greek Matthew and one of the sources of the Synoptic tradition itself. That is Part H.

### PART A, THE WITNESSES THAT MATTHEW WROTE IN HEBREW

#### A1. Papias of Hierapolis, around 110 to 130

Papias was bishop of Hierapolis in Asia Minor, a hearer of men who had known the apostles, and the earliest Christian writer to say anything about how the Gospels were written. His five books, *Expositions of the Sayings of the Lord*, are lost, but Eusebius quoted them. Eusebius, *Church History* 3.39.16: "Matthew put together the oracles in the Hebrew language, and each one interpreted them as best he could." The Greek word for oracles is *logia*, the word for divine sayings. Papias is saying three things. Matthew wrote. He wrote in Hebrew. And the Greek speaking churches had to translate him themselves. Tier 1. Read it at [https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm), chapter 39.

In the very next sentence, 3.39.17, Eusebius says Papias "relates another story of a woman who was accused of many sins before the Lord, which is contained in the Gospel according to the Hebrews." So the first writer to say Matthew wrote in Hebrew also told a story that Eusebius found in the Gospel according to the Hebrews. Tier 1, same link.

#### A2. Irenaeus of Lyon, around 180

Irenaeus was bishop of Lyon in Gaul, raised in Asia Minor, a hearer of Polycarp. His *Against Heresies* is the first great anti-heresy book. In 3.1.1 he writes, "Matthew also issued a written Gospel among the Hebrews in their own dialect, while Peter and Paul were preaching at Rome and laying the foundations of the Church." That puts the Hebrew Matthew in the sixties. Read at [https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103301.htm](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103301.htm). Tier 1.

In 1.26.2 he describes the Ebionites. They "use the Gospel according to Matthew only, and repudiate the Apostle Paul, maintaining that he was an apostate from the law." They are circumcised, keep the customs of the law, and "adore Jerusalem as if it were the house of God." Read at [https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103126.htm](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103126.htm). In 3.11.7 he repeats that the Ebionites use only Matthew. Read at [https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103311.htm](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103311.htm). Tier 1.

#### A3. Pantaenus, around 180 to 190, reported by Eusebius and Jerome

Pantaenus was the first known head of the Christian school at Alexandria and the teacher of Clement. Eusebius, *Church History* 5.10.3, says Pantaenus went as far as India and found that the Gospel of Matthew had arrived before him, because the apostle Bartholomew had preached there "and left with them the writing of Matthew in the Hebrew letters," which they had kept until that time. Read at [https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250105.htm](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250105.htm), chapter 10. Jerome, *On Illustrious Men* 36, adds that Pantaenus brought the book back to Alexandria. Read at [https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2708.htm](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2708.htm). Tier 1 for the report, Tier 2 for the India detail.

#### A4. Origen of Alexandria, around 230 to 250

Origen was the greatest scholar of the early church, a textual critic who compiled the Old Testament in six parallel columns. Eusebius, *Church History* 6.25.4, quotes him: "The first was written by Matthew, who was once a tax collector but afterward an apostle of Jesus Christ, and it was prepared for the converts from Judaism and published in the Hebrew language." Read at [https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250106.htm](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250106.htm), chapter 25. Origen also quotes the Gospel according to the Hebrews at least twice by name (Part E). Tier 1.

#### A5. Eusebius of Caesarea, around 300 to 325

Eusebius was bishop of Caesarea, where the library of Origen and Pamphilus was kept, and the author of the first church history. In 3.24.6 he writes that Matthew preached first to the Hebrews and, when he was about to go to other peoples, "committed his Gospel to writing in his native tongue." In 3.25.5 he lists the Gospel according to the Hebrews among the disputed books and says it is the one "in which those of the Hebrews who have accepted Christ take special delight." In 3.27.4 he says the Ebionites "use only the so-called Gospel according to the Hebrews and make small account of the rest." In 4.22.8 he says Hegesippus, a Hebrew believer writing around 170, quoted "from the Gospel according to the Hebrews and from the Syriac, and particularly from the Hebrew language." Read Book 3 at [https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm) and Book 4 at [https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250104.htm](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250104.htm). Tier 1.

Notice what Irenaeus and Eusebius together say. Irenaeus says the Ebionites use only Matthew. Eusebius says the Ebionites use only the Gospel according to the Hebrews. They are describing the same people and the same single Gospel under two names.

#### A6. Epiphanius of Salamis, 377

Epiphanius was bishop of Salamis on Cyprus, born in Judea, and wrote the *Panarion*, or Medicine Chest, a catalogue of eighty heresies. He is openly hostile to the Nazarenes and Ebionites, which makes what he concedes about them a hostile witness. English translation by Frank Williams, [https://brill.com/display/title/16955](https://brill.com/display/title/16955).

On the Nazarenes, *Panarion* 29.9.4: they have the Gospel according to Matthew complete, in Hebrew, still written in Hebrew letters as it was originally written. He adds that he does not know whether they removed the genealogies. Tier 2.

On the Ebionites, 30.3.7: they also accept the Gospel according to Matthew and use it alone, and they call it "according to the Hebrews," which Epiphanius says is fair, because Matthew alone in the New Testament set out the Gospel in Hebrew and in Hebrew script. And 30.13.1 to 2: in the Gospel they use, called "according to Matthew," which he says is not complete but corrupted and mutilated, "and they call it Hebrew." Tier 2. Epiphanius then quotes it, and those quotations are in Part D.

Epiphanius also tells a story, 30.3.8 and 30.6.9, about a convert named Joseph of Tiberias who found Hebrew translations of the Gospel of John, the Acts and Matthew kept in a sealed treasury by the Hebrew authorities there. Tier 3, but it shows that in the fourth century a Hebrew Matthew was still physically known in Galilee.

#### A7. Jerome, 392 to 415

Jerome translated the Latin Vulgate, lived in Bethlehem, learned Hebrew from Hebrew teachers, and is the father who had the most direct contact with the book. Three statements matter.

*On Illustrious Men* 3 (392). "Matthew, also called Levi, apostle and formerly tax collector, composed a Gospel of Christ, at first published in Judea in Hebrew for the sake of those of the circumcision who believed, but this was afterwards translated into Greek, though by what author is uncertain. The Hebrew itself has been preserved until the present day in the library at Caesarea which Pamphilus so diligently gathered. I also was allowed by the Nazarenes who use this volume in the Syrian city of Beroea to copy it." He adds that wherever this Matthew quotes the Old Testament it follows the Hebrew and not the Greek Septuagint, for example "Out of Egypt have I called my son" and "he shall be called a Nazarene." Read at [https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2708.htm](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2708.htm). Tier 1.

*Commentary on Matthew* 12.13 (398). "In the Gospel which the Nazarenes and the Ebionites use, which we have recently translated from Hebrew into Greek, and which is called by most people the authentic Gospel of Matthew." Tier 1. This is the single most important sentence in this sheet, and Part C explains why.

*Against the Pelagians* 3.2 (415). "In the Gospel according to the Hebrews, which is written in the Chaldean and Syrian language but in Hebrew letters, and is used by the Nazarenes to this day, I mean the Gospel according to the Apostles, or, as most people maintain, the Gospel according to Matthew, a copy of which is in the library at Caesarea." Read at [https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3011.htm](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3011.htm). Tier 1. By "Chaldean and Syrian" Jerome means Aramaic, the Hebrew people's spoken language in the first century, written in the square Hebrew alphabet. The fathers use "Hebrew" for both.

### PART B, ONE BOOK UNDER SEVERAL NAMES

Here are all the names the book goes by, where each comes from, and who used it.

| Name | Where it comes from | Who is said to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Matthew "in the Hebrew dialect" | Papias, Irenaeus, Origen, Eusebius, Jerome | The Hebrew believers |
| Gospel according to Matthew | Irenaeus 1.26.2, Epiphanius 30.3.7 and 30.13.2 | The Ebionites |
| Gospel according to the Hebrews | Papias via Eusebius 3.39.17, Hegesippus via Eusebius 4.22.8, Clement, Origen, Eusebius 3.25.5 and 3.27.4, Jerome | The Ebionites (Eusebius), the Nazarenes (Jerome) |
| "The Hebrew" Gospel | Epiphanius 30.13.2, quoting what the Ebionites called it | The Ebionites |
| Matthew complete in Hebrew | Epiphanius 29.9.4 | The Nazarenes |
| Gospel according to the Apostles | Jerome, Against the Pelagians 3.2, as another name for the Hebrews Gospel | The Nazarenes |
| Gospel of the Twelve | Origen, Homilies on Luke 1 | Not stated |
| The authentic Matthew | Jerome, Commentary on Matthew 12.13 | The Nazarenes and the Ebionites |
| "The Jewish" (to Ioudaikon) | Margin notes in Greek manuscripts of Matthew (Part F) | Not stated |
| Gospel of the Ebionites | Coined by the French scholar Richard Simon in 1689 | No ancient source |
| Gospel of the Nazarenes | Modern scholarly label, made standard in the twentieth century | No ancient source |

Read the table left to right and the identifications chain together. The Ebionites' book is Matthew (Irenaeus). The Ebionites' book is the Gospel according to the Hebrews (Eusebius). The Ebionites' Matthew is called the Hebrew Gospel (Epiphanius). The Nazarenes' book is the Gospel according to the Hebrews, which is the Gospel according to the Apostles, which is Matthew (Jerome). The Nazarenes and the Ebionites use the same Gospel, the authentic Matthew (Jerome). And the Ebionites' Gospel, as Epiphanius quotes it, is narrated by the twelve apostles in the first person, which is exactly what a book called "according to the Apostles" or "of the Twelve" would be (Part D, fragment D5).

### PART C, WHY THEY ARE THE SAME DOCUMENT

#### C1. The argument, as James R. Edwards makes it

James R. Edwards is a New Testament scholar, long a professor at Whitworth University in Spokane, Washington, and the author of major commentaries on Mark and Luke. In *The Hebrew Gospel and the Development of the Synoptic Tradition* (Eerdmans, 2009), he gathers every reference to a Gospel in Hebrew in Christian writing from the second century through the ninth, about seventy-five of them, and prints them in the original Greek and Latin in an appendix. Product page at [https://www.logos.com/product/49552/the-hebrew-gospel-and-the-development-of-the-synoptic-tradition](https://www.logos.com/product/49552/the-hebrew-gospel-and-the-development-of-the-synoptic-tradition). His conclusion is that these references describe one Hebrew Gospel, attributed to the apostle Matthew, and that the scholarly habit of splitting it into a Gospel of the Hebrews, a Gospel of the Nazarenes and a Gospel of the Ebionites is a modern construction that the ancient sources do not support. A review that summarizes his thesis fairly is at [https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/themelios/review/the-hebrew-gospel-and-the-development-of-the-synoptic-tradition/](https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/themelios/review/the-hebrew-gospel-and-the-development-of-the-synoptic-tradition/). The reasons follow.

#### C2. Every ancient witness speaks of one Hebrew Gospel

Across seven centuries of references, no father, friend or enemy, ever says there were two or three different Gospels in Hebrew. They call it by different names, but always in the singular, and they connect it to Matthew. The scholar who wants three Gospels has to say that the people who saw the book were all confused in the same way, and that we, who have never seen it, know better.

#### C3. The three-Gospel names are modern

"Gospel of the Ebionites" first appears in 1689, in the work of the French priest Richard Simon. No ancient author uses it. Epiphanius, the only father who quotes the book scholars give that name to, calls it the Gospel "according to Matthew" and says the Ebionites call it "the Hebrew" Gospel. "Gospel of the Nazarenes" is likewise a modern label. The full three-way division was worked out by German scholars in the twentieth century, most influentially Philipp Vielhauer and Georg Strecker in the standard handbook *New Testament Apocrypha*, edited by Wilhelm Schneemelcher. Before that, a single Hebrew Gospel was a common view, argued for example by E. B. Nicholson in *The Gospel according to the Hebrews* (1879). The split is a twentieth century hypothesis, not an ancient report.

#### C4. Jerome puts both groups on one book

The split depends on assigning the Ebionites one Gospel and the Nazarenes another. Jerome, the only father who says he copied the Nazarenes' book, says in plain words that it was "the Gospel which the Nazarenes and the Ebionites use" (*Commentary on Matthew* 12.13). Eusebius independently says the Ebionites' only Gospel was the Gospel according to the Hebrews. Jerome says the Nazarenes' Gospel was the Gospel according to the Hebrews. Two witnesses, one book.

#### C5. The differences are the differences of one book in several copies

The fragments do differ. Some are clearly from a Greek copy, some show signs of a Hebrew or Aramaic one, and the Ebionite copy lacked the opening genealogy and birth story. But the fathers themselves explain why. Papias says each church translated Matthew "as best he could." Jerome says he translated it into Greek and Latin. Epiphanius says the Nazarenes had it complete and the Ebionites had a shortened, altered copy of the same book, and his whole complaint is that the Ebionites cut and changed Matthew, not that they had some other Gospel. A book that circulates for three centuries in Hebrew, Aramaic, Greek and Latin, among scattered communities, with no central authority over its text, will differ from copy to copy. Our Greek Gospels did exactly that. Mark has three different endings in the manuscripts. Luke has the long and short forms of the Last Supper. Nobody concludes there were three Gospels of Mark.

#### C6. The theology is the same across the "three"

The fragments scholars assign to the three Gospels share the same distinctive beliefs. In the Ebionite fragments the Spirit descends and enters into Yeshua at the Jordan and the voice says "this day have I begotten you." In the Hebrews fragments the whole fount of the Spirit descends, rests on him, and calls him "my firstborn son," and Yeshua calls the Spirit "my mother." Both teach that Yeshua's sonship is revealed or conferred by the Spirit at the baptism. Both put the family of Yeshua in the story, his mother and brothers in the Hebrews fragments, his mother and brothers in the Ebionite fragments. Both are in the same Hebrew believers' world of law, prophets and the righteous poor. A scholar who reads these as three different theologies is dividing the material by the father who happened to quote it, not by what it says.

#### C7. The ancient corroboration points at Matthew

The Ebionite baptism scene has a great light shining around the place. That same light appears inside the text of Matthew 3.15 in two Latin manuscripts of the Gospels, Codex Vercellensis, copied in the fourth century and the oldest surviving Latin Gospel book, and Codex Sangermanensis, copied in 822 but preserving Old Latin readings in Matthew, which read that when he was baptized "a great light shone from the water, so that all who had come were afraid." Read at [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codex_Vercellensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codex_Vercellensis). Justin Martyr, around 155, says a fire was kindled in the Jordan when Yeshua went down into the water (*Dialogue with Trypho* 88). And the words "this day have I begotten you" stand in Luke 3.22 in Codex Bezae and in several early Latin witnesses, and Justin quotes them twice. The Ebionite fragment is not a late eccentric text. Its readings were in the oldest Latin Gospel book in existence and in the hands of a second century apologist, and the light reading was attached to Matthew specifically. Tier 1 for the manuscripts.

### PART D, THE FRAGMENTS PRESERVED BY EPIPHANIUS

These are the passages scholars call the Gospel of the Ebionites. Epiphanius quotes them in *Panarion* 30 to show how the Ebionites had altered Matthew. The order below is the order of the story. English follows the public domain translation of M. R. James (1924), lightly modernized. All are collected at [https://earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelebionites.html](https://earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelebionites.html). Tier 1 as quotations, Tier 2 because the quoter is hostile.

#### D1. The beginning, Panarion 30.13.6 and 30.14.3

"It came to pass in the days of Herod the king of Judea, when Caiaphas was high priest, that there came one named John, baptizing with the baptism of repentance in the river Jordan. It was said of him that he was of the lineage of Aaron the priest, a son of Zacharias and Elisabeth, and all went out to him."

The book opens with John, not with a genealogy or a birth story, the way Mark does. John is a priest of the line of Aaron. Luke 1.5 gives the same priestly descent but then, in Luke 1.36, adds that Elisabeth was a kinswoman of Mary, making John a relative of Yeshua. The Hebrew Gospel, as quoted, has the priestly line without the family tie. That is the simpler form, and the family tie looks like Luke's addition.

#### D2. John's food, Panarion 30.13.4 to 5

"And it came to pass that John was baptizing, and there went out to him Pharisees and were baptized, and all Jerusalem. And John had raiment of camel's hair and a leathern girdle about his loins, and his food was wild honey, whose taste was the taste of manna, as a cake dipped in oil."

Canonical Matthew 3.4 says John ate locusts and wild honey. Here there are no locusts. In Greek, locust is *akris* and cake is *enkris*, so Epiphanius accuses the Ebionites of changing one letter to make John a man who ate no living creature. But the phrase is not a random pun. "Its taste was like a cake made with oil" is the Greek Bible's own description of manna in Numbers 11.8, and Exodus 16.31 uses the same word *enkris* for manna. The fragment is making John the man who lived on the bread of the wilderness. Whichever reading came first, the Hebrew believers' Gospel had a Baptist who did not eat flesh, the same as the James the Just described by Hegesippus, who "ate no flesh" (Eusebius, *Church History* 2.23.5, [https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250102.htm](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250102.htm)).

#### D3. The baptism, Panarion 30.13.7 to 8

"When the people had been baptized, Yeshua also came and was baptized by John. And as he came up from the water the heavens were opened, and he saw the Holy Spirit in the form of a dove descending and entering into him. And a voice came from heaven, saying, You are my beloved Son, in you I am well pleased. And again, This day have I begotten you. And immediately a great light shone round about the place. And when John saw it, he said to him, Who are you, Lord? And again a voice from heaven said to him, This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased. Then John fell down before him and said, I pray you, Lord, baptize me. But he forbade him, saying, Let it be, for thus it is fitting that all things be fulfilled."

Three things. The Spirit enters into Yeshua, not merely rests on him. The voice quotes Psalm 2.7, "this day have I begotten you," the coronation psalm of the king of Israel, the same words Codex Bezae and Justin have at Luke 3.22. And the light is the light in the Old Latin Matthew 3.15 (Part C7).

#### D4. The family of Yeshua, Panarion 30.14.5

"Who is my mother, and who are my brothers? And he stretched out his hand toward his disciples and said, These are my brothers and mother and sisters, who do the will of my Father." Compare Matthew 12.48 to 50.

#### D5. The choosing of the twelve, Panarion 30.13.2 to 3

"There was a certain man named Yeshua, and he was about thirty years old, who chose us. And coming to Capernaum he entered into the house of Simon who was surnamed Peter, and opened his mouth and said, As I passed along by the lake of Tiberias, I chose John and James the sons of Zebedee, and Simon and Andrew and Thaddaeus and Simon the Zealot and Judas the Iscariot, and you, Matthew, I called as you sat at the tax office, and you followed me. You therefore I will to be twelve apostles for a testimony to Israel."

The Gospel is told by the apostles themselves, "who chose us," and Matthew alone is addressed directly, "and you, Matthew." A Gospel in the voice of the Twelve, singling out Matthew, is what Jerome's title "according to the Apostles" and the attribution to Matthew both describe. The twelve are chosen "for a testimony to Israel," not for the nations.

#### D6. The end of the sacrifices, Panarion 30.16.4 to 5

"I came to abolish the sacrifices, and if you do not cease from sacrificing, the wrath will not cease from you."

This is the saying for which the Hebrew Gospel is best known. It stands in the line of the prophets. Jeremiah 7.22, "I did not speak to your fathers or command them in the day that I brought them out of the land of Egypt concerning burnt offerings or sacrifices." Hosea 6.6, "I desire mercy and not sacrifice," which Yeshua quotes twice in canonical Matthew (9.13 and 12.7). Isaiah 1.11 to 15, "your hands are full of blood." The saying ties the coming wrath, which the Hebrew believers saw fall on Jerusalem in 70, to the continuing sacrifices.

#### D7. The Passover, Panarion 30.22.4

The disciples ask, "Where will you have us prepare for you to eat the Passover?" He answers, "Have I desired with desire to eat this flesh, the Passover, with you?"

Epiphanius complains that the Ebionites added the word "flesh" and turned the sentence into a question, so that Yeshua refuses the Passover lamb. Compare Luke 22.15 to 16, "With desire I have desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer, for I tell you I will not eat it until it is fulfilled in the kingdom of God." Two observations. First, "with desire I have desired" is a Hebrew idiom, the infinitive absolute, translated word for word into Greek. Luke's own wording of this line is a Semitism, exactly the kind of Hebrew trace Edwards argues Luke took from the Hebrew Gospel (Part H). Second, even Luke's Yeshua says, with the emphatic Greek double negative *ou mē*, that he will not eat it. The two versions agree that Yeshua did not eat the Passover lamb. They differ on whether he wanted to. When two forms of a saying exist and one is harder for later believers to accept, textual critics generally hold that the harder one is likelier to be original, because copyists smooth difficulties rather than create them. That rule is called *lectio difficilior*, the harder reading.

#### D8. What Epiphanius says the book left out

Epiphanius says the Ebionites' copy lacked the genealogies and began with John (30.14.3). He says they use the book because they deny the virgin birth (30.14.2 to 4). And he says they also read other books, chiefly the *Circuits of Peter*, which they claimed Clement of Rome wrote, in which Peter abstains from flesh and condemns the sacrifices (30.15.1 to 3). Tier 2.

### PART E, THE FRAGMENTS PRESERVED BY CLEMENT, ORIGEN, EUSEBIUS AND JEROME

These are the passages scholars divide between a "Gospel of the Hebrews" and a "Gospel of the Nazarenes." Collected at [https://earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelhebrews-mrjames.html](https://earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelhebrews-mrjames.html), [https://earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelhebrews-throck.html](https://earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelhebrews-throck.html) and [https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/nazoreans-ogg.html](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/nazoreans-ogg.html).

#### Sayings and stories

**E1. The Spirit at the Jordan.** Jerome, *Commentary on Isaiah* 11.2, quoting "the Gospel written in the Hebrew speech, which the Nazarenes read." "And it came to pass when the Lord had come up out of the water, the whole fount of the Holy Spirit descended upon him and rested on him, and said to him, My Son, in all the prophets I was waiting for you, that you should come and I might rest in you. For you are my rest, you are my firstborn Son, who reigns forever." The Spirit speaks as Wisdom speaks in Sirach 24.7, seeking a resting place among the people. Compare Isaiah 11.2, "the Spirit of the Lord shall rest upon him." Tier 1.

**E2. My mother the Holy Spirit.** Origen, *Commentary on John* 2.12.87, and *Homilies on Jeremiah* 15.4. Jerome repeats it in his commentaries on Micah 7.6, Isaiah 40.9 and Ezekiel 16.13. "Just now my mother, the Holy Spirit, took me by one of my hairs and carried me away to the great mountain Tabor." This is the temptation, told by Yeshua himself, with the Spirit carrying him the way the Spirit carried Ezekiel by a lock of his hair (Ezekiel 8.3). Jerome explains the "mother" plainly, in his comment on Isaiah 40.9, that the word for spirit, *ruach*, is feminine in Hebrew. Origen uses the saying respectfully, prefacing it with "if anyone accepts the Gospel according to the Hebrews." Tier 1. This is one of the strongest signs that the book was composed in a Semitic language, since in Greek the Spirit is neuter and in Latin masculine.

**E3. Seek, marvel, reign, rest.** Clement of Alexandria, *Miscellanies* 2.9.45.5, "as it is written in the Gospel according to the Hebrews, He who marvels shall reign, and he who has reigned shall rest." And 5.14.96.3, the fuller form, "He who seeks will not rest until he finds, and he who has found shall marvel, and he who has marvelled shall reign, and he who has reigned shall rest." Read at [https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/02102.htm](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/02102.htm) and [https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/02105.htm](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/02105.htm). The same saying, nearly word for word, is saying 2 of the Gospel of Thomas, and its Greek form survives on Oxyrhynchus papyrus 654 from around 200. Read Thomas at [https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/thomas-lambdin.html](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/thomas-lambdin.html). Compare Matthew 7.7, "seek and you shall find." Tier 1.

**E4. The appearance to James.** Jerome, *On Illustrious Men* 2, from "the Gospel also which is called according to the Hebrews, and which I have recently translated into Greek and Latin, which Origen also often uses." "And when the Lord had given the linen cloth to the servant of the priest, he went to James and appeared to him. For James had sworn that he would not eat bread from that hour in which he had drunk the cup of the Lord until he should see him rising again from among those that sleep." And a little later, "Bring a table and bread, said the Lord." And, "He took the bread, blessed it, broke it, and gave it to James the Just and said to him, My brother, eat your bread, for the Son of Man is risen from among those that sleep." Read at [https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2708.htm](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2708.htm). The risen Yeshua appears to James first, James was at the Last Supper, and he is recognized in the breaking of bread. Paul's list in 1 Corinthians 15.7 has an appearance to James. Luke 24.30 to 35 has an unnamed disciple who recognizes the risen Yeshua "in the breaking of the bread" at Emmaus. Tier 1.

**E5. Why should I be baptized.** Jerome, *Against the Pelagians* 3.2. "Behold, the mother of the Lord and his brothers said to him, John the Baptist baptizes for the remission of sins, let us go and be baptized by him. But he said to them, In what have I sinned, that I should go and be baptized by him? Unless perhaps this very thing that I have said is ignorance." Read at [https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3011.htm](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3011.htm). The whole family goes to John together, and the brothers are believers in the movement of repentance before the ministry begins. Tier 1.

**E6. Seventy times seven, and sin even in the prophets.** Jerome, *Against the Pelagians* 3.2. "If your brother has sinned in word and has made you amends, receive him seven times in a day. His disciple Simon said to him, Seven times in a day? The Lord answered and said to him, Yes, I tell you, until seventy times seven. For even among the prophets, after they were anointed with the Holy Spirit, a word of sin was found." Compare Matthew 18.21 to 22 and Luke 17.4. Tier 1.

**E7. The rich man and the law.** Origen, *Commentary on Matthew* 15.14, surviving in an old Latin translation. "The second of the rich men said to him, Master, what good thing must I do to live? He said to him, Man, fulfil the law and the prophets. He answered him, I have done that. He said to him, Go, sell all that you have and distribute it to the poor, and come, follow me. But the rich man began to scratch his head, and it did not please him. And the Lord said to him, How can you say, I have fulfilled the law and the prophets? For it is written in the law, You shall love your neighbor as yourself, and behold, many of your brothers, sons of Abraham, are covered in filth and dying of hunger, and your house is full of many good things, and nothing at all goes out of it to them. And he turned and said to Simon his disciple, who was sitting by him, Simon son of Jonah, it is easier for a camel to enter through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the kingdom of heaven." Compare Matthew 19.16 to 24. In the Hebrew Gospel the answer to "what must I do to live" is "fulfil the law and the prophets," and the law is read through love of neighbor. Tier 1.

**E8. The man with the withered hand.** Jerome, *Commentary on Matthew* 12.13, from "the Gospel which the Nazarenes and the Ebionites use." The man is a mason who says, "I was a mason, seeking a living with my hands. I pray you, Yeshua, restore my health to me, that I may not beg my bread in shame." Tier 1.

**E9. The talents.** Eusebius, *Theophany* 4.22, surviving in Syriac, from "the Gospel that has come down to us in Hebrew characters." There were three servants. One squandered his master's money with harlots and flute girls, one multiplied it, and one hid it. The one who multiplied it was accepted, the one who hid it was rebuked, and the one who squandered it was thrown into prison. Compare Matthew 25.14 to 30, where the man who hid the money is the one condemned. The Hebrew Gospel puts the judgment on the man who lived riotously. Tier 1.

**E10. I choose the worthy.** Eusebius, *Theophany* 4.12, from "the Gospel that is current among the Hebrews in the Hebrew tongue." "I choose for myself the most worthy. The most worthy are those whom my Father in heaven has given me." Compare John 17.6. Tier 1.

**E11. Never be glad.** Jerome, *Commentary on Ephesians* 5.4. "In the Hebrew Gospel we read that the Lord said to his disciples, And never be joyful, except when you look upon your brother with love." Tier 1.

**E12. The greatest sins.** Jerome, *Commentary on Ezekiel* 18.7. "In the Gospel according to the Hebrews, which the Nazarenes are accustomed to read, among the greatest crimes is counted, he who has grieved the spirit of his brother." Tier 1.

**E13. The woman accused of many sins.** Papias, reported by Eusebius 3.39.17 (Part A1). Eusebius gives no text. Many scholars connect it with the story of the woman taken in adultery, which is missing from the oldest manuscripts of John and floats between John 7.53 and Luke 21.38 in later ones. Tier 1 for the report, Tier 3 for the identification.

**E14. Not a bodiless spirit.** Jerome, *On Illustrious Men* 16, says that Ignatius of Antioch, around 110, quoted the Gospel Jerome translated when he wrote that the risen Yeshua said to Peter and those with him, "Take hold, handle me, and see that I am not a bodiless demon" (Ignatius, *Smyrnaeans* 3.2). Compare Luke 24.39. Origen attributes the same words to a book called the *Teaching of Peter*, so the source is disputed. Tier 2.

**E15. Matthew and Levi.** Didymus the Blind, an Alexandrian teacher of the fourth century, *Commentary on Psalms* 184.9 to 10, says the Gospel according to the Hebrews shows that Levi and Matthias are one man with two names, and Matthew is a different man. Tier 2, a single late reference in a papyrus commentary.

**E16. The pre-existence and the coming.** A Coptic sermon on Mary attributed to Cyril of Jerusalem (its authorship is doubted) quotes the Gospel of the Hebrews: when Christ wished to come to earth, the Father summoned a mighty power in heaven called Michael and entrusted Christ to it, and the power came into the world and was called Mary, and Christ was in her womb seven months. Tier 3. This fragment is late, in Coptic, attributed to a writer who probably did not write it, and it does not fit the other fragments. It is listed for completeness and should not be relied on.

#### Readings Jerome reports from the Hebrew text

**E17. Out of Egypt, and a Nazarene.** Jerome, *On Illustrious Men* 3. The Hebrew Matthew quotes "Out of Egypt have I called my son" (Hosea 11.1) and "he shall be called a Nazarene" from the Hebrew Bible rather than the Greek. These are Matthew 2.15 and 2.23. Two things follow. The Nazarenes' copy included Matthew's infancy chapter. And "he shall be called a Nazarene" is a quotation of the prophets, *netser*, the Branch of Isaiah 11.1, not a reference to a hometown. Tier 1.

**E18. Bread for tomorrow.** Jerome, *Commentary on Matthew* 6.11. Where the Greek Lord's Prayer has the mysterious word *epiousios*, which Jerome translated into Latin as "supersubstantial," the Hebrew Gospel had *mahar*, "tomorrow," "give us today our bread for tomorrow." Tier 1.

**E19. Hosanna in the highest.** Jerome, *Letter* 20.5, to Pope Damasus. Matthew, who wrote in Hebrew, put it "osanna barrama," hosanna in the highest. Read at [https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3001020.htm](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3001020.htm). Tier 1.

**E20. Son of Jehoiada.** Jerome, *Commentary on Matthew* 23.35. "In the Gospel which the Nazarenes use, instead of son of Barachiah we have found written son of Jehoiada." Canonical Matthew 23.35 says the murdered Zechariah was "son of Barachiah," which confuses him with the prophet Zechariah son of Berechiah (Zechariah 1.1). The Zechariah stoned in the Temple court was the son of the priest Jehoiada (2 Chronicles 24.20 to 22). The Hebrew Gospel has the correct father, and our Greek Matthew has the mistake. Tier 1. This is a point worth knowing, because it shows the Hebrew text better than the Greek at a place where they can be checked.

**E21. Barabbas.** Jerome, *Commentary on Matthew* 27.16. In "the so-called Gospel according to the Hebrews," Barabbas is interpreted as "son of their teacher." *Bar abba* is Aramaic for "son of the father," and the Hebrew Gospel reads *bar rabban*, son of their master. Tier 1.

**E22. The lintel of the Temple.** Jerome, *Commentary on Matthew* 27.51, and *Letter* 120.8 to Hedibia. "In the Gospel which is written in Hebrew letters we read, not that the veil of the Temple was torn, but that the lintel of the Temple, of immense size, fell." This happens at the death of Yeshua, not during the siege of 70. Compare Matthew 27.51. Josephus records omens before the war in which the great eastern gate of the inner Temple opened by itself (*Jewish War* 6.5.3), and the Talmud remembers the Temple doors opening of themselves forty years before the destruction (*Yoma* 39b), so a tradition of damage to the Temple's doorway around the time of the crucifixion is not unique to this Gospel. Tier 1.

### PART F, THE "JEWISH" READINGS IN THE MARGINS OF MATTHEW

#### What these are

About three dozen Greek manuscripts of the Gospels, the best known being minuscule 1424 from the ninth or tenth century, end with a note saying they were copied and corrected from the ancient manuscripts kept in Jerusalem on the holy mountain, Mount Zion. Scholars call this the "Zion Gospel" edition. In their margins, beside the text of Matthew, an early editor recorded how a book he calls *to Ioudaikon*, "the Jewish" Gospel, read at certain places. That book is the Hebrew Gospel or a Greek copy of it. The list follows Ron Cameron, *The Other Gospels* (Westminster, 1982), reproduced at [https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/nazoreans-ogg.html](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/nazoreans-ogg.html). Tier 2, real ancient readings but transmitted in medieval manuscripts.

- **Matthew 4.5.** The devil takes Yeshua not "into the holy city" but "to Jerusalem."
- **Matthew 5.22.** The words "without a cause" in "whoever is angry with his brother without a cause" are absent, so the saying forbids anger at a brother outright.
- **Matthew 7.5, or 7.21 to 23.** "If you are in my bosom and do not do the will of my Father who is in heaven, I will cast you out of my bosom."
- **Matthew 10.16.** "Wise more than serpents," instead of "wise as serpents."
- **Matthew 11.12.** The kingdom of heaven "is plundered."
- **Matthew 11.25.** "I thank you, Father."
- **Matthew 12.40.** The Hebrew Gospel lacks "three days and three nights" in the sign of Jonah.
- **Matthew 15.5.** "Corban is what you should obtain from us."
- **Matthew 16.2 to 3.** The weather sayings, "when it is evening you say fair weather," are absent, as they are in Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus.
- **Matthew 16.17.** Peter is "son of John," not "son of Jonah." Compare John 1.42 and 21.15, which also say son of John.
- **Matthew 18.22.** After "seventy times seven," the addition, "For even among the prophets, after they were anointed with the Holy Spirit, a word of sin was found." This matches Jerome's quotation in E6, which ties the Zion margin to the same book Jerome read.
- **Matthew 26.74.** "And he denied, and swore, and cursed."
- **Matthew 27.65.** "And he gave them armed men, that they might sit over against the cave and guard it day and night."

Note what this list shows. Most of the readings are small variations on Matthew's own text, verse by verse. This is an edition of Matthew, close to our Greek Matthew in order and content, which is exactly what the fathers said it was. And one of its readings (Matthew 18.22) appears word for word in Jerome's "Gospel according to the Hebrews," connecting the scholars' "Nazarene" Matthew and their "Hebrews" Gospel through a single sentence.

### PART G, LATE AND MEDIEVAL WITNESSES

#### G1. The Historia Passionis Domini

A fourteenth century Latin work on the Passion cites "the Gospel of the Nazarenes" several times, including that at the word of Yeshua on the cross many thousands of the Hebrews standing around it believed. Tier 3. Its source is unknown and it may draw on earlier commentaries rather than on the book itself.

#### G2. Shem Tov's Hebrew Matthew

In 1380, in Castile, a Hebrew scholar named Shem Tov ben Isaac ben Shaprut wrote *Even Bohan*, "The Touchstone," a treatise arguing against Christianity, and copied into it the entire Gospel of Matthew in Hebrew, with his own comments. In 1987 and again in 1995, George Howard of the University of Georgia published the text with an English translation as *Hebrew Gospel of Matthew* (Mercer University Press). Howard argues that Shem Tov did not translate it himself but copied an older Hebrew text, and that a text of this type goes back much earlier. Among its features he lists the divine name written with a special abbreviation where a medieval Hebrew translator of a Christian book would never have put it, a larger role for John the Baptist, and readings that agree with the Old Syriac, the Old Latin, Codex Sinaiticus and the Gospel of Thomas against the standard Greek. Its Matthew 28.19 sends the disciples to teach all nations to keep everything Yeshua commanded, without the formula "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit." Ross Nichols has argued that its Matthew 23.2 to 3 tells the disciples to keep what Moses says, not what the Pharisees say, [https://bibleinterp.arizona.edu/comment/1477](https://bibleinterp.arizona.edu/comment/1477).

The honest position. Most reviewers accept Howard's first point, that Shem Tov copied an existing Hebrew Matthew, but think it is a free medieval translation from Greek or Latin, not a descendant of the ancient Hebrew Gospel. See the review at [https://library.biblicalarchaeology.org/department/bible-books-15/](https://library.biblicalarchaeology.org/department/bible-books-15/). Shem Tov's Matthew is Tier 3. It is a fourteenth century text, and nothing in this sheet depends on it. Where it agrees with an early witness, the early witness is the evidence.

There are two other medieval Hebrew Matthews, the Du Tillet text (copied from Hebrew believers' manuscripts in Rome and published in 1555) and the Münster text (1537). Tier 3 as well.

### PART H, THE HEBREW GOSPEL AND LUKE

The second half of Edwards's book makes a claim larger than the identity of the fragments. He argues that the Gospel of Luke used the Hebrew Gospel as a written source, and that this explains the most puzzling feature of Luke.

**The puzzle.** Luke writes the most polished Greek of the Gospels. Yet the material found only in Luke, called Special Luke or L, is full of Semitisms, Greek phrases that are word for word Hebrew. Edwards counts them and finds them concentrated in that Luke-only material, far more than in the parts Luke shares with Mark. A writer of good Greek does not invent bad Greek. The obvious explanation is that he was translating a Hebrew document.

**The clue from the fathers.** Luke says in 1.1 to 3 that many had already compiled accounts, handed down by eyewitnesses. The fathers say the first account was Matthew's, in Hebrew. And when the Hebrew Gospel fragments are set beside the canonical Gospels, many of their closest parallels are to Luke, not to Greek Matthew.

**Examples that line up.**

- The Baptist's priestly line. The Hebrew Gospel, "of the lineage of Aaron, a son of Zacharias and Elisabeth." Luke 1.5, the same. Only Luke adds the family tie to Mary (1.36).
- The baptismal voice. The Hebrew Gospel, "this day have I begotten you." Luke 3.22 in Codex Bezae, the same.
- The Passover. The Hebrew Gospel, "Have I desired with desire to eat this flesh, the Passover, with you?" Luke 22.15 to 16, "With desire I have desired," the same Hebrew idiom, and "I will not eat it." No other Gospel has the saying.
- The appearance in the breaking of bread. The Hebrew Gospel, the risen Yeshua breaks bread and gives it to James. Luke 24.30 to 35, the risen Yeshua is known "in the breaking of the bread" at Emmaus by Cleopas and an unnamed companion. Only Luke has the scene.
- Seven times in a day. The Hebrew Gospel, "receive him seven times in a day." Luke 17.4, "seven times in a day," against Matthew's form.
- Not a bodiless spirit. The fragment Jerome attributes to his Gospel, "handle me and see." Luke 24.39, "handle me and see, for a spirit does not have flesh and bones."

**What follows if Edwards is right.** The Hebrew Gospel is older than Luke and a source of Luke. Greek Matthew, in Edwards's view, is the last of the Synoptics, a separate work carrying the apostle's name and tradition. And the Hebrew believers' Gospel, the one the Nazarenes and Ebionites kept, is not a late sectarian rewrite of the church's Gospels. It stands near the head of the Gospel tradition. Where Luke and the Hebrew Gospel differ, as at the Passover, the question becomes which one changed the other, and the harder reading points to Luke softening the Hebrew Gospel, not the reverse.

Edwards himself states some of this with caution. He says the evidence is not conclusive on whether the Hebrew Gospel can account for all the material Matthew and Luke share (the material usually assigned to Q), and he does not attempt to reconstruct the book.

### PART I, A TIMELINE OF THE HEBREW GOSPEL

- **Thirties to sixties.** Matthew writes a Gospel in Hebrew for the Hebrew believers (Irenaeus, while Peter and Paul were in Rome; Eusebius, before Matthew left Judea).
- **Around 110 to 130.** Papias says Matthew compiled the oracles in Hebrew and each translated as best he could, and uses the Gospel according to the Hebrews.
- **Around 155.** Justin Martyr knows the fire in the Jordan and "this day have I begotten you."
- **Around 170.** Hegesippus, a Hebrew believer, quotes the Gospel according to the Hebrews.
- **Around 180.** Irenaeus says the Ebionites use only Matthew.
- **Around 180 to 190.** Pantaenus finds Matthew in Hebrew letters in India.
- **Around 200.** Clement of Alexandria quotes "he who marvels shall reign."
- **Around 230 to 250.** Origen quotes the Spirit as mother and the rich man, and says Matthew wrote first, in Hebrew.
- **Before 309.** A copy is placed in the library at Caesarea by Pamphilus.
- **Around 324.** Eusebius says the Ebionites use only the Gospel according to the Hebrews.
- **377.** Epiphanius quotes the Ebionites' Matthew, "I came to abolish the sacrifices."
- **392 to 415.** Jerome copies the Nazarenes' book at Beroea, says it is used by Nazarenes and Ebionites alike and called the authentic Matthew, and quotes it about twenty times.
- **Fifth century onward.** The Zion edition editor records "the Jewish" readings in the margins of Matthew.
- **1380.** Shem Tov copies a Hebrew Matthew into *Even Bohan*.
- **1689.** Richard Simon coins the name "Gospel of the Ebionites."
- **Twentieth century.** The three-Gospel division becomes standard in the Hennecke-Schneemelcher *New Testament Apocrypha*.
- **2009.** James R. Edwards argues for one Hebrew Gospel and its use by Luke.

### PART J, OBJECTIONS AND ANSWERS

**"Scholars agree there were three different Jewish-Christian Gospels."** The three-Gospel scheme is the majority view in modern handbooks, and it is a twentieth century reconstruction. No ancient source names three Gospels, or two. The names "Gospel of the Ebionites" and "Gospel of the Nazarenes" appear in no ancient document. Every father who mentions the book speaks of it in the singular and ties it to Matthew, and Jerome says the Nazarenes and Ebionites used the same one. The burden is on the person dividing the evidence to explain why every witness who saw the book was wrong. Edwards, a mainstream scholar publishing with a mainstream press, reviewed all seventy-five or so references and concluded they describe one book.

**"There are two different baptism stories, so there must be two Gospels."** This is the objection the reviewer John Kloppenborg raised against Edwards, [https://muse.jhu.edu/article/422277](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/422277). Look at what the passages are. Jerome's *Against the Pelagians* 3.2 is a scene at home before the baptism, the family proposing to go to John and Yeshua asking what sin he has committed. Jerome's *Isaiah* 11.2 is the descent of the Spirit as Yeshua comes out of the water. Epiphanius's 30.13.7 to 8 is the voice, the light and John's question. A reluctance at home, then the going, then the Spirit, then the voice and light, is one story in sequence. Where the extracts overlap, the Spirit descending and the Son declared, they agree in substance and differ in wording, as the canonical Gospels themselves do at the baptism. And the father quoting each piece chose the line that served his own argument, Jerome the Spirit for his comment on Isaiah 11, Epiphanius the voice and the begetting for his attack on Ebionite Christology.

**"The Ebionite fragments were written in Greek. Look at the akris and enkris pun."** The copy Epiphanius held was in Greek. Nobody disputes that, and it is what the fathers lead us to expect. Papias says Matthew was translated by each as best he could. Jerome says he translated the book into Greek and Latin himself. A Greek copy of a Hebrew book will carry Greek-language variants. Our Greek Gospels carry thousands. And the enkris reading is not just a pun. It is the Greek Bible's word for manna (Exodus 16.31, Numbers 11.8), which makes the reading a deliberate statement that John lived on the wilderness bread. A Greek copy tells you the language of the copy, not that it is a different book.

**"The Ebionite Gospel is just a harmony of Matthew, Mark and Luke."** The fragments do combine wording found in more than one canonical Gospel, the baptism with two voices being the main example. Two explanations are possible. Either a later writer stitched the canonical Gospels together, or the canonical Gospels each drew on a common earlier text and the Hebrew Gospel preserves more of it. Edwards's work on the Semitisms of Luke supports the second. The harmony objection assumes the canonical Gospels came first, which is the point in question.

**"The Gospel of the Hebrews calls the Spirit Jesus' mother, and the Ebionites said Joseph was his father, so they cannot be the same book."** This argument, from Vielhauer, confuses two kinds of sonship. The Spirit as mother in the Hebrew Gospel appears at the baptism and the temptation. It is about Yeshua's begetting as Son by the Spirit at the Jordan, "my firstborn Son" in Jerome's fragment and "this day have I begotten you" in Epiphanius's. That is exactly the Ebionite teaching, that Yeshua was born of Joseph and Mary and became the anointed Son when the Spirit came into him at the baptism. The Spirit-mother saying is not a rival birth story. It is the Ebionite theology in Semitic dress, made possible because spirit is feminine in Hebrew.

**"The Nazarene Gospel had Matthew's infancy chapter and the Ebionite Gospel did not."** Epiphanius explains this himself. He says the Nazarenes had Matthew complete and the Ebionites had a copy with the genealogies cut. He is describing two copies of one book, one complete and one shortened, and he says outright that the Ebionites' book is Matthew. The difference is between copies, not between books.

**"Jerome is unreliable. He probably never translated the whole thing, and he took some quotations from Origen."** Many scholars doubt that Jerome translated the entire book, and some of his quotations may come from earlier commentators. Grant all of that. The identity of the book does not rest on Jerome alone. Irenaeus says the Ebionites used only Matthew. Eusebius, independently, says the Ebionites used only the Gospel according to the Hebrews. Epiphanius, independently and with hostility, says the Ebionites called their Matthew the Hebrew Gospel. Jerome, who at minimum handled the Nazarenes' copy at Beroea and knew the copy at Caesarea, says the Nazarenes and Ebionites used one Gospel. Four witnesses across two hundred and fifty years, three of them independent of Jerome, agree.

**"Papias meant only a collection of sayings, the logia, not a Gospel."** Papias uses the same word, *logia*, for Mark's Gospel in the sentence just before, saying Peter did not make an ordered account of "the Lord's logia." Mark is a narrative Gospel. So for Papias *logia* covers a Gospel with stories in it. And every father after Papias understood him to mean a Gospel.

**"If Matthew wrote in Hebrew, why is our Matthew in Greek and copied from Mark?"** Our Greek Matthew is not a translation of the Hebrew Gospel, and this sheet does not claim it is. Edwards's view is that the Hebrew Gospel and Greek Matthew are separate works, and that the Hebrew one came first. Greek Matthew's use of Mark is no objection to a prior Hebrew Gospel. It is an objection only to the idea that Greek Matthew is that Gospel.

**"These are heretics' books. Why should anyone care?"** The fathers who preserved them did not treat the book as heretical. Origen used it, Clement introduced it with "it is written," Eusebius listed it among the books not universally received while noting the Hebrew believers' special delight in it, and Jerome called it the Gospel most people regarded as the authentic Matthew. What Epiphanius objected to was what it said, chiefly the end of the sacrifices and the flesh of the Passover. The people who kept it were the Hebrew believers of Judea, Galilee and Syria, the community James led, who had a better claim than any Gentile church to know what Yeshua taught.

**"Shem Tov's Hebrew Matthew proves the original was Hebrew."** It does not, and this sheet does not claim it does. It is a fourteenth century text, and most reviewers consider it a medieval translation. The case for the Hebrew Gospel rests on Papias, Irenaeus, Origen, Eusebius, Epiphanius and Jerome, not on Shem Tov.

**Modern scholarship**

- James R. Edwards, *The Hebrew Gospel and the Development of the Synoptic Tradition* (Eerdmans, 2009), [https://www.logos.com/product/49552/the-hebrew-gospel-and-the-development-of-the-synoptic-tradition](https://www.logos.com/product/49552/the-hebrew-gospel-and-the-development-of-the-synoptic-tradition). The case for one Hebrew Gospel and its use by Luke. Its appendix prints every patristic reference in the original language.
- Review of Edwards by Michael Thate, *Themelios*, [https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/themelios/review/the-hebrew-gospel-and-the-development-of-the-synoptic-tradition/](https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/themelios/review/the-hebrew-gospel-and-the-development-of-the-synoptic-tradition/)
