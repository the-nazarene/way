# Polemic Answers, The Q Source, Why We Use It and Why Scholarship Finds It Credible

> **What this sheet is for.** When the claim is made that the earliest layer of Yeshua's teaching has no cross, no atonement, no resurrection and no Paul, the usual reply is that this rests on Q, and that Q is a fantasy no one has ever seen. This sheet is the answer. It is written for a reader meeting the subject for the first time. It introduces every source before using it, defines every term, and walks each argument in full. It does four things in order. It explains what Q is and is not. It lays out, piece by piece, why the majority of New Testament scholars for a century and a half have concluded that a lost written source sits behind Matthew and Luke. It explains why Q matters and what, precisely, is and is not being claimed when it is used. And it gives the standard objections with their answers, including the honest limits of the hypothesis, because an argument that hides its weak points does not deserve to be believed. The last section gives the argument in short form.

> **A note on terms.** This sheet says "Hebrew" where most books say "Jewish," and "Yeshua" for Jesus. Scripture is quoted from the American Standard Version. References use a period, so Luke 11.2 is chapter eleven, verse two. Q passages are cited by their Luke chapter and verse with a Q in front, so Q 11.2 is the Q saying that stands at Luke 11.2; this is the convention of every modern edition and it is explained in Part A. Each point is tiered. **Tier 1** means a canonical text, a non Christian source, or a mainstream church father speaking against his own interest. **Tier 2** means a mainstream modern scholar, including believing scholars who concede the point. **Tier 3** means contested or inferential, useful but to be stated carefully.

---

### THE SHAPE OF THE ARGUMENT

The objection comes in two forms. The soft form says, "Q is just a theory." The hard form says, "Q is a liberal invention, nobody ever saw it, and you are using an imaginary book to overrule the real ones." The sheet answers both in four layers.

1. **Q is a hypothesis, and we say so.** Nobody claims a manuscript. The claim is that the roughly 235 verses Matthew and Luke share, which are not in Mark, came from a written source both of them copied. That is an inference from the texts, not a discovery in a cave. Part A.
1. **The inference is strong, and the people who hold it are not fringe.** Verbatim Greek agreement, the independence of Matthew and Luke from each other, the preservation of a common order, doublets, and the discovery of a real sayings gospel at Nag Hammadi all point the same way. The hypothesis has been the majority position since the 1860s and is held by Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran, evangelical and secular scholars. Part B.
1. **Q matters for a narrow and defensible reason.** Whatever one thinks of the document, the material itself is in the Bible, in two Gospels, and it is older than Mark. In that material Yeshua affirms the Torah down to the stroke of a letter, sends his students to Israel, promises the twelve tribes a restored kingdom, and never once mentions his death as a sacrifice for sin. That is the earliest reachable layer of what his followers thought he taught. Part C.
1. **The objections have answers, and the limits are real.** The best case against Q is the Farrer theory, that Luke simply copied Matthew. It is a serious minority view and is treated here as one. But even if Farrer is right, the sayings do not go away, they move from a lost document into the Gospel of Matthew, and nothing in the argument depends on the document rather than the sayings. Part D.

### PART A, WHAT Q IS AND IS NOT

#### A1. The Synoptic Problem

Matthew, Mark and Luke are called the *Synoptic Gospels*, from a Greek word meaning "seen together," because they tell the same story in largely the same order and often in the same words, so that they can be printed in parallel columns and read side by side. John stands apart. The *Synoptic Problem* is the question of how three documents came to share so much wording. Agreement this close in Greek cannot come from three men independently remembering Aramaic preaching. Somebody copied somebody. The problem is to work out who copied whom. Tier 1 for the texts themselves; anyone can lay them side by side at [https://biblehub.com/parallel/matthew/3-7.htm](https://biblehub.com/parallel/matthew/3-7.htm) and the like.

#### A2. Markan priority

The first result, settled for the great majority of scholars since the mid nineteenth century, is that Mark was written first and that Matthew and Luke each used Mark. About ninety percent of Mark reappears in Matthew and about half in Luke. Where all three overlap, Matthew and Luke follow Mark's order; when one of them departs from it, the other almost always stays with Mark. Mark's Greek is rougher and his portrait of the disciples and of Yeshua is less flattering, and it is far easier to explain Matthew and Luke smoothing Mark than Mark roughening them. This is called *Markan priority* and it is the floor the rest of the argument stands on. The Farrer theory discussed in Part D accepts it. Tier 2, as close to consensus as New Testament scholarship gets.

#### A3. The double tradition

Once Mark is subtracted, Matthew and Luke still share a large body of material that is not in Mark at all. Scholars call it the *double tradition*, because it is attested twice, as against the *triple tradition* that is in all three. It is almost entirely sayings and speeches: the preaching of John the Baptist, the temptation dialogue, the Beatitudes and the rest of the Sermon material, the Lord's Prayer, the mission instructions, "consider the lilies," the Beelzebul controversy, the lament over Jerusalem, the parable of the great supper, the sayings on the day of the Son of Man, and more. By most counts it comes to between 230 and 240 verses. Tier 1, this is simply a description of the texts.

#### A4. The name

Because Matthew and Luke do not seem to have known each other's Gospels (Part B3), the simplest explanation of shared material that is not in Mark is a second shared source, now lost. The German scholars who worked this out in the nineteenth century called it *die Quelle*, "the source," and the letter Q became its name. C. H. Weisse proposed the two source arrangement in 1838; H. J. Holtzmann's 1863 study made it the standard view in Germany; Adolf von Harnack reconstructed the text in 1907 (English, *The Sayings of Jesus*, 1908); and B. H. Streeter's *The Four Gospels* (1924) fixed it for the English speaking world (the full book is online at [https://gospelstudies.org.uk/biblicalstudies/pdf/4gospels_streeter/complete.pdf](https://gospelstudies.org.uk/biblicalstudies/pdf/4gospels_streeter/complete.pdf)). The resulting model is the *Two Source Hypothesis*: Matthew and Luke each had Mark and each had Q, and each worked independently. Tier 2.

#### A5. Why Q is cited by Luke's numbers

Q has no chapters of its own. Modern editions cite it by the Luke passage where each saying stands, because Luke is judged to have kept Q's order more faithfully than Matthew (Part B4). So "Q 11.2 to 4" means the Lord's Prayer as it appears at Luke 11.2 to 4, with the Matthew parallel at 6.9 to 13 understood. When this sheet writes Q 16.17, go to Luke 16.17 and Matthew 5.18 and compare them.

#### A6. What the reconstructions are

Since 1985 the International Q Project, a working group of more than forty scholars under the Society of Biblical Literature, has produced a critical text. *The Critical Edition of Q* (Fortress and Peeters, 2000), edited by James M. Robinson, Paul Hoffmann and John S. Kloppenborg, prints the Matthew and Luke texts in parallel, the reconstructed Greek of Q between them, and parallels from Mark, Thomas and John, with translations into English, German and French ([https://www.fortresspress.com/store/product/9780800631499](https://www.fortresspress.com/store/product/9780800631499)). A short English text, *The Sayings of Jesus: The Sayings Gospel Q in English* (Fortress, 2002), runs to about fifty pages. A free overview with the contents listed is at [https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/q.html](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/q.html). Be clear about what these are. They are reconstructions built by comparing two Gospels, graded by the editors' own confidence, and they say so on every page. They are not editions of a recovered manuscript. Anyone who calls *The Critical Edition of Q* "the text of Q" without that qualification is overstating, and this sheet does not. Tier 2.

#### A7. What Q contains

Following the Critical Edition's order, the document opens with John the Baptist's preaching of coming judgment (Q 3.7 to 9, 16 to 17), the temptation dialogue in the wilderness (Q 4.1 to 13), and then a long inaugural sermon: the Beatitudes, love of enemies, judging, the tree and its fruit, and the house on the rock (Q 6.20 to 49). It continues with the centurion's servant (Q 7.1 to 10), John's question from prison and Yeshua's words about John (Q 7.18 to 35), the cost of following (Q 9.57 to 60), the mission instructions to the seventy (Q 10.2 to 16), the thanksgiving to the Father (Q 10.21 to 24), the Lord's Prayer (Q 11.2 to 4), ask and it will be given (Q 11.9 to 13), the Beelzebul controversy and the return of the unclean spirit (Q 11.14 to 26), the sign of Jonah (Q 11.29 to 32), the lamp and the eye (Q 11.33 to 35), the woes on the Pharisees and lawyers (Q 11.39 to 52), fearless confession (Q 12.2 to 12), anxiety and the lilies (Q 12.22 to 31), treasure in heaven and the watchful servant (Q 12.33 to 46), the mustard seed and leaven (Q 13.18 to 21), the narrow door (Q 13.24 to 29), the lament over Jerusalem (Q 13.34 to 35), the great supper (Q 14.16 to 24), the lost sheep (Q 15.4 to 7), serving two masters and the permanence of the law (Q 16.13, 16 to 18), forgiveness and faith (Q 17.1 to 6), the day of the Son of Man (Q 17.23 to 37), the parable of the entrusted money (Q 19.12 to 26), and it closes with the promise that the twelve will judge the twelve tribes of Israel (Q 22.28 to 30). All of it can be read in Luke and Matthew at [https://biblehub.com/asv/luke/1.htm](https://biblehub.com/asv/luke/1.htm) and [https://biblehub.com/asv/matthew/1.htm](https://biblehub.com/asv/matthew/1.htm). Tier 1 for the texts, Tier 2 for the delimitation.

#### A8. What Q does not contain

This is the part that matters most, so it is stated carefully and only as far as the evidence goes. The double tradition has no birth story, no genealogy, no account of Yeshua's baptism as an event (only John's preaching), no Last Supper, no arrest, no trial, no crucifixion, no burial, no empty tomb, no resurrection appearance, and no commission to the Gentiles. It has no sentence that calls Yeshua's death a sacrifice, a ransom, an atonement, or a payment for sin. It never uses the word "church." It never mentions Paul. Its one reference to the cross is the saying that whoever does not carry his own cross cannot be a disciple (Q 14.27), which is about the follower's death, not the teacher's. The Critical Edition's own publisher description notes that narrative is largely absent and that "most conspicuously of all" the Passion Narrative is missing. Tier 1 for the absence, since anyone can check the list; Tier 3 for any claim about *why* it is absent, which Part D takes up.

#### A9. The date

Mark is generally placed between 65 and 75. Q has to be earlier than both Matthew and Luke, which used it, and since it does not know Mark's passion story and does not look back on the destruction of the Temple in 70 the way Matthew and Luke do, most scholars place it between the 40s and the 60s, in the same window as Paul's letters or earlier. Kloppenborg's reconstruction of its growth puts its formative layer in the 50s and its final form before 70. A minority allows a date in the 70s. The careful statement is "somewhere between the 40s and the 60s, before Mark," not a single year. Tier 2.

### PART B, WHY SCHOLARSHIP FINDS IT CREDIBLE

No single argument below is decisive on its own, and the sheet does not pretend otherwise. The case is cumulative. Each piece of evidence is something a reader can check in an English Bible, and each is better explained by a shared written source than by any alternative that has so far been proposed.

#### B1. The agreement is in Greek, and it is often word for word

The first and strongest fact. Yeshua taught in Aramaic. If Matthew and Luke were each independently writing down what they remembered of his preaching, or each translating their own oral tradition, they would agree in substance and differ in wording, the way two people retelling the same story do. Instead, in long stretches of the double tradition they agree in Greek, word for word, down to the particles and the word order.

The cleanest example is John the Baptist's sermon. Luke 3.7 to 9: "Ye offspring of vipers, who warned you to flee from the wrath to come? Bring forth therefore fruits worthy of repentance, and begin not to say within yourselves, We have Abraham to our father: for I say unto you, that God is able of these stones to raise up children unto Abraham. And even now the axe also lieth at the root of the trees: every tree therefore that bringeth not forth good fruit is hewn down, and cast into the fire." Set it against Matthew 3.7 to 10 at [https://biblehub.com/parallel/luke/3-7.htm](https://biblehub.com/parallel/luke/3-7.htm). In the Greek, of roughly sixty three words, about sixty are identical and in the same order. The only real difference is that Matthew writes "think not to say" where Luke writes "begin not to say."

The thanksgiving to the Father is nearly as tight. Luke 10.21 to 22, "I thank thee, O Father, Lord of heaven and earth, that thou didst hide these things from the wise and understanding, and didst reveal them unto babes... no one knoweth who the Son is, save the Father; and who the Father is, save the Son, and he to whomsoever the Son willeth to reveal him," against Matthew 11.25 to 27 ([https://biblehub.com/parallel/luke/10-21.htm](https://biblehub.com/parallel/luke/10-21.htm)). The lament over Jerusalem, Luke 13.34 to 35 against Matthew 23.37 to 39, is the same, with the rare word for "hen" and the unusual "how often would I have gathered thy children together, even as a hen gathereth her own brood under her wings" preserved in both ([https://biblehub.com/parallel/luke/13-34.htm](https://biblehub.com/parallel/luke/13-34.htm)). Ask, seek, knock, Luke 11.9 to 13 and Matthew 7.7 to 11, is near verbatim through the whole run ([https://biblehub.com/parallel/luke/11-9.htm](https://biblehub.com/parallel/luke/11-9.htm)).

That level of agreement in Greek requires a written Greek text in front of both authors. Oral tradition does not reproduce particles. The Q hypothesis and the Farrer theory agree on this point; the dispute between them is only whether the written text in front of Luke was a lost document or the Gospel of Matthew. Tier 1 for the texts, Tier 2 for the inference, which every side accepts.

#### B2. The agreement is uneven, which is what copying from a document looks like

If the verbatim stretches were the whole story, Farrer would have an easy case. But alongside passages that agree almost perfectly, the double tradition contains passages that share the same saying in visibly different wording: the Lord's Prayer (Luke 11.2 to 4 is much shorter than Matthew 6.9 to 13), the Beatitudes (Luke has four, in the second person, "Blessed are ye poor"; Matthew has eight, in the third, "Blessed are the poor in spirit"), the parable of the entrusted money (Luke's pounds, Matthew's talents), the great supper. Scholars have measured this. Agreement across the double tradition ranges from near one hundred percent down to around ten percent, with a spread in between. That pattern is what you get when two authors copy a common text and each freely rewrites parts of it. It is harder to explain if Luke is copying Matthew directly, because Luke would then be reproducing Matthew letter for letter in one paragraph and rewriting him almost beyond recognition in the next, with no obvious principle governing which. Tier 2; this is the variable agreement argument, and it is one of the main points in the modern defense of Q.

#### B3. Matthew and Luke do not seem to know each other

The Q hypothesis exists because the simpler alternative, that one of them copied the other, runs into a wall. The evidence that Luke did not use Matthew is of several kinds, and all of it is in the text.

First, where Matthew adds to Mark, Luke never has the addition. Matthew expands Mark's story with Peter walking on the water (Matthew 14.28 to 31), the blessing of Peter as the rock on which the church will be built (16.17 to 19), the dream of Pilate's wife and Pilate washing his hands (27.19, 24), the saints rising from their tombs at the crucifixion (27.52 to 53), and the guard at the tomb (27.62 to 66). If Luke had Matthew on his desk, he passed over every one of these and kept only Mark. Read them at [https://biblehub.com/asv/matthew/16.htm](https://biblehub.com/asv/matthew/16.htm) and [https://biblehub.com/asv/matthew/27.htm](https://biblehub.com/asv/matthew/27.htm) and look for them in Luke.

Second, where Matthew and Luke both add to Mark, they add differently. They give incompatible genealogies, incompatible birth stories, incompatible accounts of Judas's death (Matthew 27.3 to 10 against Acts 1.18 to 19), and incompatible resurrection geographies, Matthew in Galilee, Luke in Jerusalem. If Luke knew Matthew, he contradicted him at every point where he could have followed him.

Third, Luke never places the double tradition material where Matthew places it. Matthew gathers Yeshua's sayings into five great discourses, the Sermon on the Mount, the mission discourse, the parables, the community discourse, and the discourse on the end. Luke scatters the same sayings across his Gospel, mostly through the long travel narrative of chapters 9 to 18, and almost never in the Markan context where Matthew has them. Streeter's observation, still the standard form of the argument, is that if Luke had known Matthew he would have had to take the Sermon on the Mount apart and sprinkle its pieces through ten chapters for no reason anyone has ever been able to state. Tier 2 for the inference; Tier 1 for every text cited.

#### B4. A common order survives in both

When the double tradition passages are listed in the order they appear in Luke and then in the order they appear in Matthew, a shared sequence shows through, especially in the opening blocks: John's preaching, the temptation, the sermon, the centurion, the material about John the Baptist. Where the two orders diverge, Matthew's arrangement is explicable as topical, pulling sayings into his five discourses, while Luke's is harder to explain as a rearrangement of Matthew and easier to explain as the order of a source he kept. That is why Q is cited by Luke's chapters. Two writers independently following a common document would be expected to preserve its order in exactly this partial, lumpy way. Tier 2.

#### B5. Doublets

A *doublet* is the same saying appearing twice in one Gospel. Matthew and Luke each have several, and in a striking number of cases one copy stands in a Markan context and the other in a double tradition context. "Whosoever hath, to him shall be given" appears in Luke 8.18 (from Mark 4.25) and again at Luke 19.26 (in the parable of the pounds, with Matthew 25.29); Matthew has it at 13.12 (Mark) and 25.29 (Q). The saying about denying or being ashamed of the Son of Man is in Mark 8.38, copied at Luke 9.26 and Matthew 16.27, and again in a different form at Luke 12.8 to 9 and Matthew 10.32 to 33. The mustard seed is in Mark 4.30 to 32, and both Matthew (13.31 to 32) and Luke (13.18 to 19) have a version that agrees with each other against Mark and pairs it with the leaven, which Mark does not have. The Beelzebul accusation is in Mark 3.22 to 27, and Matthew 12.22 to 30 and Luke 11.14 to 23 share a longer version with sayings Mark lacks. The simplest account of a Gospel containing two versions of one saying, one in Markan dress and one not, is that the author had two sources and used both. Tier 2; this was already Streeter's argument.

#### B6. Alternating primitivity

If Luke copied Matthew, then wherever their versions of a saying differ, Matthew's should be the earlier form and Luke's the revision. That is not what we find. Sometimes Matthew looks earlier, sometimes Luke does, and the two alternate. Luke's Beatitude "Blessed are ye poor" (6.20) is almost universally judged more original than Matthew's spiritualized "poor in spirit" (5.3). Luke's short Lord's Prayer (11.2 to 4) is almost universally judged earlier than Matthew's expanded liturgical form (6.9 to 13), on the ordinary rule that scribes and editors lengthen prayers rather than cut them. But Matthew's "your heavenly Father will give good things" (7.11) is probably earlier than Luke's "will give the Holy Spirit" (11.13), which reads like Luke's own emphasis. Matthew's form of the lost sheep may be earlier than Luke's. When neither Gospel consistently has the earlier form, neither is the other's source; both are drawing on a third text and each is revising it in his own way. Tier 2; this argument is in every standard introduction.

#### B7. A real sayings gospel turned up

For a century the standing objection was that no such book as Q had ever existed anywhere: a gospel made of sayings alone, with no narrative, no passion and no resurrection, was a genre critics had invented to suit their theory. In 1945 the Nag Hammadi library was found in Egypt, and in it a complete Coptic text of the Gospel of Thomas, a collection of one hundred and fourteen sayings of Yeshua with no narrative frame at all, no birth, no miracles, no passion, no resurrection. Greek fragments of it had been found at Oxyrhynchus in 1897 and 1903 without being recognized. Read it at [http://www.earlychristianwritings.com/thomas.html](http://www.earlychristianwritings.com/thomas.html). About a third of its sayings have parallels in the double tradition. Thomas is not Q, its date is disputed, and some of its content is plainly later and Gnostic, but it proved that early Christians did produce books of exactly the shape Q was supposed to have. The objection from genre is dead. Tier 1 for the existence of Thomas.

#### B8. Papias and the logia

The earliest statement about the writing of the Gospels is from Papias, bishop of Hierapolis, writing around 100 to 130, preserved by Eusebius in *Church History* 3.39.16: "Matthew composed the oracles [*logia*] in the Hebrew dialect, and each one interpreted them as he was able." Read it at [https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm). Our Greek Matthew is not a translation from Hebrew or Aramaic, which nearly all scholars agree on, so Papias cannot be describing the Gospel we have. Older Q scholarship, from Schleiermacher on, suggested he was describing an earlier Hebrew sayings collection standing behind Matthew, which would make Papias the one ancient witness to something like Q. Modern scholars are divided; some read *logia* as "oracles" meaning scriptural proof texts, some read "Hebrew dialect" as "in a Hebrew manner," and some take Papias to be simply mistaken. This is corroboration, not proof. What can be said is that the earliest tradition about Matthew describes him as compiling sayings in the Hebrew tongue, that this does not match our Greek Matthew, and that it is at least consistent with a sayings collection standing behind it. Tier 3.

#### B9. Who holds it

The Two Source Hypothesis is not a position of the skeptics. It is the standard view in the Catholic Church's own scholarship: Joseph Fitzmyer, a Jesuit and one of the two or three most respected Catholic New Testament scholars of the twentieth century, defends Q in his Anchor Bible commentary on Luke. Christopher Tuckett, Anglican, Oxford, wrote *Q and the History of Early Christianity* (1996). John Kloppenborg, Toronto, a Catholic by training, wrote *The Formation of Q* (1987) and *Excavating Q* (2000), the two standard studies. Harry Fleddermann produced *Q: A Reconstruction and Commentary* (2005). The International Q Project ran for fifteen years under the Society of Biblical Literature, the main professional body of the field, with Robinson, Hoffmann and Kloppenborg as editors. Evangelical introductions by Carson, Moo and Morris, by Robert Stein and by Craig Blomberg all teach the Two Source Hypothesis as the most probable solution. When someone says Q is a liberal invention, the honest answer is that the seminaries that train Catholic priests and evangelical pastors teach it. Tier 2.

#### B10. The honest summary

The case for Q is that one lost document explains, at once, the verbatim Greek agreement, the variable agreement, the mutual independence of Matthew and Luke, the surviving common order, the doublets, and the alternating primitivity, while the alternatives each explain some of these and strain at others. It has been the majority view for about a hundred and sixty years and it remains the majority view. It is not a consensus, and this sheet does not call it one.

### PART C, WHY Q MATTERS

This part states the position and the reasons for it. Each claim is kept to what the texts support.

#### C1. It is the earliest reachable layer of what his followers thought he taught

Everything we know about Yeshua comes through documents written by other people after his death. The question is always which documents are closest to him. Paul's undisputed letters are the earliest Christian writings we possess, from the 50s, but Paul never met Yeshua during his life, says almost nothing about what Yeshua taught, and by his own account received his gospel "not from man" but by revelation (Galatians 1.11 to 12, [https://biblehub.com/asv/galatians/1.htm](https://biblehub.com/asv/galatians/1.htm)). Mark, the earliest Gospel, is from around 70. The Q material is earlier than Mark, as early as Paul or earlier, and unlike Paul it consists of the teaching. If you want to know what the first generation of his followers believed Yeshua had said, before the Gentile mission reshaped the story, this is as close as the evidence allows you to get. That is why it matters. Tier 2.

#### C2. It has no cross, no atonement and no resurrection

Part A8 listed what the double tradition lacks. Here is why it matters. The gospel preached in the churches today, and by Paul in the 50s, is that Yeshua died for our sins and rose again. The earliest collection of his teaching that we can reach contains neither. It contains a man announcing the kingdom of God, calling Israel to repentance, teaching the Torah, warning of judgment, and promising that his followers will sit on thrones judging the twelve tribes. His death is never explained as a sacrifice. The resurrection is never narrated. A community that compiled and preserved his sayings as its gospel, and that is what the Critical Edition's editors call it, a Sayings Gospel, was living on his words, not on a theory of his death. This is stated as the plain content of the document. The claim is not that Q's compilers denied the crucifixion; they were living after it and knew it had happened. The claim is that they did not build their faith on it, because their book does not. Tier 1 for the content, Tier 3 for inferences about the compilers' beliefs; the two should be kept apart.

#### C3. It affirms the Torah to the last stroke

Q 16.17, which is Luke 16.17 and Matthew 5.18: "But it is easier for heaven and earth to pass away, than for one tittle of the law to fall." Read it at [https://biblehub.com/asv/luke/16.htm](https://biblehub.com/asv/luke/16.htm) and [https://biblehub.com/asv/matthew/5.htm](https://biblehub.com/asv/matthew/5.htm). The "tittle" is the Greek *keraia*, the small hook or stroke that distinguishes one Hebrew letter from another. Q 11.42, which is Luke 11.42 and Matthew 23.23, rebukes the Pharisees for tithing their garden herbs while neglecting justice and the love of God, and then adds, "but these ought ye to have done, and not to leave the other undone," that is, keep tithing. In the earliest layer, Yeshua is a Torah teacher calling Israel back to the weightier matters of the law while refusing to let the lighter ones go. Put that beside Paul, who writes that "Christ is the end of the law" (Romans 10.4, [https://biblehub.com/asv/romans/10.htm](https://biblehub.com/asv/romans/10.htm)) and that the law was "our tutor to bring us unto Christ" and is now set aside (Galatians 3.24 to 25). The earliest Yeshua tradition and Paul are not saying the same thing about the Torah. Tier 1.

#### C4. It is a Hebrew document addressed to Israel

Q knows Galilean villages by name, Chorazin and Bethsaida and Capernaum (Q 10.13 to 15), and curses them for not repenting. Its mission instructions send the students out with no provisions, to Israelite towns, to announce that the kingdom of God has come near (Q 10.2 to 12). Its last saying promises the students that they will sit on thrones judging the twelve tribes of Israel (Q 22.28 to 30, Luke 22.28 to 30 and Matthew 19.28). It opens with John the Baptist warning the children of Abraham not to rely on their descent, which is an argument inside Israel, not outside it. Its one Gentile, the centurion, is praised precisely because such faith has not been found "in Israel" (Q 7.9), which makes him the exception that proves the audience. There is no Gentile mission in Q, no table fellowship controversy, no circumcision debate. Kloppenborg's *Excavating Q* argues at length that the document comes from Torah observant villagers in Galilee in the decades before the war. The world of Q is the world of the Nazarenes and of James's Jerusalem church, not of Antioch and Corinth. Tier 2 for the setting, Tier 1 for every text.

#### C5. It stands close to James

The Epistle of James, attributed to Yeshua's brother and the head of the Jerusalem church, is the one New Testament letter that reads like the Q material. It is wisdom teaching, not doctrine about a death. And it echoes Q saying after saying: ask of God who gives to all liberally (James 1.5, with Q 11.9); be doers of the word and not hearers only, like a man who looks in a mirror and forgets (James 1.22 to 25, with Q 6.46 to 49, the house on the rock); God has chosen the poor to be heirs of the kingdom (James 2.5, with Q 6.20); do not swear at all, but let your yea be yea (James 5.12, with Matthew 5.34 to 37); do not judge your brother (James 4.11 to 12, with Q 6.37); the rich should weep for the miseries coming on them (James 5.1, with Q 6.24). Read James at [https://biblehub.com/asv/james/1.htm](https://biblehub.com/asv/james/1.htm). Patrick Hartin's *James and the Q Sayings of Jesus* (1991) is the standard study of the overlap. James never quotes Yeshua by name and the parallels are to the sayings' substance rather than their exact Greek, so this is not proof that James used Q. It is evidence that the brother of Yeshua and the compilers of Q were drawing on the same stream of teaching and that the stream had the same shape: Torah, wisdom, the poor, the coming judgment, no atonement. Tier 2 for the parallels, Tier 3 for dependence.

#### C6. What is and is not being claimed

This is the line the objection tries to blur, so it is stated plainly. The claim is not that Q is a recovered book. The claim is not its exact wording. No theology is being built on Kloppenborg's three strata. The claim is three things. First, that the double tradition exists, in the Bible, in two Gospels, and that it is the earliest body of Yeshua's teaching that can be isolated, which the great majority of scholars accept. Second, that its content is what Part C2 to C5 describe, which anyone can verify by reading Luke 3 through 22 with Matthew open beside it. Third, that this content matches James and the Nazarenes and does not match Paul. None of those three claims depends on whether Q was one document or several, or on whether Luke read Matthew.

### PART D, OBJECTIONS AND ANSWERS

**"Nobody has ever seen Q. No manuscript, no quotation, no church father."** True, and nothing in this sheet says otherwise. Q is inferred from Matthew and Luke the way a shared ancestor is inferred from two descendants. Mark's source for Peter's preaching has no manuscript either, and the Hebrew Matthew that Papias, Irenaeus, Origen, Eusebius, Epiphanius and Jerome all describe has no surviving manuscript either, yet the church fathers never doubted it existed. Documents got lost. Once Matthew and Luke had absorbed Q, nobody had a reason to keep copying it, which is exactly what happened to the Hebrew Gospel the fathers do mention. The absence of a manuscript is expected, not suspicious.

**"Luke just used Matthew. Occam's razor."** This is the Farrer theory, named for Austin Farrer's 1955 essay "On Dispensing with Q," carried forward by Michael Goulder and today by Mark Goodacre of Duke, whose *The Case Against Q* (2002) is the best statement of it, with a companion site at [https://markgoodacre.org/Q/index.htm](https://markgoodacre.org/Q/index.htm) and a short list, "Ten Reasons to Question Q," linked from it. It deserves respect and it is a minority view. Its problems are the ones in Part B3: it has to explain why Luke took apart the Sermon on the Mount and scattered it, why he dropped every one of Matthew's additions to Mark, why he contradicts Matthew's genealogy, birth story and resurrection geography, and why he sometimes has the more primitive form of a saying that he is supposed to be copying from Matthew. Goodacre has answers to each, and the debate is real. Alan Garrow has recently argued the reverse direction, that Matthew used Luke ([https://www.alangarrow.com/mch.html](https://www.alangarrow.com/mch.html)), which shows how open the question is among the minority. Occam's razor cuts both ways: one lost document is a simpler explanation of six separate phenomena than six separate explanations.

**"The minor agreements prove Luke knew Matthew."** The *minor agreements* are places where Matthew and Luke agree against Mark in a passage all three share, which should not happen if they used Mark independently. The famous one is at the mocking of Yeshua: Mark 14.65 has the guards say "Prophesy," while Matthew 26.68 and Luke 22.64 both add "who is he that struck thee?" ([https://biblehub.com/parallel/matthew/26-68.htm](https://biblehub.com/parallel/matthew/26-68.htm)). This is the strongest single argument against the Two Source Hypothesis and it should be conceded as a real difficulty. The standard answers are that some agreements are coincidental improvements of Mark's Greek any two editors would make, that some arise where Mark and Q overlapped so both Matthew and Luke had a second version to draw on, and that a few, including this one, may be the result of early scribes harmonizing one Gospel to another, since the manuscripts of Luke 22.64 are not uniform. Richard Vinson's essay "How Minor?" in *Questioning Q* (2004) is the fair statement of how far they go. They are a problem; they are not a refutation.

**"Q has no passion story because it was only a supplement. The compilers assumed the cross."** This is possible, and the sheet has already said that the compilers are not claimed to have denied the crucifixion. But notice what the objection concedes: that a community in the first generation, which knew Yeshua had died, chose to write a gospel of his sayings without a word about the atonement. If the cross had been the center of their faith, it is strange to leave it out of their one book. Mark, writing a few years later, could not imagine telling the story without it. The difference between a sayings gospel and a passion gospel is a difference in what the authors thought mattered.

**"This uses Kloppenborg's layers. That is speculation stacked on speculation."** It does not. Kloppenborg's *The Formation of Q* (1987) proposed that Q grew in three stages, a formative layer of wisdom speeches, a redactional layer of judgment sayings, and a late addition of the temptation story (the scheme is summarized at [https://hts.org.za/index.php/hts/article/view/2938/5820](https://hts.org.za/index.php/hts/article/view/2938/5820)). It is influential and it is contested, including by scholars who accept Q. Nothing in Part C uses it. Every text cited is from Q as a whole, which is to say from Luke and Matthew.

**"The Jesus Seminar built on Q, so Q is a Jesus Seminar thing."** The Jesus Seminar used Q, and so did every Catholic and evangelical introduction to the New Testament written in the last century. The hypothesis predates the Seminar by a hundred and fifty years and is taught at the Pontifical Biblical Institute and at Dallas Theological Seminary. Who else used a tool does not change what the tool shows.

**"Papias says Matthew wrote the logia, so the sayings source is just Matthew."** Papias says Matthew wrote them "in the Hebrew dialect" and that others translated them as they could. Our Matthew is a Greek composition that uses the Greek Mark, which nearly every scholar on every side agrees is not a translation from Hebrew. So either Papias is wrong or he is describing a different document from the Greek Matthew we have. Either way he is not a witness against a sayings collection; if anything he is the one ancient witness for one.

**"Even if Q existed, it is a reconstruction, and you cannot build on a reconstruction."** Correct, which is why nothing here is built on the reconstruction. It is built on the verses in Luke and Matthew that the reconstruction is made from. When the sheet says "Q 16.17 says not one stroke of the law will fall," the reader can open Luke 16.17 and Matthew 5.18 and see it in the canon. The hypothesis is about where those verses came from, and it supports an early date for them. The verses themselves need no hypothesis.

**"Suppose Farrer is right and there was no Q. Then what?"** Then the double tradition sayings were composed or collected by the author of Matthew around 80 to 90, and Luke copied them from him. The sayings do not vanish. They are still the words the earliest Gospel tradition put in Yeshua's mouth, still Torah affirming, still addressed to Israel, still silent on atonement. What is lost is the claim that they predate Mark; everything else stays. That is why the argument is built on the content and not on the document.

---

### PART E, THE ARGUMENT IN BRIEF

Three sentences that carry the whole sheet, for anyone who wants to state the case accurately without overstating it.

1. Q is a scholarly reconstruction from Matthew and Luke, not a recovered book; cite it by Luke's chapter with the Matthew parallel beside it, and anyone who checks will find every verse in the Bible.
1. The point is the content, not the hypothesis. "The earliest layer of his teaching that scholars can isolate" is the accurate description, and it stands or falls on what Luke and Matthew say.
1. The Farrer theory is a real minority view, and the argument survives it, because the sayings are in the canon either way (Part D, last answer).

---

### PART F, SHORT VERSIONS

**Standard version.**
Q is a reconstruction, nobody is claiming a manuscript, and it's been the majority position in New Testament scholarship since the 1860s. It is taught in Catholic and evangelical seminaries, Fitzmyer the Jesuit defended it, the Society of Biblical Literature ran a fifteen year project to establish its text. The reason is in your Bible. Matthew and Luke share about 235 verses that aren't in Mark, often word for word in Greek, and they never put them in the same place, never share each other's additions to Mark, and contradict each other's birth and resurrection stories. That's two writers copying one document, not one copying the other. And here is why it matters. That document, the earliest layer of his teaching anyone can isolate, has no cross, no atonement, no resurrection, no church and no Paul. It has Yeshua saying not one stroke of the Torah will fall (Luke 16.17, Matthew 5.18), sending his students to Israel, and promising them thrones over the twelve tribes. You can read every verse of it in Luke and Matthew. The hypothesis is about where those verses came from. The verses are in the canon.

**Short version.**
Q is a scholarly reconstruction and nobody serious claims otherwise. It's also the majority view in the field, held by Catholic, Anglican and evangelical scholars, because Matthew and Luke share 235 verses not in Mark, often verbatim in Greek, while contradicting each other everywhere else. Two copies of one source. And every verse of it is in your Bible, which is where it gets cited from. In that earliest layer he affirms the Torah to the last stroke and never says a word about dying for sins. Check Luke 16.17.

**"Luke just copied Matthew" version.**
That's the Farrer theory, Goodacre's view, and it's a real minority position. It has to explain why Luke took the Sermon on the Mount apart and scattered it over ten chapters, dropped every one of Matthew's additions to Mark, including Peter the rock, and contradicted Matthew's genealogy, birth story and resurrection geography while supposedly copying him. But say it's right. Then the sayings were put in his mouth by the author of Matthew around 85 instead of by a Galilean source in the 50s. They still affirm the Torah to the stroke, still send the students to Israel only, and still never mention atonement. You moved the sayings from a lost book into the Gospel of Matthew. That doesn't help you.
