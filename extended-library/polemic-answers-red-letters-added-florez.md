# Polemic Answers, The Red Letters That Were Added, Every Saying of Yeshua That Scholarship Says He Never Said


> **What this sheet is for.** A red-letter Bible prints the words of Yeshua in red so the reader can treat them as the one part of the book that comes straight from his mouth. This sheet shows, passage by passage, that the red ink is not a transcript. Some red sentences were written into the text by copyists in the centuries after the Gospels were finished, and the church's own Greek editions now mark them as additions. Other red sentences are in every manuscript but were composed by the Gospel writers, and mainstream scholarship, including many believing scholars, says so. The sheet is written so that someone with no background can read it from the top and come away able to explain each case, name the sources, and know which claims are safe to say on camera and which are not.


> **A note on terms.** This sheet says "Hebrew," not "Jewish," for Yeshua and the early believers. It uses "Yeshua" for Jesus. It quotes the American Standard Version (ASV, 1901), and where the ASV already omits a disputed passage it says so and gives the King James wording. Scripture references are written with a period, so Mark 16.9 means chapter 16 verse 9. Every passage carries one of three tiers. **ESTABLISHED** means the words are bracketed or omitted in the critical Greek text, or the point is near-consensus among critical scholars, and conservative scholars concede it. **CONTESTED** means serious, qualified scholars are divided and the sheet reports both sides. **QUARANTINED** means a popular claim with no scholarly support in the form it circulates; the sheet explains why it fails and what the defensible version is. Do not use a QUARANTINED claim on camera.


## THE SHAPE OF THE ARGUMENT

1. Red ink is a publisher's decision first made in 1899, not a feature of any ancient manuscript, and the manuscripts themselves disagree about which sentences belong in the text at all (Terms, and Part A).
1. A first group of red-letter passages, including the last twelve verses of Mark and the woman taken in adultery, is missing from the oldest Greek copies and was written in by later scribes; the standard scholarly Greek New Testament, the one behind the NIV, ESV, NRSV, and the Catholic NABRE, brackets them or leaves them out (Part A).
1. The people who admit this are the church's own textual critics: the editors of that Greek text were churchmen, and the leading evangelical textual critic in America agrees that Mark did not write Mark 16.9 to 20 (Part A, entries A1 and A2).
1. A second group is in every manuscript, so the Greek is secure, but the Gospel writers composed it: Matthew's baptismal formula, Matthew's "upon this rock," Mark's "making all meats clean," John's long speeches in which Yeshua talks about himself; here the argument is literary and historical, and the mainstream of critical scholarship makes it, not only its skeptical wing (Part B).
1. When scribes changed the text on the nature of Yeshua, they changed it in one direction, toward a higher Son and away from anything that could support a merely human or adopted Messiah; the direction is itself evidence of what the earlier text said (Part A, christological sub-section).
1. Whole doctrines rest on these specific sentences: baptism as the condition of salvation and snake-handling on Mark 16, the Trinitarian formula on Matthew 28.19, the papacy on Matthew 16.18, the cup as blood covenant on Luke 22.20 and John 6, the end of the food laws on Mark 7.19, the divorce exception on Matthew 19.9, the deity of the Son on John (Part C).
1. Every standard objection has an answer drawn from the church's own sources, and the sheet closes with paste-ready comments and a list of items still to check before anything is said in public (Parts D and E, and the closing callout).
1. Behind all of it stands the question of where the Greek Gospels came from in the first place: the earliest sources show they were assembled from a multitude of earlier texts, the Hebrew Gospel among them, so every red letter passed through at least one earlier document before an evangelist arranged it (Part F).

## TERMS YOU WILL MEET

**Textual criticism** is the discipline of comparing the surviving handwritten copies of a text, noting where they differ, and working out which reading is earliest. Origen practiced it in the third century and Jerome in the fourth; today it is practiced at Dallas Theological Seminary and Tyndale House in Cambridge, two of the most conservative evangelical research centers in the world. Every modern Bible is a product of it.

**Manuscript** means a handwritten copy. No original of any New Testament book survives; we have several thousand Greek copies made between about 125 and the invention of printing, and they do not all agree. Where they differ we speak of a "variant reading."

**Papyrus** is the reed paper of the earliest copies, surviving as fragments, mostly from Egypt, numbered with a P. P66 is a copy of John from about 200; P75 holds Luke and John from the third century and is one of the most carefully copied manuscripts in existence; P45 is a third-century copy of the Gospels and Acts. When a papyrus from the year 200 lacks a passage, no fourth-century copy can rescue it.

**Codex, uncial, minuscule.** A codex is a bound book. The great fourth- and fifth-century codices are written in capitals called uncials and cited by capital letters. Later copies, from the ninth century on, use a small joined script, are called minuscules, and are cited by plain numbers (33, 565, 700); related minuscules form families, f1 and f13. Minuscules are far more numerous, so "the majority of manuscripts" nearly always means "the later manuscripts."

**Codex Sinaiticus** (ℵ), mid-fourth century, found at St. Catherine's Monastery in the Sinai, is the oldest complete New Testament. Its original scribe is written ℵ*, later correctors ℵ1, ℵ2a, and so on. It is online at [https://codexsinaiticus.org/en/](https://codexsinaiticus.org/en/). **Codex Vaticanus** (B), also fourth century, has been in the Vatican Library since at least the fifteenth century. Together they are the most important witnesses to the text, and when they agree with the early papyri the critical editions nearly always follow them. The Vatican's own oldest Bible lacks the last twelve verses of Mark.

**Codex Bezae** (D) is a fifth-century Greek and Latin codex given to Cambridge by the Reformer Theodore Beza, and the chief Greek witness to the "Western" text, a freer form that circulated in the Latin world. When Bezae agrees with Sinaiticus and Vaticanus against everything else, two very different streams are testifying together. Also cited here: Alexandrinus (A) and Ephraemi Rescriptus (C), fifth century; Washingtonianus (W), fifth century, in the Smithsonian's Freer Gallery; Regius (L), eighth century but copied from a good early exemplar; and Bobiensis (it^k), an Old Latin copy from about 400.

**Text types.** The Alexandrian text (Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, the papyri, the Coptic) is the shortest and most carefully copied and is regarded as closest to the originals. The Western text (Bezae, the Old Latin) is early but free. The Byzantine or Majority text became standard in the Greek church from about the ninth century; it is the fullest form because it absorbed additions over centuries, and it survives in the most copies because that church kept copying it.

**Textus Receptus and the King James Version.** The Textus Receptus is the printed Greek text first produced by Erasmus in 1516 from a handful of late Byzantine minuscules. The King James of 1611 was translated from it. Nearly every passage in Part A is in the KJV because it is in the Textus Receptus, and absent from modern Bibles because it is absent from older manuscripts Erasmus never saw.

**NA28 and UBS5.** The Nestle-Aland Novum Testamentum Graece, 28th edition, and the United Bible Societies Greek New Testament, 5th edition, print the same Greek text and are the standard editions, produced by an international committee of Protestant and Catholic scholars for Bible translators. The NIV and ESV are translated from them. **Double brackets**, [[ ]], mean the editors judge the words not original but print them for their antiquity and place in tradition. **Single brackets**, [ ], mean the editors are uncertain. The UBS edition grades its confidence in the printed text: {A} certain, {B} some doubt, {C} considerable doubt, {D} very high doubt.

**Interpolation** is a passage inserted by someone other than the author; in Part A it means words a copyist added. It does not imply malice; most were liturgical habits, marginal notes copied into the text, or attempts to make one Gospel match another. **Harmonization** is a change that makes one Gospel agree with another; most small additions in Part A are harmonizations. A **floating passage** appears in different manuscripts at different places; authors do not write paragraphs that wander, so floating is the fingerprint of an insertion. A **Western non-interpolation** is Westcott and Hort's 1881 term for a few places where the Western text is shorter than everything else and they judged the short reading original.

**Versions** are ancient translations: the Old Latin (it, with a letter per codex), the Vulgate (Jerome's Latin revision, about 400), the Syriac (Sinaitic and Curetonian Old Syriac, Peshitta, Harklean), the Coptic (Sahidic, Bohairic), and the Armenian, Georgian, Ethiopic, and Gothic. Each shows what Greek text its translator had, often earlier than our surviving Greek copies.

**Church fathers and patristic citation.** When an early writer such as Irenaeus (about 180), Clement of Alexandria (about 200), Tertullian (about 200), Origen (about 230), Eusebius (about 320), or Jerome (about 400) quotes a verse, we learn what text existed in his time and place. A father who quotes a verse proves it existed; a father who comments on a chapter line by line and skips a passage proves it was absent from his copy.

**Bruce Metzger** (1914 to 2007), of Princeton Theological Seminary, was an ordained Presbyterian minister, chaired the NRSV committee, and sat on the UBS editorial committee for decades. His *Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament* (2nd edition, 1994) records the committee's reasoning. When this sheet says "Metzger," it is quoting the most respected churchman in the field.

**Bart Ehrman**, Metzger's doctoral student, now at the University of North Carolina, wrote *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* (1993) and *Misquoting Jesus* (2005). He is an agnostic and the best-known voice arguing that scribes changed the text for doctrinal reasons. The sheet uses him where his argument is strong and reports where others disagree; on several items he defends the traditional reading against the critical text.

**Daniel Wallace** is a professor at Dallas Theological Seminary and founder of the Center for the Study of New Testament Manuscripts (CSNTM), which photographs manuscripts worldwide. He is the standing evangelical concession in this sheet: when Wallace agrees a passage is not original, no one can call the point a liberal attack. **Tyndale House Greek New Testament (2017)**, edited by Dirk Jongkind and Peter Williams at the evangelical center in Cambridge, is more conservative than NA28 in a few places and marks disputed passages with a diamond; where it keeps a passage, that is the strongest conservative position in print.

**Redaction criticism.** A redactor is an editor. Redaction criticism studies how each Gospel writer (each "evangelist") edited the sources in front of him. Mark was written first, about 70; Matthew and Luke each used Mark and a sayings source scholars call Q; John came last, about 90 to 100. When Matthew's version of a scene adds three verses Mark lacks, redaction criticism asks whether they are Matthew's own. Part B rests on this method. **Synoptic** means Matthew, Mark, and Luke, which share most of their material.

**Jesus Seminar.** A group convened by Robert Funk in 1985, about 150 members at its height, scholars and lay people, who voted on each saying with colored beads and published *The Five Gospels* (1993). The book's key: red, "Jesus undoubtedly said this or something very like it"; pink, "Jesus probably said something like this"; gray, "Jesus did not say this, but the ideas contained in it are close to his own"; black, "Jesus did not say this; it represents the perspective or content of a later or different tradition" (quoted at [https://bible-researcher.com/5gospels.html](https://bible-researcher.com/5gospels.html)). The Seminar is one voice, tilted toward a non-apocalyptic Yeshua, and treated skeptically by Dale Allison, John Meier, E. P. Sanders, and N. T. Wright. The sheet reports its colors because people ask, not as consensus. Colors for sayings shared with Luke are taken from the Seminar's published vote tallies, posted by the Westar fellow Mahlon Smith at [https://virtualreligion.net/forum/RLQ/q_segments.html](https://virtualreligion.net/forum/RLQ/q_segments.html); where a color could not be confirmed, the sheet says so and does not give one.

**Docetism and adoptionism.** Docetism held that Christ only seemed to have a body and did not really suffer. Adoptionism held that Yeshua was a man who became God's Son at his baptism or resurrection rather than being God from eternity. Many scribal changes in Part A read as attempts to make the text harder to use for one or the other. **Orthodox corruption** is Ehrman's term for a change made to protect the scribe's own doctrine; his thesis is that such changes run one way, from lower to higher Christology.


## PART A, WORDS ADDED BY SCRIBES

Everything here has one thing in common: the oldest manuscripts do not contain it, and the standard Greek New Testament either omits it or brackets it. These are not arguments about interpretation; they are facts about ink on parchment that anyone can check in the online Sinaiticus.


### A1. Mark 16.9 to 20, the Longer Ending: the risen Yeshua commands baptism, promises tongues, serpents, and poison

**The text.** The ASV prints the verses in the running text, unbracketed, after a paragraph break; the 1881 Revised Version it follows set them off with an extra space and a margin note. Yeshua says, "He that believeth and is baptized shall be saved" (16.16), that believers "shall take up serpents" (16.18), and that poison "shall in no wise hurt them."

**What it is used to prove.** Baptism as a condition of salvation; tongues as the sign of belief; snake handling in the Appalachian Holiness churches, which rests on 16.18 and nothing else; faith healing; a Great Commission outside Matthew.

**The manuscripts.** Sinaiticus and Vaticanus both end Mark at 16.8, with the women fleeing in fear. Vaticanus leaves a blank column after 16.8, the only one in the codex, as if the scribe knew something was missing and did not have it. The Old Latin Bobiensis, the Sinaitic Syriac, about a hundred Armenian manuscripts, and the two oldest Georgian manuscripts (897 and 913) also end at 16.8. Eusebius, in his *Ad Marinum* (early fourth century), says the accurate copies end at 16.8; Jerome, in Epistle 120 to Hedibia, repeats that almost all Greek copies lack the ending. Clement of Alexandria and Origen show no knowledge of it. Many later copies that have the verses mark them with asterisks or obeli, the ancient footnote warning. On the other side, the verses are in Alexandrinus, Ephraemi, Bezae, Washingtonianus, the Byzantine majority, most Old Latin, the Vulgate, and the Peshitta; Irenaeus quotes 16.19 in *Against Heresies* 3.10.5 (about 180) and Justin probably echoes 16.20 in *First Apology* 45 (about 155). So the ending existed by the mid-second century but was not in the ancestor of Sinaiticus and Vaticanus. There is more: the manuscripts preserve four endings. The abrupt ending at 16.8; a one-sentence "Shorter Ending" in Codex Regius and a few others, most of which then add the long ending too; the long ending; and the "Freer Logion," an extra paragraph after 16.14 found only in Washingtonianus and known to Jerome, in which the disciples excuse themselves and Christ replies. Four endings in circulation proves the original had an ending problem that scribes solved in different ways.

**The verdict.** Metzger, for the UBS committee, concludes that "the earliest ascertainable form of the Gospel of Mark ended with 16:8," notes seventeen words Mark never uses elsewhere, and points to the seam at verse 9, where Mary Magdalene is reintroduced as a new character; his full comment is at [https://bible-researcher.com/endmark.html](https://bible-researcher.com/endmark.html). NA28 and UBS5 print the verses in double brackets. Ehrman makes it his showcase in *Misquoting Jesus* chapter 3. Daniel Wallace argues in *Perspectives on the Ending of Mark* (2008) that Mark ended at 16.8 and the long ending is secondary. Even the Majority Text defenders Maurice Robinson and James Snapp concede the verses are absent from Sinaiticus and Vaticanus; only that small school defends them as Mark's. See the ending in Sinaiticus at [https://codexsinaiticus.org/en/](https://codexsinaiticus.org/en/) (navigate to Mark 16) and background at [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_16](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_16).

**Which Bibles drop or bracket it.** The ASV prints it unbracketed. NRSV brackets it under the headings "The Shorter Ending" and "The Longer Ending." NIV sets it off with a rule and a note that the earliest manuscripts lack it. ESV double-brackets it. NABRE prints it with a note that it is not Marcan though canonical.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED.

**How to present this.** The oldest two Bibles in the world end Mark with the women running away in fear; everything after that, the baptism saying, the serpents, the poison, was written by someone else, and the church's own Greek text brackets it. Do not say the church hid it; the footnote is in every modern Bible. Say that a footnote is not the same as telling people from the pulpit.


### A2. John 7.53 to 8.11, the woman taken in adultery: "Neither do I condemn thee"

**The text.** The ASV prints the story in square brackets, from "[And they went every man unto his own house" (7.53) to the close of 8.11. Yeshua says, "He that is without sin among you, let him first cast a stone" (8.7) and "Neither do I condemn thee" (8.11).

**What it is used to prove.** That Yeshua set aside the Torah's capital penalty; arguments against the death penalty; the "who are you to judge" ethic; Yeshua's literacy, since he writes on the ground.

**The manuscripts.** Absent from P66 (about 200) and P75 (third century), the two earliest substantial copies of John, and from Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Regius, Washingtonianus, and more than a dozen other uncials and early minuscules; absent from the oldest Syriac, the Sahidic and oldest Bohairic Coptic, the Gothic, and some Armenian and Georgian. Alexandrinus and Ephraemi are damaged here, but Metzger notes the missing leaves could not have held it. No Greek commentator knows it before the twelfth century: Tertullian, Cyprian, Origen (whose commentary passes from 7.52 straight to 8.12), and Chrysostom all skip it. Where later copies include it, many mark it with asterisks, and it floats: manuscript 225 puts it after John 7.36, family 1 after John 21.25, family 13 after Luke 21.38, a corrector of 1333 after Luke 24.53. A story that wanders between two Gospels belongs to neither. On the other side, Bezae (fifth century) is the earliest Greek witness to have it, with most Old Latin, the Vulgate, the Ethiopic, and more than nine hundred later Greek copies; Jerome, Ambrose, and Augustine cite it. Augustine (*De adulterinis coniugiis* 2.7) claimed some removed it for fear it licensed adultery, which cannot explain its absence from the whole early Greek and Eastern tradition. Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.17) says Papias told of a woman accused of many sins before the Lord, possibly an early form of the tradition.

**Where the story came from.** The earliest mention of it is not in John at all. Papias, writing around 110 to 130, told of a woman "accused of many sins before the Lord," and Eusebius, who reports this, says the story stands in the Hebrew Gospel, the Gospel according to the Hebrews (*Church History* 3.39.17, [https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm)). So the church knew the story a century before any Greek copy of John contains it, and knew it as a Hebrew Gospel story. The Greek church later wrote it into John, and in family 13 into Luke, after 21.38, which is why it floats. The story is old; its place in John is not. See Part F.

**The verdict.** Metzger: "the evidence for the non-Johannine origin" is "overwhelming," the vocabulary and style are not John's, the committee was unanimous, and it is printed in double brackets only because of its antiquity; see [https://www.bible-researcher.com/adult.html](https://www.bible-researcher.com/adult.html). Ehrman uses it as his second showcase in *Misquoting Jesus* chapter 2. Wallace agrees it is not original and has called it his favorite passage that is not in the Bible. The standard monograph is Chris Keith, *The Pericope Adulterae, the Gospel of John, and the Literacy of Jesus* (2009); the full history is Jennifer Knust and Tommy Wasserman, *To Cast the First Stone* (2019). Background at [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_and_the_woman_taken_in_adultery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_and_the_woman_taken_in_adultery).

**Which Bibles drop or bracket it.** The ASV prints it in square brackets. NRSV and ESV double-bracket it; NIV sets it off with a rule and a note; NABRE includes it with the note that it is missing from all early Greek manuscripts.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED.

**How to present this.** The most quoted mercy story in the Gospels is in no Greek copy of John before the fifth century, and in later copies it moves around, sometimes landing in Luke; the Catholic Bible's own note says it is missing from every early Greek manuscript. Add that it may preserve an early tradition; the point is that John did not write it, and that is the church's verdict.


### A3. Luke 22.43 to 44, the angel and the bloody sweat

**The text.** Narrator's text, not red letter, but it frames the prayer. ASV: an angel strengthens him and "his sweat became as it were great drops of blood."

**What it is used to prove.** Hematidrosis as medical evidence of the passion; Yeshua's full humanity; the devotional agony in the garden.

**The manuscripts.** Absent from P75, the corrector of Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus, Vaticanus, Washingtonianus, the Old Latin Brixianus, the Sinaitic Syriac, the Sahidic, and some Bohairic, Armenian, and Georgian. P69 also lacks them, though it also lacks verse 42, so Tyndale House does not count it. Clement and Origen do not know the verses; Hilary and Jerome report that many copies, Hilary says "the most complete," lack them. Family 13 and some lectionaries move them into Matthew 26 after verse 39, so they float. On the other side, the original hand of Sinaiticus has them, with Bezae, Regius, a fragment (0171) from about 300, family 1, the Byzantine majority, most Latin, and the Syriac; Justin's *Dialogue* 103 (about 160) is the earliest witness, followed by Irenaeus, Hippolytus, Eusebius, Chrysostom, Jerome, and Augustine.

**The verdict.** NA28 and UBS5: double brackets. Metzger: absent from the oldest and best witnesses, likely added from an early extra-canonical tradition, perhaps against docetism. Ehrman (*Orthodox Corruption*, and with Mark Plunkett in 1983) argues the verses break Luke's balanced structure in 22.40 to 46 and are the only place Luke shows Yeshua in agony, which clashes with Luke's calm portrait; see [https://ehrmanblog.org/why-did-scribes-add-the-bloody-sweat/](https://ehrmanblog.org/why-did-scribes-add-the-bloody-sweat/). Tyndale House keeps the verses with a diamond, arguing the omission could itself be a harmonization and no ordinary scribal habit explains dropping them; see [https://tyndale.cam.ac.uk/research/the-greek-new-testament/drop-of-blood-luke-22/](https://tyndale.cam.ac.uk/research/the-greek-new-testament/drop-of-blood-luke-22/). Lincoln Blumell and Claire Clivaz also argue for originality. Overview at [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luke_22:43%E2%80%9344](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luke_22:43%E2%80%9344). UBS grade on the verification list.

**Which Bibles drop or bracket it.** The ASV prints the verses unbracketed in the running text. NRSV, NIV, ESV, and NABRE include the verses with a footnote.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED by the critical text, with genuine dissent from the evangelical Tyndale House edition. Say both.


### A4. Luke 23.34a, "Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do"

**The text.** The first word from the cross, red letter, as the ASV gives it above.

**What it is used to prove.** Unconditional forgiveness of enemies; the model for Stephen's dying prayer (Acts 7.60).

**The manuscripts.** The rare case where the two best Alexandrian witnesses and the chief Western witness agree in omission: P75, Vaticanus, the original hand of Bezae, Washingtonianus, and the corrector of Sinaiticus, with two Old Latin codices, the Sinaitic Syriac, and the Sahidic. On the other side, the original hand of Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus, Ephraemi, a later corrector of Bezae, Regius, families 1 and 13, the Byzantine majority, most Latin, and most Syriac have it, and the patristic trail is early: Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.18.5), Hegesippus as preserved in Eusebius (James the brother of Yeshua echoes it in his own dying prayer), Origen, Hippolytus, and apparently Clement.

**The verdict.** NA28 and UBS5: double brackets. Metzger says the omission by early and diverse witnesses "is most impressive" but the saying "bears self-evident tokens of its dominical origin," and suggests scribes deleted it believing the fall of Jerusalem proved God had not forgiven the Hebrews who killed him. Ehrman here sides with the traditional text: the saying is original and was cut for anti-Jewish reasons, in *Orthodox Corruption* and at [https://ehrmanblog.org/did-jesus-pray-father-forgive-them-from-the-cross](https://ehrmanblog.org/did-jesus-pray-father-forgive-them-from-the-cross). Whitlark and Parsons (2006) argue it was added to make seven last words; Nathan Eubank (2010) defends originality. Tyndale House prints it with a diamond; see [https://tyndale.cam.ac.uk/research/the-greek-new-testament/father-forgive-luke-23-34/](https://tyndale.cam.ac.uk/research/the-greek-new-testament/father-forgive-luke-23-34/). UBS grade on the verification list.

**Which Bibles drop or bracket it.** The ASV prints it unbracketed. NRSV, NIV, ESV, and NABRE include it with a footnote.

**Tier.** CONTESTED. The best manuscripts omit it; the earliest fathers and the character of the saying favor it.

**How to present this.** Do not list it alongside Mark 16 as an addition. Say the oldest copies lack it and the critical text brackets it, but the earliest Christian writers knew it and even Ehrman thinks it is original and was cut out; the manuscripts and the history point different ways.


### A5. Luke 22.19b to 20, the longer institution words: "the new covenant in my blood"

**The text.** ASV: "which is given for you: this do in remembrance of me," then "This cup is the new covenant in my blood." The short text ends at "This is my body."

**What it is used to prove.** The cup as "the new covenant in my blood"; "do this in remembrance of me," which appears only in Luke and in Paul (1 Corinthians 11.24 to 25).

**The manuscripts.** The short text is in Bezae and five Old Latin codices; the Sinaitic and Curetonian Syriac and two more Old Latin codices rearrange the verses, showing early disturbance. Everything else, including P75, Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus, and Vaticanus, has the long text.

**The verdict.** Westcott and Hort listed this among their nine Western non-interpolations and double-bracketed it; the RSV (1946) dropped 19b to 20 to a footnote. The UBS committee reversed course: UBS3 graded the long text {C}, UBS4 raised it to {B}, and Metzger notes it is in all Greek manuscripts except Bezae. Ehrman (*Orthodox Corruption* chapter 5) defends the short text, arguing the long text imports Paul's atonement language ("poured out for you") into Luke, who otherwise never says the death was "for" anyone. Jeremias (*The Eucharistic Words of Jesus*) and Fitzmyer (Anchor Luke) accept the long text. See [https://bible-researcher.com/noninterp.html](https://bible-researcher.com/noninterp.html) and [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_non-interpolations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_non-interpolations).

**Which Bibles drop or bracket it.** RSV omitted it; the ASV prints it unbracketed; NRSV, NIV, ESV, and NABRE include it, NRSV with a footnote. NA28: in the text, no brackets.

**Tier.** CONTESTED. The critical text prints it, but the question is alive because it is the only place Luke gives the death a saving purpose.

**How to present this.** Luke's cup word is missing from one of the five great codices and from the early Latin, the RSV dropped it in 1946, and the leading textual critic of our generation thinks it came from Paul; in Mark and Matthew the word "new" is itself an addition (A24).


### A6. Matthew 6.13b, the doxology of the Lord's Prayer

**The text.** The ASV already omits it, with no margin note, and ends at "deliver us from the evil one." The KJV reads: "For thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, for ever. Amen."

**What it is used to prove.** Nothing controversial; the point is that the prayer as most Protestants recite it ends with words Yeshua is not recorded saying in the earliest text.

**The manuscripts.** Absent from Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Bezae, Codex Z, family 1, several Old Latin, the Vulgate, and some Bohairic. The earliest commentaries on the prayer, by Tertullian, Cyprian, and Origen, go phrase by phrase and end at "deliver us from evil." Luke 11.4 has no doxology in any manuscript. The doxology appears in Regius, Washingtonianus, family 13, the Byzantine majority, and several versions, but in varying forms, the usual sign of liturgical growth. The *Didache* 8.2 (about 100) has a two-part doxology, "for thine is the power and the glory for ever," showing an ending was in use early but not yet standardized.

**The verdict.** Metzger: it "was composed (perhaps on the basis of 1 Chr 29:11-13)" for liturgical use; UBS {A} for omission; NA28 omits. Agreement is essentially universal; even Byzantine-priority scholars acknowledge the growth. NET Bible note at [https://netbible.org/bible/Matthew+6](https://netbible.org/bible/Matthew+6); background at [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_6:13](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_6:13).

**Which Bibles drop or bracket it.** The ASV omits it. NRSV, NIV, ESV, and NABRE omit it with a footnote.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED.


### Smaller harmonizing additions

The pattern in each of these is the same: a sentence genuine in one Gospel was copied by scribes into another where the earliest manuscripts lack it, or a phrase was padded to match a parallel. None changes the doctrinal weight of a saying, because the saying survives where it belongs. Their value is cumulative: they show how freely the text grew and why the KJV is longer than Bibles made from older manuscripts. The ASV, following Westcott and Hort, omits every one of them from its running text except Matthew 21.44, which it prints, so the KJV wording is given; where a whole verse is dropped, the ASV keeps the verse number and gives the words in a margin note beginning "Many ancient authorities insert" or similar. ### Christologically loaded variants, and why the direction of change matters

The entries above are mostly harmless harmonizations. The entries below are different: in each, the two readings carry different pictures of who Yeshua is, and in nearly every case the later reading is the higher one. That is the heart of Ehrman's *Orthodox Corruption of Scripture*. Scribes did not wobble randomly between high and low Christologies. When they touched a text bearing on the nature of Christ, they moved it toward the orthodoxy of their own day and away from anything that could support the Ebionite, Nazarene, or adoptionist view that Yeshua was a man anointed by God, or anything that limited the Son. A scribe who believed Yeshua was God from eternity had every motive to remove "today I have begotten thee," add "Son of God" to the first line of Mark, turn "only begotten Son" into "only begotten God," replace "his father" with "Joseph," and drop "neither the Son" from a sentence about what the Son does not know. No scribe had a motive to do the reverse. That one-way traffic is the argument. Several of these cases are genuinely CONTESTED, and the sheet says where.

**A27. Luke 3.22, the voice at the baptism: "today I have begotten thee."** Not red letter but the voice from heaven. Nearly every Greek manuscript reads, with the ASV, "Thou art my beloved Son; in thee I am well pleased." Bezae alone in Greek, with seven Old Latin codices, reads "Thou art my Son; today I have begotten thee," Psalm 2.7 word for word. What makes this more than a Bezae oddity is the patristic trail: Justin (*Dialogue* 88 and 103), Clement of Alexandria (*Paedagogus* 1.25), Methodius, Lactantius, Juvencus, Hilary, Augustine (who knew copies with it), and the *Gospel of the Ebionites* in Epiphanius (*Panarion* 30.13) all give the Psalm 2.7 form. Every second- and third-century father who quotes the verse knows the Bezae reading. NA28 prints the majority reading; Metzger calls the Western reading an assimilation to Psalm 2.7. Ehrman (*Orthodox Corruption* chapter 2, and a blog series from [https://ehrmanblog.org/church-fathers-and-the-voice-at-jesus-baptism/](https://ehrmanblog.org/church-fathers-and-the-voice-at-jesus-baptism/) to [https://ehrmanblog.org/a-final-post-on-luke-322/](https://ehrmanblog.org/a-final-post-on-luke-322/)) argues the Bezae reading is original: it is what the earliest fathers knew, it fits Luke's speeches in Acts which treat the baptism as the anointing (Acts 10.38; 4.27), and orthodox scribes changed it because it sounded adoptionist. Most scholars (Fitzmyer, Bovon, Marshall) stay with the majority reading, noting that Luke uses Psalm 2.7 at Acts 13.33 for the resurrection and that Bezae's Latin allies are a single stream. UBS grade on the verification list. NRSV footnotes the Bezae reading. Used to prove the adoptionist, Ebionite and Nazarene reading of the baptism as the moment of sonship. CONTESTED, with Ehrman's case the best-known minority position and taken seriously.

**A28. Matthew 24.36, "neither the Son."** Red letter, and here the KJV lacks the words. ASV: "not even the angels of heaven, neither the Son, but the Father only." The phrase is in the original Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Bezae, Codex Θ, family 13, the Old Latin, and the Ethiopic; omitted by the corrector of Sinaiticus, Regius, Washingtonianus, family 1, 33, the Byzantine majority, the Vulgate, the Syriac, and the Coptic, and Ambrose and Jerome discuss its absence. NA28 includes it. Metzger: the phrase is in the Mark 13.32 parallel in every manuscript and was dropped in Matthew by scribes who felt it limited the Son's omniscience; the harder reading is original. Ehrman uses it as a textbook orthodox corruption. Wallace, unusually, leans toward omission in his study at [https://bible.org/node/2478](https://bible.org/node/2478), treating the longer reading as a harmonization to Mark, a minority view among evangelicals too; further discussion at [https://www.biblicaltraining.org/learn/institute/nt605-textual-criticism/nt605-32-some-famous-textual-problems-matthew-24-36](https://www.biblicaltraining.org/learn/institute/nt605-textual-criticism/nt605-32-some-famous-textual-problems-matthew-24-36). UBS grade on the verification list. NRSV, NIV, ESV, and NABRE include it. Used to prove a Son of limited knowledge; the orthodox reply is that Mark 13.32 says it in every copy anyway. ESTABLISHED, in the sense that the omission is the corruption.

**A29. Mark 1.1, "the Son of God."** Narrator's text, the first line of the first Gospel. The original Sinaiticus, Codex Θ, a Sahidic manuscript, some Armenian, and the oldest Georgian omit it, and Origen omits it several times, as do Asterius, Serapion, Cyril of Jerusalem, Epiphanius, Basil, and once Jerome; the corrector of Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus, Vaticanus, Bezae, Regius, Washingtonianus, and the Byzantine majority include it, with Irenaeus, Ambrose, and Augustine. NA28 single brackets; UBS {C}. Metzger allows an accidental omission in a string of abbreviated sacred names but calls Origen's repeated omission weighty. Ehrman favors omission as original; Peter Head (1991) argues for omission on scribal grounds; Tommy Wasserman (2011) defends inclusion. Modern Bibles include it with a footnote. A Mark that did not open with "Son of God" matters to anyone studying the Nazarene reading. See [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_1). CONTESTED, with good names on both sides.

**A30. John 1.18, "only begotten God" or "only begotten Son."** Narrator's text in the Prologue. The ASV reads "the only begotten Son" with the margin "God only begotten." P66, P75, the original Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, the original Ephraemi, Regius, 33, the Bohairic, and the Peshitta read "God," with Irenaeus (in Latin), Clement, Origen, and Didymus; Alexandrinus, Washingtonianus, the Byzantine majority, the Old Latin, the Vulgate, and the Curetonian Syriac read "Son," with Tertullian and Hippolytus. NA28 reads "God"; UBS {B}. Metzger: the Alexandrian support is decisive and "Son" is the easier reading, since John uses "only Son" at 3.16 and 3.18. Ehrman (*Orthodox Corruption* chapter 3) argues "Son" is original and "God" an anti-adoptionist Alexandrian change, since "only begotten God" is unparalleled in John and in Christian usage. Wallace and most text critics reply that P66 and P75 are too early and too geographically spread to be a doctrinal coordination. NRSV "God the only Son"; NIV "the one and only Son, who is himself God"; ESV "the only God"; NABRE "the only Son, God." See [https://netbible.org/bible/John+1](https://netbible.org/bible/John+1) and [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_1:18](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_1:18). CONTESTED.

**A31. Luke 2.33 and 2.43, "his father" and "his parents" versus "Joseph."** Narrator's text. The ASV reads "his father and his mother" at 2.33 and "his parents" at 2.43, the text of Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Bezae, Regius, Washingtonianus, the Vulgate, the Sinaitic Syriac, and the Sahidic. Alexandrinus, Codex Θ, family 13, the Byzantine majority, and the Peshitta read "Joseph and his mother" in both places; 2.41 ("his parents") and 2.48 ("thy father and I") were left alone, so the change was not systematic. Metzger: "Joseph" was introduced to safeguard the virgin birth; the harder readings are original. Ehrman: textbook anti-adoptionist changes. NA28 and modern Bibles read "father" and "parents." It shows Luke calling Joseph the father of Yeshua without embarrassment, and scribes adjusting the text to protect a doctrine. The Sinaitic Syriac of Matthew 1.16 goes further. Where the Greek reads "Joseph the husband of Mary, of whom was born Jesus," it reads, in English, "Joseph; and Joseph, to whom was betrothed the virgin Mary, begat Jesus who is called the Christ." Von Soden adopted that reading in his critical edition and the 1952 RSV printed it in the margin; Metzger answers that "there is no evidence that [this reading] ever existed in a Greek manuscript of the first Gospel." So cite it as an early Syriac reading, not a Greek one; see [https://www.bible-researcher.com/text-mat1.html](https://www.bible-researcher.com/text-mat1.html). See [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textual_variants_in_the_Gospel_of_Luke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textual_variants_in_the_Gospel_of_Luke). ESTABLISHED.

**A32. Matthew 1.25, "her firstborn son."** Narrator's text. The ASV reads "a son," the text of Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, families 1 and 13, 33, Bobiensis, the Curetonian Syriac, and the Sahidic; Ephraemi, Bezae, Regius, Washingtonianus, the Byzantine majority, the Vulgate, and the Peshitta read "her firstborn son," a harmonization to Luke 2.7. NA28 and modern Bibles read "a son." UBS grade on the verification list. Both sides of the perpetual-virginity debate have leaned on "firstborn"; the clause is not Matthew's, though Luke 2.7 keeps it. See [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_1:25](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_1:25). ESTABLISHED.

**A33. Mark 1.41, Yeshua "angry" or "moved with compassion."** Narrator's text on his reaction to the leper. ASV: "moved with compassion." Bezae and four Old Latin codices read "angry"; everything else reads "compassion," and so does NA28. Ehrman (*Misquoting Jesus* chapter 5; [https://ehrmanblog.org/why-would-jesus-get-angry-at-this-poor-leper/](https://ehrmanblog.org/why-would-jesus-get-angry-at-this-poor-leper/)) argues "angry" is original: scribes change anger to compassion, not the reverse; Matthew 8.3 and Luke 5.13 both drop the emotion word, which makes sense if their Mark read "angry" (they regularly cut his harsh emotions, as at Mark 3.5 and 10.14) and not otherwise. The NIV 2011 reads "indignant" and the NRSVue (2021) "Moved with anger," so two major translations disagree with the critical text. Peter Williams of Tyndale House replies that the Latin witnesses are not independent and one Greek manuscript is thin; see [https://tyndalebulletin.org/api/v1/articles/29416-counting-witnesses-for-the-angry-jesus-in-mark-1-41-interdependence-and-insularity-in-the-latin-tradition.pdf](https://tyndalebulletin.org/api/v1/articles/29416-counting-witnesses-for-the-angry-jesus-in-mark-1-41-interdependence-and-insularity-in-the-latin-tradition.pdf). CONTESTED.

**A34. Matthew 27.49, the spear thrust in Sinaiticus and Vaticanus.** Narrator's text, and the one famous place where the two great codices carry an obvious insertion. After "let us see whether Elijah cometh to save him," Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Ephraemi, Regius, and a long list of minuscules add that another took a spear and pierced his side, and water and blood came out. Everything else omits it. It is a harmonization from John 19.34; Metzger says it "must be regarded as an early intrusion"; UBS {B} for omission; NA28 omits. Dirk Jongkind of Tyndale House calls it "clearly secondary" at [https://tyndale.cam.ac.uk/research/the-greek-new-testament/was-jesus-pierced-matthew-27/](https://tyndale.cam.ac.uk/research/the-greek-new-testament/was-jesus-pierced-matthew-27/); see also [https://bible-researcher.com/noninterp.html](https://bible-researcher.com/noninterp.html). It matters twice: it proves Sinaiticus and Vaticanus are not infallible (Part D), and it puts the spear before the death in verse 50, which would make the spear the cause of death and contradict John. ESTABLISHED as an intrusion.

**A35. 1 John 5.7 to 8, the Johannine Comma.** Not a Gospel, not red letter, but the only explicit Trinitarian sentence in the New Testament and a forgery by any standard. KJV: "the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one." The ASV omits it. It is in no Greek manuscript before the fourteenth century, and the handful that have it (61, 629, several only in the margin) are late; absent from every ancient version except Latin, and from the oldest Vulgate manuscripts, Fuldensis and Amiatinus. First clear citation: Priscillian, Spain, about 380. It entered the Greek Textus Receptus through Erasmus's third edition (1522) under pressure. Metzger: UBS {A} for omission; "the passage is clearly a later addition." NA28 and all modern Bibles omit it. See [https://www.bible-researcher.com/comma.html](https://www.bible-researcher.com/comma.html) and [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannine_Comma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannine_Comma). ESTABLISHED.


## PART B, WORDS COMPOSED BY THE EVANGELISTS

Everything in this Part is in every manuscript. The Greek text is secure. The argument is literary and historical: did the Gospel writer compose a saying or receive it? Nothing here was forged into a manuscript; the evangelist wrote it that way. These are harder arguments to make in a comment thread, which is why each entry states the argument in full and names the scholars, so a reader can see the claim is mainstream.


### B1. Matthew 28.19, the Trinitarian baptismal formula

**The text.** ASV: "make disciples of all the nations, baptizing them into the name of the Father," and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit.

**What it is used to prove.** The Trinity as Yeshua's own teaching; Trinitarian baptism as his command; the Gentile mission as his explicit instruction.

**The manuscript fact, first.** Every Greek manuscript of Matthew 28.19 has the triadic formula. There is no variant in the Greek tradition, and every ancient version has it. No papyrus covers the verse: the only papyrus of Matthew 28, P105 from the fifth or sixth century, preserves just 28.2 to 5, and Ephraemi breaks off after 28.14, so the earliest Greek witnesses are Sinaiticus and Vaticanus (manuscript list at [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_28](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_28)). This is why the popular claim that the formula was "forged at Nicaea in 325" fails before it starts. The *Didache* 7.1, a church manual dated about 90 to 120, says to baptize in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. Justin quotes the formula in *First Apology* 61 (about 155), Irenaeus in *Against Heresies* 3.17.1 (about 180), Tertullian in *De Baptismo* 13 and *Adversus Praxean* 26 (about 200 to 210), Cyprian in Epistle 73 (about 255). A council in 325 cannot insert words Irenaeus quoted in 180. Metzger's *Textual Commentary* does not list the verse, because there is no variant to discuss.

**The argument that the Caesarean text lacked it (Conybeare).** In 1901 and 1902 the Oxford scholar F. C. Conybeare published "The Eusebian Form of the Text Matth. 28,19" in the *Zeitschrift für die neutestamentliche Wissenschaft* and "Three Early Doctrinal Modifications of the Text of the Gospels" in the *Hibbert Journal* (public domain on [archive.org](http://archive.org) under that title). He observed that Eusebius of Caesarea (died 339), who had the great Caesarean library, quotes the verse in a short form, "Go and make disciples of all nations in my name," in his pre-Nicene works and never gives the long form before Nicaea: *Demonstratio Evangelica* 3.6, 3.7 (several times), and 9.11; *Ecclesiastical History* 3.5.2; the Syriac *Theophania* 4.16, 5.17, 5.46; his commentaries on Psalms and Isaiah; the oration in praise of Constantine 16.8. Benjamin Hubbard counted about sixteen such quotations or allusions. The long form appears only in Eusebius's post-Nicene *Contra Marcellum* 1.1, *De ecclesiastica theologia* 3.5, his letter to Caesarea defending the Nicene creed, and *Theophania* 4.8. Conybeare concluded that the Caesarean text read "in my name" and the triad was a second- or third-century liturgical expansion. He was followed by Kirsopp Lake (*Encyclopaedia of Religion and Ethics*, 1909), the *Peake's Commentary* note (1919), Hans Kosmala (1965), David Flusser, and George Howard, whose *Hebrew Gospel of Matthew* (1995) notes that the fourteenth-century Hebrew Matthew preserved by Shem-Tob ends with a command to teach what Yeshua commanded and has no baptismal clause.

**The mainstream pushback.** E. Riggenbach (1903) and F. H. Chase (*Journal of Theological Studies*, 1905) replied that Eusebius habitually abbreviates; that in the short-form passages he is stressing the universal mission, not baptism; that "in my name" is his own summary; and that the *Didache* shows the triad in use a generation after Matthew. Hubbard's *The Matthean Redaction of a Primitive Apostolic Commissioning* (1974) analyzed every Eusebian citation and concluded the short form is Eusebian abbreviation. Jane Schaberg, *The Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit: The Triadic Phrase in Matthew 28:19b* (1982), argued the triad is Matthew's own, drawn from the pattern in Daniel 7 of the Ancient of Days, the Son of Man, and the angels. The standard commentaries, Davies and Allison (ICC, 1997), Luz (Hermeneia), Hagner (WBC), Nolland (NIGTC), and France (NICNT), all judge the long form original to Matthew; Alfred Plummer: "The evidence for its genuineness is overwhelming." A conservative summary citing Riggenbach, Hubbard, Davies and Allison, and Plummer is at [https://torahresource.com/article/matthew-2819/](https://torahresource.com/article/matthew-2819/). Overview at [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Commission).

**The defensible claim.** The formula is original to Matthew as written, about 80 to 90. But almost no critical scholar thinks the historical Yeshua spoke it. Bultmann (*History of the Synoptic Tradition*) treats it as a church formulation; John Meier, the Catholic priest who wrote *A Marginal Jew*, regards it as reflecting the baptismal practice of Matthew's community; Vermes, Ehrman, Sanders, and Allison agree the Great Commission is a post-Easter construction; the Jesus Seminar colors 28.18 to 20 black, as it does all speech of the risen Yeshua. Four arguments. First, Acts never records anyone baptizing with this formula; every baptism in Acts is in the name of Yeshua (Acts 2.38; 8.16; 10.48; 19.5), and Paul speaks the same way (Romans 6.3; Galatians 3.27; 1 Corinthians 1.13). Second, Matthew 10.5 to 6 and 15.24 restrict the mission to Israel, and the Jerusalem church in Acts 10, 11, and 15 is stunned that Gentiles are included, which makes no sense if the risen Yeshua had commanded a mission to all nations. Third, "all nations," "Father, Son, Holy Spirit," and "all that I commanded you" is Matthean vocabulary and theology packed into one sentence. Fourth, the *Didache* itself has both formulas, triadic in 7.1 and "in the name of the Lord" in 9.5, so around 100 the triad was one liturgical option, not a command everyone knew. Conybeare's Eusebian material is best read as evidence that a shorter liturgical form circulated alongside the triadic one in the third and fourth centuries, not that Matthew's Greek lacked it.

**The scholars.** Against Yeshua having said it: Bultmann, Meier, Vermes, Sanders, Allison, Ehrman, the Jesus Seminar (black). For Matthew having written it: Davies and Allison, Luz, Hagner, Nolland, France, Schaberg, Hubbard, Plummer. The two lists do not contradict; they answer different questions.

**Tier.** "Nicaea forged it": QUARANTINED. "Eusebius's manuscripts lacked it": CONTESTED, a small minority with respectable twentieth-century names, weakened by Hubbard and Schaberg. "Matthew composed it; Yeshua did not say it": ESTABLISHED in critical scholarship.

**How to present this.** Every Greek manuscript has it, and it was quoted a century and a half before Nicaea, so nobody forged it into the Bible. But Acts never records a baptism in those words; every baptism in Acts is in the name of Yeshua. The mainstream view is that Matthew wrote the formula his own church used and put it in the mouth of the risen Lord. Never say Nicaea.


### B2. Matthew 16.17 to 19, Peter, the rock, and the keys

**The text.** ASV: "thou art Peter, and upon this rock I will build my church," and "I will give unto thee the keys of the kingdom."

**What it is used to prove.** The papacy and Petrine primacy. Protestants read "this rock" as Peter's confession; both sides are arguing over a saying Mark and Luke do not have.

**The argument.** In Mark 8.29 to 30 and Luke 9.20 to 21 the same scene plays out: Peter confesses the Messiah and Yeshua immediately commands silence. No blessing, no rock, no keys. Matthew has inserted three verses into Mark's scene. The word ekklēsia, "church," appears in the four Gospels only here and twice in Matthew 18.17; it is the vocabulary of the community after Easter (see B3). Peter's conduct in Mark, rebuked as "Satan" four verses later (8.33) and denying Yeshua three times, and Paul's confrontation with him in Galatians 2.11 to 14, sit awkwardly with a charter of supreme authority. At the same time, verses 18 to 19 have strong Semitic coloring ("Bar-Jonah," "flesh and blood," "gates of Hades," "bind and loose"), so most scholars date the tradition early and Palestinian, but after Easter.

**The scholars.** Bultmann saw a resurrection-appearance story moved back into the ministry. Raymond Brown, the Catholic priest who was the leading American Catholic biblical scholar of his generation, and the ecumenical team behind *Peter in the New Testament* (1973), judged it most likely a post-resurrection saying relocated by Matthew. Davies and Allison and Luz call it pre-Matthean but post-Easter. Meier, in *A Marginal Jew* volume 3 (2001), pages 224 to 234, argues that 16.18 most likely ties the explanation of Simon's new name to Peter's role in establishing the community after Easter (summarized with page numbers at [https://library.biblicalarchaeology.org/?p=61017](https://library.biblicalarchaeology.org/?p=61017)); quote him from those pages, not from this summary. Vermes and Ehrman treat it as church creation. Oscar Cullmann (*Peter: Disciple, Apostle, Martyr*, 1953) defended authenticity but moved it to the Last Supper. Jesus Seminar: black. Synopsis at [https://www.virtualreligion.net/primer/Repute/rock.html](https://www.virtualreligion.net/primer/Repute/rock.html); background at [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confession_of_Peter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confession_of_Peter).

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as post-Easter or Matthean; authenticity CONTESTED by a few.

**How to present this.** Mark and Luke tell the same scene, and Peter is told to keep quiet; the rock, the keys, and the church are only in Matthew, and the leading Catholic scholar of the last century thought the saying came from after the resurrection.


### B3. Matthew 18.17 to 18, "tell it unto the church," and the word ekklēsia

**The text.** ASV: if he refuses to hear "the church also, let him be unto thee as the Gentile and the publican," followed by binding and loosing repeated from 16.19.

**What it is used to prove.** Church discipline and excommunication; "where two or three are gathered together in my name" (18.20).

**The argument.** Matthew 18.15 to 20 is a three-step disciplinary procedure: private rebuke, then two or three witnesses per Deuteronomy 19.15, then the assembly. It closely parallels the Qumran *Community Rule* (1QS 5.25 to 6.1) and *Damascus Document* (CD 9.2 to 8), and presupposes an organized congregation with power to expel. Its punchline, treat him as a Gentile and tax collector, is strange from the Yeshua attacked for eating with tax collectors (Matthew 9.10 to 11; 11.19). Luke 17.3 preserves the shorter Q saying, rebuke and forgive, and Matthew has built the procedure around it. Verse 18 extends the bind-and-loose power of 16.19 to the whole community. Verse 20 is a Christianized form of the rabbinic saying that the Divine Presence rests on two who study Torah (Mishnah Avot 3.2, 3.6).

**The scholars.** Bultmann: church rule. Davies and Allison, Luz: Matthean community regulation. Jesus Seminar: Matthew 18.15 and 18.16 to 17 black, and the shorter Luke 17.3 black as well (tally at [https://virtualreligion.net/forum/RLQ/q_scandals.html](https://virtualreligion.net/forum/RLQ/q_scandals.html)); its color for 18.18 is unconfirmed. See [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_18](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_18).

**Ekklēsia as a fact.** The word appears in the Gospels three times, all in Matthew: 16.18 once, 18.17 twice. Zero times in Mark, Luke, and John. The Greek Old Testament uses it for the assembly of Israel, so an Aramaic original is possible, but the word clusters in the two passages already flagged as Matthean community material, which is why most scholars treat it as the vocabulary of Matthew's congregation. Paul's letters (about 50 to 60) use it dozens of times; the Gospels, written after Paul, avoid it except here. See [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesia_(ancient_Greece)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesia_(ancient_Greece)).

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as a Matthean community rule.


### B4. Mark 7.19, "making all meats clean"

**The text.** ASV: "This he said, making all meats clean," printed as the narrator's aside. The KJV has Yeshua say that food goes out "into the draught, purging all meats."

**What it is used to prove.** That Yeshua himself abolished the food laws.

**The argument.** Two things are happening, one textual and one literary. Textually, the critical reading (Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus, Vaticanus, Regius, Washingtonianus) has a masculine participle, katharizōn, agreeing with "he said" in 7.18, so the clause is the evangelist's comment: Yeshua said this, thereby cleansing all foods. The Byzantine text has a neuter, katharizon, making it part of Yeshua's own sentence about the latrine, hence the KJV. The masculine is earlier, and it makes the clause Mark's words; several English Bibles print it in parentheses for exactly that reason. Then the literary argument. Matthew 15.17, copying this verse, drops the clause and ends with "that which proceedeth out of the mouth, this defileth the man" (15.18 to 20); Matthew either lacked the clause or rejected it, and Matthew's Yeshua keeps the Law to the last stroke (5.17 to 19). The Jerusalem church in Acts 10, 11, and 15 and Galatians 2 is still fighting over food purity decades later, which is impossible if he had plainly declared all foods clean. And 7.15 itself is defensible as prophetic hyperbole about priorities, in the manner of Hosea 6.6, which Hebrew listeners would recognize; the absolute, Law-abolishing reading is Mark's, writing for Gentiles in Rome. The KJV's Mark 7.16, "if any man have ears to hear," is itself a scribal addition (A10) that sets up 7.15 as a riddle for 7.19 to decode.

**The scholars.** Meier, *A Marginal Jew* volume 4, *Law and Love* (2009), concludes after more than a hundred pages that the historical Yeshua did not abolish the food laws and that 7.19b is Mark's. Daniel Boyarin, *The Jewish Gospels* (2012), argues even 7.15 is about hand-washing, not kosher. James Crossley, *The Date of Mark's Gospel* (2004), reads 7.15 within halakhic debate. Adela Yarbro Collins (Hermeneia) and Joel Marcus (Anchor Bible) treat 7.19b as Mark's editorial comment. Ehrman uses the Mark and Matthew contrast in *Jesus, Interrupted*. The Jesus Seminar's colors for 7.15 and 7.18 to 19 could not be confirmed; do not quote them. See [https://netbible.org/bible/Mark+7](https://netbible.org/bible/Mark+7) and [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_7).

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as the narrator's gloss.

**How to present this.** The sentence about all foods being clean is not something Yeshua says; it is Mark's comment, modern Bibles put it in parentheses, Matthew left it out, and the apostles in Acts were still arguing about food years later.


### B5. Matthew 5.32 and 19.9, the exception clause, "except for fornication"

**The text.** ASV 19.9: "Whosoever shall put away his wife, except for fornication, and shall marry another, committeth adultery."

**What it is used to prove.** "Biblical grounds for divorce"; the Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant divide on remarriage.

**The argument.** The divorce prohibition appears five times: Mark 10.11 to 12, Luke 16.18, 1 Corinthians 7.10 to 11 (where Paul cites "the Lord"), Matthew 5.32, Matthew 19.9. Only Matthew has an exception. The three earliest witnesses, Mark, Q as reflected in Luke, and Paul, all give the absolute form. Matthew added the exception, either to align Yeshua with the Shammaite side of the Hillel-Shammai debate (Mishnah Gittin 9.10) or, as Fitzmyer argued in "The Matthean Divorce Texts and Some New Palestinian Evidence" (*Theological Studies*, 1976), to exempt incestuous marriages, with porneia carrying the sense of the Hebrew zenut as in the Qumran Temple Scroll, the problem addressed in Acts 15.20.

**The scholars.** Meier, volume 4: the absolute prohibition is from Yeshua; the exception is Matthew's. Davies and Allison, Luz, Hagner agree. Jesus Seminar: gray for Luke 16.18, Matthew 5.32, Matthew 19.9, Mark 10.11 to 12, and 1 Corinthians 7.10 to 11 alike, so its colors do not separate the exception from the absolute form (tally at [https://virtualreligion.net/forum/RLQ/q_law.html](https://virtualreligion.net/forum/RLQ/q_law.html)). See [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_5:32](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_5:32).

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as Matthean.


### B6. John's "I am" discourses and the Farewell Discourse (John 13.31 to 17.26)

**The text.** "I am the bread of life," "the light of the world," "the good shepherd," "the resurrection and the life," "the way, and the truth, and the life," "the true vine"; "Before Abraham was born, I am" (8.58); "I and the Father are one" (10.30); and four chapters of farewell speech.

**What it is used to prove.** The deity of Christ, pre-existence, the Trinity (the Paraclete sayings), and exclusive salvation ("no one cometh unto the Father, but by me," 14.6).

**The argument.** The Yeshua of the Synoptics speaks in short sayings and parables about the kingdom of God and almost never about himself. The Yeshua of John speaks in long monologues about himself, and "kingdom of God" nearly vanishes (only 3.3 and 3.5). Both cannot be transcripts of the same man. In John, Yeshua, the Baptist, and the narrator all speak in one idiom; compare 3.16 to 21 with 3.31 to 36 and with 1 John, and the idiom turns out to be the author's. The Farewell Discourse shows its seams: at 14.31 Yeshua says "Arise, let us go hence," then speaks for three more chapters before anyone moves (18.1); at 16.5 he says none of them has asked where he is going, though Peter asked exactly that at 13.36; chapters 15 and 16 repeat 14 in a different key.

**The scholars.** Bultmann (1941) posited a revelation-discourse source and rearranged the chapters. Raymond Brown (Anchor Bible *John*, 1966 to 1970; *The Community of the Beloved Disciple*, 1979; *Introduction to the Gospel of John*, 2003) explained the seams as successive editions, with 15 to 17 added later, and described the discourses as Johannine meditation spoken in the voice of the risen Lord, not recordings. Meier (volume 1) uses John for chronology but not for sayings, because "the discourses are Johannine theology." Vermes (*The Changing Faces of Jesus*) calls John's Yeshua "a stranger" to the Synoptic one. Ehrman, *Jesus, Interrupted* chapter 3; Maurice Casey, *Is John's Gospel True?* (1996). The Jesus Seminar colors nearly all of John black; which few sayings, if any, it printed gray could not be confirmed, so do not name them. The pushback comes from Richard Bauckham (*Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*, 2006), Craig Keener, and the SBL "John, Jesus, and History" project (Anderson, Just, Thatcher), who argue John preserves independent tradition about events and places. Even they do not claim the discourses are verbatim. See [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farewell_Discourse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farewell_Discourse) and [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_am_(biblical_term)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_am_(biblical_term)).

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as Johannine composition.

**How to present this.** In three Gospels Yeshua talks about the kingdom and almost never about himself; in the fourth he talks almost only about himself, in long speeches, in the narrator's own voice; even the scholars who defend John's history admit the speeches are John's theology.


### B7. John 3.16, and where the red letters stop

**The text.** ASV: "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son."

**What it is used to prove.** "The Gospel in a verse"; substitutionary and universal-love readings.

**The argument.** Greek manuscripts have no quotation marks, so every red-letter Bible decides where Yeshua stops and John starts. Yeshua is clearly speaking at 3.10 to 12, in the second person. At 3.13 to 15 the Son of Man is in the third person. At 3.16 to 21 the style shifts to commentary: "God so loved the world, that he gave" is past tense; "this is the judgment" is the narrator's reflective voice; and "only begotten" (monogenēs) is the narrator's word from 1.14 and 1.18, never elsewhere Yeshua's description of himself. John 3.31 to 36 is a parallel case where the Baptist's speech drifts into the narrator's.

**The scholars.** Raymond Brown (Anchor John volume 1) treats 3.16 to 21 as the evangelist's reflection; C. K. Barrett, Rudolf Schnackenburg, Ernst Haenchen, and the NET Bible notes discuss the same break. Red-letter Bibles and the NIV end the quotation at 3.21; some NRSV editions drop quotation marks after 3.15; the NABRE paragraphs 3.16 to 21 as commentary. Jesus Seminar: black. See [https://netbible.org/bible/John+3](https://netbible.org/bible/John+3) and [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_3:16](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_3:16).

**Tier.** CONTESTED on the boundary; ESTABLISHED as Johannine wording either way. John 3.16 is John's summary of John's theology, whether or not a publisher prints it in red.


### B8. Mark 10.45, the ransom saying

**The text.** ASV: the Son of man came "to give his life a ransom for many."

**What it is used to prove.** Substitutionary atonement from Yeshua's own mouth; the only Synoptic place where he gives a theory of his death.

**The argument.** Luke 22.27 has what looks like the same saying, "I am in the midst of you as he that serveth," at the Last Supper with no ransom clause. The ransom clause uses the soteriology of the Hellenistic church (1 Timothy 2.6; Titus 2.14) and reads like a rider attached to a service saying. Bultmann called it a Hellenistic dogmatic reworking; Morna Hooker (*Jesus and the Servant*, 1959) denied the Isaiah 53 background. On the other side, Jeremias, Peter Stuhlmacher, Rikki Watts, Peter Head, and Adela Yarbro Collins argue the saying is pre-Markan and Semitic ("the many" from Isaiah 53.11 to 12, the Son of Man from Daniel 7); Dale Allison (*Constructing Jesus*, 2010) argues Yeshua likely anticipated his death and gave it saving meaning, so 10.45 is plausible in substance if not wording; N. T. Wright accepts it. Jesus Seminar: black. See [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_10).

**Tier.** CONTESTED, with critical weight slightly against verbatim authenticity.


### B9. The passion predictions (Mark 8.31; 9.31; 10.33 to 34)

**The argument.** The three predictions grow in detail and match the passion narrative exactly: handed over, condemned, delivered to the Gentiles, mocked, spat upon, scourged, killed, risen after three days. Bultmann and William Wrede called them vaticinia ex eventu, prophecies written after the event. The disciples' total surprise at the arrest and resurrection is hard to square with having heard this three times. Rudolf Pesch defended a long pre-Markan passion source containing them; Allison, Meier, and Sanders hold that Yeshua probably anticipated a violent death, as any prophet watching the fate of John the Baptist would, and may have spoken of vindication, but that the wording is the church's. Mark 9.31, "The Son of man is delivered up into the hands of men," is the candidate most often allowed as an authentic riddle (Jeremias). Jesus Seminar: black. See [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confession_of_Peter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confession_of_Peter).

**Tier.** Detailed form ESTABLISHED as redactional; the core CONTESTED.


### B10. Mark 13, the "Little Apocalypse"

**What it is used to prove.** Every modern prophecy chart: the abomination of desolation, the gospel to all nations before the end, the fig tree and "this generation."

**The argument.** In 1864 Timothée Colani proposed that Mark 13.5 to 31 incorporates a Jewish-Christian apocalyptic pamphlet from the war of 66 to 70 which Mark attributed to Yeshua; the discourse is a sustained midrash on Daniel (9.27; 11.31; 12.11) and the only long apocalyptic speech in the Gospels. Bultmann accepted a Jewish source; Lars Hartman (*Prophecy Interpreted*, 1966) analyzed it as Daniel midrash; Pesch (1968) argued for a pamphlet from the Caligula crisis of the year 40, when the emperor tried to put his statue in the Temple, a view Gerd Theissen developed (*The Gospels in Context*, 1991), though Pesch later moved toward Markan composition. G. R. Beasley-Murray (*Jesus and the Last Days*, 1993) defended substantial authenticity. Allison (*Constructing Jesus*) argues Yeshua was an apocalyptic prophet and that 13.30 ("this generation shall not pass away") and 13.32 ("neither the Son") are too embarrassing to invent; Ehrman agrees Yeshua expected the imminent kingdom. The Seminar colors nearly all of it black, consistent with its non-apocalyptic Yeshua, which is exactly where Allison and Ehrman part from the Seminar. See [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olivet_Discourse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olivet_Discourse).

**Tier.** CONTESTED.


### B11. Matthew 23, the woes expanded

**The argument.** Matthew 23 is built on Q woes that Luke preserves more briefly in 11.39 to 52: seven woes to Luke's six, differently ordered. Matthew adds the refrain "scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites" seven times (Luke never uses it), the Moses' seat saying (23.2 to 3), the ban on being called rabbi, father, or teacher (23.8 to 10, unknown to Luke), and the "whited sepulchres" image in its present form. The chapter reflects the conflict after 70 between Matthew's Hebrew-believing community and the emerging rabbinic synagogue: Anthony Saldarini (*Matthew's Christian-Jewish Community*, 1994), J. Andrew Overman, Luz, Davies and Allison. Jesus Seminar: mostly black; 23.13 and 23.25 to 26 gray; only the saying on seats of honor and greetings, 23.5 to 7, pink, as are its parallels in Luke 11.43 and Mark 12.38 to 39 (tally at [https://virtualreligion.net/forum/RLQ/q_pharisees.html](https://virtualreligion.net/forum/RLQ/q_pharisees.html)). The core criticism of the Pharisees may be authentic; the sevenfold indictment is Matthew's. And the woe KJV readers quote most, 23.14, is a scribal addition too (A9). See [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woes_of_the_Pharisees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woes_of_the_Pharisees).

**What it is used to prove.** Blanket condemnation of "the Pharisees" and, historically, of the Hebrew people.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as redactional expansion.


### B12. Luke 22.35 to 38, the two swords

**The text.** ASV: "he that hath none, let him sell his cloke, and buy a sword," the citation of Isaiah 53.12, then "It is enough."

**What it is used to prove.** Christian arguments for armed self-defense; Isaiah 53 as Yeshua's self-interpretation.

**The argument.** Found only in Luke. It reverses Luke 10.4 ("carry no purse, no wallet, no shoes") and quotes Isaiah 53.12 as fulfilled, the only explicit citation of Isaiah 53 by Yeshua in the Gospels now that Mark 15.28 is shown to be a scribal import (A21). Hans Conzelmann (*The Theology of St Luke*) and Fitzmyer (Anchor Luke) treat it as Lukan composition or Lukan special material shaped to prepare for the sword stroke at 22.49 to 51 and to frame Yeshua as the suffering servant; Raymond Brown (*The Death of the Messiah*) surveys the options; "It is enough" is read as Yeshua shutting down a misunderstanding. Jesus Seminar: black. See [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sell_your_cloak_and_buy_a_sword](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sell_your_cloak_and_buy_a_sword).

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as Lukan; CONTESTED whether a source stands behind it.


### B13. Matthew 10.5 to 6 against Matthew 28.19

**The argument.** Matthew alone has Yeshua restrict the mission: "Go not into any way of the Gentiles" (10.5), and "only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (15.24). Matthew alone also has the universal commission (28.19). Both cannot be his unqualified words. Jeremias (*Jesus' Promise to the Nations*, 1958) argued Yeshua restricted his own mission to Israel while expecting the Gentiles to come in at the end, so 10.5 to 6 is authentic and 28.19 the church's. Meier (volume 3) accepts the Israel-centered mission of the Twelve. The alternative, that 10.5 to 6 was invented by a conservative Hebrew-believing faction, has few defenders because Acts 10 to 15 independently shows the Jerusalem church had no memory of a Gentile mandate. Jesus Seminar: 28.19 black; its color for 10.5 to 6 could not be confirmed. See [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_10).

**Tier.** The tension ESTABLISHED; which saying is authentic CONTESTED, weight toward 10.5 to 6.


### B14. Matthew 26.28 "unto remission of sins," and Mark 14.24 "my blood of the covenant"

**The text.** ASV Matthew 26.28: "my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many unto remission of sins." Mark 14.24 lacks the last four words.

**What it is used to prove.** The cup as the instrument of forgiveness; the eucharist as the moment sins are remitted.

**The argument.** Matthew alone adds "unto remission of sins" to the cup word. The same phrase sits in Mark 1.4 on John's baptism, "the baptism of repentance unto remission of sins"; Matthew 3.2, copying Mark, drops it from John, and it reappears at the cup. Davies and Allison, Luz, and Meier read this as deliberate Matthean redaction moving forgiveness from John's baptism to Yeshua's blood. The words are textually secure; the "new" in "new covenant" is the scribal addition (A24). Mark 14.24 itself is harder. The Seminar colors the words of institution black (the bread word possibly gray); Bultmann saw a Hellenistic cult legend; Jeremias defended an Aramaic original; Paul's independent version in 1 Corinthians 11.23 to 25 (about 55) shows the cup word is at least pre-Markan and pre-50; Meier and Allison accept that Yeshua held a final meal with symbolic words about his death. The standing objection, from Vermes (*The Religion of Jesus the Jew*) and Casey, is whether a Torah-observant Galilean would ever say "drink my blood," even symbolically, given Leviticus 17.10 to 14. The *Didache* 9 to 10 describes a eucharist with no body or blood words at all. See [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Words_of_Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Words_of_Institution).

**Tier.** Matthew's addition ESTABLISHED; Mark's cup word CONTESTED.


### B15. John 6.51c to 58, eating flesh and drinking blood

**The text.** ASV 6.53: unless you "eat the flesh of the Son of man and drink his blood," you have no life.

**What it is used to prove.** Transubstantiation and the real presence; also the dispute over whether John 6 is about the eucharist at all.

**The argument.** The bread-of-life discourse (6.35 to 50) is about believing. At 6.51c the subject shifts abruptly to eating flesh and drinking blood, with sacramental vocabulary (trōgōn, "munching," 6.54, 56 to 58), and at 6.63 the discourse returns to Spirit and words as if the flesh section were not there. Bultmann assigned 6.51c to 58 to an "ecclesiastical redactor" who added sacraments to a gospel that otherwise has none; Raymond Brown argued it is a eucharistic homily originally set at the Last Supper and moved here in a later edition; Schnackenburg and Peder Borgen (*Bread from Heaven*, 1965) defend the unity of the chapter as a midrash on Exodus 16 and Psalm 78. All agree none of it is a transcript of a synagogue sermon in Capernaum. Jesus Seminar: black. See [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bread_of_Life_Discourse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bread_of_Life_Discourse).

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as Johannine; CONTESTED whether a later layer within John.


### B16. Luke 24.44 to 49, the risen Yeshua explains the scriptures and commissions

**The text.** ASV: all things "written in the law of Moses, and the prophets, and the psalms, concerning me"; repentance and remission "unto all the nations, beginning from Jerusalem"; "Ye are witnesses"; "tarry ye in the city."

**The argument.** Every clause is the program of Acts stated in advance (Acts 1.4 to 8; 2.38; 3.18; 10.43; 13.38); the Jerusalem-outward scheme and the word "witnesses" are Luke's signature. Fitzmyer and Conzelmann: Lukan composition. Jesus Seminar: black. Note the tension with Matthew 28.16 to 20 (commission in Galilee, not Jerusalem) and with 24.51 against Acts 1.3 (Easter-night ascension against forty days) by the same author (A25). See [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luke_24](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luke_24).

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as Lukan.


### B17. Matthew 12.40, Jonah, "three days and three nights"

**The text.** ASV: the Son of man will be "three days and three nights in the heart of the earth."

**The argument.** The Q saying survives in Luke 11.29 to 30: no sign "but the sign of Jonah," and Jonah is a sign to this generation as he was to the Ninevites, that is, preaching repentance to Gentiles who heeded it (11.32). Matthew 12.40 replaces that with a quotation of Jonah 1.17 and an explicit resurrection typology. Matthew 16.4 repeats the sign of Jonah with no explanation, matching Luke, so Matthew knew the short form. Davies and Allison, Luz, Fitzmyer: 12.40 is Matthean exegesis. Jesus Seminar: black for Luke 11.29 to 30, Matthew 12.39 to 40, and Matthew 16.4 alike; only Mark 8.12, the flat refusal of any sign, is gray (tally at [https://virtualreligion.net/forum/RLQ/q_jonah.html](https://virtualreligion.net/forum/RLQ/q_jonah.html)). The Seminar's colors therefore do not mark the Matthew and Luke difference; the redactional argument stands on the commentaries named. "Three days and three nights" also clashes with a Friday-to-Sunday chronology and fuels Wednesday-crucifixion theories; Matthew created that problem. See [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sign_of_Jonah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sign_of_Jonah).

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as Matthean.


### B18. A flag for consistency: Matthew 5.17 to 19

The same scholars who call Matthew 28.19 Matthean also treat Matthew 5.17 to 19, "one jot or one tittle shall in no wise pass away," as the work of Matthew's Hebrew-believing community defending Torah observance; Luz does, Davies and Allison call it pre-Matthean and Jewish-Christian, and Luke 16.17 preserves a Q version of 5.18. Meier (*Law and Love*) accepts a Torah-observant Yeshua but treats 5.17 as Matthean. Jesus Seminar: 5.18 and 5.19 black, the Q form in Luke 16.17 gray (tally at [https://virtualreligion.net/forum/RLQ/q_law.html](https://virtualreligion.net/forum/RLQ/q_law.html)); its color for 5.17 could not be confirmed. Anyone making the Nazarene argument cannot accept the redactional verdict on 28.19 and reject the same method's verdict on 5.17 to 19 without saying why. The defensible position: 5.18 has a Q parallel and 28.19 has none, and 5.17 to 19 agrees with Yeshua's conduct everywhere in the Synoptics while 28.19 contradicts the apostles' conduct everywhere in Acts. Say that rather than pretending the problem is not there. CONTESTED.


## PART C, WHAT THIS DOES TO THE DOCTRINES

**Snake-handling, tongues as proof, and "believe and be baptized."** The Appalachian Holiness practice of handling serpents and drinking poison rests entirely on Mark 16.18; there is no other verse. The claim that tongues are the appointed sign of a believer rests on 16.17, and the rule that baptism is a condition of salvation has 16.16 as its clearest text. All three stand on twelve verses that the two oldest Bibles in the world do not contain, that Eusebius and Jerome said the accurate copies lacked, and that the church's own Greek edition brackets. A doctrine resting on a single passage is only as secure as that passage, and this one is marked as an addition by the scholars who produce the Bibles its defenders carry.

**The Trinity formula.** The Trinitarian baptismal formula has exactly one home in the words of Yeshua, Matthew 28.19, and the only explicit Trinitarian sentence anywhere else in the New Testament, 1 John 5.7, is a fourth-century Latin forgery that entered the Greek text in 1522. The honest case is not that 28.19 was forged into the manuscripts; it was not, and that claim stays quarantined. The honest case is that the formula is Matthew's, that every baptism recorded in Acts is in the name of Yeshua alone, and that the apostles' own practice is the strongest evidence they never heard the command in that form. A Trinitarian can hold the doctrine on other grounds; what the Trinitarian cannot say is that Yeshua taught the formula and his apostles obeyed it.

**Papal primacy.** The Petrine office is built on Matthew 16.18 to 19, and the parallel scenes in Mark and Luke do not contain the saying. The leading Catholic scholars of the last century, Raymond Brown and John Meier, both concluded it is post-Easter or Matthean in its present form. That does not disprove the papacy as an institution, since Rome's claim also rests on tradition, but it removes the claim that Yeshua personally founded the office in Galilee in the hearing of the Twelve. It compounds the problem this channel raised in its apostolic-succession episode: the office claims a charter whose only source is a saying two of three Synoptic writers did not know, using a word, ekklēsia, that no other Gospel writer puts in his mouth.

**The eucharist as a blood covenant.** Three texts carry the blood theology of the Lord's Supper, and all three are compromised in different ways. Luke 22.20, "the new covenant in my blood," is the only place Luke gives the death a saving purpose, is missing from Bezae and the Old Latin, was dropped by the RSV, and is argued to be a Pauline import by the most prominent textual critic alive. The word "new" in Matthew 26.28 and Mark 14.24 is a scribal import from Luke and Paul, so in the earliest Mark and Matthew he speaks of "the covenant," in Exodus 24.8 language, not a "new testament." Matthew's "unto remission of sins" is Matthew's relocation of a phrase Mark attached to John's baptism. And the flesh-and-blood section of John 6 is, by the agreement of every major Johannine commentator, a composition rather than a transcript, and in Bultmann's view a later insertion into a Gospel with no sacraments. Whether a Torah-observant Galilean ever asked anyone to drink his blood, even in symbol, is a question Vermes and Casey have put on the table, and the *Didache*, the earliest church manual we have, describes a eucharist with no body or blood words at all.

**The abolition of the food laws.** The one verse in the Gospels that says Yeshua declared all foods clean is not a saying of Yeshua. It is Mark's parenthetical comment, as the Greek grammar shows and as modern Bibles print it. Matthew, with Mark in front of him, deleted it and had Yeshua say the opposite about the Law. The apostles in Acts behaved as if no such declaration had been made. Meier's hundred-page study in *Law and Love* concludes that the historical Yeshua did not abolish the food laws. For a channel arguing that the Nazarene movement kept Torah, this is the most important entry in Part B and the one where the mainstream is most clearly on the channel's side.

**Divorce.** The exception clause allowing divorce for sexual immorality is in Matthew alone. Mark, Luke, and Paul, all earlier, give the prohibition without exception, and Paul says he is quoting the Lord. Every denomination arguing about "biblical grounds for divorce" is arguing about a clause the earliest witnesses lack and the major commentaries attribute to Matthew.

**The high Christology of John as composed rather than remembered.** Nearly every verse used to prove that Yeshua claimed to be God in the flesh comes from John's discourses: "I and the Father are one," "before Abraham was, I am," "no one cometh unto the Father, but by me." The scholarly agreement, including among those who defend John's value for events, is that the discourses are the evangelist's theology spoken in the voice of the risen Lord. Add the one-way direction of the scribal changes in Part A: "today I have begotten thee" removed from the baptism; "Son of God" added to the first line of Mark; "only begotten Son" made into "only begotten God"; "his father" made into "Joseph"; "neither the Son" deleted from a sentence about what the Son does not know. In each case the earlier text is the lower one. That is what one would expect if the first-generation Hebrew believers held a lower view of him than the Gentile church of the fourth century, and the text was gradually brought into line with the later view.


## PART D, OBJECTIONS AND ANSWERS

**"These are footnoted in every Bible. Nobody is hiding them."** True, and the sheet says so at every entry. The footnote is the concession. The objection confuses a footnote with a teaching. A note in six-point type reading "some ancient authorities lack" does not tell a congregation that the two oldest Bibles in the world end Mark at 16.8, that the adultery story wanders between John and Luke, or that the Trinitarian sentence in 1 John was absent from every Greek copy for thirteen centuries. The point is not that the church hides it; the point is that the church prints it and then preaches as if it had not.

**"The longer ending of Mark is in ninety-nine percent of manuscripts."** It is in the great majority because the great majority are Byzantine minuscules copied after the ninth century from a text that had already absorbed it. Manuscripts are weighed, not counted; a thousand copies of a tenth-century text are one witness to the tenth century. Sinaiticus and Vaticanus are two witnesses to the fourth, Bobiensis and the Sinaitic Syriac add the Latin and Syriac worlds, Eusebius and Jerome report what the accurate copies of their day contained, and Clement and Origen never saw the ending. Add the four competing endings and the asterisks in the copies that have it. The ninety-nine percent figure is the Majority Text school's argument, and even its leaders, Robinson and Snapp, concede the two oldest codices lack the verses.

**"The church fathers quote these verses."** Some do, and the sheet reports every case. Irenaeus quotes Mark 16.19 around 180, Justin knows the bloody sweat around 160, Irenaeus knows "Father, forgive them." That proves the passages existed in the second century, not that the evangelists wrote them; the second century is when the text was most fluid, and a saying circulating in Lyons in 180 could enter copies of Mark by then without having been in Mark in 70. Set the fathers who quote a passage against those who do not: Clement and Origen never cite the long ending; Origen's John commentary skips from 7.52 to 8.12; Tertullian, Cyprian, and Origen each expound the Lord's Prayer phrase by phrase and stop before the doxology. A father who comments line by line and skips a verse outweighs a father who quotes it once in passing. And where the fathers overwhelmingly favor the minority reading, Luke 3.22, Ehrman uses exactly this argument for the Bezae reading, so the patristic argument does not run only one way.

**"Textual criticism is a liberal attack on the Bible."** The critical Greek text behind every modern evangelical Bible was edited by a committee that included Bruce Metzger, an ordained Presbyterian minister who chaired the NRSV; the NIV and ESV are translated from it. Daniel Wallace of Dallas Theological Seminary, who holds to inerrancy, agrees that Mark 16.9 to 20 and John 7.53 to 8.11 are not original and calls the latter his favorite passage that is not in the Bible. Tyndale House in Cambridge produced its own Greek New Testament in 2017, and where it disagrees with NA28 this sheet says so. Origen practiced textual criticism in the third century and Jerome in the fourth; Erasmus was practicing it, badly, from six late manuscripts, when he compiled the text the King James translators used. The only alternative is to pick one manuscript tradition by faith and refuse to look at the others, which is the position of a small King James Only movement that no seminary of any denomination teaches.

**"Even if scribes added them, the Holy Spirit guided the canon."** A theological claim, so answer it as one. If the Spirit guided the church to receive Mark 16.9 to 20, the Spirit guided the fourth-century Alexandrian church to reject it, since Sinaiticus and Vaticanus were produced there, and guided Eusebius and Jerome to say the accurate copies lacked it. If the Spirit guided the Byzantine church to delete "neither the Son" from Matthew 24.36, it guided the earlier church to keep it. The appeal to providence has to pick which century's text the Spirit guided, and the moment it picks, it is doing textual criticism with a theological label. More simply, the claim that the Spirit guided a scribe to add words to a Gospel is a claim that scripture was still being written in the sixth century, which is not what anyone making the objection believes.

**"The Jesus Seminar is fringe."** The sheet says at the outset that the Seminar is one voice, tilted toward a non-apocalyptic Yeshua, and treated skeptically by Allison, Meier, Sanders, and Wright. Now look at who else is cited in Part B. Raymond Brown and John Meier are Catholic priests. Davies and Allison, Luz, Hagner, Nolland, and France wrote the standard commentaries on Matthew, several of them evangelical. Fitzmyer was a Jesuit. The verdicts that Matthew composed 28.19, that 16.17 to 19 is post-Easter, that Mark 7.19b is Mark's gloss, and that John's discourses are John's theology do not depend on the Seminar. Remove the Seminar from this sheet and every Part B verdict stands on the commentaries the objector's own pastor owns.

**"Eusebius was an Arian."** Eusebius had Arian sympathies and signed the Nicene creed reluctantly; fair. It is irrelevant to Mark 16, where his report is confirmed by Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Bobiensis, the Sinaitic Syriac, the Armenian and Georgian manuscripts, and Jerome, who was no Arian. For Matthew 28.19 the objection has more bite, and the sheet already concedes it: Conybeare's argument from Eusebius's short quotations is CONTESTED and weakened by Hubbard's showing that Eusebius abbreviates habitually. The sheet does not rest the Matthew 28.19 case on Eusebius. It rests it on Acts, where the apostles baptize in the name of Yeshua every time, and on the mainstream redactional verdict. Anyone who answers "Eusebius was an Arian" has answered the version of the argument this sheet does not make.

**"You can't trust Sinaiticus and Vaticanus; they are Alexandrian corruptions."** Then trust Bezae, the chief Western witness, which lacks "Father, forgive them" in its original hand, lacks the long Lukan cup words, and reads "today I have begotten thee" at the baptism. Trust P66 and P75, a century older than Sinaiticus, which lack the adultery story. Trust the Old Latin and Old Syriac, translated in the second and third centuries, which lack the long ending of Mark. Part A does not depend on two codices; it depends on the agreement of the Egyptian, Western, Syriac, and Latin streams against the later Byzantine text. And the sheet has shown where the two codices are wrong: both carry the spear thrust in Matthew 27.49 (A34), and the critical editions reject them there. No one treats them as infallible. They are weighed like every witness, and they usually win because they usually agree with the papyri and the versions.

**"If John composed the discourses, so what? They're inspired."** Then the claim has changed, and the change should be said out loud. "Yeshua said 'I and the Father are one'" is a claim about history, about what a man said in Jerusalem around the year 30. "John was inspired to write that Yeshua said it" is a claim about the author of a book written sixty years later. The second may be true, but it is not the first, and a red-letter Bible asserts the first. Everything in Part B argues that the first claim is false for the passages listed, and the objector who retreats to the second has agreed. Whoever then wants to argue that the apostles' Hebrew teacher taught Nicene Christology has to do it without the red letters of John, which is the point.


## PART E, COMMENT LENGTH VERSIONS

**(1) Lead version.** Pull up a red letter Bible and look at six passages. Mark 16.9 to 20, the believe and be baptized verse and the snake handling verse, is missing from both of the two oldest Bibles in existence, Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, and Eusebius and Jerome both said the accurate copies ended at 16.8. John 7.53 to 8.11, the woman caught in adultery, is in no Greek manuscript before the 5th century and in later copies it floats around, sometimes landing in Luke. Luke 22.43 to 44, the bloody sweat, is missing from the oldest papyrus of Luke and from Vaticanus. Luke 23.34, Father forgive them, is missing from the oldest papyrus, from Vaticanus, and from Codex Bezae, three different streams agreeing. Luke 22.20, the new covenant in my blood, is missing from Bezae and the early Latin and the RSV dropped it in 1946. Matthew 6.13, for thine is the kingdom, is missing from every early manuscript and from the three oldest commentaries on the prayer. The Greek New Testament used to make your NIV and ESV puts the first four in double brackets and leaves the last one out. That is not me saying it. That is the church's own text.

**(2) Short version.** The two oldest Bibles in the world end Mark at 16.8 with the women running away scared. Everything after that, believe and be baptized, speak in tongues, take up serpents, drink poison, was added later, and the Greek text your NIV is translated from prints it in brackets. The woman caught in adultery is not in any Greek copy of John before the 5th century. Father forgive them is missing from the oldest copies of Luke. For thine is the kingdom is missing from every early manuscript of Matthew. Red ink is a printing decision from 1899. It is not evidence he said it.

**(3) Matthew 28.19 version.** Every Greek manuscript of Matthew 28.19 has the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit formula, and it is quoted in the Didache around the year 100 and by Irenaeus around 180, so nobody forged it in at Nicaea and anyone who tells you that has not read the sources. The real problem is different. Open the book of Acts. Every single baptism recorded there is in the name of Yeshua. Acts 2.38, 8.16, 10.48, 19.5. Not one is in the three names. Paul says the same in Romans 6.3 and Galatians 3.27. If the risen Lord had commanded his apostles to baptize in the three names, they disobeyed him every time, starting on Pentecost. The mainstream scholarly view, in the standard commentaries by Davies and Allison, Luz, and Meier, is that Matthew wrote down the baptism formula his own church was using around the year 85 and put it in the mouth of the risen Yeshua. Matthew wrote it. The apostles never used it.

**(4) The scribes always moved the text one direction.** Look at what the copyists changed when they changed something about who Yeshua was. At Luke 3.22 the voice from heaven in Codex Bezae and in every 2nd and 3rd century church father who quotes it says today I have begotten thee, Psalm 2.7, and the later copies changed it to in thee I am well pleased. At Mark 1.1 the original hand of Sinaiticus and Origen have no Son of God, later copies add it. At John 1.18 the later copies read only begotten Son where the early Egyptian copies read only begotten God. At Luke 2.33 the early copies say his father and his mother and the later copies changed it to Joseph and his mother. At Matthew 24.36 the early copies say not even the Son knows the day, and the later copies, and your KJV, delete nor the Son. Every change goes the same way, from a lower view of him to a higher one, never the reverse. Scribes who believed he was God from eternity fixed the text to match. The text they were fixing is the one the first Hebrew believers read.


## PART F, THE EARLIEST SOURCES, HOW THE GREEK GOSPELS WERE BUILT

Parts A and B asked whether particular red-letter sentences belong in the Greek gospels at all. This Part goes one level down and asks where the Greek gospels came from. None of the four was written from nothing. Each was assembled by an author with earlier material on his desk: the Gospel of Mark; a gospel written in Hebrew, which the church fathers say the apostle Matthew composed first and which this sheet calls the Hebrew Gospel; collections of sayings like those preserved in the Gospel of Thomas; and a sayings source scholars call Q. Luke admits it in his first sentence: "many have taken in hand to draw up a narrative" before him ([Luke 1.1 to 4, ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+1%3A1-4&version=ASV)). This matters because when a saying of Yeshua survives in an earlier source in one form and in a Greek gospel in another, the differences show what the gospel writer did to it. This Part sets the surviving fragments beside their Greek parallels and ends with a map of which gospel drew on which source.


### F1. Terms you will meet in this Part

**The Hebrew Gospel.** Several early church writers say the apostle Matthew wrote a gospel in the Hebrew language before any Greek gospel existed, and that Hebrew-speaking believers kept using it for centuries. The fathers call it by three names: Matthew in Hebrew, the Gospel according to the Hebrews, and the gospel of the Ebionites, the Greek church's name for Hebrew believers who kept the Torah. This sheet follows James R. Edwards of Whitworth University, whose *The Hebrew Gospel and the Development of the Synoptic Tradition* (Eerdmans, 2009) argues that the three names refer to one book ([Eerdmans](https://www.eerdmans.com/9780802862341/the-hebrew-gospel-and-the-development-of-the-synoptic-tradition/)). Jerome, the most learned scholar of the Latin church, says so himself. In his *Commentary on Matthew* 12.13 he speaks of "the Gospel which the Nazarenes and the Ebionites use," called by most "the authentic [Gospel] of Matthew" ([ECW Nazoreans](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelnazoreans.html)). In *Against the Pelagians* 3.2 he describes the Gospel according to the Hebrews, written "in the Chaldee and Syrian language, but in Hebrew characters," used by the Nazarenes, and called "according to the Apostles, or... according to Matthew" ([Against Pelagians 3](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/30113.htm)). One book, three names, one sentence. The book is lost; about seventy-five references and quotations survive in roughly two dozen early writers, by Edwards's count ([Whitworth release](https://news.whitworth.edu/2010/04/whitworth-professor-of-theology.html)). The quotations are called **fragments**.

**The Gospel of Thomas.** A collection of 114 sayings attributed to Yeshua, with no narrated miracles, no trial, no crucifixion, and no resurrection. The complete text survives only in Coptic, the language of Egyptian Christians, in a book found buried near Nag Hammadi in Upper Egypt in December 1945 (Nag Hammadi Codex II, tractate 2), copied shortly before about 350. Earlier, Bernard Grenfell and Arthur Hunt had dug three Greek scraps of the same work out of the ancient rubbish heaps of Oxyrhynchus, another Egyptian town, publishing them in 1897 and 1904 as P.Oxy 1, 654, and 655; the oldest was copied shortly after 200 ([ECW Thomas](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/thomas.html)). Each saying is a **logion** (plural **logia**), Greek for "saying."

**Q.** From the German *Quelle*, "source." Matthew and Luke share roughly 230 verses, nearly all sayings, that Mark lacks. Most scholars explain this by a lost written collection of sayings that both used. No manuscript of Q exists; it is reconstructed from the places where Matthew and Luke agree ([Wikipedia: Q source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q_source)). Q is cited by Luke's numbering, because Luke is thought to keep Q's order better, so "Q 6.20" means the Q saying at Luke 6.20.

**Markan priority.** The conclusion, held by nearly all scholars, that Mark was the first of the three Greek gospels and that Matthew and Luke copied from it. The evidence is shared order, shared wording, and the amount of Mark the other two reproduce.

**M and L.** M is material found only in Matthew, such as the wise men. L is material found only in Luke, such as the Good Samaritan, the prodigal son, Zacchaeus, and Emmaus. Whether each came from one written document is debated; that the material exists is not.

**The Two-Source hypothesis.** The standard model: Matthew and Luke each used Mark and Q, independently of each other. Christian Weisse proposed it in 1838 and Heinrich Holtzmann developed it in 1863; B. H. Streeter's *The Four Gospels* (1924) added M and L. It is the majority view.

**The Critical Edition of Q.** The reconstructed text of Q produced by the International Q Project of the Society of Biblical Literature, begun in 1985 and published in 2000, edited by James M. Robinson, Paul Hoffmann, and John S. Kloppenborg ([Wikipedia: Q source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q_source)).


### F2. The witnesses to the Hebrew Gospel

The witnesses are listed in the order they wrote. Edwards notes they are "as ancient as patristic witnesses to any of the four canonical Gospels" ([McGrath review](https://www.patheos.com/blogs/religionprof/2010/07/review-of-the-hebrew-gospel-and-the-development-of-the-synoptic-tradition.html)).

**Ignatius of Antioch (about 110).** Bishop of Antioch, writing on his way to execution in Rome. In *Smyrnaeans* 3.2 he quotes the risen Lord: "Lay hold, handle me, and see that I am not a bodiless demon" ([Ignatius, Smyrnaeans](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0109.htm)). He names no source; Jerome later places the saying in the Hebrew Gospel (F3.5). CONTESTED as a Hebrew Gospel quotation, because Origen assigns it to the Teaching of Peter and Eusebius says he does not know its source.

**Papias of Hierapolis (about 110 to 130).** Bishop of Hierapolis in Asia Minor, who collected reports from those who had known the apostles. He says Matthew "compiled the logia in the Hebrew language, and each interpreted them as he could" (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.16, [HE 3](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm)). He also told "of a woman accused of many sins before the Lord," a story Eusebius says the Gospel according to the Hebrews contains (*HE* 3.39.17, same link). ESTABLISHED as text; the meaning of "logia" is CONTESTED.

**Hegesippus (about 180).** Author of five lost books of memoirs on the early church. Eusebius reports that he quoted from the Gospel according to the Hebrews, from the Syriac, and from the Hebrew language, and concludes that Hegesippus was himself a believer from among the Hebrews (*HE* 4.22.8, [HE 4](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250104.htm)). ESTABLISHED.

**Irenaeus of Lyons (about 180).** Bishop of Lyons in Gaul, who as a boy had heard Polycarp. Matthew "issued a written Gospel among the Hebrews in their own dialect" (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1, [AH 3.1](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103301.htm)). The Ebionites "use the Gospel according to Matthew only, and repudiate the Apostle Paul" (1.26.2, [AH 1.26](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103126.htm)); they "use Matthew's Gospel only" (3.11.7, [AH 3.11](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103311.htm)). ESTABLISHED.

**Pantaenus (late second century).** Head of the Christian school at Alexandria and teacher of Clement. Eusebius records that Pantaenus found in India the gospel of Matthew "in Hebrew letters," left there by the apostle Bartholomew (*HE* 5.10.3, [HE 5](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250105.htm)). ESTABLISHED that Eusebius reports it; the historicity is CONTESTED.

**Clement of Alexandria (about 200).** Teacher in Alexandria. He quotes the Gospel according to the Hebrews by name: "He that wonders shall reign, and he that reigns shall rest" (*Stromata* 2.9.45, [Strom. 2](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/02102.htm); longer form at 5.14.96, [Strom. 5](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/02105.htm)). ESTABLISHED.

**Origen (about 230).** The early church's greatest Bible scholar, at Alexandria and then Caesarea, where he built the library Jerome later used. Eusebius preserves his statement that Matthew wrote first, in Hebrew, for believers from among the Hebrews (*HE* 6.25.4, [HE 6](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250106.htm)). Origen quotes the Hebrew Gospel's "my mother, the Holy Spirit" (*Commentary on John* 2.12, [Comm. John 2](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/101502.htm); *Homilies on Jeremiah* 15.4), and a Latin version of his *Commentary on Matthew* 15.14 preserves the second rich man ([ECW Nazoreans](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelnazoreans.html)). His first homily on Luke names a "Gospel according to the Twelve Apostles" among the "many" of Luke 1.1 (identification CONTESTED). *HE* 6.25.4 is ESTABLISHED.

**Eusebius of Caesarea (about 320).** Bishop of Caesarea and the church's first historian. Matthew wrote in his native tongue before leaving for other peoples (*HE* 3.24.6); the Hebrew believers "especially delight" in the Gospel according to the Hebrews (3.25.5); the Ebionites "used only the so-called Gospel according to the Hebrews" (3.27.4), all at [HE 3](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm). Beside Irenaeus: same people, same book, two names. Eusebius quotes the book himself in *Theophania* 4.12 ([Theophania 4](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/eusebius_theophania_05book4.htm)), and a Greek fragment of the same work gives its parable of the talents ([ECW Nazoreans](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelnazoreans.html)). ESTABLISHED.

**Epiphanius of Salamis (about 377).** Bishop of Salamis on Cyprus, born in the land of Israel, author of the *Panarion*, a "medicine chest" against eighty heresies. He is a hostile witness. The Nazoraeans have Matthew "most complete, in Hebrew" (*Panarion* 29.9.4, [Panarion 29](https://nazarenejudaism.com/?page_id=638)). The Ebionites accept Matthew and "call it 'according to the Hebrews'" (30.3.7); their copy is "falsified and mutilated" (30.13.2) and lacks the genealogies (30.14.3), all at [ECW Ebionites](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelebionites-panarion.html). He then quotes it at length in Greek, which Edwards identifies as "a Greek translation of the Hebrew Gospel" ([Edwards NTS 2002](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/new-testament-studies/article/gospel-of-the-ebionites-and-the-gospel-of-luke/EF0F206B16D7853D6C12597C9B7113B9)). ESTABLISHED.

**Didymus the Blind (late fourth century).** Alexandrian teacher. In his *Commentary on the Psalms* 184.9 to 10 (Tura papyri) he says Matthew seems to be called Levi in Luke, and the Gospel according to the Hebrews shows Levi is Matthias. ESTABLISHED as a Didymus reference (A. F. J. Klijn, *Jewish-Christian Gospel Tradition*, Brill, 1992; Edwards, Appendix I); exact wording unverified, no free online text.

**Jerome (about 390 to 415).** Translator of the Latin Vulgate, who learned Hebrew and lived in Bethlehem. He calls it the Gospel according to the Hebrews, "which I have recently translated into Greek and Latin" (*Lives of Illustrious Men* 2). Matthew "published in Judea in Hebrew"; a copy is "preserved until the present day in the library at Caesarea"; Jerome copied it from "the Nazarenes of Beroea" in Syria (*Lives* 3), both at [DVI](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2708.htm). His one-book statements are quoted in F1. He quotes the book more than anyone. ESTABLISHED.

**The *****to Ioudaikon***** margin notes (archetype about 370 to 500).** A group of Greek gospel manuscripts, among them codices 4, 273, 566, 899, and 1424, compare Matthew's wording in the margin with the reading of *to Ioudaikon*, the manuscripts' own Greek label for the Hebrew Gospel ([ECW Nazoreans](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelnazoreans.html); [ECW Hebrews, M.R. James](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelhebrews-mrjames.html)). Greek scribes still had access to the book into the fifth century. ESTABLISHED as a manuscript phenomenon.

**The Stichometry of Nicephorus (ninth century, copying an older list).** A list attached to the chronicle of Nicephorus, patriarch of Constantinople, gives the Gospel according to the Hebrews 2,200 lines against 2,500 for Matthew ([ECW Hebrews, M.R. James](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelhebrews-mrjames.html)). It was a full gospel, slightly shorter than Greek Matthew. ESTABLISHED.

**Late medieval witnesses.** Haimo of Auxerre (about 850) reports a tradition about the Hebrews at the cross (F3.10). Sedulius Scotus (ninth century) cites the gospel "according to the Hebrews"; the fourteenth-century *Historia Passionis Domini* cites "the Gospel of the Nazarenes"; a thirteenth-century note to Peter of Riga's *Aurora* says rays from Yeshua's eyes drove the crowd from the Temple. Loci unverified or witnesses very late: QUARANTINED for direct quotation.


### F3. The fragments and their parallels

Entries are grouped: (a) parallels found only in Luke; (b) earlier or shorter forms; (c) same scene, different detail; (d) absent from the Greek gospels. Edwards argues the Hebrew Gospel was the source of Luke's special material: the fragments show no agreement with what is unique to Mark, some with Matthew's special material, and "a preponderance of points of intersection" with Luke ([McGrath review](https://www.patheos.com/blogs/religionprof/2010/07/review-of-the-hebrew-gospel-and-the-development-of-the-synoptic-tradition.html)). In his model Luke drew on "two prior documents, the Hebrew Gospel and the Gospel of Mark" ([Whitworth release](https://news.whitworth.edu/2010/04/whitworth-professor-of-theology.html)). Group (a) is what the fragments themselves show when laid beside Luke, in line with Edwards's thesis; it is this sheet's compilation, not a table from his book.


#### (a) Found only in Luke

**F3.1. "In the days of Herod the king of Judaea"**

**The fragment.** Epiphanius says the book opens: "It came to pass in the days of Herod the king of Judaea," when Caiaphas was high priest, and introduces John as of the line of Aaron, son of Zacharias and Elisabeth (*Panarion* 30.13.6, 30.14.3, [ECW Ebionites](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelebionites-panarion.html)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** Luke 1.5: "There was in the days of Herod, king of Judæa," a priest Zacharias, whose wife Elisabeth was "of the daughters of Aaron" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+1%3A5&version=ASV)). Caiaphas: Luke 3.2 ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+3%3A2&version=ASV)). Matthew and Mark have none of these names.

**What the comparison shows.** Found only in Luke. Four details in one opening, the phrase itself, Aaron's line, Zacharias and Elisabeth, and Caiaphas, appear in no other Greek gospel. Edwards raised this case in 2002 ([Edwards NTS 2002](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/new-testament-studies/article/gospel-of-the-ebionites-and-the-gospel-of-luke/EF0F206B16D7853D6C12597C9B7113B9)).

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as texts. Luke parallel: strong.

**F3.2. "About thirty years of age"**

**The fragment.** The apostles speak in their own voice: "There appeared a certain man named Jesus, of about thirty years of age," who "chose us." He calls Matthew "at the receipt of custom" and chooses "twelve apostles for a testimony unto Israel" (*Panarion* 30.13.2 to 3, [ECW Ebionites](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelebionites-panarion.html)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** Luke 3.23: he "was about thirty years of age" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+3%3A23&version=ASV)). Luke 6.13: he "chose from them twelve, whom also he named apostles" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+6%3A13&version=ASV)). The tax office: Matthew 9.9 ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+9%3A9&version=ASV)).

**What the comparison shows.** Found only in Luke: no other Greek gospel gives Yeshua's age, and "chose... apostles" is Luke's wording. The scene itself differs in detail: it is told in the first person, by the twelve.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as texts. "About thirty": strong. "Chose... apostles": moderate.

**F3.3. "Have I desired with desire to eat flesh with you this Passover?"**

**The fragment.** "Have I desired with desire to eat flesh with you this Passover?" (*Panarion* 30.22.4, [ECW Ebionites](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelebionites-panarion.html)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** Luke 22.15: "With desire I have desired to eat this passover with you before I suffer" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+22%3A15&version=ASV)). Matthew and Mark have no such saying.

**What the comparison shows.** Found only in Luke. The doubled Hebrew idiom "with desire I have desired" is in Luke alone among the Greek gospels, and in the Hebrew Gospel. There it is a question expecting the answer no, with "flesh" as its object: Yeshua declines the Passover meat. In Luke it is a statement of longing. This is the standing Edwards case.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as texts. Luke parallel: strong.

**F3.4. "Seven times in a day" and "seventy times seven" in one exchange**

**The fragment.** "If your brother sin against you in word, and make amends," the Lord says, "receive him seven times in a day." Simon asks, "Seven times in a day?" He answers, "until seventy times seven" (Jerome, *Against the Pelagians* 3.2, [Against Pelagians 3](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/30113.htm)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** Luke 17.4: "if he sin against thee seven times in the day" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+17%3A3-4&version=ASV)). Matthew 18.22: "Until seventy times seven" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+18%3A21-22&version=ASV)).

**What the comparison shows.** Found only in Luke for the first half; found only in Matthew for the second. The Hebrew Gospel holds both in one conversation. Luke kept one half and Matthew the other, the pattern expected if both drew on one source.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as text. Luke parallel: strong.

**F3.5. "Handle me, and see"**

**The fragment.** Ignatius: "Lay hold, handle me, and see that I am not a bodiless demon" (*Smyrnaeans* 3.2, [Ignatius, Smyrnaeans](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0109.htm)). Jerome places it in "the Gospel which I have recently translated": "Touch me and see me how that I am not an incorporeal spirit" (*Lives* 16, [DVI](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2708.htm)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** Luke 24.39: "handle me, and see; for a spirit hath not flesh and bones" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+24%3A37-39&version=ASV)). No other Greek gospel has it.

**What the comparison shows.** Found only in Luke, and an earlier, rougher form. "Bodiless demon" is the more Semitic expression; Luke's version reads as the same saying smoothed into an argument.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as texts. Luke parallel: strong. The attribution is CONTESTED (Origen, *On First Principles* preface 8, assigns it to the Teaching of Peter; Eusebius, *HE* 3.36.11, does not know its source). Say "Jerome places it in the Hebrew Gospel."

**F3.6. "This day have I begotten thee"**

**The fragment.** The Spirit descends as a dove "and enters into him"; the voice says, "Thou art my beloved Son," and "This day have I begotten thee." Then "a great light shone round about the place," and John asks Yeshua to baptize him (*Panarion* 30.13.7 to 8, [ECW Ebionites](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelebionites-panarion.html)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** The standard text of Luke 3.22: "Thou art my beloved Son; in thee I am well pleased" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+3%3A22&version=ASV)). But Codex Bezae and the Old Latin, with Justin (*Dialogue* 88, 103), read Psalm 2.7 at Luke 3.22: "this day have I begotten thee" ([Wikipedia: Codex Bezae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codex_Bezae)). John's request to be baptized by Yeshua is in Matthew 3.14 alone ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+3%3A13-15&version=ASV)).

**What the comparison shows.** Found only in Luke, in Luke's early Western text. The great light is absent from every Greek gospel text; it survives in two Old Latin manuscripts (a and g1) at Matthew 3.15, and Justin speaks of fire in the Jordan (*Dialogue* 88).

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as texts. Luke parallel: strong for the Lukan textual tradition. Whether Bezae preserves what Luke wrote is CONTESTED; Bart Ehrman argues it does.

**F3.7. The woman accused of many sins**

**The fragment.** Papias told "of a woman accused of many sins before the Lord," which Eusebius says the Gospel according to the Hebrews contains (*HE* 3.39.17, [HE 3](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** Either the adulteress of John 7.53 to 8.11, absent from the earliest manuscripts of John and placed after Luke 21.38 in family 13 ([Wikipedia: pericope adulterae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_and_the_woman_taken_in_adultery)), or the sinful woman of Luke 7.36 to 50, "a woman who was in the city, a sinner" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+7%3A36-50&version=ASV)), found only in Luke.

**What the comparison shows.** Found only in Luke on either reading: a story the manuscripts park in Luke, or a Luke-only scene. Either way, its earliest attestation is the Hebrew Gospel, through Papias.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as texts. Which story Papias meant is CONTESTED. Luke parallel: moderate.

**F3.8. "In Jerusalem"**

**The fragment.** A *to Ioudaikon* note at Matthew 4.5 says the Hebrew Gospel reads "in Jerusalem" for "the holy city" ([ECW Nazoreans](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelnazoreans.html)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** Matthew 4.5: "into the holy city" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+4%3A5&version=ASV)). Luke 4.9: "he led him to Jerusalem" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+4%3A9&version=ASV)).

**What the comparison shows.** Found only in Luke: the Hebrew Gospel agrees with Luke against Matthew.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as a manuscript note. Luke parallel: moderate.

**F3.9. No "three days and three nights"**

**The fragment.** A note at Matthew 12.40 says the Hebrew Gospel lacks "three days and three nights" ([ECW Nazoreans](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelnazoreans.html)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** Matthew 12.40: "three days and three nights in the heart of the earth" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+12%3A40&version=ASV)). Luke 11.30 has only "as Jonah became a sign unto the Ninevites" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+11%3A29-30&version=ASV)).

**What the comparison shows.** Found only in Luke, and shorter. The Hebrew Gospel agrees with Luke and lacks the clause Part B (B17) identified as Matthew's composition.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as a manuscript note. Luke parallel: moderate.

**F3.10. "Father, forgive them" and the Hebrews at the cross**

**The fragment.** Haimo of Auxerre (about 850) reports that at "Father, forgive them," many thousands of the Hebrews standing around the cross believed (*Commentary on Isaiah* 53.12, paraphrase, [ECW Nazoreans](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelnazoreans.html)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** Luke 23.34: "Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+23%3A34&version=ASV)), only in Luke and textually uncertain (Part A, A4).

**What the comparison shows.** Found only in Luke, with a mass response by Hebrews that no Greek gospel records.

**Tier.** CONTESTED (late witness). Luke parallel: moderate.

**F3.11. The servant who "devoured" with harlots**

**The fragment.** A Greek fragment of Eusebius's *Theophania* gives the Hebrew Gospel's talents: one servant multiplied the money, one hid it, one "devoured his master's substance with harlots and flute-girls"; one was accepted, one rebuked, one imprisoned ([ECW Nazoreans](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelnazoreans.html)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** Matthew 25.14 to 30; Luke 19.11 to 27. The wasteful servant matches the prodigal, who "hath devoured thy living with harlots" (Luke 15.30, [ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+15%3A13-30&version=ASV)), found only in Luke.

**What the comparison shows.** The same parable with a different detail, carrying an echo of a Luke-only parable.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as text; the *Patrologia Graeca* locus is unverified. Luke parallel: weak to moderate.


#### (b) Earlier or shorter forms

**F3.12. "Bread of tomorrow"**

**The fragment.** For the Greek word translated "daily," the Hebrew Gospel has *mahar*, "of tomorrow" (Jerome, *Commentary on Matthew* 6.11, [ECW Nazoreans](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelnazoreans.html)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** Matthew 6.11 and Luke 11.3: "Give us this day our daily bread" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+6%3A11&version=ASV)). The Greek word, *epiousios*, is found nowhere before the gospels.

**What the comparison shows.** An earlier form. A plain Hebrew word explains an otherwise unknown Greek one; the direction runs from Hebrew to Greek.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED.

**F3.13. Seek, find, marvel, reign, rest**

**The fragment.** "He that wonders shall reign, and he that reigns shall rest" and "He that seeks will not rest until he finds" (Clement, *Stromata* 2.9.45, [Strom. 2](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/02102.htm); 5.14.96, [Strom. 5](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/02105.htm)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** Matthew 7.7 and Luke 11.9: "seek, and ye shall find" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+7%3A7&version=ASV)). Thomas logion 2 has the full chain (F4).

**What the comparison shows.** The Hebrew Gospel and Thomas carry five steps; the Greek gospels keep two. Two early witnesses agree on the longer chain.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as texts. Which form is older is CONTESTED.

**F3.14. Zechariah son of Jehoiada**

**The fragment.** Where Matthew has "son of Barachiah," the Hebrew Gospel reads "son of Jehoiada" (Jerome, *Commentary on Matthew* 23.35, [ECW Nazoreans](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelnazoreans.html)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** Matthew 23.35: "Zachariah son of Barachiah" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+23%3A35&version=ASV)). Luke 11.51 gives no father ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+11%3A51&version=ASV)). The priest murdered in the Temple court was son of Jehoiada (2 Chronicles 24.20 to 22, [ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2+Chronicles+24%3A20-22&version=ASV)).

**What the comparison shows.** An earlier form. The Hebrew Gospel has the historically correct name; Greek Matthew confuses the murdered priest with the prophet Zechariah son of Berechiah (Zechariah 1.1). Luke avoids the error by naming no father.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED that Matthew's name is a known problem.

**F3.15. "Hosanna barrama"**

**The fragment.** Jerome writes that Matthew, "who wrote his Gospel in the Hebrew language," put *osianna barrama*, "Hosanna in the highest" (*Letter* 20.5, paraphrase, [Letter 20](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3001020.htm)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** Matthew 21.9: "Hosanna in the highest" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+21%3A9&version=ASV)); Mark 11.10.

**What the comparison shows.** An earlier form. "Hosanna" is untranslated Hebrew in the Greek; the Hebrew phrase stands behind the transliteration.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as text; link unverified.

**F3.16. No weather signs**

**The fragment.** A note at Matthew 16.2 to 3 says the Hebrew Gospel lacks the weather-signs passage ([ECW Nazoreans](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelnazoreans.html)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** Matthew 16.2 to 3, "It will be fair weather: for the heaven is red" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+16%3A2-3&version=ASV)), itself absent from Sinaiticus and Vaticanus; Luke 12.54 to 56 has a different form.

**What the comparison shows.** The shorter form, agreeing with the oldest Greek manuscripts of Matthew.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as a manuscript note.


#### (c) Same scene, different detail

**F3.17. The mason with the withered hand**

**The fragment.** "I was a mason seeking a livelihood with my hands," the man says, asking to be restored so he "may not shamefully beg" (Jerome, *Commentary on Matthew* 12.13, [ECW Nazoreans](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelnazoreans.html)). The same passage holds Jerome's one-book statement (F1).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** Matthew 12.9 to 14, "Stretch forth thy hand" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+12%3A9-14&version=ASV)); Mark 3.1 to 6; Luke 6.6 to 11.

**What the comparison shows.** Same scene, different detail: the man has a trade and a working man's reason.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED.

**F3.18. "These are my brethren and mother"**

**The fragment.** "These are my brethren and mother and sisters," those "who do the will of my Father" (*Panarion* 30.14.5, [ECW Ebionites](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelebionites-panarion.html)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** Matthew 12.50: "whosoever shall do the will of my Father who is in heaven" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+12%3A46-50&version=ASV)); Mark 3.35 has "the will of God"; Luke 8.19 to 21. Thomas 99 has the same saying (F4).

**What the comparison shows.** Same scene, different detail. The Hebrew Gospel, Matthew, and Thomas say "my Father"; Mark says "God."

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED.

**F3.19. "The whole fount of the Holy Spirit"**

**The fragment.** "The whole fount of the Holy Spirit descended upon him and rested on him," and the Spirit says, "My Son, in all the prophets I was waiting for thee," calling him "my first-begotten Son" (Jerome, *Commentary on Isaiah* 11.2, [ECW Vielhauer and Ogg](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelhebrews-ogg.html)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** Mark 1.10 to 11; Matthew 3.16 to 17; Luke 3.22; John 1.32, where the Spirit "abode upon him" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+1%3A32-33&version=ASV)); Isaiah 11.2, "the Spirit of Jehovah shall rest upon him" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah+11%3A2&version=ASV)).

**What the comparison shows.** Same scene, different detail, with material found nowhere in the Greek gospels: the Spirit, not a voice from heaven, speaks, has waited through all the prophets, and names Yeshua firstborn. It fits the Hebrew Gospel's picture of the Spirit as mother (F3.23).

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED.

**F3.20. Barabbas, "son of their teacher"**

**The fragment.** The Hebrew Gospel interprets Barabbas as "son of their teacher," *bar rabban* (Jerome, *Commentary on Matthew* 27.16, [ECW Nazoreans](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelnazoreans.html)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** Matthew 27.16, "a notable prisoner, called Barabbas" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+27%3A16&version=ASV)); Mark 15.7; Luke 23.18 to 19; John 18.40.

**What the comparison shows.** Same scene, different detail: the name is read as Hebrew and given a meaning.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED.

**F3.21. The remaining margin notes**

**The fragment.** The *to Ioudaikon* notes also record ([ECW Nazoreans](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelnazoreans.html)): at Matthew 7.21 to 23, "If ye be in my bosom" yet do not his Father's will, he will cast them out of his bosom; at 10.16, "wise more than serpents"; at 11.12, the kingdom "is plundered"; at 11.25, a different verb for "I thank thee"; at 15.5, a gloss on Corban; at 16.17, "son of John" for "Bar-Jona"; at 26.74, Peter "denied and swore and cursed"; at 27.65, armed men guarding "day and night."

**The Greek gospel parallel.** The matching verses of Matthew, with Luke 13.25 to 27, 16.16, 10.21, and Mark 7.11. On "son of John," compare John 1.42, "Simon the son of John" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+1%3A42&version=ASV)), and John 21.15 to 17.

**What the comparison shows.** Same scene, different detail; "son of John" agrees with John against Greek Matthew.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as manuscript notes.


#### (d) Absent from the Greek gospels

**F3.22. "I came to abolish sacrifices"**

**The fragment.** "I came to abolish sacrifices," and "if ye cease not from sacrificing, the wrath will not cease from you" (*Panarion* 30.16.4 to 5, [ECW Ebionites](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelebionites-panarion.html)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** None. The nearest theme is Hosea 6.6 quoted in Matthew 9.13 and 12.7, "I desire mercy, and not sacrifice" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+9%3A13&version=ASV)), and Mark 12.33. The form mirrors Matthew 5.17, "I came not to destroy, but to fulfil" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+5%3A17&version=ASV)).

**What the comparison shows.** Absent from the Greek gospels. The Hebrew Gospel's Yeshua says outright what the Greek gospels only approach through Hosea. The Clementine *Recognitions* 1.39 says baptism was given so sins would be remitted "instead of sacrifices" ([Homilies 3](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/080803.htm)), a thematic parallel, not a quotation.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as text.

**F3.23. "My mother, the Holy Spirit"**

**The fragment.** "My mother, the Holy Spirit, take me by one of my hairs," and carry him to "the great mountain Tabor" (Origen, *Commentary on John* 2.12, [Comm. John 2](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/101502.htm); [ECW Vielhauer and Ogg](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelhebrews-ogg.html)). Origen repeats it in *Homilies on Jeremiah* 15.4; Jerome on Micah 7.6 and Ezekiel 16.13 ([ECW Hebrews, M.R. James](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelhebrews-mrjames.html)), and reportedly on Isaiah 40.9.

**The Greek gospel parallel.** The temptation, "led up of the Spirit" to "an exceeding high mountain" (Matthew 4.1, 8, [ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+4%3A1-8&version=ASV); Luke 4.1, 5), or the transfiguration (Mark 9.2).

**What the comparison shows.** Absent from the Greek gospels. In Hebrew the word for spirit, *ruach*, is feminine, so the saying is natural in Hebrew and strange in Greek, where the word is neuter; Jerome makes the point himself. A saying that only works in Hebrew was composed in Hebrew.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED.

**F3.24. The honey-cake**

**The fragment.** John's food was wild honey tasting of manna, "as a cake dipped in oil"; no locusts (*Panarion* 30.13.4 to 5, [ECW Ebionites](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelebionites-panarion.html)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** Mark 1.6: John "did eat locusts and wild honey" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+1%3A6&version=ASV)); Matthew 3.4.

**What the comparison shows.** Same scene, but the locusts are absent: the Hebrew believers' text gives John a diet without animal flesh. The wording plays on Greek *enkris*, "cake," against *akris*, "locust," so this detail belongs to the Greek translation Epiphanius was reading.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as text.

**F3.25. The second rich man**

**The fragment.** There are two rich men. To the second the Lord says, "How sayest thou, I have kept the law?" and "Many of thy brethren, sons of Abraham, are clad in filth," while his house is full of good things. Simon, "son of Jonah," sits beside him (Latin *Commentary on Matthew* 15.14 attributed to Origen, [ECW Nazoreans](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelnazoreans.html)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** Matthew 19.16 to 24: "All these things have I observed"; "give to the poor" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+19%3A16-24&version=ASV)); Mark 10.17 to 25; Luke 18.18 to 25.

**What the comparison shows.** The scene is shared; the second rich man and the rebuke are absent from the Greek gospels. Keeping the Torah is tied to love of neighbor: a man whose fellow Hebrews go in rags has not kept the law.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as text; Origen's authorship of the Latin commentary is CONTESTED.

**F3.26. The risen Yeshua appears to James**

**The fragment.** After giving "his grave clothes to the servant of the priest," the Lord goes to James, who had vowed not to eat until he saw him risen, takes bread, blesses and breaks it: "my brother eat your bread, for the son of man is risen" (Jerome, *Lives* 2, [DVI](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2708.htm)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** No Greek gospel has an appearance to James; Paul does: "then he appeared to James" (1 Corinthians 15.7, [ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Corinthians+15%3A7&version=ASV)). Bread blessed and broken by the risen Lord is in Luke's Emmaus story alone (Luke 24.30, 35, [ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+24%3A30-35&version=ASV)).

**What the comparison shows.** Absent from the Greek gospels. James, Yeshua's brother, is a primary resurrection witness, and Yeshua himself ends the fast James had vowed. The bread-breaking echoes a Luke-only scene; that is this sheet's observation.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as text.

**F3.27. The lintel**

**The fragment.** "The lintel of the Temple, of immense size, was broken and split" (Jerome, *Commentary on Matthew* 27.51 and *Letter* 120.8, [ECW Nazoreans](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelnazoreans.html)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** Matthew 27.51: "the veil of the temple was rent in two" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+27%3A51&version=ASV)); Mark 15.38; Luke 23.45.

**What the comparison shows.** Same moment, different portent; the broken lintel is absent from the Greek gospels. A torn veil is a theological sign; a cracked stone lintel is a physical event in the building. Compare the portents before 70 in Josephus, *War* 6.293 to 296.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as text.

**F3.28. "Never be joyful except when you look on your brother in love"**

**The fragment.** "Never be ye joyful, save when ye behold your brother with love" (Jerome, *Commentary on Ephesians* 5.4). With it: among the greatest crimes is "he that has grieved the spirit of his brother" (*Commentary on Ezekiel* 18.7), both at [ECW Vielhauer and Ogg](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelhebrews-ogg.html).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** None. Compare Matthew 5.22, "every one who is angry with his brother" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+5%3A22&version=ASV)), and Matthew 18.6; Thomas 25 shares the theme.

**What the comparison shows.** Absent from the Greek gospels. Love of the brother is the measure of joy itself.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as texts.

**F3.29. "What sin have I committed?"**

**The fragment.** Yeshua's mother and brothers say, "let us go and be baptized by him." He answers, "What sin have I committed that I should go and be baptized by him?" In the same passage, "even the prophets, after they were anointed with the Holy Spirit," spoke sinful words (Jerome, *Against the Pelagians* 3.2, [Against Pelagians 3](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/30113.htm)); a margin note at Matthew 18.22 repeats the prophets' line ([ECW Nazoreans](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelnazoreans.html)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** None for the family's invitation. Matthew 3.14 has an objection, but John's: "I have need to be baptized of thee" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+3%3A14&version=ASV)).

**What the comparison shows.** Absent from the Greek gospels. Thomas 104 asks the same question in nearly the same words (F4).

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED as text.

**F3.30. "I will select to myself"**

**The fragment.** "I will select to myself these things," and "very very excellent are those whom my Father" has given him, which Eusebius found in the gospel written "in the Hebrew language" (*Theophania* 4.12, [Theophania 4](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/eusebius_theophania_05book4.htm)).

**The Greek gospel parallel.** None in the Synoptics; compare John 17.6, "the men whom thou gavest me" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+17%3A6&version=ASV)), and John 6.37.

**What the comparison shows.** Absent from the Greek gospels, with a Johannine echo.

**Tier.** ESTABLISHED.

**Excluded attributions.** A Coptic sermon attributed to Cyril of Jerusalem quotes a "Gospel of the Hebrews" in which the power Michael entered Mary; it is probably a different text. Hippolytus's gospel of the Naassenes belongs to a Gnostic sect, not the Nazarenes ([ECW Nazoreans](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/gospelnazoreans.html)). "Unless you fast from the world" is Thomas 27, not the Hebrew Gospel. All QUARANTINED as Hebrew Gospel quotations.


### F4. The Gospel of Thomas

**What it is.** A list of sayings, each opening "Jesus said," with almost no story. About half of its 114 sayings have a parallel in Matthew, Mark, or Luke, and roughly a third have one in Q; the rest appear nowhere in the Greek gospels. The Oxyrhynchus fragments prove it existed in Greek by about 200, and Hippolytus of Rome mentions it in his *Refutation* 5.7.20, written about 222 to 235 ([ECW Thomas](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/thomas.html)). Check every saying in Thomas Lambdin's translation ([ECW Lambdin](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/thomas-lambdin.html)), the Scholars Version by Stephen Patterson and Marvin Meyer ([ECW Patterson and Meyer](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/thomas-scholars.html)), and the collected commentary ([ECW Thomas commentary](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/thomas/)).

**The dating debate, stated fairly.** The early camp says Thomas preserves a sayings tradition independent of the Greek gospels. Helmut Koester of Harvard (*Ancient Christian Gospels*, 1990) put its earliest form in the first century. Stephen Patterson (*The Gospel of Thomas and Jesus*, 1993) argued its sayings lack Matthew's and Luke's editorial fingerprints and dated it about 70 to 80. John Dominic Crossan put its earliest layer in the 50s; Stevan Davies dated it 50 to 75. April DeConick (*Recovering the Original Gospel of Thomas*, 2005) proposed a "kernel" from about 30 to 50 with additions to about 120.

The late camp says Thomas used the Greek gospels. Mark Goodacre (*Thomas and the Gospels*, Eerdmans, 2012) argues Thomas reproduces wording Matthew and Luke themselves created, which he calls "diagnostic shards." His lead case is logion 79, which joins two Luke-only sayings, Luke 11.27 to 28 and 23.29; he adds logion 5's Lukan wording, logion 14's "eat what is set before you" (Luke 10.8), and logia 65 to 66, which follow the Synoptic order of tenants and then the rejected stone. Simon Gathercole (*The Composition of the Gospel of Thomas*, Cambridge, 2012) dates it to the second century and argues it used Matthew and Luke. Nicholas Perrin (*Thomas and Tatian*, 2002) ties it to Tatian's gospel harmony of the 170s, a minority view.

Fairly stated: Thomas as a finished book may be second century while some sayings preserve forms older than the Greek gospels'. Every date is CONTESTED.

**The eight logia judged earlier than the canonical form.** In each case named scholars argue Thomas's form is older, usually because it lacks an allegory or setting the Greek gospels added. The parallels are ESTABLISHED; each priority judgment is CONTESTED.

**Thomas 9, the sower.** Thomas gives the parable with no explanation. Mark adds an allegory, "The sower soweth the word" (Mark 4.14, [ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+4%3A3-20&version=ASV)), copied in Matthew 13 and Luke 8. Koester, Patterson, and Crossan judge the bare parable earlier. CONTESTED.

**Thomas 20, the mustard seed.** In Thomas the seed puts out a great branch that shelters the birds. Luke's "became a tree" (Luke 13.19, [ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+13%3A18-19&version=ASV)) and Mark's "great branches" (Mark 4.32) move toward the prophets' great cedar. Crossan and Patterson judge Thomas earlier. CONTESTED.

**Thomas 54, the poor.** "Blessed are the poor, for yours is the kingdom of heaven" ([ECW Lambdin](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/thomas-lambdin.html)). Luke 6.20, "Blessed are ye poor" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+6%3A20&version=ASV)), agrees; Matthew 5.3 has "poor in spirit" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+5%3A3&version=ASV)). CONTESTED for Thomas's priority; that "in spirit" is Matthew's addition is widely held.

**Thomas 64, the great supper.** Guests excuse themselves for business, and the saying ends: "Businessmen and merchants will not enter the places of my Father" ([ECW Lambdin](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/thomas-lambdin.html)). Luke 14.16 to 24 is close ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+14%3A16-24&version=ASV)). Matthew 22.1 to 14 makes the host a king who "burned their city" and adds the wedding garment ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+22%3A1-14&version=ASV)). Crossan and Patterson judge Thomas earlier. CONTESTED.

**Thomas 65, the tenants.** Tenants beat the servants and kill the son, and the story ends. Mark 12.1 to 9 opens with Isaiah 5's hedge, winepress, and tower, making it an allegory of Israel, and ends with the owner who "will come and destroy the husbandmen" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+12%3A1-9&version=ASV)). Koester, Crossan, and Patterson judge Thomas earlier; Klyne Snodgrass (*The Parable of the Wicked Tenants*, 1983) disagrees. CONTESTED.

**Thomas 76, the pearl.** A merchant with a consignment of goods comes upon a pearl and sells everything for it; a treasure follows that no moth devours. Matthew 13.45 has "a merchant seeking goodly pearls" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+13%3A45-46&version=ASV)); the treasure line matches Luke 12.33 ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+12%3A33&version=ASV)). Crossan judges Thomas earlier. CONTESTED.

**Thomas 100, Caesar's coin.** Yeshua is shown a gold coin, with no plot by Pharisees and Herodians, and answers that Caesar, God, and he each receive their own, ending "give me what is mine" ([ECW Lambdin](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/thomas-lambdin.html)). Mark 12.13 to 17 sets the saying inside a trap ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+12%3A13-17&version=ASV)). Patterson and Crossan judge the bare setting earlier. CONTESTED.

**Thomas 107, the lost sheep.** The lost sheep is the largest, and the shepherd says, "I love you more than the ninety-nine" ([ECW Patterson and Meyer](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/thomas-scholars.html)). Compare Luke 15.4 to 7 ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+15%3A4-7&version=ASV)) and Matthew 18.12 to 14. Patterson argues priority; others read "the largest" as a later touch making the sheep a special elect soul. CONTESTED, with the counter-reading strong.

**Thomas and the Hebrew Gospel.** Three sayings match Hebrew Gospel fragments closely. Thomas 2 has the same chain, seek, find, marvel, reign, rest, as Clement's quotation (F3.13), confirmed in Greek by P.Oxy 654 ([Wikipedia: P.Oxy 654](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papyrus_Oxyrhynchus_654)). Thomas 99, "Those here who do the will of my father," matches *Panarion* 30.14.5 (F3.18). Thomas 104 sets a call to fasting and prayer against "What is the sin that I have committed," the question Jerome quotes from the Hebrew Gospel (F3.29). All three are ESTABLISHED as texts; which book borrowed, or whether both drew on a shared sayings tradition, is unknown. Two thematic overlaps are weaker and CONTESTED: Thomas 101's "true mother" beside "my mother, the Holy Spirit," and Thomas 25's love of the brother beside F3.28.

**Thomas 12, James the Just.** The disciples ask who will lead them after him, and he sends them to James the Just, "for whose sake heaven and earth came into being" ([ECW Lambdin](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/thomas-lambdin.html)). No Greek gospel has this; the nearest is the argument over "who was the greatest" (Mark 9.34, [ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+9%3A33-35&version=ASV)), where no successor is named. Thomas names James, Yeshua's brother, not Peter, as does the Hebrew Gospel's resurrection appearance (F3.26). Two early traditions put James first; the Greek gospels do not. ESTABLISHED as text and as a thematic match.

**Thomas 14, on food.** Thomas 14 warns that fasting, prayer, and alms bring harm, tells the disciples to "eat what they will set before you" and heal the sick, and says "what goes into your mouth will not defile you" ([ECW Lambdin](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/thomas-lambdin.html)). The parallels are Luke 10.8 ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+10%3A8-9&version=ASV)) and Mark 7.15 ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+7%3A15&version=ASV)). Present this honestly. Thomas has the mouth saying but not Mark's comment "making all meats clean," which Part B (B4) showed is Mark's gloss; like Mark 7.15, it contrasts outward and inward defilement and voids no law. Patterson judges the food saying early; Goodacre says "eat what is set before you" is Luke's wording copied by Thomas. CONTESTED. Do not cite Thomas 14 for abstaining from meat; it does not say that. Likewise Thomas 53, calling physical circumcision useless, is usually read as later polemic; using it to prove an anti-Torah Yeshua is QUARANTINED.


### F5. Q, the sayings source

**What it is.** Q is the material Matthew and Luke share that Mark lacks: about 230 verses, nearly all sayings, including the Beatitudes, love your enemies, the Lord's Prayer, ask, seek, knock, do not worry, and the lament over Jerusalem ([Wikipedia: Q source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q_source)). Friedrich Schleiermacher in 1832 read Papias's "logia" as a sayings collection; the letter Q came into use about fifty years later. ESTABLISHED as the majority view; the hypothesis itself is CONTESTED.

**Kloppenborg's layers.** In *The Formation of Q* (1987), John Kloppenborg argued Q grew in three stages. The first is six wisdom speeches: the sermon (Q 6.20 to 49), mission, prayer, fearless confession, anxiety, and discipleship. The second adds judgment: John's preaching of wrath, the controversies and woes against the Pharisees, and the coming of the Son of Man. The third is late additions: the temptation story (Q 4.1 to 13) and two Torah-affirming glosses (Q 11.42c and 16.17). Others, such as Dale Allison, layer Q differently. CONTESTED.

**What Q contains and lacks.** Q has no birth story, no genealogy, no Last Supper, no passion narrative, no empty tomb or resurrection appearance, and no teaching that Yeshua died "for our sins." "Son of God" appears in Q only in the devil's mouth at the temptation, "If thou art the Son of God" (Q 4.3, 9; [Luke 4.3, ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+4%3A3&version=ASV)), a story assigned to Q's latest layer. One saying speaks of "the Son" and "the Father" absolutely (Q 10.22, [ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+10%3A22&version=ASV)). No one in Q confesses Yeshua as Son of God, and there is no atoning death. The dominant titles are "Son of Man" and the envoy of Wisdom: "wisdom is justified of all her children" (Q 7.35, [ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+7%3A35&version=ASV)); "said the wisdom of God" (Q 11.49). Q's Lord's Prayer is Luke's short form, "Father, Hallowed be thy name" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+11%3A2-4&version=ASV)), with no "which art in heaven" and no "thy will be done." ESTABLISHED.

**The Farrer and Goodacre objection.** A significant minority says Luke used Mark and Matthew both, so Q never existed. Austin Farrer ("On Dispensing with Q," 1955), Michael Goulder (*Luke: A New Paradigm*, 1989), and Mark Goodacre (*The Case Against Q*, 2002) point to the "minor agreements," where Matthew and Luke agree in small details against Mark, and to "editorial fatigue," where Luke begins changing a Matthean passage and lapses back into Matthew's wording ([Wikipedia: Farrer hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farrer_hypothesis); [Goodacre's Case Against Q site](https://www.markgoodacre.org/Q/)). Alan Garrow argues Matthew used Mark and Luke; William Farmer's revived Griesbach hypothesis puts Matthew first and Mark last. All CONTESTED. Every model has Matthew and Luke copying written sources.

**Edwards and Q.** Write this carefully. Edwards puts the Hebrew Gospel behind Luke's special material, not as a one-for-one replacement for Q. He doubts Q is necessary and objects to it as a sayings collection without narrative; in his model, Luke's two prior documents are the Hebrew Gospel and Mark ([Whitworth release](https://news.whitworth.edu/2010/04/whitworth-professor-of-theology.html); [McGrath review](https://www.patheos.com/blogs/religionprof/2010/07/review-of-the-hebrew-gospel-and-the-development-of-the-synoptic-tradition.html)). The reports of his chapter 7 disagree, so on camera say only that. This sheet's own observation, not Edwards's claim: several fragments touch Q material. Seek and find (F3.13) is Q 11.9; seven times in a day (F3.4) is Q 17.3 to 4; bread of tomorrow (F3.12) is Q 11.3; Zechariah (F3.14) is Q 11.51. CONTESTED.


### F6. How the Greek gospels were assembled

**Mark, copied.** Matthew reproduces about 90 percent of Mark's subject matter, around 600 of Mark's 661 verses; Luke about 55 percent, around 350 ([Whitworth release](https://news.whitworth.edu/2010/04/whitworth-professor-of-theology.html)). Streeter: Matthew reproduces "90 per cent," Luke "rather more than half" (*The Four Gospels*, 1924). The copying, often word for word and in order, is not in doubt. ESTABLISHED.

**Q.** On the majority view, Matthew and Luke each also used Q's 230 verses of sayings (F5). CONTESTED, majority view.

**M and L.** Matthew alone has the antitheses of the Sermon on the Mount, the parables of chapters 13 and 25, and the "formula quotations" ("that it might be fulfilled"). Luke alone has the birth narratives, the Good Samaritan, the prodigal son, Zacchaeus, and Emmaus. ESTABLISHED as material; CONTESTED as single documents.

**The Hebrew Gospel behind Luke's special material.** Edwards's thesis is that Luke's special material came from the Hebrew Gospel. Luke's L material is heavily Hebraic, and Edwards calls some verses "hyper-Semitic," Greek that reads like translated Hebrew ([McGrath review](https://www.patheos.com/blogs/religionprof/2010/07/review-of-the-hebrew-gospel-and-the-development-of-the-synoptic-tradition.html)). The fragments show the pattern: Herod and Zacharias, the age of thirty, "with desire I have desired," seven times a day, "handle me and see," all in Luke alone (F3.1 to F3.5). The fathers, from Papias to Jerome, also place the Hebrew Gospel behind Matthew's tradition. CONTESTED, as Edwards's attributed thesis; Andrew Gregory argues the reverse direction ([Gregory NTS 2005](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/new-testament-studies/article/prior-or-posterior-the-gospel-of-the-ebionites-and-the-gospel-of-luke/00A0A8D125D13E2C501DB54732EA2067)).

**Thomas-type sayings collections.** Q and Thomas show that sayings collections without narrative were a real kind of early book; whether such collections fed the Greek gospels is CONTESTED (F4).

**John's signs source.** John numbers the miracles: "This beginning of his signs" (John 2.11, [ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+2%3A11&version=ASV)), then "the second sign" (John 4.54, [ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+4%3A54&version=ASV)), though 2.23 has already mentioned other signs. Rudolf Bultmann (1941) and Robert Fortna (*The Gospel of Signs*, 1970) argued John took over a written collection of numbered miracles, which also explains why 20.30 to 31 sounds like the end of a book ([Wikipedia: Signs Gospel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signs_Gospel)). CONTESTED.

**Prophecy testimonia.** Lists of messianic proof-texts existed: 4QTestimonia (4Q175) from Qumran is one ([Wikipedia: 4Q175](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4Q175)). Mark 1.2 introduces a quotation as "written in Isaiah the prophet" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+1%3A2-3&version=ASV)), but its first line is Malachi 3.1 and only the second is Isaiah 40.3, the kind of merged citation a list produces. Rendel Harris (*Testimonies*, 1916 to 1920) and Martin Albl (1999) trace Matthew's formula quotations to such lists. CONTESTED; the genre is ESTABLISHED.

**A pre-Markan passion narrative.** Rudolf Pesch and Raymond Brown (*The Death of the Messiah*, 1994) hold that Mark used an existing account of the arrest, trial, and crucifixion, which John may have known independently. CONTESTED.

**Luke 1.1 to 4.** "Many have taken in hand to draw up a narrative," and Luke, having traced everything, writes "in order" ([ASV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+1%3A1-4&version=ASV)). He claims to be a compiler, not an eyewitness. ESTABLISHED. Edwards links the "many" to the Hebrew Gospel (CONTESTED).

**Papias on Mark.** Mark, "having become the interpreter of Peter," wrote down accurately what he remembered, "not, indeed, in order" (*HE* 3.39.15, [HE 3](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm)). The earliest testimony about the earliest Greek gospel calls it secondhand memory. ESTABLISHED.

### F7. What this does to the red letters

Part A showed scribes adding sentences to the Greek gospels; Part B showed the gospel writers composing some of the red letters. Part F adds the layer underneath: the Greek gospels are compositions built from earlier sources, and one of them, the Hebrew Gospel, is partly recoverable. Where its fragments survive, we can watch a red-letter saying before it reached the Greek page.

Take the woman taken in adultery (A2). The church's own textual critics agree John did not write it; it floats in later copies, landing after Luke 21.38 in family 13. Part F supplies its earliest home. Papias, early in the second century, knew a story of a woman accused of many sins before the Lord, and Eusebius says the Hebrew Gospel contained it (F3.7). The story was first in the Hebrew Gospel and was later inserted into John and, in some copies, Luke.

Take the voice at the baptism. Parts C and D showed that Codex Bezae, the Old Latin, and the early fathers read "this day have I begotten thee" at Luke 3.22, and that later copies replaced it with "in thee I am well pleased." The Hebrew Gospel has "this day have I begotten thee" at the baptism (F3.6). The reading the scribes removed from Luke is the Hebrew Gospel's reading, and Luke's earliest Western text kept it. When the scribes raised the Christology, they moved Luke away from its Hebrew source.

Take the sayings the Greek gospels lack. "I came to abolish sacrifices" is in no Greek gospel (F3.22); they keep only Hosea's "mercy, and not sacrifice." Beside it stand the Spirit as Yeshua's mother (F3.23), John's food without locusts (F3.24), the rich man whose brethren, sons of Abraham, go in rags (F3.25), the appearance to James (F3.26), the broken lintel (F3.27), and joy measured by love of the brother (F3.28). None of it is in red in any Bible.

Take Part B's verdicts. B17 argued that Matthew's "three days and three nights" is Matthew's composition; the Hebrew Gospel lacks it (F3.9). A4 found "Father, forgive them" uncertain in Luke; the Hebrew Gospel tradition knew it and tied it to Hebrews at the cross (F3.10). Part C's discussion of the Passover and the blood covenant meets F3.3: in Luke, Yeshua longs to eat this Passover; in the Hebrew Gospel he asks whether he has desired to eat flesh with them, a question whose answer is no. The doubled Hebrew idiom shows they are one saying, and the Hebrew Gospel's form carries the Semitic grammar.

The result is a layered picture. At the bottom is the Hebrew Gospel's Yeshua, the earliest red-letter layer we can reach in a named document. On it, and on Mark and the sayings collections, the Greek gospel writers built their compositions, as Luke says he did. On top came the scribes of Part A. A red-letter Bible prints all three layers in one ink; the earliest was written in Hebrew, by and for Hebrew believers.


### F8. Comment-length versions

**(1) The Hebrew Gospel came first.** Every early church writer who mentions it says Matthew wrote first and wrote in Hebrew. Papias around 110 to 130 says Matthew compiled the sayings in the Hebrew language. Irenaeus around 180 says Matthew issued a gospel among the Hebrews in their own dialect. Origen says Matthew wrote first, in Hebrew, for believers from among the Hebrews. Jerome says a copy sat in the library at Caesarea and he copied it from the Nazarenes in Beroea, and in Against the Pelagians 3.2 he gives its names in one sentence: according to the Hebrews, according to the Apostles, according to Matthew. Our Greek Matthew copies about 90 percent of Mark, so it is not that book. The Hebrew Gospel came first, and the fathers quote it about 75 times.

**(2) Luke used the Hebrew Gospel.** Lay the surviving fragments of the Hebrew Gospel next to Luke. It opens in the days of Herod the king of Judaea with Zacharias, Elisabeth, and the line of Aaron. Only Luke has that, Luke 1.5. It says Yeshua was about thirty. Only Luke, 3.23. It has desired with desire at the Passover. Only Luke, 22.15. It has forgive your brother seven times in a day and seventy times seven in one conversation, and Luke 17.4 kept the first half while Matthew 18.22 kept the second. It has handle me and see after the resurrection. Only Luke, 24.39. James Edwards laid out this thesis in The Hebrew Gospel and the Development of the Synoptic Tradition, Eerdmans 2009, and Luke 1.1 says many wrote before him.

**(3) The Greek gospels were assembled from sources.** Matthew copies about 90 percent of Mark and Luke about 55 percent, often word for word. Matthew and Luke also share about 230 verses of sayings Mark does not have, which most scholars call Q. Luke's own material lines up with the Hebrew Gospel the church fathers quote, which Edwards argues was Luke's source. The Gospel of Thomas, found in 1945, with Greek fragments from around 200, has the sower and the tenants without the allegories the Greek gospels attached. Luke opens by saying many had already written accounts. Papias says Mark wrote what he remembered from Peter, not in order. Every red letter passed through one of these sources before a gospel writer arranged it.
