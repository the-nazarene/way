# 🛡️ Polemic Answers — Was Yeshua God?

> **What this sheet is for.** This is a teaching document, not a cheat sheet. It is written for someone who has never studied the question and needs to be able to follow the argument, check every verse, and explain it to the next person. The question it answers is the biggest one in the whole set: did Yeshua claim to be God, did the people who knew him believe he was God, and does the New Testament actually say so? It is built in layers. First what Yeshua said about himself in the gospels. Then what the first believers said about him before any creed existed. Then every proof text the other side uses, one at a time, with the Greek or Hebrew where a word carries the weight and the manuscript history where a scribe changed it. Then the scholarship, who dates what, who wrote what, and what the scribes did to the text. Every source is introduced before it is used and linked where it sits. Every claim that is weaker than it sounds is marked. Read it top to bottom once, then use the objection list and the comment versions at the end.

## Shape of the argument

The thesis in plain language is this. Yeshua, speaking in the first three gospels, never once says he is God. He prays to God, calls the Father the only true God, says the Father is greater than he is, says there are things the Father knows that he does not, says no one is good except God, and after his resurrection still calls the Father "my God." The men who walked with him, preaching in the weeks after his death, called him a man approved by God, a servant God raised, a man God made Lord and Messiah. The Hebrew believers who kept his teaching for the next three centuries, the Nazarenes and Ebionites, held him to be the Messiah and the True Prophet, a man anointed by God, and the church fathers who hated them say so. The clear statements that Yeshua is God sit in the latest gospel, written sixty to eighty years after his death, and in a handful of letters that are either not by the man whose name they carry or rest on a text that scribes altered. The doctrine was argued over for three hundred years and was settled by a council convened by a Roman emperor in the year 325 using a Greek word that appears nowhere in scripture. That is the case. The rest of the sheet shows the work.

One distinction has to be made up front because the other side blurs it on purpose. **Exalted is not the same as God.** The New Testament says plainly that God raised Yeshua, seated him at his right hand, gave him a name above every name, and will have every knee bow to him. Our side agrees with all of that. A man can be raised, seated, honoured, and given authority by God and still be a man, which is exactly what Psalm 110 describes and what Peter preached at Pentecost. The question is never whether Yeshua was exalted. It is whether he was the God of Israel, the one who did the exalting. Keep that line clear and most of the proof texts fall away on their own.

## Terms you will meet

- **The Shema.** Deuteronomy 6.4, "Hear, O Israel, the LORD our God, the LORD is one." The confession of faith of Israel, recited twice a day. Yeshua quotes it as the first of all commandments. Everything below is measured against it. [Deuteronomy 6.4 at Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Deuteronomy.6.4).
- **Christology.** The study of who or what Christ is. **High Christology** means a view on which he is divine, pre existent, God. **Low Christology** means a view on which he is a human being chosen by God. Scholars speak of **exaltation Christology**, the view that a man was raised to divine honour at his resurrection or baptism, and **incarnation Christology**, the view that a divine being came down and became a man. The first is the earliest layer of the New Testament. The second is the latest.
- **Adoptionism.** The old name for exaltation Christology, the belief that Yeshua was adopted as God's Son at a point in time, usually his baptism or resurrection. The church later called it a heresy. It is what the earliest sources say.
- **Trinity.** The doctrine that God is one being in three persons, Father, Son and Holy Spirit, co equal and co eternal. The word does not appear in the Bible. The Greek *trias* is first used of God by Theophilus of Antioch around 180 and the Latin *trinitas* by Tertullian around 210.
- **Homoousios.** Greek for "of the same substance." The word the Council of Nicaea chose in 325 to say the Son is of one substance with the Father. It is not in scripture, and the council knew it was not, which is why it was fought over.
- **Septuagint, LXX.** The Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures made from the third century before Yeshua. The New Testament writers quoted scripture from it. Several arguments below turn on the difference between the Hebrew and the Greek.
- **ASV 1901.** The American Standard Version, the standing translation for this channel because it is literal, public domain and free of modern doctrinal smoothing. Verse links go to it at Blue Letter Bible.
- **Undisputed letters.** The seven letters scholars across the spectrum agree Paul wrote himself. Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, Philemon. The other six are disputed, and the three Pastorals (1 and 2 Timothy, Titus) are held by most scholars to be written in his name after his death.
- **Pseudonymity.** Writing a book in the name of a famous dead person. Common in the ancient world. When a verse is cited from a pseudonymous letter it tells you what a later writer taught, not what the apostle wrote.
- **Textual criticism.** The discipline that compares the surviving handwritten copies of the New Testament to work out what the earliest text said and where scribes changed it. The standard reference is Bruce Metzger, *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament* (2nd ed., 1994), [openlibrary](https://openlibrary.org/search?q=metzger+textual+commentary+greek+new+testament). For the specific question of scribes changing the text toward deity the reference is Bart Ehrman, *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* (1993), [openlibrary](https://openlibrary.org/search?q=ehrman+orthodox+corruption+of+scripture).
- **The great codices.** Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, two fourth century manuscripts that are the oldest nearly complete Bibles we have. Codex Bezae is a fifth century manuscript of the gospels and Acts whose text goes back to the second century and often preserves older readings. Papyri with a P number (P66, P75) are older fragments, second to third century. [Codex Sinaiticus online](https://www.codexsinaiticus.org/en/manuscript.aspx).
- **Nomina sacra.** "Sacred names." The scribal habit of abbreviating God, Lord, Jesus, Christ with a line over the top. Mentioned because a scholar named Hurtado uses them as evidence of early devotion, and because one of the most important scribal changes below (1 Timothy 3.16) happened inside one.
- **Harmonization.** Finding a reading on which two passages that seem to contradict can both be true. It is the other side's main tool. This sheet names each harmonization before answering it.
- **Hostile witness.** A source that has every reason to disagree with us and still confirms a fact. Raymond Brown, a Catholic priest, counting how few verses call Jesus God is a hostile witness. Epiphanius, who hated the Ebionites, reporting what they believed is a hostile witness.

## PART A. WHAT YESHUA SAID ABOUT HIMSELF AND GOD

Start with his own mouth, because if he was God he had three years of public ministry in which to say so, and the first three gospels report none of it.

### A1. He said God is one, and meant the Shema

**The text.** Mark 12.28 to 34. A scribe asks which commandment is first of all. Yeshua answers, "Hear, O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is one, and thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart." The scribe replies, "Of a truth, Teacher, thou hast well said that he is one, and there is none other but he," and Yeshua tells him, "Thou art not far from the kingdom of God." [Mark 12.29 ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/mar/12/29/).

**What it means.** A Jewish teacher in the year 30 recites the Shema, a Jewish scribe restates it in the strictest possible form, "none other but he," and Yeshua approves. There is no revision, no "and I am included in that one," no hint that the oneness has three persons inside it. If the Trinity were true this was the moment to say so, to a man who had just affirmed the opposite, and the answer was "well said."

**The standard harmonization.** "One (Hebrew *echad*) can mean a compound unity, like one cluster of grapes." *Echad* is the ordinary word for the number one and is used thousands of times for single things. Where the Hebrew Bible wants to say God is uniquely one it adds "and there is none else" (Deuteronomy 4.35, Isaiah 45.5), which is exactly what the scribe adds. The grape cluster argument is answered in full by James McGrath, *The Only True God, Early Christian Monotheism in Its Jewish Context* (2009), [openlibrary](https://openlibrary.org/search?q=mcgrath+the+only+true+god).

### A2. "Why callest thou me good?"

**The text.** Mark 10.17 to 18. A man kneels and says "Good Teacher, what shall I do that I may inherit eternal life?" Yeshua answers, "Why callest thou me good? None is good save one, even God." Luke 18.19 has the same words. [Mark 10.18 ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/mar/10/18/).

**What it means.** He separates himself from God by name and by the quality of goodness. The sentence has no meaning if the speaker is God.

**Watch Matthew change it.** Matthew 19.17, reporting the same scene, reads "Why askest thou me concerning that which is good? One there is who is good." The question about Yeshua's goodness has been turned into a question about goodness in general. This is the earliest datable edit toward a higher Christology in the gospel tradition, and you can see it happen by laying the two verses side by side. Matthew used Mark as a source, and this is one of the places where he was uncomfortable with what Mark said. [Matthew 19.17 ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/mat/19/17/).

**The standard harmonization.** "He was inviting the man to realise that Yeshua is in fact God." There is no verse in which that realisation happens. The man walks away sad about money. Mark's reader is never told that the question was a riddle. And Matthew would not have needed to rewrite a riddle.

### A3. The Father knows what the Son does not

**The text.** Mark 13.32, speaking of the end, "But of that day or that hour knoweth no one, not even the angels in heaven, neither the Son, but the Father." Matthew 24.36 adds the word *monos*, "the Father alone." [Mark 13.32 ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/mar/13/32/).

**What it means.** There is a fact that the Father knows and the Son does not. An omniscient being cannot not know something. The verse lists in descending order who lacks the knowledge, men, angels, the Son, and names the single exception, the Father.

**Scribes deleted it.** Many later manuscripts of Matthew 24.36 drop the words "neither the Son." That is why the King James Version does not have the phrase in Matthew. Scribes removed it because they read it the way we read it. The phrase is present in Sinaiticus, Vaticanus and Bezae, so it is original, and Metzger's *Textual Commentary* says the omission is doctrinally motivated.

**The standard harmonization.** Calvin argued that "know" here means "make known," as in 1 Corinthians 2.2 where Paul says he "determined not to know anything among you save Jesus Christ." The Greek breaks this. Paul's sentence is *ou gar ekrina ti eidenai*, "I decided not to know," and the idea of a choice comes from *ekrina*, "I resolved," which governs the verb. Mark 13.32 has no such verb. It is a flat present, *oudeis oiden*, "no one knows." And if "know" meant "announce," the verse would say the Father alone is here to announce it, which makes no sense, because the Father is not speaking in the passage. The second harmonization, used by most modern Trinitarians, concedes real ignorance and assigns it to his human nature, citing Philippians 2.7 and Luke 2.52. That is an admission that in the text as written, the Son does not know. The two natures are a theory brought to the verse from outside it, defined at Chalcedon in 451. The verse itself just says the Son does not know.

**Related.** Luke 2.52, he "advanced in wisdom." Hebrews 5.8, he "learned obedience." Acts 1.7, after the resurrection, the Father has set the times "by his own authority." Revelation 1.1, "the Revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave unto him." Even the exalted Yeshua receives knowledge of the future from God. The knowledge always flows to him, never from him.

### A4. "The Father is greater than I" and "the only true God"

**John 14.28.** "I go unto the Father, for the Father is greater than I." [ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/jhn/14/28/).

**John 17.3.** Praying to the Father, "this is life eternal, that they should know thee the only true God, and him whom thou didst send, even Jesus Christ." The only true God is "thee," the one being prayed to. The one sent is named separately. [ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/jhn/17/3/).

**John 20.17.** After the resurrection, to Mary, "I ascend unto my Father and your Father, and my God and your God." The risen Yeshua has a God. God does not have a God. [ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/jhn/20/17/).

**John 5.19 and 5.30.** "The Son can do nothing of himself." "I can of myself do nothing, as I hear, I judge." [ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/jhn/5/19/).

**John 10.29.** "My Father, who hath given them unto me, is greater than all."

These are all from John, the gospel the other side relies on most. The same book that has the highest statements also has the plainest subordination, and the subordination statements are the ones placed in Yeshua's own mouth as direct speech about himself. The Trinitarian answer, that he is speaking as a man, is treated under Objections.

### A5. He prayed, he was tempted, he died

**He prayed.** Mark 14.36, "Abba, Father, all things are possible unto thee, remove this cup from me, howbeit not what I will, but what thou wilt." Two wills, one submitting to the other. Luke 6.12, he spent the night in prayer to God. God does not pray to God. [Mark 14.36 ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/mar/14/36/).

**He was tempted.** Matthew 4.1 to 11, Hebrews 4.15, he was "in all points tempted like as we are." James 1.13, "God cannot be tempted with evil." The brother of Yeshua wrote that sentence. [James 1.13 ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/jam/1/13/).

**He died.** The whole gospel turns on it. 1 Timothy 6.16 says God "only hath immortality." Habakkuk 1.12, "O LORD my God, my Holy One, thou diest not" (the Hebrew reads "we shall not die" in the received text because the scribes altered it, one of the eighteen *tiqqune sopherim*, scribal corrections, but the original "thou diest not" is what the tradition records they changed). Numbers 23.19, "God is not a man." Hosea 11.9, "I am God, and not man." [Numbers 23.19 at Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Numbers.23.19).

**The standard harmonization.** The two natures again. The human nature prayed, was tempted, died, while the divine nature did none of those. Notice what this does. Every verse that shows Yeshua as a man is assigned to the human nature and every verse that exalts him is assigned to the divine nature, so no verse can ever count against the doctrine. A theory that no evidence can touch is not a reading of the text. It is a filter placed in front of it. And it was not defined until 451.

### A6. The titles, and what they meant to a Hebrew

**Son of God.** In the Hebrew Bible this is a title for the king (Psalm 2.7, "Thou art my son, this day have I begotten thee," spoken at the king's coronation, and 2 Samuel 7.14 of Solomon, "I will be his father, and he shall be my son"), for Israel (Exodus 4.22, "Israel is my son, my firstborn"), for angels (Job 1.6), and in Luke's genealogy for Adam (Luke 3.38, "Adam, the son of God"). It never means God. Nathanael says it in John 1.49 in the same breath as "King of Israel," and Matthew 16.16 pairs it with "the Christ." It is a royal and messianic title. [Psalm 2.7 at Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Psalms.2.7).

**Son of man.** Yeshua's own preferred name for himself, used over eighty times. In Hebrew and Aramaic *ben adam*, *bar enash*, it means a human being. God addresses Ezekiel as "son of man" ninety times. Daniel 7.13 has "one like unto a son of man" brought before the Ancient of Days and given a kingdom, which is a figure who receives authority from God, not God. [Daniel 7.13 at Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Daniel.7.13).

**Lord.** Greek *kyrios*, used for God, for masters, for husbands, for anyone owed respect. Sarah calls Abraham lord (1 Peter 3.6). Pilate is addressed as lord (Matthew 27.63). The verse the New Testament uses most to call Yeshua Lord is Psalm 110.1, "The LORD said unto my lord," and in Hebrew the first is YHWH and the second is *adoni*, the ordinary word for a human superior, not *Adonai*, which is reserved for God. Two different parties, and the psalm makes the second one sit at the first one's right hand. [Psalm 110.1 at Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Psalms.110.1).

**Messiah, Christ.** Hebrew *mashiach*, Greek *christos*, "anointed one." Every king and high priest of Israel was a messiah. Cyrus the Persian is called God's messiah in Isaiah 45.1. The word tells you the man was chosen and anointed by God. It tells you nothing about his being God.

### A7. When he was accused of making himself God, he denied it

**The text.** John 10.30 to 36. Yeshua says "I and the Father are one." The crowd takes up stones and says "thou, being a man, makest thyself God." His reply, verses 34 to 36, "Is it not written in your law, I said, Ye are gods? If he called them gods, unto whom the word of God came, say ye of him whom the Father sanctified and sent into the world, Thou blasphemest, because I said, I am the Son of God?" [John 10.34 to 36 ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/jhn/10/34/).

**What it means.** Given the direct accusation, he does not say "yes, I am God." He quotes Psalm 82.6, where God calls human judges "gods," and says in effect, if scripture can call men gods, how is it blasphemy for me to say I am the Son of God? He downgrades the charge from God to Son of God and defends the lesser title with a verse about human beings. And "one" in John 10.30 is explained by John himself at 17.21 to 22, where Yeshua prays that his disciples "may be one, even as we are one." The oneness is the oneness of purpose that the disciples are to share. Nobody thinks the disciples are to become one being.

**John 5.18.** The narrator says the Jews sought to kill him because he "called God his own Father, making himself equal with God." That is the accusers' interpretation, and Yeshua's immediate reply in 5.19 is "the Son can do nothing of himself, but what he seeth the Father doing." The verse the other side quotes is a charge, and the next verse is the defence.

### A8. When he forgave sins, the crowd knew what it meant

**The text.** Mark 2.5 to 12, the paralytic. Yeshua says "thy sins are forgiven." The scribes think, "who can forgive sins but one, even God?" He heals the man to show "that the Son of man hath authority on earth to forgive sins." Now read the ending Matthew gives it. Matthew 9.8, "when the multitudes saw it, they were afraid, and glorified God, who had given such authority unto men." [Matthew 9.8 ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/mat/9/8/).

**What it means.** The crowd's conclusion is that God has given authority to a man. Not that a man is God. And in John 20.23 Yeshua gives the same authority to the disciples, "whose soever sins ye forgive, they are forgiven," without anyone concluding the disciples are God. The scribes' objection in Mark 2.7 is the objection of the people who wanted him dead. It is not the narrator's teaching.

### A9. He was worshipped, and so were kings

The Greek word *proskyneō* means to bow down, to prostrate yourself. It is used of worship given to God and of the respect given to kings and superiors. Matthew 18.26, the servant "fell down and worshipped" the king in a parable, same word. 1 Chronicles 29.20 in the Septuagint, the people "worshipped" the LORD and the king, same verb, both objects. Revelation 3.9, Yeshua promises that his enemies will come and "worship before thy feet" at the church of Philadelphia, the same word, directed at Christians. The word does not settle who is God. When Yeshua is bowed to in the gospels he is bowed to as a man people believe is the Messiah and King. When Cornelius bows to Peter, Peter stops him (Acts 10.26). When John bows to an angel, the angel stops him (Revelation 19.10). Yeshua does not stop people, and the reason is that bowing to the anointed King of Israel was never forbidden. [Matthew 18.26 ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/mat/18/26/).

## PART B. WHAT THE FIRST BELIEVERS SAID

This is the layer that decides the question historically. Before John, before the councils, what did the people who had known Yeshua say he was, in the earliest material we have?

### B1. Peter's first sermons

**Acts 2.22.** "Jesus of Nazareth, a man approved of God unto you by mighty works and wonders and signs which God did by him in the midst of you." A man. God did the works through him. [ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/act/2/22/).

**Acts 2.36.** "God hath made him both Lord and Christ, this Jesus whom ye crucified." God made him Lord. If he were God by nature, he could not be made Lord by someone else.

**Acts 3.13.** "The God of Abraham, and of Isaac, and of Jacob, the God of our fathers, hath glorified his Servant Jesus." The Greek is *pais*, servant or child, the Septuagint's word for the servant of Isaiah. God of Abraham on one side, his servant on the other.

**Acts 10.38.** "God anointed him with the Holy Spirit and with power, who went about doing good, for God was with him." God was with him. Not God was him.

**Acts 17.31.** Paul in Athens, God "will judge the world in righteousness by the man whom he hath ordained."

**Why this matters.** Luke wrote Acts late, around 85 to 120, and had every opportunity to put high Christology in Peter's mouth. He did not. Scholars of Acts (Martin Dibelius, Henry Cadbury, and more recently C.K. Barrett) treat these speeches as Luke's own compositions, which makes the point stronger, not weaker. Even a late writer composing freely gave the apostles an exaltation Christology, because that is what the tradition he had received contained.

### B2. The oldest creed in the New Testament calls him appointed

**Romans 1.3 to 4.** "Concerning his Son, who was born of the seed of David according to the flesh, who was declared to be the Son of God with power, according to the spirit of holiness, by the resurrection from the dead." [ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/rom/1/3/).

**What it is.** Scholars across the spectrum recognise this as a pre Pauline formula Paul is quoting at the opening of Romans, older than any letter, because its vocabulary ("spirit of holiness," "seed of David") is not Paul's. The Greek verb is *horisthentos*, "appointed, designated." The ASV's "declared" softens it. The creed says Yeshua, a descendant of David, was appointed Son of God by the resurrection. That is exaltation at the resurrection, in the earliest Christian confession we possess. Bart Ehrman lays this out as the centre of *How Jesus Became God* (2014), [openlibrary](https://openlibrary.org/search?q=ehrman+how+jesus+became+god).

**Philippians 2.9.** After the hymn describes his humbling, "Wherefore also God highly exalted him, and gave unto him the name which is above every name." God is the one who exalts and gives. The hymn is treated in full in C5, but notice already that its climax is an act God does to Yeshua.

**Acts 13.33.** Paul at Antioch applies Psalm 2.7, "Thou art my Son, this day have I begotten thee," to the resurrection. The begetting as Son happens on a day.

### B3. The voice at the baptism said "today I have begotten thee"

**The received text.** Luke 3.22, at the baptism, the voice from heaven says "Thou art my beloved Son, in thee I am well pleased." [ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/luk/3/22/).

**The older reading.** Codex Bezae, with the Old Latin manuscripts, reads instead "Thou art my Son, this day have I begotten thee," which is Psalm 2.7 word for word from the Septuagint. This reading is quoted as the text of Luke by Justin Martyr (Dialogue with Trypho 88 and 103, around 155), Clement of Alexandria (Paedagogus 1.6, around 200), Origen (Commentary on John, book 1), Methodius, and the Gospel of the Ebionites as reported by Epiphanius (Panarion 30.13.7). Augustine (De consensu evangelistarum 2.14) reports it as the reading of some manuscripts in his day. In other words, the earliest church fathers who quote the verse, from Rome, Alexandria and Caesarea, all knew it as "today I have begotten thee." [Justin, Dialogue 88 at New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/01286.htm), [Clement, Paedagogus 1.6 at New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/02091.htm).

**Why it matters.** "This day have I begotten thee" is an adoption formula. It is the line spoken over the king at his coronation. If that is what Luke wrote, Luke's gospel presents Yeshua becoming God's Son at his baptism, which is exactly what the Ebionites believed and exactly what the later church called heresy. Ehrman argues in *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* that the Bezae reading is original and the received reading is a scribal correction toward orthodoxy, on the standard principle that the harder reading, the one a scribe would be tempted to change, is more likely to be the original. A scribe had every reason to change "begotten thee today" to "well pleased." No scribe had any reason to change it the other way.

> **How to present this.** This is a strong argument but it is not uncontested, and the other side will know the counter. The oldest papyrus of Luke 3 (P4, third century) reads "well pleased," as do Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, and the majority of scholars still print the received text. The honest claim is this. The reading "today I have begotten thee" is the one every church father who quotes the verse before the year 250 knew, it is the harder reading, and a major textual critic argues it is original. Say that. Do not say "the original Luke said begotten" as a settled fact. The patristic breadth is your strongest card, not the manuscript age.

### B4. James, the brother, wrote a whole letter and never said it

The letter of James is written in the name of the brother of Yeshua, head of the Jerusalem church. It mentions Yeshua twice (1.1, 2.1), as "the Lord Jesus Christ." It contains no incarnation, no pre existence, no atonement by blood, no Trinity, and its theology of God is Jewish monotheism throughout (2.19, "thou believest that God is one, thou doest well"). Whoever wrote it, it is the voice of the Jerusalem tradition, and that voice does not know a divine Yeshua.

### B5. Hebrews says the Son has a God

**Hebrews 1.9.** Quoting Psalm 45.7 of the Son, "Therefore God, thy God, hath anointed thee with the oil of gladness above thy fellows." The Son has a God who anoints him and he has fellows. [ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/heb/1/9/). This matters for C7 below, because the verse immediately before it, Hebrews 1.8, is one of the proof texts for deity, and the verse after it says the one addressed has a God.

**Hebrews 5.7 to 8.** He "offered up prayers and supplications with strong crying and tears unto him that was able to save him from death" and "learned obedience by the things which he suffered."

### B6. The Hebrew believers, from the mouths of their enemies

**Who they were.** The Nazarenes and Ebionites were the Torah keeping Jewish followers of Yeshua, the direct heirs of the Jerusalem church of James. They are treated in full on the Nazarene and Ebionite sheet. Here only their view of who Yeshua was.

**Irenaeus, around 180.** *Against Heresies* 1.26.2. The Ebionites use only the gospel of Matthew, reject Paul as an apostate from the law, and hold the same opinion of Christ as Cerinthus and Carpocrates, whom he has just described as teaching that Jesus was born of Joseph and Mary like other men and that the Christ descended on him at baptism. [New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103126.htm).

**Eusebius, around 324.** *Church History* 3.27. There are two kinds of Ebionites. One holds Christ was "a plain and common man" born of Joseph. The other accepts the virgin birth but "refuses to acknowledge that he pre existed as God, the Word, and Wisdom." Both kinds deny that he was God. [New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm).

**Epiphanius, around 377.** *Panarion* 30.16.4, the Ebionites held that Christ was not begotten of God the Father but created as one of the archangels, ruling over the angels, who came into Adam and the patriarchs and finally clothed himself in the body of Jesus. A high created being, not God. And in Panarion 29.7.6, on the Nazarenes, he admits he does not know whether they held him to be a mere man or taught the virgin birth. [Panarion 29 and 30, Frank Williams PDF](https://yahadblogs.org/vero-yahad/Documents/Panarion-Ebionites.pdf).

**What this adds up to.** Three church fathers across two centuries, every one of them hostile, agree that the Hebrew followers of Yeshua did not hold him to be God. They disagreed among themselves about the virgin birth and about pre existence, which is why this sheet does not claim they held one fixed Christology. What they held in common is the one thing that matters here. He was the Messiah, anointed by God, and he was not God.

> **How to present this.** Say "the Ebionites held he was a man," because the fathers say so plainly. Be careful with the Nazarenes, because Epiphanius himself says he is unsure of their view on his nature. The safe statement is that neither group is recorded as holding him to be God, and the Ebionites are recorded as denying it.

### B6b. Irenaeus and Justin on the virgin birth, in their own words

**Who they are.** Justin Martyr (died around 165) and Irenaeus, bishop of Lyons (writing around 180), are the two earliest church writers who argue for the virgin birth against believers who denied it. Both had every reason to make the deniers look small, late and foolish. What they actually wrote shows that belief in a natural birth was held by followers of Yeshua in their own day, that it rested on the Hebrew text of Isaiah, and that the defenders of the virgin birth had to lean on a Greek translation to answer it. All quotations are from the Ante-Nicene Fathers translation (1885), which is public domain.

**Irenaeus,** ***Against Heresies*** **3.21.1.** [New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103321.htm)

> "God, then, was made man, and the Lord did Himself save us, giving us the token of the Virgin. But not as some allege, among those now presuming to expound the Scripture, [thus:] 'Behold, a young woman shall conceive, and bring forth a son,' as Theodotion the Ephesian has interpreted, and Aquila of Pontus, both Jewish proselytes. The Ebionites, following these, assert that He was begotten by Joseph."

**What it shows.** The whole dispute turns on one Hebrew word in Isaiah 7.14, *almah*. It means a young woman of marriageable age. The Hebrew word for a virgin is *betulah*, and Genesis 24.16 uses both words of Rebekah side by side, which shows they are not the same word. The Septuagint, the Greek translation made before Yeshua, rendered *almah* as *parthenos*, "virgin." Two second century translators who worked directly from the Hebrew, Theodotion and Aquila, rendered it *neanis*, "young woman." Irenaeus admits that the Ebionites followed the translation that matched the Hebrew, and on that basis held that Joseph was Yeshua's father. Then read what he does next. In 3.21.2 his answer is not the Hebrew. It is the legend that seventy elders translated the Septuagint separately and produced word for word identical Greek, proving it inspired. The defence of the virgin birth, at its first full statement, is a defence of a Greek translation against the Hebrew original. [Isaiah 7.14 at Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Isaiah.7.14), [Genesis 24.16 at Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.24.16).

**Irenaeus,** ***Against Heresies*** **5.1.3.** [New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103501.htm)

> "Vain also are the Ebionites, who do not receive by faith into their soul the union of God and man, but who remain in the old leaven of [the natural] birth, and who do not choose to understand that the Holy Ghost came upon Mary... Therefore do these men reject the commixture of the heavenly wine, and wish it to be water of the world only."

**What it shows.** Two things in one passage. First, the Ebionites rejected "the union of God and man," that is, the incarnation, and held a natural birth. Second, they celebrated the Lord's meal with water only, no wine, which matches the Nazirite abstinence of James the Just (see the James the Just sheet and the Abstained From Flesh sheet). The denial of the virgin birth and the denial of the incarnation travel together, and they travel with the Torah keeping, wine refusing Hebrew believers.

**Old and new, in his own words.** This point is made by Charles Vaclavik in *The Origin of Christianity*, citing this passage from the Ante-Nicene Fathers, Volume 1, page 527. Irenaeus calls the Ebionite belief in a natural birth "the old leaven," and in the same passage he calls the virgin birth "a new [kind of] generation" that God "showed forth." By his own labels, the natural birth is the old teaching the Hebrew believers remained in, and the virgin birth is the new one. The words are his. Those who object will say "old leaven" is Paul's phrase from 1 Corinthians 5.7 and refers to the old Adamic nature, but that only moves the question, because Irenaeus is the one who places the natural birth on the side of the old and the virgin birth on the side of the new.

**Justin Martyr,** ***Dialogue with Trypho*** **48 to 49, around 155.** [New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/01284.htm)

The *Dialogue* is a debate, written up by Justin, between himself and a Jewish teacher named Trypho. It is twenty five years older than Irenaeus and is the earliest place the question is argued at all. Trypho objects that a pre existent God born of a virgin is "not merely paradoxical, but also foolish." Justin answers:

> "Now assuredly, Trypho, [the proof] that this man is the Christ of God does not fail, though I be unable to prove that He existed formerly as Son of the Maker of all things, being God, and was born a man by the Virgin... For there are some, my friends, of our race, who admit that He is Christ, while holding Him to be man of men; with whom I do not agree."

Trypho replies:

> "Those who affirm him to have been a man, and to have been anointed by election, and then to have become Christ, appear to me to speak more plausibly than you who hold those opinions which you express. For we all expect that Christ will be a man [born] of men."

**What it shows.** Three things. First, the most educated Christian writer of the second century concedes that the case for Yeshua as Messiah stands even if pre existence and the virgin birth cannot be proved. He treats them as separable, secondary claims. Second, in his own day there were believers who confessed Yeshua as the Christ and held him to be "man of men," and Justin disagrees with them without calling them heretics. Third, Trypho states the Jewish expectation plainly. The Messiah was to be a man born of men and anointed by God, which is exactly what Peter preached in Acts 2.22 and 10.38 (B1). The natural birth view was not an invention of a later sect. It was the ordinary Hebrew expectation of the Messiah, and it was still held inside the movement in the 150s.

> **A note on "our race."** The manuscript of the *Dialogue* reads "our race." Justin was born in Samaria to a Gentile family, so some editors have suggested emending it to "your race," which would make those believers Hebrews. Either way the point holds. Believers who held Yeshua to be the Christ and a man born of men existed in 155, and the leading defender of the virgin birth admitted it was a claim he might not be able to prove.

**What this adds up to.** The two earliest defenders of the virgin birth both record believers who denied it. Justin, the earlier, concedes the doctrine is separable from the Messiahship and may not be provable. Irenaeus, the later, admits the deniers had the Hebrew of Isaiah on their side and answers with a legend about a Greek translation. And the people he names, the Ebionites, are the same Torah keeping, Matthew only, Paul rejecting Hebrew believers recorded in B6.

### B7. The first century outside the New Testament

**1 Clement, around 96.** A letter from Rome to Corinth, the only Christian writing outside the New Testament that is securely first century. It calls Yeshua Lord, Christ, high priest, "the sceptre of the majesty of God." It never calls him God. [New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1010.htm).

**The Didache, late first century.** A church manual. Yeshua is "thy servant" (*pais*) in its prayers, 9.2 and 10.2. Not God. [Early Christian Writings](http://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/didache-roberts.html).

**Ignatius of Antioch, around 107 to 110.** The first writer to call Yeshua "our God" without qualification, repeatedly (Ephesians, opening and 18.2; Romans 3.3; Smyrnaeans 1.1). Ignatius was bishop of Antioch, which was Paul's base church, and he wrote in the second century. That is where the plain statement first appears, in the Pauline Gentile church, eighty years after the crucifixion. [Ignatius to the Ephesians at New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0104.htm).

**Pliny the Younger, around 112.** A Roman governor reports to the emperor Trajan that Christians in Bithynia sing to Christ "as to a god" (*quasi deo*). Earliest outside testimony to Christ devotion, and it concerns Gentile converts in Pauline territory. [Pliny, Letters 10.96](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/pliny.html).

## PART C. THE PROOF TEXTS, ONE AT A TIME

What follows is every verse the other side will bring, in the order they usually bring them. For each, the verse, what it actually says, the standard harmonization, and the answer. Keep in mind the count at the end. A Catholic scholar did it for us.

### C1. John 1.1, "the Word was God"

**The text.** "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." [ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/jhn/1/1/).

**Three things to know.** First, the Greek. In the second clause "God" has the definite article, *ton theon*, the God. In the third clause it does not, *theos*. John distinguished them on purpose. A word with the article names a specific being. A word without it describes a quality. The most literal rendering is "the Word was with the God, and the Word was divine," or "what God was, the Word was," which is how the New English Bible put it. The Word is not said to be *the* God it was with, because that would make the sentence contradict itself. Second, what "the Word" meant to a Jewish reader. The *Logos* was a known idea. Philo of Alexandria, a Jewish philosopher who died around the year 50, wrote constantly of God's Logos as God's agent in creation and even called it "the second God" (Questions on Genesis 2.62) without for a moment thinking there were two Gods. Proverbs 8.22 to 31 has Wisdom personified, present with God at creation, and Wisdom says "the LORD created me." The prologue of John is written in that register. God's word and wisdom are God's own, divine, and distinct from God's person. Third, the prologue is a hymn, with a different vocabulary and rhythm from the rest of the gospel, and most scholars think it was composed separately and placed at the front. The Logos never appears again after 1.14. The gospel's own stated purpose at 20.31 is "that ye may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God." Not that he is God.

**John 1.18, the verse that shows the scribes at work.** The earliest manuscripts (P66, P75, Sinaiticus, Vaticanus) read "the only begotten God." The great majority of later manuscripts read "the only begotten Son." Either a scribe raised Son to God or lowered God to Son. Ehrman argues the former. Either way, the manuscripts of the very chapter the other side leans on show the text moving on exactly this question. [Metzger on John 1.18, summary at bible-researcher.com](https://www.bible-researcher.com/john1.18.html).

### C2. John 8.58, "before Abraham was, I am"

**The text.** "Before Abraham was born, I am." The crowd picks up stones. [ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/jhn/8/58/).

**The claim.** That *egō eimi*, "I am," is the divine name from Exodus 3.14, so Yeshua is claiming to be YHWH.

**The answer.** In the Septuagint of Exodus 3.14 God says *egō eimi ho ōn*, and the name he tells Moses to give is *ho ōn*, "the One Who Is," not *egō eimi*. *Egō eimi* is simply the Greek for "I am" or "it is I," and it is said by all sorts of people. In John 9.9, eleven verses after the supposed divine claim, the blind man who was healed says *egō eimi*, "I am he," and nobody stones him. In John 4.26 Yeshua says *egō eimi* to the Samaritan woman to mean "I am the Messiah you mentioned." What provokes the stoning in chapter 8 is the claim to have existed before Abraham. Pre existence is not the same as being God. The Ebionites, as Epiphanius reports, believed Christ pre existed as a created archangel. Angels pre exist Abraham. And in John's own frame, the Logos through which all things were made (1.3) pre existed, and the gospel places that Logos in Yeshua. None of that makes him the God he prays to in chapter 17.

### C3. John 10.30, "I and the Father are one"

Treated in A7. John 17.21 to 22 defines "one" as the unity the disciples are to share. The crowd's accusation in 10.33 is answered in 10.34 to 36 with a verse about human judges being called gods.

### C4. John 20.28, Thomas, "my Lord and my God"

**The text.** Thomas, shown the wounds, says "My Lord and my God." [ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/jhn/20/28/).

**The honest assessment.** This is the strongest verse the other side has and this sheet says so. Thomas addresses Yeshua and the words are "my God." The answers in order of strength. First, eleven verses earlier, 20.17, the risen Yeshua says "my God and your God," so whatever Thomas means, John has just told the reader that Yeshua himself has a God. Second, three verses later, 20.31, John states the purpose of his whole book, that you may believe Yeshua is "the Christ, the Son of God." If 20.28 meant Yeshua is God, the author's summary of his own point would say God, and it says Son of God. Third, in John's own theology Yeshua is the one in whom the Father is seen, 14.9, "he that hath seen me hath seen the Father," and the one to whom the Father has given everything, so to see God in him is John's teaching without making him the Father. Fourth, the Jewish frame. Psalm 82.6 calls men gods, Exodus 7.1 says God made Moses "as God to Pharaoh," and judges in Exodus 21.6 and 22.8 are called *elohim*. Thomas, a Jew, had vocabulary for a man who stood in God's place. Fifth, and weakest, some have read it as an exclamation of astonishment rather than an address. Do not lead with that one.

### C5. Philippians 2.6 to 11, "in the form of God"

**The text.** "Who, existing in the form of God, counted not the being on an equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant, being made in the likeness of men, and being found in fashion as a man, he humbled himself, becoming obedient even unto death, yea, the death of the cross. Wherefore also God highly exalted him, and gave unto him the name which is above every name, that in the name of Jesus every knee should bow, and that every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father." [ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/phl/2/6/).

**What to know.** This is a pre Pauline hymn Paul is quoting, and it is genuine Paul, so it cannot be dismissed. It is the other side's best Pauline text. Three answers. First, "form of God" is *morphē theou*, and the same word is used three lines later for "form of a servant." He was not literally a servant by nature, he took on the role. The word is about appearance and status, not substance. Second, James Dunn, in *Christology in the Making* (1980), argued that the hymn is an Adam Christology. Adam was made in the image of God and grasped at equality with God (Genesis 3.5, "ye shall be as God"). Yeshua, the second Adam, had the image and did not grasp. On that reading the hymn is about a man who reversed Adam's choice, and the whole "he was God and came down" picture is read into it. [Dunn at openlibrary](https://openlibrary.org/search?q=dunn+christology+in+the+making). Third, and decisive on any reading, the climax. God exalted him. God gave him the name. Every tongue confesses him Lord "to the glory of God the Father." The one who exalts is God. The one exalted is Yeshua. The confession ends with glory going to the Father, not to Yeshua. The hymn's last line is subordination.

**Isaiah 45.23.** The other side points out that "every knee shall bow" is lifted from Isaiah 45.23, where it is YHWH to whom every knee bows, so applying it to Yeshua makes him YHWH. But Paul applies it to Yeshua as the one to whom God has given the name, in verse 9, and ends with the glory going to the Father. If the knees bowing made Yeshua YHWH, the glory would go to him. It goes to the Father. The verse is about God delegating his honour to his anointed, which is Psalm 110 again.

### C6. Romans 9.5, "who is over all, God blessed for ever"

**The text.** "Of whom is Christ as concerning the flesh, who is over all, God blessed for ever. Amen." That is one way to punctuate it. The other is "of whom is Christ as concerning the flesh. He who is God over all be blessed for ever. Amen." [ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/rom/9/5/).

**The answer.** The Greek manuscripts have no punctuation. Whether the verse calls Christ God or ends with a doxology to God depends entirely on where a modern editor places a full stop. The ASV prints the first reading in the text and the second in the margin. The RSV of 1952 printed the second. Paul nowhere else calls Christ God, and he closes doxologies with "blessed for ever" addressed to God elsewhere (Romans 1.25, 2 Corinthians 11.31). A doctrine cannot rest on a comma.

### C7. Hebrews 1.8, "thy throne, O God, is for ever"

**The text.** "But of the Son he saith, Thy throne, O God, is for ever and ever." [ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/heb/1/8/).

**The answer.** Three parts. First, this is a quotation of Psalm 45.6, a wedding song for a human king of Israel, which addresses the king and then says in the next verse "therefore God, thy God, hath anointed thee." The psalm calls the king *elohim* and in the same breath says the king has a God. Hebrews quotes both verses, 1.8 and 1.9. So Hebrews itself says the Son has a God who anointed him. Second, the Hebrew of Psalm 45.6 can be read "thy throne is God for ever," meaning God is the foundation of the throne, and the RSV printed "Your divine throne endures for ever." The Greek of Hebrews 1.8 can be read the same way, "God is thy throne." Third, Hebrews is anonymous. Origen in the third century said "who wrote the epistle, in truth God knows" (quoted by Eusebius, Church History 6.25). It is not by Paul, not by an apostle, and its author is unknown. [Psalm 45.6 to 7 at Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Psalms.45.7).

### C8. Colossians 1.15 to 20 and 2.9, "the fulness of the Godhead bodily"

**The text.** 1.15, "who is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation." 2.9, "in him dwelleth all the fulness of the Godhead bodily." [ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/col/1/15/).

**The answer.** First, "image of the invisible God" means he is not the invisible God, he is the image of him, as Adam was (Genesis 1.27). "Firstborn of all creation" places him inside creation as its first member, which is the Ebionite created archangel idea and the Wisdom of Proverbs 8.22. The Arians at Nicaea quoted this verse for exactly that reason. Second, "the fulness dwelleth in him" is said in 1.19 to be the Father's pleasure, something put into him, and Ephesians 3.19 prays that ordinary believers "may be filled unto all the fulness of God," same word *plērōma*. Third, Colossians is a disputed letter. Its vocabulary, sentence structure and theology differ from the undisputed seven, and a majority of critical scholars hold it was written in Paul's name by a follower, some time between 60 and 85. The Christ hymn in 1.15 to 20 is likely older than the letter and is quoted in it.

### C9. Titus 2.13 and 2 Peter 1.1, "our great God and Saviour Jesus Christ"

**The texts.** Titus 2.13, "the glory of our great God and Saviour Jesus Christ." 2 Peter 1.1, "the righteousness of our God and Saviour Jesus Christ." [Titus 2.13 ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/tit/2/13/).

**The answer.** The reading that makes Yeshua God depends on a Greek grammatical rule (the Granville Sharp rule, named for an eighteenth century English abolitionist who formulated it) about two nouns joined by "and" sharing one article. The rule is real but contested in its application to proper names, and the ASV margin gives the alternative, "the great God and our Saviour Jesus Christ," two persons. More important, these are the two books of the New Testament whose authorship is most widely rejected. Titus is one of the Pastorals, dated 100 to 130 by most scholars on vocabulary, church structure and the absence from Marcion's canon and from P46. 2 Peter is the latest book in the canon, dated 120 to 150, doubted even in antiquity (Origen, Eusebius and Jerome all record doubts, and Calvin and Erasmus repeated them). These verses tell you what Christians were saying in the second century. They are not apostolic testimony.

### C10. 1 Timothy 3.16, "God was manifested in the flesh"

**The text.** The KJV reads "God was manifest in the flesh." The ASV reads "He who was manifested in the flesh." [ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/1ti/3/16/).

**What happened.** In the earliest manuscripts the word is ΟΣ, *hos*, "who." The nomen sacrum for God is ΘΣ, *theos* abbreviated. The difference is two small strokes, a bar inside the O and a line above. A scribe added them. In Codex Sinaiticus you can see the original hand's ΟΣ and the later corrector's marks turning it into ΘΣ. Metzger's *Textual Commentary* calls the original reading certain. Ehrman uses it as the lead example in *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* of a scribe changing the text to make it say Jesus is God. And the letter is pseudonymous regardless. [Codex Sinaiticus, navigate to 1 Timothy 3](https://www.codexsinaiticus.org/en/manuscript.aspx), [summary at bible-researcher.com](https://www.bible-researcher.com/1tim3.16.html).

### C11. 1 John 5.7, the three heavenly witnesses

**The text as the KJV has it.** "For there are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost, and these three are one."

**What happened.** This sentence, the only explicit statement of the Trinity in the Bible, is in no Greek manuscript before the fourteenth century. It arose as a note in Latin manuscripts in North Africa or Spain, entered the Latin text, and was put into the printed Greek New Testament by Erasmus in his third edition of 1522 under pressure, after he had left it out of the first two. From Erasmus it passed into the Textus Receptus and the King James Version. Not one Greek church father cites it during the fourth century Trinitarian controversies, which they would have done daily if it had been in their Bibles. The Revised Version of 1881 removed it and no critical edition contains it. Ehrman calls it the most obvious instance of a theologically motivated corruption in the New Testament. [bible-researcher.com on the Comma](https://www.bible-researcher.com/comma.html).

### C12. Matthew 28.19, baptising in the name of the Father, Son and Holy Spirit

**The text.** "Baptizing them into the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit." [ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/mat/28/19/).

**Two answers.** First, even as it stands, naming three is not saying three are one God. Second, there is a real question whether the formula is original. Eusebius of Caesarea, writing before Nicaea, quotes the verse many times (Demonstratio Evangelica 3.6, 3.7, Church History 3.5.2) as "Go and make disciples of all nations in my name," with no triadic formula. After Nicaea he quotes it with the formula. And Acts, which reports the actual baptisms, has every one "in the name of Jesus" (2.38, 8.16, 10.48, 19.5), never in the triple name.

> **How to present this.** Every surviving Greek manuscript of Matthew contains the triadic formula. The argument is from Eusebius's quotations alone, first made by F.C. Conybeare in 1901, and most scholars think Eusebius was paraphrasing. Say that Eusebius quoted it without the formula before Nicaea and Acts baptises only in Yeshua's name. Do not say "the manuscripts lack it," because they do not.

### C13. Matthew 1.23, "Immanuel, God with us"

**The claim.** The child is named "God with us," so he is God. [ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/mat/1/23/).

**The answer.** Hebrew names describe God, they do not identify the bearer with God. Elijah means "my God is YHWH." Ezekiel means "God strengthens." Ishmael means "God hears." Nobody thought those men were God. In Isaiah's own book the name Immanuel is used in 8.8 and 8.10 to mean that God is with Judah against her enemies, and the sign in 7.14 is given to King Ahaz about a child who will be born in his own time, before the two kings he fears are gone (7.16). Matthew applies it typologically. And Yeshua was never actually called Immanuel. His name was Yeshua. [Isaiah 7.14 to 16 at Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Isaiah.7.14).

### C14. Isaiah 9.6, "Mighty God, Everlasting Father"

**The text.** "His name shall be called Wonderful, Counsellor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace." [Isaiah 9.6 at Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Isaiah.9.6).

**The answer.** First, the same principle as Immanuel. It is a throne name describing what God does through the king, and Hebrew royal names are sentences about God. If it identified the child with God he would also be "Everlasting Father," and the Trinity says the Son is not the Father. The other side cannot take "Mighty God" literally and "Everlasting Father" figuratively in the same name. Second, the Hebrew can be read as a sentence, "the Mighty God, the Everlasting Father, the Prince of Peace is planning a wonder," which is how Jewish commentators read it. Third, the Septuagint, the Bible of the early church, translated the name as "Messenger of great counsel," *angelos megalēs boulēs*, which shows that the Greek speaking Jews who made it did not read "Mighty God" as naming the child God. Fourth, in context (9.1 to 7, and the Talmud, Sanhedrin 94a) the child is Hezekiah, born in Isaiah's time, and the Syrian and Assyrian crisis is the setting.

### C15. Isaiah 48.12 to 16, "the Lord GOD hath sent me, and his Spirit"

**The claim.** Verse 16, "and now the Lord GOD hath sent me, and his Spirit," is said by the one who in verse 12 called himself "the first and the last," so a divine speaker is sent by God and the Spirit, three persons. [Isaiah 48.16 at Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Isaiah.48.16).

**The answer.** The Hebrew marks a change of speaker. Verse 16 opens with *ve'attah*, "and now," which throughout the prophets signals a shift from God's words to the prophet's own voice. Verses 12 to 15 are God speaking. At "and now" the prophet speaks, saying God and his Spirit have sent him. This is how Ibn Ezra and Rashi read it in the medieval commentaries and how the JPS translation and the NIV footnote read it today. A prophet saying "God sent me, with his Spirit" is the most ordinary sentence in the prophetic books.

### C16. Genesis 1.26, "let us make man"

**The claim.** The plural shows a plurality in God. [Genesis 1.26 at Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.1.26).

**The answer.** The very next verse, 1.27, switches to the singular, "and God created man in his own image," *he* created. The plural in 1.26 is God addressing his heavenly court, the divine council that appears throughout the Hebrew Bible, 1 Kings 22.19 ("all the host of heaven standing by him"), Job 1.6, Psalm 82.1 ("God standeth in the congregation of God, he judgeth among the gods"), Isaiah 6.8 ("whom shall I send, and who will go for us?"). This is the standard reading in critical scholarship and in Jewish commentary and it needs no second person of a Trinity. No Jewish reader in 2,000 years read it as three.

### C17. Revelation, Alpha and Omega

**The claim.** God is "the Alpha and the Omega" in Revelation 1.8 and Yeshua is "the Alpha and the Omega" in 22.13, so Yeshua is God. [Revelation 1.8 ASV](https://www.blueletterbible.org/asv/rev/1/8/).

**The answer.** Revelation is the book that most clearly keeps God and Yeshua apart. 1.1, "the Revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave unto him." 3.12, the risen Yeshua says "my God" four times in one verse. 3.14, he is "the beginning of the creation of God." 1.6, he has made us "a kingdom, priests unto his God and Father." Titles are shared because God delegates them, which is the whole pattern of the book, where the Lamb receives the scroll from the hand of the one on the throne (5.7). The one who gives and the one who receives are two.

### C18. The count

Raymond Brown was a Catholic priest and the leading American New Testament scholar of his generation, author of the standard commentary on John. In a 1965 article, "Does the New Testament Call Jesus God?" (Theological Studies 26), later included in his *Introduction to New Testament Christology* (1994), he went through every verse the other side uses and sorted them. His conclusion was that only three passages clearly call Jesus God, John 1.1, John 20.28 and Hebrews 1.8, a few more probably do (Titus 2.13, 2 Peter 1.1, 1 John 5.20, Romans 9.5 if punctuated one way), and the rest do not. He also noted that the clear ones are all late and all in works associated with the Johannine or Pauline circles, and that the Synoptic gospels, Acts, and the early speeches never do it. That is a Catholic scholar counting for the other side and arriving at three verses, every one of which is answered above. [Brown, Introduction to New Testament Christology at openlibrary](https://openlibrary.org/search?q=raymond+brown+introduction+to+new+testament+christology).

## PART C2, THE REMAINING PROOF TEXTS, ONE AT A TIME

Part C handled the big names: John 1.1, John 8.58, John 20.28, Philippians 2, Hebrews 1.8, Titus 2.13, and the rest. The passages below are the second wave, the ones that come out once the first wave has been answered. Some are stronger than they look and some are much weaker than they look. Each entry says which.

Standing definitions, so you do not have to hunt. The **Masoretic Text** (MT) is the traditional Hebrew Bible as preserved by medieval Hebrew scribes, the Masoretes, who added the vowel points. The **Septuagint** (LXX) is the Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible made before Yeshua's time; New Testament writers usually quote it rather than the Hebrew. **Nestle-Aland** (NA27, NA28) is the standard scholarly Greek New Testament; the number is the edition. A **textual variant** is a place where the manuscripts disagree. **Textual criticism** is the discipline of deciding which reading is earliest. The **Shema** is Deuteronomy 6.4, "Yahweh our God is one Yahweh." **Agency** (Hebrew *shaliach*) is the legal principle that a sent representative acts with the sender's authority, so what the agent does counts as done by the sender. The ASV (1901) is this sheet's standing translation; where it prints "Jehovah" for the divine name YHWH, I write Yahweh.

### C19. John 1.14, "the Word became flesh"

**The text.** "And the Word became flesh, and dwelt among us (and we beheld his glory, glory as of the only begotten from the Father)." [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/john/1.htm)

**The claim.** John 1.1 said the Word was God; now the Word became flesh; so God became a man. Raymond Brown, the Catholic author of the Anchor Bible commentary on John (1966), treats this as the hinge of the Prologue, and Larry Hurtado, *Lord Jesus Christ: Devotion to Jesus in Earliest Christianity* (Eerdmans, 2003), reads it as John's highest christological statement [review by David Meconi, First Things, December 2004](https://firstthings.com/?p=13887).

**The answer.** First, the verse does not say God became flesh. It says the *Logos* (λόγος, "word," "plan," "reason") became flesh [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/john/1-14.htm). What the Logos is was settled in Part C: God's own word and wisdom personified, as in Proverbs 8 and Psalm 33.6, "by the word of Yahweh were the heavens made." James D. G. Dunn, *Christology in the Making* (1980; 2nd ed. SCM 1989), argued that the Prologue is a Wisdom hymn and that only at 1.14 does it speak of a person: God's self-expression became embodied in a man. That is incarnation of the Word, not incarnation of a second divine person.

Second, the verb *eskēnōsen* (ἐσκήνωσεν) means "pitched a tent," the verb behind *skēnē*, tabernacle [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/john/1-14.htm). The Hebrew background is God's presence dwelling in the wilderness tent (Exodus 25.8, 40.34) and Wisdom told to "make thy dwelling in Jacob" (Sirach 24.8, in the Greek Apocrypha). God's word has done in a man what it did in the tent.

Third, the glory is "as of the only begotten *from* the Father," *para Patros* (παρὰ Πατρός). The one made flesh is distinguished from the Father in the same verse, and his glory is derived. The same Gospel has him say the Father is "greater than I" (14.28), "the only true God" (17.3), and, risen, "my God" (20.17).

**The standard harmonization.** The Logos is personal in 1.1 ("with God"); "became flesh" implies a prior personal existence; and the Father-Son distinction is exactly what the Trinity teaches, distinction of person not of nature.

**Why this matters.** John 1.14 proves the incarnation of a pre-existent divine *person* only if John 1.1 already did. The weight rests on the earlier verse.

> **How to present this.** Do not say "it is just God's plan becoming a man" as if obvious. Say the verse speaks of the Word, that the Word in the Hebrew scriptures is God's own speech and wisdom, and that John distinguishes the one made flesh from the Father in the same sentence. Then send people to the John 1.1 entry.

### C20. The absolute "I am" sayings: John 8.24, 8.28, 13.19, 18.5–6

**The text.** 8.24, "except ye believe that I am he, ye shall die in your sins." 8.28, "then shall ye know that I am he." 13.19, "that ye may believe that I am he." 18.5–6, "I am he... they went backward, and fell to the ground." [ASV John 8](https://biblehub.com/asv/john/8.htm), [John 13](https://biblehub.com/asv/john/13.htm), [John 18](https://biblehub.com/asv/john/18.htm)

**The claim.** Each is *egō eimi* (ἐγώ εἰμι, "I am") with no predicate; the ASV italicizes "he." This is said to echo Yahweh's *ani hu* ("I am he") in Isaiah 41.4, 43.10, 43.25, 46.4 and the name in Exodus 3.14, and the soldiers' fall in 18.6 is read as the reaction to hearing the divine name. Raymond Brown's John commentary takes the absolute uses as Isaiah echoes; James White, *The Forgotten Trinity* (1998), gives the set a chapter.

**The answer.** First, John uses the same words of a man who is plainly not God. The blind man in 9.9 says *egō eimi* [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/john/9-9.htm), and the ASV translates "I am he" there too. In 4.26 Yeshua answers the woman's mention of the Messiah with *egō eimi* [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/john/4-26.htm). In 18.5 the soldiers have just named "Jesus of Nazareth" and he says "I am he," the ordinary answer to a question about identity.

Second, the Isaiah echo cuts the other way. Isaiah 43.10 opens, "Ye are my witnesses, saith Yahweh, and my servant whom I have chosen" [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/isaiah/43.htm). The chapter is about Yahweh's *servant* being the proof that Yahweh is God. Yeshua applying "that ye may believe that I am he" to himself is a claim to be that servant, which is what 8.28 says in the same breath: "I do nothing of myself, but as the Father taught me."

Third, the falling soldiers. John's Yeshua is in command of his own arrest (10.18, 18.4, 18.8, 19.11); the fall shows no one could take him until he gave himself up. Men fall before an angel in Daniel 10.9 and before the glorified Messiah in Revelation 1.17 (C32) without either being Yahweh. And if the words themselves felled soldiers, the beggar in 9.9 would have cleared a street.

Fourth, the author's frame: 17.3, "the only true God," of the Father; 20.17, "my God."

**The standard harmonization.** The predicate-less uses are a distinct category from 9.9 and 4.26; 8.58 anchors the set; John layers a divine echo under an ordinary meaning for readers who know Isaiah.

**Why this matters.** This argument is everywhere and depends on nobody checking John 9.9.

> **How to present this.** Do not say "egō eimi never means anything special." Say John uses it for the beggar and the Messiah alike, that the Isaiah passages are about Yahweh's servant, and that in 8.28 itself Yeshua says he does nothing of himself.

### C21. John 14.9, "he that hath seen me hath seen the Father"

**The text.** "He that hath seen me hath seen the Father; how sayest thou, Show us the Father?" [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/john/14.htm)

**The claim.** Only God can be the visible presence of God; so Yeshua shares the Father's nature.

**The answer.** First, read the next verse. 14.10: "the words that I say unto you I speak not from myself: but the Father abiding in me doeth his works." 14.24: "the word which ye hear is not mine, but the Father's who sent me." Yeshua explains the seeing himself: Philip has seen the Father because the Father's words and works are what Yeshua has been doing. That is agency. The Mishnah states the rule: "a man's agent is equivalent to himself" (Berakhot 5.5) [Soncino text](https://halakhah.com/berakoth/berakoth_34.html). An ambassador speaking the king's words is the king's presence without being the king.

Second, the same chapter: "the Father is greater than I" (14.28). A being greater than you is not you.

Third, John's own theology of seeing God. 1.18: "No man hath seen God at any time; the only begotten Son... hath declared him," verb *exēgēsato* (ἐξηγήσατο), "explained" [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/john/1-18.htm). 12.45: "he that beholdeth me beholdeth him that sent me." Seeing the sent one is seeing the sender.

Fourth, the Hebrew Bible already has this. Moses is "as God to Pharaoh" (Exodus 7.1). The angel of Yahweh is addressed as Yahweh (Genesis 16.13, Exodus 3.2–6, Judges 13.21–22) without being Yahweh.

**The standard harmonization.** Agency is true but insufficient; a mere agent does not say that seeing him is seeing the Father. Read with 10.30, 1.1, Colossians 1.15, and Hebrews 1.3 (C28), the Son is the perfect image because he shares the nature.

**Why this matters.** 14.9 is strong only without 14.10 and 14.28. Teach the paragraph, not the line.

### C22. John 17.5, "the glory which I had with thee before the world was"

**The text.** "Glorify thou me with thine own self with the glory which I had with thee before the world was." [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/john/17.htm)

**The claim.** Yeshua claims to have existed with glory before creation. Pre-existence plus glory equals deity.

**The answer.** First, pre-existence is not deity. Angels pre-exist the world (Job 38.7); so does Wisdom (Proverbs 8.22–31). Even granted, 17.5 would prove a pre-existent Son, not an uncreated one.

Second, the Greek: *doxē hē eichon para soi* (δόξῃ ᾗ εἶχον παρὰ σοί), "glory which I was having with you" [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/john/17-5.htm). In the same prayer, 17.22 uses the same noun: "the glory which thou hast given me." Given glory is derived glory. 17.24: "thou lovedst me before the foundation of the world." 17.3: "thee the only true God." The one asking for glory calls the one he asks the only true God.

Third, the Hebrew category of things that exist "before the world" in God's purpose. The Talmud lists the Messiah's name among seven things created before the world, with the Torah, repentance, Eden, Gehenna, the Throne of Glory, and the Temple (Pesachim 54a; Nedarim 39b) [Soncino text](https://halakhah.com/nedarim/nedarim_39.html). 1 Peter 1.20: the Messiah "foreknown before the foundation of the world." 2 Timothy 1.9: grace "given us in Christ Jesus before times eternal," though that grace did not personally pre-exist. What is fixed in God's plan "exists" with God before it appears. Dunn, *Christology in the Making*, is the full-length argument that John's pre-existence language grows out of Wisdom and purpose language.

Fourth, 17.5 is a request to be glorified *now*. He does not yet possess it in full. That is how a sent one speaks who expects reward (Hebrews 12.2), not how an unchanging divine person speaks.

**The standard harmonization.** "Had" is past tense; *para soi* means personal presence, not an idea in a mind; 1.1–3 and 8.58 confirm literal pre-existence; the Son eternally receives from the Father, so given glory is no problem.

**Why this matters.** A genuinely strong text for a pre-existent Son and a weak one for the Son being Yahweh. Keep the two claims apart.

> **How to present this.** Do not deny pre-existence flat and move on; hearers know this verse and will feel dodged. Say that even granting it, the same prayer says "the only true God" of the Father and "glory thou hast given me" of the Son. Offer ideal pre-existence as the Hebrew alternative, not as a certainty.

### C23. Mark 14.62, "I am" before Caiaphas, and the Son of Man at the right hand

**The text.** "And Jesus said, I am: and ye shall see the Son of man sitting at the right hand of Power, and coming with the clouds of heaven." [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/mark/14.htm)

**The claim.** *Egō eimi* [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/mark/14-62.htm) as the divine name, plus Daniel 7.13 and Psalm 110.1 applied to himself, plus a blasphemy verdict that only makes sense if he claimed to be God. Darrell Bock, *Blasphemy and Exaltation in Judaism* (1998), argues the last point.

**The answer.** First, the question was "Art thou the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?" (14.61). "I am" answers yes. Matthew has "Thou hast said" (26.64) and Luke "Ye say that I am" (22.70). Neither heard a divine name.

Second, Daniel 7.13: "one like unto a son of man... came even to the ancient of days... and there was *given* him dominion" [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/daniel/7.htm). The figure is brought to God and receives a kingdom; in 7.27 the same dominion is given to "the saints of the Most High." J. R. Daniel Kirk, *A Man Attested by God: The Human Jesus of the Synoptic Gospels* (Eerdmans, 2016), argues that the Synoptics present Yeshua as an "idealized human figure," a category Scripture already uses for Adam, Moses, David, and Daniel's son of man [overview by David Capes](https://davidbcapes.com/2016/09/09/a-man-attested-by-god/).

Third, Psalm 110.1: "Yahweh saith unto my lord, Sit thou at my right hand." The seated one is *adoni* (אדני), "my lord," the word for a human master, not *Adonai* [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/psalms/110-1.htm). Two figures; Yeshua claims to be the second. Every New Testament use distinguishes the one seated from the God who seats him (Acts 2.34–36, Hebrews 1.13, 1 Corinthians 15.25–28).

Fourth, blasphemy. Strictly, the blasphemer "is not liable unless he utters the Name" (Mishnah Sanhedrin 7.5), which did not happen; "Power" is a reverent substitute. Loosely, as in Mark 2.7 and John 10.33 (Part C), it was an intolerable claim to God's prerogatives, and a condemned Galilean saying he would sit beside God and judge his judges qualifies without being a claim to be Yahweh.

Fifth, Mark's frame: 10.18, "none is good save one, even God"; 13.32, the Son does not know the hour; 15.34, "My God, my God."

**The standard harmonization.** Cloud-riding is God's act (Psalm 104.3); sitting on God's throne is sharing it (Revelation 3.21); the Sanhedrin heard a claim to divine status; Bock argues a human seated beside God in heaven was itself the blasphemy.

**Why this matters.** The strongest Synoptic text for exaltation and the weakest for identity, because both scriptures he quotes have two figures and he is the second.

### C24. Luke 1.43, "the mother of my Lord"

**The text.** "And whence is this to me, that the mother of my Lord should come unto me?" [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/luke/1.htm)

**The claim.** Spirit-filled Elizabeth (1.41) calls the unborn child "my Lord," *tou Kyriou mou* (τοῦ Κυρίου μου) [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/luke/1-43.htm), and "Lord" earlier in the chapter means God (1.6, 1.9, 1.11). Catholic apologists make this the root of "Mother of God."

**The answer.** First, *kyrios* is the ordinary word for lord, master, sir; Luke uses it of a landowner (16.3), a master (12.42–47), and as polite address (13.8). In Psalm 110.1 David calls the Messiah "my lord," *adoni*, Greek *tō kyriō mou*. Elizabeth's "my Lord" is Psalm 110.1 language, and Yeshua cites that psalm for exactly this point (Luke 20.41–44).

Second, the same chapter. 1.32: the child "shall be called the Son of the Most High, and the Lord God shall give unto him the throne." Giver and receiver are two. 1.47: Mary calls God "my Saviour."

Third, Luke's wider usage. Acts 2.36, by the same author: God "hath made him both Lord and Christ." Lordship is conferred.

**The standard harmonization.** Luke uses *kyrios* of God and Yeshua interchangeably on purpose (1.76); C. Kavin Rowe, *Early Narrative Christology* (2006), argues the blurring is deliberate.

**Why this matters.** A weak text that sounds strong. Two minutes on Psalm 110.1 ends it.

> **How to present this.** Do not mock "Mother of God." Say "my Lord" is what David calls the Messiah in the psalm Yeshua quoted, and that Luke's own chapter has God *giving* the throne to the child.

### C25. Romans 10.9–13, "call upon the name of the Lord," and Joel 2.32

**The text.** "If thou shalt confess with thy mouth Jesus as Lord... thou shalt be saved... for, Whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved." [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/romans/10.htm)

**The claim.** Verse 13 quotes Joel 2.32, where the Hebrew has "the name of *Yahweh*" [ASV Joel](https://biblehub.com/asv/joel/2.htm), and Paul applies it to confessing "Jesus as Lord." David Capes, *Old Testament Yahweh Texts in Paul's Christology* (1992), is the scholarly statement; Richard Bauckham uses it in *Jesus and the God of Israel* (Eerdmans, 2008).

**The answer.** First, Paul wrote *onoma Kyriou* (ὄνομα Κυρίου), "name of Lord," no article [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/romans/10-13.htm). He is quoting the Septuagint, which had already replaced YHWH with *kyrios*, the word Paul uses of God and of the Messiah. The identification is supplied by the reader.

Second, Paul's own logic. 10.9: confess Yeshua as Lord *and* believe "that God raised him from the dead." The God who raised and the one raised are two in the sentence that supposedly merges them. 10.12: "the same Lord is Lord of all," because God made him so (Acts 2.36, 10.36, Philippians 2.9–11, where the name is *given*).

Third, Peter's reading of the same Joel verse. Acts 2.21 quotes it; Acts 2.36 explains it: "God hath made him both Lord and Christ." Calling on the Lord Yeshua is how one now calls on Yahweh, because Yeshua is Yahweh's appointed agent of salvation. Acts 4.12 is channel language, not identity language.

Fourth, Romans itself: 15.6, "the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ"; 16.27, "the only wise God, through Jesus Christ."

Fifth, scholarship. James McGrath, *The Only True God: Early Christian Monotheism in Its Jewish Context* (Illinois, 2009), argues that applying Yahweh texts to an exalted agent stayed inside first-century Hebrew monotheism, since God's agent bears God's name (Exodus 23.21, "my name is in him"). Dunn, *Did the First Christians Worship Jesus?* (SPCK, 2010), says the same: derivative lordship.

**The standard harmonization.** Paul applies Yahweh texts to Yeshua repeatedly (Romans 14.11, Philippians 2.10–11, 1 Corinthians 1.31), a pattern not a slip; and saving by calling on the name is Yahweh's alone (Isaiah 43.11, C33).

**Why this matters.** A real argument. The honest answer is agency with the name given; the honest concession is that Paul does use Yahweh texts for Yeshua.

> **How to present this.** Do not say "Paul never applies Yahweh verses to Yeshua." He does. Say he does it *because* God gave Yeshua the name (Philippians 2.9), and point to Acts 2.36 where Peter explains the same Joel quote that way.

### C26. 1 Corinthians 8.6, "one God the Father... one Lord Jesus Christ," and the "split Shema"

**The text.** "Yet to us there is one God, the Father, of whom are all things, and we unto him; and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things, and we through him." [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/1_corinthians/8.htm)

**The claim.** The Greek Shema reads "the Lord our God is one Lord." Paul, it is said, split its two words: "one God" to the Father, "one Lord" to Yeshua, placing Yeshua inside the Shema. N. T. Wright, *The Climax of the Covenant* (1991), chapter 6; Richard Bauckham, *God Crucified* (1998), reprinted in *Jesus and the God of Israel* (2008), the basis of his "divine identity" christology; Gordon Fee, *Pauline Christology* (2007), who says Paul "divided the Shema into two parts" [summary at Perriman's blog](https://postost.net/comment/4904).

**The answer.** First, the Greek. *Heis Theos ho Patēr* (εἷς Θεὸς ὁ Πατήρ), "one God, the Father." *Heis Kyrios Iēsous Christos*, "one Lord, Jesus Christ" [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/1_corinthians/8-6.htm). Paul does not say "one God, the Father and the Son." If this is a creed, its first clause says the one God is the Father. The Trinitarian reading must make "God" mean "the Father as one person of the Godhead," which the text does not say.

Second, the prepositions. The Father is the one *ex hou* (ἐξ οὗ, "out of whom"); Yeshua the one *di' hou* (δι᾿ οὗ, "through whom") [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/1_corinthians/8-6.htm). Source and channel. The same letter: 3.23, "Christ is God's"; 11.3, "the head of Christ is God"; 15.24–28, the Son hands the kingdom back and is himself "subjected."

Third, specialists have challenged the split Shema. Andrew Perry and Thomas Gaston, "Christological Monotheism," *Horizons in Biblical Theology* 39.2 (2017), argue *kyrios* in 8.6 is a descriptive title answering the "many lords" of verse 5, not a stand-in for the Name [quoted in the comment thread at Perriman's blog](https://postost.net/comment/4904). Andrew Perriman, *In the Form of a God* (2022), calls the pairing "functionally asymmetrical": the Shema is distributed, Yeshua is not incorporated. McGrath, *The Only True God*, chapter 2, reads "one Lord" as the exalted agent of Psalm 110.1. Dunn, *Did the First Christians Worship Jesus?*, says the wider narrative, not the Shema, controls "one Lord."

Fourth, the Hebrew Bible already pairs one God with one lord under him: Psalm 110.1, Daniel 7.13–14. That is the grammar of 8.6.

**The standard harmonization.** "Through whom are all things" makes Yeshua agent of creation, the unique prerogative of God in Second Temple thought; Hebrew monotheism had no category for a created being through whom God created; the lexical match with the Greek Shema is too close for accident.

**Why this matters.** The "divine identity" argument is the most sophisticated academic case for high christology in Paul and 8.6 is its flagship.

> **How to present this.** Do not call Bauckham or Wright fools. Say their reading requires "God" to mean "the Father as one person of the Trinity" where Paul wrote "one God, the Father," and that Perry, Gaston, McGrath and Dunn do not accept the split-Shema reading.

### C27. 1 Corinthians 10.4, "the rock was Christ"

**The text.** "They drank of a spiritual rock that followed them: and the rock was Christ." [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/1_corinthians/10.htm)

**The claim.** Deuteronomy 32.4 calls Yahweh "the Rock"; the wilderness rock was Christ; so Christ was present as Yahweh in the Exodus.

**The answer.** First, Paul's own word: *pneumatikēs petras* (πνευματικῆς πέτρας), "spiritual rock" [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/1_corinthians/10-4.htm), parallel to "spiritual food" and "spiritual drink." He is reading the Exodus as type, the way Galatians 4.24–25 says "Hagar is Mount Sinai" and the women "are two covenants."

Second, "the rock that followed them" is not in the Hebrew Bible. It is a Hebrew legend of a travelling well: Tosefta Sukkah 3.11 has the well going up the mountains and down into the valleys with Israel, and Pseudo-Philo, *Biblical Antiquities* 10.7 (first century), has "a well of water following them" [Pseudo-Philo text](https://www.earlyjewishwritings.com/text/pseudophilo.html). Paul uses a known midrashic image and says Christ is what it pointed to. Philo had already allegorized the rock as God's Wisdom, "the abrupt rock is the wisdom of God" (*Allegorical Interpretation* 2.86) [Yonge translation](http://www.earlyjewishwritings.com/text/philo/book3.html), and Christ is God's Wisdom in 1 Corinthians 1.24, 1.30.

Third, the chapter's point is moral (10.5–12): Israel had Christ's gifts in type and still fell. Same letter: 8.6, 11.3, 15.28 (C26).

**The standard harmonization.** Paul says "was," not "represented"; 10.9 ("tempt the Lord," with "Christ" in some manuscripts) implies Christ's presence in the Exodus; typology does not exclude presence.

**Why this matters.** Weak. It is here because it comes up.

> **How to present this.** Say "spiritual rock," cite Galatians 4.24, move on.

### C28. Hebrews 1.3, "express image," "upholding all things"

**The text.** "Who being the effulgence of his glory, and the very image of his substance, and upholding all things by the word of his power." [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/hebrews/1.htm)

**The claim.** Radiance is inseparable from its source; the Son is the exact "image of his substance" (*hypostasis*, the Nicene word); he upholds the universe, which only God does.

**The answer.** First, the words. *Apaugasma* (ἀπαύγασμα), "radiance"; *charaktēr* (χαρακτήρ), "stamp," the image struck on a coin; *hypostaseōs* (ὑποστάσεως), "substance," "real nature"; *pherōn* (φέρων), "bearing," "upholding" [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/hebrews/1-3.htm). Every one is Wisdom-tradition vocabulary. Wisdom of Solomon 7.25–26 (Greek Apocrypha) calls Wisdom "an *apaugasma* of eternal light... an image of his goodness." Philo describes the rational soul as a coin "stamped and impressed with the seal of God, the impression of which is the eternal word" (*On Planting* 18) [Yonge translation](http://www.earlyjewishwritings.com/text/philo/book12.html), and calls the Logos "the archetypal seal," "the idea of ideas, the Reason of God," after which the visible world is made (*On the Creation* 25) [Yonge translation](http://www.earlyjewishwritings.com/text/philo/book1.html). Hebrews describes the Son in the vocabulary already used for God's personified Wisdom, which was God's attribute, not a second person.

Second, a *charaktēr* is a copy. A coin bears the king's image and is not the king. "Image of God" is said of Adam (Genesis 1.26–27) and of the Messiah as new Adam (Colossians 1.15, 2 Corinthians 4.4). To be the image of X is to be not-X.

Third, the frame. 1.2, "appointed heir"; 1.4, "became" better than angels, "having inherited" the name; 1.9, "God, thy God, hath anointed thee" (Part C, Hebrews 1.8); 2.9, "made a little lower than the angels"; 3.2, "him that appointed him"; 5.5, "glorified not himself"; 5.8–9, "learned obedience... having been made perfect." No book in the New Testament has more subordination language, and 1.3 lives inside it.

Fourth, "upholding all things by the word of *his* power," *tēs dynameōs autou*, where "his" is God's [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/hebrews/1-3.htm). The agent through whom God made the ages (1.2, *di' hou*) carries them by the Father's word, not his own.

Fifth, Raymond Brown, "Does the New Testament Call Jesus God?", *Theological Studies* 26 (1965) 545–573, counts Hebrews 1.8 as a clear case but does not list 1.3 among passages that call Jesus God at all [PDF](https://theologicalstudies.net/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/26.4.1.pdf). Kenneth Schenck, *Cosmology and Eschatology in Hebrews* (Cambridge, 2007), shows the Son language is Wisdom language transferred to the exalted human Yeshua.

**The standard harmonization.** Radiance is co-eternal and identical in nature with its source; "image of his substance" means the Son perfectly expresses the Father's being; the Wisdom tradition was the category used to speak of a divine person distinct from the Father; the subordination verses describe the incarnate Son's human experience.

**Why this matters.** Hebrews 1 is the strongest sustained passage for an exalted Son and one of the strongest for a Son who is appointed, anointed, and made. Teach both or lose credibility.

### C29. Hebrews 1.6, "let all the angels of God worship him"

**The text.** "And when he again bringeth in the firstborn into the world he saith, And let all the angels of God worship him." [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/hebrews/1.htm)

**The claim.** God commands angels to worship the Son; angels worship only God (Revelation 22.9); the quotation comes from Deuteronomy 32.43 LXX or Psalm 97.7, where the one worshipped is Yahweh.

**The answer.** First, the Greek. *Prōtotokon* (πρωτότοκον), "firstborn"; *proskynēsatōsan* (προσκυνησάτωσαν), "let them bow down"; *angeloi Theou*, "angels of God" [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/hebrews/1-6.htm). The one honored is the *firstborn*, a title Scripture gives to creatures: Israel (Exodus 4.22) and David, "my firstborn, the highest of the kings" (Psalm 89.27). Angels honoring God's installed king is Psalm 2.6–8, quoted one verse earlier (1.5).

Second, *proskyneō* is bowing, used of homage to kings as well as worship of God; see A9, the Part C worship entry, and C40, where 1 Chronicles 29.20 has Israel bow to Yahweh and the king in one act.

Third, the source. The line matches the Greek Deuteronomy 32.43, which has two extra clauses the Hebrew lacks. The Greek manuscripts split on the wording: the critical editions (Swete, Rahlfs) print "let all the *sons* of God bow down to him" and then "let all the *angels* of God strengthen themselves in him" [Swete's LXX](https://biblehub.com/sepd/deuteronomy/32.htm), while the Odes appendix to the Greek Psalter (Odes 2.43) and the text used by the Greek church read "let all the *angels* of God bow down to him," Hebrews' exact words [Greek church text with English](https://www.ellopos.net/elpenor/greek-texts/septuagint/chapter.asp?book=5&page=32). Gareth Lee Cockerill, "Hebrews 1:6: Source and Significance," *Bulletin for Biblical Research* 9 (1999), from p. 51, argues that Hebrews quotes a Greek rendering of the longer Hebrew of Deuteronomy 32.43 found at Qumran (4QDeut q, also numbered 4Q44), where the line reads "bow down to him, all you gods (*elohim*)," and that "angels of God" was the original Greek translation of that *elohim* [abstract](https://www.galaxie.com/article/bbr09-1-04). Some commentators propose Psalm 97.7 ("worship him, all ye gods") as the source instead. The Masoretic Hebrew of Deuteronomy 32.43 has no such line. The quotation rests on a Greek text of a Hebrew line the Masoretes did not preserve.

Fourth, Hebrews' logic is "unto which of the angels said he at any time..." (1.5, 1.13). The point is the Son's superiority *to angels* by inheritance, appointment, and anointing. In 2.5–9 the author explains: the world to come is subjected to a *man*, "made a little lower than the angels," now "crowned with glory." The angels' obeisance in 1.6 is that owed to the exalted man of Psalm 8, and it is tied to his enthronement ("when he again bringeth in"), as in Philippians 2.9–11, where every knee bows *because* God exalted him, "to the glory of God the Father."

**The standard harmonization.** Hebrews applies to the Son a text whose object was Yahweh, which it would not do unless the Son shares Yahweh's identity; angels bowing in heaven is worship in the full sense; Revelation 5.13–14 gives the Lamb the same praise as the one on the throne.

**Why this matters.** The strong form depends on the source being Deuteronomy 32.43 with Yahweh as object. Knowing the line is absent from the MT and that the chapter's argument is heir-over-angels relieves the pressure.

> **How to present this.** Do not say "the verse is not in the Bible." Say the quoted line is in Greek Deuteronomy and a Qumran scroll but not in the Masoretic Hebrew, that the one bowed to is "the firstborn," and that two chapters later the author says the one crowned is a man made lower than angels.

### C30. Hebrews 1.10–12 and Psalm 102.25–27 applied to the Son

**The text.** "Thou, Lord, in the beginning didst lay the foundation of the earth, and the heavens are the works of thy hands: they shall perish; but thou continuest." [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/hebrews/1.htm); source Psalm 102.25–27 [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/psalms/102.htm).

**The claim.** Psalm 102 is addressed to Yahweh the Creator; Hebrews, continuing the "unto the Son" of 1.8, applies it to the Son. Often called the strongest text in Hebrews 1.

**The answer.** First, the author quotes the Septuagint, which differs from the Hebrew where it matters. The Hebrew of 102.25 has no "Lord" [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/psalms/102-25.htm); the Greek (Psalm 101.26 LXX) adds *Kyrie* [interlinear Hebrews 1.10](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/hebrews/1-10.htm). More important, the Greek of 101.24 reads *apekrithē autō en hodō ischyos autou· tēn oligotēta tōn hēmerōn mou anangeilon moi*, "He answered him in the way of his strength: Tell me the fewness of my days" [Greek with English](https://www.ellopos.net/elpenor/greek-texts/septuagint/chapter.asp?book=24&page=101), which can be read as God *answering* someone, so that 26–28 ("In the beginning thou, O Lord, didst lay the foundation of the earth") become God's words *to* that one. The Hebrew does not read this way. The Dianoigo blog's detailed study argues the author of Hebrews read the Greek psalm as containing two Lords, as he read Psalm 110.1 (quoted in 1.13), and so took 25–27 as God's address to the Son [post](https://blog.dianoigo.com/2018/01/how-hebrews-came-to-interpret-psalm.html). The application to the Son depends on a feature of the Greek that is not in the Hebrew.

Second, the method. Hebrews 1.5–13 is a chain of quotations in which God speaks to or about the Son. If the author found God addressing a second Lord in the Greek psalm, he could apply Creator language to the one "through whom" God made the ages (1.2, *di' hou*) without making him the Father. Compare Proverbs 3.19, "Yahweh by wisdom founded the earth."

Third, the purpose. The quotation proves permanence: angels are "winds" and "flames" (1.7); the Son "continuest" and "thy years shall not fail" (1.12; compare 7.24). It is about the Son outlasting angels, not about identity with the Creator.

Fourth, the frame: 1.2 appointed, 1.4 became, 1.9 "thy God," 2.9 made lower, 5.5 glorified not himself. The author applied a Creator psalm to the Son two verses after calling the Father the Son's God.

**The standard harmonization.** Whatever the Greek reads, the author knew the psalm was about Yahweh and applied it anyway, which is only possible if the Son is Yahweh; "through whom" describes the Son's role within the Godhead. Brown's 1965 article treats 1.8–9 as calling the Son God and takes 1.10–12 as supporting it.

**Why this matters.** The hardest passage in Hebrews 1 for a non-Trinitarian reading. If you cannot explain the Greek psalm, you cannot answer this verse.

> **How to present this.** Do not say "Hebrews misquotes the psalm." Say the author quotes the Greek, that the Greek reads differently at verse 24 (Greek numbering), and that his method all chapter is to find God speaking to the Son in Scripture, while calling the Father "thy God." Admit this one is strong.

### C31. 1 John 5.20, "this is the true God"

**The text.** "We are in him that is true, even in his Son Jesus Christ. This is the true God, and eternal life." [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/1_john/5.htm)

**The claim.** The nearest antecedent of "this" (*houtos*) is "Jesus Christ." Brown's 1965 article ranks it among five "probable" cases [PDF](https://theologicalstudies.net/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/26.4.1.pdf).

**The answer.** First, *houtos* (οὗτος) refers to the main subject under discussion, not automatically the nearest noun; 1 John 2.22, "this is the antichrist," is the author's own example. In 5.20 the subject all along is "him that is true," *ton alēthinon* (τὸν ἀληθινόν), named twice, and that one is the Father, since the Son gives understanding to know *him* and we are *in him* "even in his Son" [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/1_john/5-20.htm). "This is the true God" picks up "the true one."

Second, the author has already defined the phrase. John 17.3: "that they should know *thee the only true God*, and him whom thou didst send, even Jesus Christ." There "the only true God" is the Father, distinguished from the sent one, and knowing both is eternal life. 1 John 5.20 is 17.3 compressed.

Third, 1 John's usage: 1.3, "the Father, and his Son"; 4.9, 4.14, the Father *sent* the Son; 5.11, "God gave unto us eternal life, and this life is in his Son."

Fourth, the Trinitarian specialists. Brown's 1965 article claims only "a certain probability" for Jesus as the referent, admitting the grammar is open. Murray J. Harris, *Jesus as God: The New Testament Use of Theos in Reference to Jesus* (Baker, 1992), the fullest Trinitarian study of the question, concludes that 1 John 5.20 "possibly, but not likely" calls Jesus God, and takes "the true God" as the Father [review summarizing Harris's verdicts](https://faithalone.org/journal-articles/book-reviews/jesus-as-god-the-new-testament-use-of-theos-in-reference-to-jesus).

**The standard harmonization.** "Eternal life" is a title of the Son (1 John 1.2, John 11.25, 14.6), so "the true God and eternal life" fits him; nearest antecedent is the default.

**Why this matters.** Cited constantly, grammatically open, settled by the author's own definition in John 17.3.

> **How to present this.** Do not claim the grammar "proves" the Father. Say it is open, that the author defined "the only true God" as the Father in 17.3, and that Murray Harris, a Trinitarian who wrote the standard study of *theos* applied to Jesus, concludes the Father is meant here.

### C32. Revelation 1.17, "the first and the last" (and 2.8, 22.13; see C17)

**The text.** "I fell at his feet as one dead. And he laid his right hand upon me, saying, Fear not; I am the first and the last." [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/revelation/1.htm); also 2.8, "who was dead, and lived again."

**The claim.** "First and last" is Yahweh's self-title in Isaiah 44.6 and 48.12 (Part C, Isaiah 48); the speaker here is the risen Yeshua (1.18). C17 handled 1.8 and 22.13.

**The answer.** First, the Greek: *egō eimi ho prōtos kai ho eschatos* [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/revelation/1-17.htm). In Isaiah the title means Yahweh alone is God, before and after every rival (44.6, "besides me there is no God"). In Revelation it is immediately glossed: "I was dead, and behold, I am alive for evermore" (1.18); "who was dead, and lived again" (2.8). Yahweh in Isaiah cannot die (Habakkuk 1.12). Revelation's "first and last" is spoken by one who died, and the book ties it to death and resurrection: "the firstborn of the dead" (1.5), holder of "the keys of death" (1.18), "the beginning of the creation of God" (3.14).

Second, the book's distinction between God and the Lamb never breaks. 1.1, "God gave unto him"; 1.6, "his God and Father"; 3.12, "my God" four times in one verse; 3.21, "sat down with my Father in his throne"; 5.7, the Lamb takes the scroll from the one on the throne; 7.10, "unto our God... and unto the Lamb"; 11.15, "our Lord, and... his Christ"; 21.22, "the Lord God the Almighty, and the Lamb"; 22.1, "the throne of God and of the Lamb."

Third, "the Almighty" (*pantokratōr*), who speaks the Alpha-and-Omega line in 1.8 and 21.6, is always God on the throne, distinguished from the Lamb (11.17, 19.6, 21.22). In 22.12–16 the speaker shifts between angel, God, and Yeshua without clear markers; G. K. Beale (NIGTC) acknowledges the shifts. See C17.

Fourth, the fall. John falls "as one dead" and is told "fear not," exactly as Daniel before an angel (Daniel 8.17–18, 10.9–10). In 19.10 and 22.8–9 John falls before an angel and is stopped. Yeshua does not stop him, which shows he is above angels, not that he is the Almighty.

**The standard harmonization.** The title is Yahweh's and cannot be shared; the book gives the Lamb the throne, the worship, and God's titles deliberately; "I was dead" refers to the incarnate Son's human death.

**Why this matters.** Revelation is strong for the Lamb's exaltation and the weakest book for the Lamb being the Almighty, because the two sit side by side on nearly every page.

> **How to present this.** Do not say the speaker in 1.17 is the Father; verse 18 settles it. Say the title is tied to his death and rising, Isaiah's to Yahweh's deathless uniqueness, and that the same book has him say "my God" four times in one verse.

### C33. Isaiah 43.11 and 44.6, "beside me there is no saviour / no God"

**The text.** 43.11, "I, even I, am Yahweh; and besides me there is no saviour." [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/isaiah/43.htm). 44.6, "I am the first, and I am the last; and besides me there is no God." [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/isaiah/44.htm)

**The claim.** Only Yahweh saves; Yeshua saves (Luke 2.11, Titus 2.13); so Yeshua is Yahweh.

**The answer.** First, the Hebrew: *anokhi anokhi YHWH ve-ein mibbaladai moshia* (אנכי אנכי יהוה ואין מבלעדי מושיע), "I, I am Yahweh, and there is no saviour *apart from* me" [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/isaiah/43-11.htm). *Mibbaladai* means "apart from, without me." The claim is exclusivity of source, not of title. The Hebrew Bible calls men *moshia*: Othniel (Judges 3.9), Ehud (3.15), an unnamed deliverer (2 Kings 13.5), "saviours who saved them" (Nehemiah 9.27), "saviours on mount Zion" (Obadiah 21), and Isaiah himself, "he will send them a saviour" (19.20). The book that says "no saviour apart from me" expects Yahweh to *send* one.

Second, the New Testament says exactly that of Yeshua. Acts 5.31, "him did God exalt... to be a Prince and a Saviour"; Acts 13.23, "God... brought unto Israel a Saviour"; 1 John 4.14, "the Father hath sent the Son to be the Saviour"; Titus 3.4–6, "God our Saviour... through Jesus Christ our Saviour," both called Saviour, two persons.

Third, Isaiah 43–46 is a polemic against Babylon's idols (44.9–20, 46.1–7). "No God besides me" targets Bel and Nebo. Isaiah's own Messiah is "my servant" (42.1, 49.3, 52.13, 53.11), whom Yahweh upholds. "First and last" (44.6; see C32) is never applied in Isaiah to the servant.

**The standard harmonization.** Judges' saviours delivered from armies, not sin; Isaiah reserves blotting out transgressions to Yahweh (43.25) and the New Testament gives that to Yeshua; Isaiah 45.23 ("every knee shall bow") is applied to Yeshua in Philippians 2.10.

**Why this matters.** Pure syllogism, broken by the Bible's own list of human saviours. Easy and common.

> **How to present this.** Do not say "Isaiah's monotheism is not about Yeshua." Say "it is against idols, and the same Yahweh who says no saviour apart from me raised up Othniel, Ehud, and the servant." Then read Acts 5.31.

### C34. Zechariah 12.10, "they shall look upon me whom they have pierced"

**The text.** "They shall look unto me whom they have pierced; and they shall mourn for him." [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/zechariah/12.htm). John 19.37: "They shall look on him whom they pierced." [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/john/19.htm)

**The claim.** Yahweh is the speaker and says "me"; John applies it to the crucified Yeshua; so the pierced one is Yahweh.

**The answer.** First, the Hebrew as it stands. The MT reads *elai* (אלי, "to me"), then *et asher daqaru* (את אשר דקרו, "the one whom they pierced"), then *ve-safedu alav* (וספדו עליו, "and they shall mourn *for him*") [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/zechariah/12-10.htm). Biblehub shows *'ê·lay* at "unto me" and *'ā·lāw* at "for him." Even in the MT the sentence shifts from "me" to "him." The phrase *et asher* can introduce a separate object: "they shall look to me, concerning the one whom they pierced." The 1917 Jewish Publication Society version reads "they shall look unto Me because they have thrust him through," and the 1985 JPS Tanakh "they shall lament to Me about those who are slain" [translations compared](https://www.biblehub.com/zechariah/12-10.htm). Hebrew linguist Joel Hoffman argues John's "him" is not a careless misquotation but a deliberate first-century reapplication of the line to a new setting [post](https://goddidntsaythat.com/2012/11/20/the-case-of-mistaken-piercing-in-zechariah-12-10-and-john-19-37/).

Second, the variant. Some medieval Hebrew manuscripts read *elav* (אליו, "to him"), one letter different (yod for vav, nearly identical in Hebrew script); the majority read *elai*, the support for "to him" is weak in the Hebrew tradition and absent from the ancient versions, and the NET note keeps the MT as the harder reading. The RSV, NRSV ("look on the one whom they have pierced"), NAB ("look on him"), and Jerusalem Bible follow the minority; NET, NIV, ESV, and most others keep "me" [UBS translation handbook](https://tips.translation.bible/story/translation-commentary-on-zechariah-1210/). The LXX goes elsewhere: *epiblepsontai pros me anth' hōn katorchēsanto*, "they shall look to me because they have *danced in triumph* [mocked]," reading the consonants of *daqaru* as *raqadu* [Greek with English](https://www.ellopos.net/elpenor/greek-texts/septuagint/chapter.asp?book=41&page=12). John has "on him... pierced." Of three ancient witnesses, none agrees with another exactly, and it is John who says "him." The ASV follows the MT with no note.

Third, John 19.37 is a fulfilment citation about the *spear wound*, paired with 19.36 about the unbroken bones. John does not add "so the pierced one was Yahweh." He had the word "me" available and wrote "him." Revelation 1.7 quotes the same verse ("they that pierced him") and distinguishes the pierced one from "the Lord God, the Almighty" in the next verse.

Fourth, Zechariah's own theology: Yahweh speaks of a struck third party, "smite the shepherd" (13.7), and treats an attack on his shepherd as an attack on himself. Agency, not identity.

**The standard harmonization.** The MT's "me" is original; John's "him" is legitimate because the pierced one is both Yahweh (speaker) and the Son (mourned), exactly the two-persons pattern; the Hebrew's own shift to "him" shows Yahweh spoken of in the third person.

**Why this matters.** The argument rests on one word, which is followed by "him" in the same clause, varies in the manuscripts, reads "danced" in the LXX, and is changed to "him" by John.

> **How to present this.** Do not say "the Hebrew says to him." The majority text says "to me." Say the same sentence shifts to "him," that the JPS reads "to me, concerning those slain," that a minority of manuscripts read "to him," and that John chose "him."

### C35. Micah 5.2, "whose goings forth have been from of old, from everlasting"

**The text.** "Out of thee shall one come forth unto me that is to be ruler in Israel; whose goings forth are from of old, from everlasting." [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/micah/5.htm)

**The claim.** Only God is "from everlasting"; the Messiah is; so the Messiah is God. Matthew 2.6 applies the verse to Yeshua.

**The answer.** First, the Hebrew: *u-motza'otav miqqedem mimei olam* (ומוצאתיו מקדם מימי עולם), "and his origins are from of old, from the days of *olam*" [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/micah/5-2.htm). Biblehub shows *miq·qe·ḏem*, *mî·mê*, *‘ō·w·lām*. *Olam* means a long duration, antiquity, an age; only in some contexts "forever." "Days of olam" is a fixed idiom for the distant past. Micah uses the identical phrase in 7.14, "as in the days of old" (*kimei olam*), of the Exodus [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/micah/7-14.htm). Amos 9.11 uses it of David's time; Malachi 3.4 of the ancient offerings; Isaiah 63.9, 63.11 of Moses; Deuteronomy 32.7, "remember the days of old." Nobody translates Micah 7.14 "the days of eternity." ESV, NRSV, NIV, and JPS all render 5.2 "from ancient days" or "from of old."

Second, the sense. *Motza'ot*, plural, "origins," "descent." Micah has just named Bethlehem Ephrathah, David's hometown (1 Samuel 17.12). The ruler's lineage goes back to the ancient Davidic line, the promise of 2 Samuel 7.

Third, the verse itself subordinates him: he comes forth "*unto me*," *li* (לי) [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/micah/5-2.htm), and in 5.4 feeds the flock "in the majesty of the name of Yahweh *his God*."

Fourth, Matthew 2.6 quotes the verse for Bethlehem and omits the "goings forth" line entirely; the Greek runs from "out of thee shall come forth a governor" straight to "who shall be shepherd of my people Israel," with nothing corresponding to *mimei olam* [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/matthew/2.htm), [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/matthew/2-6.htm).

**The standard harmonization.** *Olam* means eternity when applied to God (Psalm 90.2); KJV and ASV chose "everlasting" deliberately; "goings forth" is the Son's eternal going forth from the Father; pairs with Isaiah 9.6 (Part C).

**Why this matters.** Rests on one English word in the KJV tradition. Micah 7.14 ends it.

> **How to present this.** Do not say "olam never means eternity." Say "the days of olam" is an idiom for ancient times, that Micah uses it so in 7.14, and that the ruler "comes forth unto me" and serves "Yahweh his God" two verses later.

### C36. Jude 5, "Jesus saved a people out of Egypt"

**The text.** ASV: "the Lord, having saved a people out of the land of Egypt." [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/jude/1.htm). ESV (2016 on): "Jesus, who saved a people out of the land of Egypt."

**The claim.** Some manuscripts read *Iēsous* ("Jesus") for *Kyrios* ("Lord"); NA28 (2012) adopted *Iēsous*; so Jude believed the pre-existent Jesus acted as Yahweh in the Exodus.

**The answer.** First, the text. The manuscripts split: *Iēsous* in Alexandrinus (A), Vaticanus (B), the minuscules 33, 81, 1241, 1739, 1881, the Vulgate, the Coptic versions, and Origen, Cyril, Jerome, and Bede; *Kyrios* (mostly with the article) in Sinaiticus (א), Ψ, and the Byzantine majority; *theos* in a few; *theos Christos* in Papyrus 72 [NET note, which lists the witnesses](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jude+1%3A5&version=NET). NA27 printed *Kyrios*; NA28 (2012), following the Editio Critica Maior, switched to *Iēsous*, and the ESV, NET, and NLT had already done so. Biblehub's Greek (Nestle 1904) still reads *Kyrios* [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/jude/1-5.htm). The change is defended by Philipp Bartholomä, "Did Jesus Save the People out of Egypt? A Re-Examination of a Textual Problem in Jude 5," *Novum Testamentum* 50.2 (2008) 143–158, and contested by Timo Flink, "Reconsidering the Text of Jude 5, 13, 15 and 18," *Filología Neotestamentaria* 20 (2007) 95–125 [article](https://www.bsw.org/filologia-neotestamentaria/vol-20-2007/reconsidering-the-text-of-jude-5-13-15-and-18/224/article-p108.html). Metzger's *Textual Commentary* (2nd ed., 1994, p. 657) reports that "a majority of the Committee was of the opinion that the reading was difficult to the point of impossibility," explained its origin as one abbreviation being taken for the other, and noted that "nowhere else does the author employ Ἰησοῦς alone, but always Ἰησοῦς Χριστός." Textual critic Tommy Wasserman also prefers *Kyrios* as the reading that best explains the others; the Trinities blog summarizes the evidence and notes Sinaiticus reads the abbreviation for *Kyrios* [post](https://trinities.org/blog/jude-5-did-jesus-deliver-the-people-out-of-egypt/).

Second, how the variant arose. Both words were written as abbreviations: ΚΣ (*Kyrios*) and ΙΣ (*Iēsous*), one letter apart, a documented confusion. *Iēsous* is also Greek for *Joshua*, who led Israel after Egypt, inviting association. And Jude never writes a bare "Jesus"; every other time it is "Jesus Christ" or "our Lord Jesus Christ" (vv. 1, 4, 17, 21, 25).

Third, even if *Iēsous* is original, Jude 4 names "our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ," and Jude 25 says "to the only God our Saviour, through Jesus Christ our Lord." Jude distinguishes God from Jesus Christ in his first and last sentences. "Jesus saved a people out of Egypt" would then be what Paul says in 1 Corinthians 10.4 (C27): the Messiah present in type or as God's agent, not identical with "the only God" of verse 25.

**The standard harmonization.** "Jesus" is the best-attested and harder reading, so likelier original; it shows the first believers identified Jesus with the Lord of Israel's history; Jude's "only God" is the Father within the Trinity.

**Why this matters.** A text-critical question that is genuinely open. Neither side should use it as settled.

> **How to present this.** Do not say "the manuscripts say Lord." Vaticanus and Alexandrinus read "Jesus." Say NA27 and NA28 chose differently, that Metzger's committee called "Jesus" nearly impossible, that Jude never elsewhere writes bare "Jesus," and that Jude 25 calls the Father "the only God."

### C37. Genesis 19.24, "Yahweh rained... from Yahweh"

**The text.** "Then Yahweh rained upon Sodom and upon Gomorrah brimstone and fire from Yahweh out of heaven." [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/genesis/19.htm)

**The claim.** One Yahweh on earth (who spoke with Abraham in chapter 18) rains fire *from* another Yahweh in heaven: two persons, both Yahweh. Michael Heiser, *The Unseen Realm* (2015), popularized it; Sanhedrin 38b records heretics using the verse.

**The answer.** First, the Hebrew: *va-YHWH himtir... me'et YHWH min ha-shamayim* (ויהוה המטיר... מאת יהוה מן השמים) [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/genesis/19-24.htm). Both are YHWH. Nothing in the verse puts the first Yahweh on earth; 18.33 says Yahweh "went his way," and 19.1 says the two who entered Sodom were *angels*.

Second, repeating a proper noun where English uses a pronoun is normal Hebrew. 1 Kings 8.1, "Solomon assembled... unto king Solomon." 1 Samuel 12.11, "Yahweh sent... and Yahweh delivered you." Hosea 1.7, Yahweh speaking: "I will save them by Yahweh their God." Exodus 24.1, Yahweh speaking: "Come up unto Yahweh." Zechariah 10.12, "in Yahweh... saith Yahweh." Genesis 19.24 is "Yahweh rained fire from himself out of heaven," stressing that it was Yahweh's own act.

Third, the Talmud cuts the other way. Sanhedrin 38b records the argument and answers it [Soncino text](https://halakhah.com/sanhedrin/sanhedrin_38.html). Rabbi Yohanan's rule is that wherever the heretics found a proof text, the refutation stands beside it. On Genesis 19.24 itself, a heretic put the verse to Rabbi Ishmael ben Yose, and a launderer answered from Genesis 4.23, where Lamech says "hear my voice, ye wives of Lamech" instead of "my wives": that is simply how Scripture speaks. The same page has Rabbi Idith answer "come up unto Yahweh" (Exodus 24.1) by naming the angel Metatron, "whose name is like his Master's" (Exodus 23.21), and has Rabbi Akiva rebuked for giving David the second throne of Daniel 7.9. The rabbis knew the "two powers in heaven" heresy (Alan Segal, *Two Powers in Heaven*, 1977) and rejected it as a misreading of the idiom.

Fourth, where the Hebrew Bible does have Yahweh's angel speak as Yahweh (Genesis 16, Exodus 3, Judges 6), the angel is *malakh*, "messenger," bearing the name (Exodus 23.21). Agency, not a second Yahweh. And 19.24 names no angel. No New Testament writer uses the verse for Yeshua's deity; Luke 17.29, 2 Peter 2.6, and Jude 7 all cite Sodom without it.

**The standard harmonization.** *Me'et* ("from") marks a source distinct from the actor; the visible-and-invisible Yahweh pattern runs through Genesis 18–19, Exodus 3, Judges 6; the rabbis' rejection of "two powers" was a reaction against Christian use.

**Why this matters.** Popular because surprising; weak because the idiom is common and the verse names no angel.

> **How to present this.** Do not say "two Yahwehs is a Christian invention"; the rabbis knew and refuted it. Give 1 Kings 8.1 and Hosea 1.7, and note the chapter says the visitors were angels.

### C38. Acts 20.28, "the church of God which he purchased with his own blood"

**The text.** "To feed the church of the Lord which he purchased with his own blood." [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/acts/20.htm); ASV margin, "Many ancient authorities read *God*."

**The claim.** "Church of God... his own blood" means God has blood, which is only true if Yeshua is God.

**The answer.** First, two textual problems. (a) *Theou* versus *kyriou*: Sinaiticus and Vaticanus (with 614, 1175, 1505, the Vulgate and Syriac) read *tou theou*, "of God" [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/acts/20-28.htm); Papyrus 74, Alexandrinus, the first hand of Ephraemi, Bezae, Laudianus (E), Ψ, 33, 1739 and the Coptic read *tou kyriou*; later Byzantine manuscripts conflate the two. The NET note calls the external evidence "evenly balanced" and prefers "God" on internal grounds [NET note](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts+20%3A28&version=NET). The ASV put "Lord" in the text and "God" in the margin; NA28, ESV, NIV, NRSV read "God." Brown's 1965 article accepts "church of God" but says "we are by no means certain that this verse calls Jesus God" [PDF](https://theologicalstudies.net/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/26.4.1.pdf). (b) Word order: the Byzantine text and KJV have *tou idiou haimatos*, "his own blood"; the oldest manuscripts (P74, א, A, B, C) have *tou haimatos tou idiou*, literally "the blood of his own" [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/acts/20-28.htm), [textual summary](https://uasvbible.org/2021/12/27/nttc-acts-2028b-is-it-which-he-god-purchased-with-the-blood-of-his-own-son-or-which-he-god-purchased-with-his-own-blood/).

Second, "the blood of his own." *Ho idios* (ὁ ἴδιος) standing alone, article and no noun, is attested in the papyri as a term of endearment for a near relative, "his own one." Metzger's *Textual Commentary* (2nd ed.) reports that this absolute use "is found in Greek papyri as a term of endearment referring to near relatives," and that "his Own" may have been an early title for Jesus like "the Beloved," comparing Romans 8.32, "his own Son." The RSV, NRSV, and NET all print "the blood of his own Son" in the text; the NRSV footnote gives the literal Greek, "with the blood of his Own" [NRSV with footnotes](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts+20%3A28&version=NRSVUE). Murray Harris, *Jesus as God* (1992), a Trinitarian, renders "through the blood of his own (Son)" and rates the verse "possibly, but not likely" a reference to Jesus as God. No emendation is needed; it is the natural reading once the idiom is known.

Third, Luke's theology in Acts. 2.22, "a man approved of God"; 2.36, "God hath made him both Lord and Christ"; 3.13, "glorified his Servant Jesus"; 4.27, "thy holy Servant"; 10.38, "God anointed him"; 17.31, "a man whom he hath ordained." Luke never speaks of the Father dying, and Metzger's own explanation for the Western change to "Lord" is that scribes "shrank from" the phrase "the blood of God."

**The standard harmonization.** "Church of God" is the best reading and Pauline (1 Corinthians 1.2, 10.32, Galatians 1.13); "his own blood" is the natural sense; "his own Son" is a conjecture to avoid it; Ignatius (c. 110) could write "the blood of God" (*Ephesians* 1.1) [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0104.htm).

**Why this matters.** Doubly disputed, and the "blood of his own Son" reading sits in the text of the RSV, NRSV, and NET, mainstream Trinitarian Bibles. Teach the footnote.

> **How to present this.** Do not say "it really says church of the Lord." The best manuscripts say "of God." Say they also say "the blood of his own," that *ho idios* is a known term for a dear relative, and that RSV, NRSV, and NET print "his own Son." Then read Acts 2.36 and 3.13.

### C39. Acts 7.59, Stephen "calling upon" Jesus, and prayer to Jesus generally

**The text.** "And they stoned Stephen, calling upon the Lord, and saying, Lord Jesus, receive my spirit." [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/acts/7.htm)

**The claim.** Stephen prays to Yeshua using *epikaloumenon* (ἐπικαλούμενον, "calling upon") [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/acts/7-59.htm), the verb of Joel 2.32 and Romans 10.13 (C25). Prayer belongs to God alone. Larry Hurtado, *One God, One Lord* (1988) and *Lord Jesus Christ* (2003), made the "binitarian devotional pattern" the center of his case: the first believers included Yeshua in prayer, hymns, baptism, and meals without precedent in Hebrew monotheism [review by Simon Gathercole, Themelios 29.3](https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/themelios/review/lord-jesus-christ-devotion-to-jesus-in-earliest-christianity/). Bauckham and Chris Tilling, *Paul's Divine Christology* (2012), build on it.

**The answer.** First, what Stephen saw. 7.55–56: "Jesus standing on the right hand of God... the Son of man standing on the right hand of God." He sees two and addresses the second, Psalm 110.1 enacted. "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit" mirrors Yeshua's own "Father, into thy hands I commend my spirit" (Luke 23.46), spoken now to the exalted man given authority over life (Acts 2.33, 3.15, 5.31; John 5.21–27).

Second, *epikaleō* means "appeal to." Acts uses it six times of appealing to Caesar (25.11–12, 25.21, 25.25, 26.32, 28.19). Appeal to a recognized authority is not, by itself, worship of that authority as God.

Third, Hurtado has been answered. James Dunn, *Did the First Christians Worship Jesus? The New Testament Evidence* (SPCK, 2010), surveys every prayer and hymn and concludes that worship in the full sense went to God alone, that prayer "as such" was addressed to God, usually *through* Yeshua, and that direct appeals to Yeshua (Acts 7.59, 1 Corinthians 16.22, 2 Corinthians 12.8, Revelation 22.20) are appeals to the exalted agent, not treatment of him as a second God. Andrew Perriman summarizes the Dunn-Hurtado exchange [post](https://postost.net/node/163). McGrath, *The Only True God*, chapter 1, argues that Second Temple monotheism was defined by *sacrificial* worship, which the New Testament never offers to Yeshua. Hurtado himself granted the believers did not think they worshipped two gods.

Fourth, the norm. Yeshua taught prayer to the Father (Matthew 6.9). Paul prays to "the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ" (Ephesians 1.3, 1.17, 3.14) "through" Yeshua (Romans 1.8, 16.27). Revelation's praise goes to God "and to the Lamb" (5.13, 7.10), the Lamb's share explained by his being slain (5.9). Luke's own frame: 2.36, 3.13, 10.38, 17.31 (C38).

**The standard harmonization.** For a first-century Hebrew, to invoke anyone in a worship setting was to treat him as divine; the believers did it and saw no conflict, which only works if Yeshua is on the divine side of the line; appeal to Caesar was not religious.

**Why this matters.** Hurtado's argument is the most influential academic case for early high christology and does not hang on one verse. Its weakness is the leap from "included in devotion" to "included in the identity of God," which Dunn and McGrath show is unnecessary.

> **How to present this.** Do not say "the New Testament never prays to Jesus." Acts 7.59 and 2 Corinthians 12.8 are direct. Say Stephen saw two figures and addressed the one beside God, that the verb is the one used for appealing to Caesar, and that Dunn's book-length study concluded prayer "as such" went to God.

### C40. Matthew 14.33 and 28.17, "they worshipped him"

**The text.** 14.33, "they that were in the boat worshipped him, saying, Of a truth thou art the Son of God." [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/matthew/14.htm). 28.17, "when they saw him, they worshipped him; but some doubted." [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/matthew/28.htm)

**The claim.** The disciples worshipped him (*proskyneō*, προσκυνέω) [interlinear 14.33](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/matthew/14-33.htm), [28.17](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/matthew/28-17.htm) and he accepted it; worship is God's alone (Matthew 4.10). General treatment of the verb is in A9 and the Part C worship entry.

**The answer.** First, the word means to bow down, do obeisance. The LXX uses it of Abraham to the Hittites (Genesis 23.7), Jacob to Esau (33.3), David to Saul (1 Samuel 24.8), the prophet Nathan to David (1 Kings 1.23). Matthew uses it of a mother asking a favor (20.20) and a servant begging his master (18.26). The ASV writes "worshipped" when the object is Yeshua and "bowed" elsewhere; that is a translator's choice.

Second, 1 Chronicles 29.20: "all the assembly... bowed down their heads, and worshipped Yahweh, *and the king*" [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/1_chronicles/29.htm), [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/1_chronicles/29-20.htm). One verb, *va-yishtachavu* (וישתחוו), rendered *prosekynēsan* in the LXX, with two objects in a single act: God and God's anointed king. The Chronicler saw no idolatry. That is the frame for bowing to the Son of David.

Third, Revelation 3.9: the risen Yeshua promises, "I will make them to come and worship (*proskynēsousin*) before thy feet" [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/revelation/3.htm), [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/revelation/3-9.htm), spoken of ordinary believers. If *proskyneō* always meant worship-as-God, Yeshua would be promising idolatry. Compare Daniel 2.46, Nebuchadnezzar "worshipped" Daniel.

Fourth, the verses themselves. In 14.33 they say "thou art the Son of God," the Davidic title (Psalm 2.7; Matthew 16.16, 27.54), not "thou art God." In 28.17–18 they bow and he says, "All authority hath been *given* unto me." The one bowed to has just been invested by another; it is Daniel 7.14 fulfilled.

Fifth, Matthew's frame: 4.10, "him only shalt thou serve," spoken by Yeshua of God; 19.17, "One there is who is good"; 24.36, "the Father only"; 27.46, "My God, my God."

**The standard harmonization.** Matthew's eight uses of *proskyneō* toward Yeshua form a pattern he would not leave unqualified for Hebrew readers who knew 4.10; 28.17 is a resurrection appearance, where kingly homage is no longer the natural category.

**Why this matters.** 1 Chronicles 29.20 and Revelation 3.9 settle the vocabulary: one is Israel bowing to Yahweh and the king together, the other is Yeshua promising people will bow before believers.

> **How to present this.** Do not say "they only bowed, they did not worship"; the English distinction is modern. Say it is one Greek word, used for kings, prophets, parents, and Daniel, that 1 Chronicles 29.20 bows to Yahweh and David in one act, and that in 28.18 the one bowed to says his authority was given.

### C41. The Granville Sharp rule in technical detail

Expands the one-sentence treatment in C9.

**The text.** Titus 2.13, "the appearing of the glory of the great God and our Saviour Jesus Christ" (margin: "of our great God and Saviour") [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/titus/2.htm). 2 Peter 1.1, "the righteousness of our God and the Saviour Jesus Christ" (margin: "our God and Saviour") [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/2_peter/1.htm). Ephesians 5.5, "the kingdom of Christ and God" [ASV](https://biblehub.com/asv/ephesians/5.htm).

**The claim.** Granville Sharp (1735–1813), English abolitionist and amateur Greek scholar, published *Remarks on the Uses of the Definitive Article in the Greek Text of the New Testament* (1798). His first rule: when *kai* ("and") joins two nouns of the same case, with the article before the first noun only, the second noun refers to the same person as the first. Titus 2.13 reads *tou megalou Theou kai Sōtēros hēmōn Iēsou Christou* (τοῦ μεγάλου Θεοῦ καὶ Σωτῆρος ἡμῶν Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ): one article, *tou*, before "great God," none before "Saviour" [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/titus/2-13.htm). So God and Saviour are one person, named Jesus Christ. Same in 2 Peter 1.1 [interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/2_peter/1-1.htm). The modern defender is Daniel B. Wallace, *Granville Sharp's Canon and Its Kin* (Peter Lang, 2009), *Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics* (1996), and "Sharp's Rule Revisited: A Response to Stanley Porter," *JETS* 56.1 (2013) 79–91 [PDF](https://etsjets.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/03/files_JETS-PDFs_56_56-1_JETS_56-1_79-91_Wallace.pdf). Stanley Porter's reply, "Granville Sharp's Rule: A Response to Dan Wallace, or Why a Critical Book Review Should Be Left Alone," is in the same issue, pp. 93–100 [PDF](https://etsjets.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/03/files_JETS-PDFs_56_56-1_JETS_56-1_93-100_Porter.pdf).

**The answer.** First, the conditions. Sharp restricted the rule to nouns that are (a) singular, (b) personal, and (c) common, not proper names; Wallace adds (d) "native Greek constructions (i.e., not translation Greek)" (JETS 2013, p. 89). Without the restrictions the exceptions pile up: plurals (Matthew 16.21, "the elders and chief priests and scribes," one article, three groups), proper names ("the Paul and Barnabas" is never one man), impersonal nouns. The rule as defended is narrow by design.

Second, the exceptions inside the narrow rule. 2 Thessalonians 1.12, *tou Theou hēmōn kai Kyriou Iēsou Christou*, is the identical construction to 2 Peter 1.1, and the ASV and nearly every Trinitarian translation render it as two, "of our God and the Lord Jesus Christ." Brown's 1965 article rejects it as a reference to Jesus as God because "our God" there means the Father [PDF](https://theologicalstudies.net/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/26.4.1.pdf). Wallace replies that *Kyrios* functions there as a proper name. But if *Kyrios* can be a proper name in 2 Thessalonians 1.12, *Sōtēr* can function as a near-title in Titus 2.13. Ephesians 5.5, *tou Christou kai Theou*, one article over "Christ" and "God," is almost never rendered "of Christ who is God," because *Christos* is treated as a proper name. Each time, the restriction saves the rule by removing the example. Wallace also notes that the church fathers broke the rule "exclusively, as far as we have seen, when discussing the Trinity" (JETS 2013, pp. 88–89), which he attributes to doctrinal development; a critic would say Greek-speaking Christians did not hear the construction as a rule.

Third, the history. Georg Benedikt Winer (1789–1858), author of the century's dominant New Testament grammar, read Titus 2.13 as two persons, and in a footnote admitted that doctrinal conviction, not grammar, drove him: "Considerations derived from Paul's system of doctrine lead me to believe that σωτῆρος is not a second predicate" (Winer-Moulton, 1882, p. 162 n., as quoted by A. T. Robertson); defenders quote this as a confession of bias [discussion](https://www.aomin.org/aoblog/?p=6900). Less often quoted: the Revised Version (1881) and the ASV (1901), produced by orthodox Trinitarian committees, put the two-person reading in the *text* of both verses and the Sharp reading in the *margin* [ASV Titus 2](https://biblehub.com/asv/titus/2.htm). The RSV (1946–52) reversed this; ESV, NIV, NRSV follow the RSV.

Fourth, the honest tier. Brown places Titus 2.13 and 2 Peter 1.1 among five "probable" cases, below the three he calls clear (John 1.1, John 20.28, Hebrews 1.8), and calls the grammatical argument "not decisive" [PDF](https://theologicalstudies.net/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/26.4.1.pdf). That is where scholarship sits: possible and probably favored, not certain.

Fifth, context. Titus 1.4 has "God the Father and Christ Jesus our Saviour," two; 3.4–6, "God our Saviour... through Jesus Christ our Saviour," two Saviours. Titus 2.13 can be read as the appearing of the Father's glory in the Son's coming, as in Matthew 16.27, "in the glory of his Father." 2 Peter 1.2, the next verse, has "the knowledge of God and of Jesus our Lord," two, with two articles; 1.17 has the Father call Jesus "my beloved Son."

Sixth, even if the construction identifies one referent, grammar does not say what "God" means of that referent. Wallace himself claims the opposite force, that the two passages "unambiguously affirm that Jesus is called θεός" (JETS 2013, p. 91), but being called *theos* and being Yahweh are two different questions. Given Psalm 45.6 calls the Davidic king "God" (Part C, Hebrews 1.8) and Exodus 7.1 makes Moses "as God," one can grant the Sharp reading and still read *theos* as the honorific for God's appointed king. That is the fallback; state it as the fallback.

**The standard harmonization.** Stated correctly, the rule has no valid New Testament exceptions; both verses meet every condition; 2 Peter 1.11, 2.20, 3.2, 3.18 use the identical construction for "our Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ" with one undisputed referent; resistance since Winer has been theological; the ASV margin shows the translators knew the Sharp reading was possible.

**Why this matters.** The most technical argument in the debate and the most cited by people who have not read Wallace.

> **How to present this.** Do not say "Granville Sharp's rule is false" or "has many exceptions"; within its conditions it is well supported. Say: the rule is narrow by design; 2 Thessalonians 1.12 and Ephesians 5.5 are the same construction and not translated that way; the orthodox ASV committee put two persons in the text and Sharp in the margin; the leading Catholic surveyor called both verses "probable," not certain; both letters distinguish God from Jesus Christ in their opening verses. Then add that even granting one referent, "God" in the Psalm 45.6 sense is on the table.

### Comment length additions

**(1) The "I am" sayings version.**

The "I am" argument falls apart the moment you read John 9.9. The blind man says ego eimi, "I am he," the same two Greek words, and nobody thinks the beggar claimed to be God. In John 4.26 Yeshua says ego eimi to the woman at the well and the context is the Messiah, "I that speak unto thee am he." In John 18.5 the soldiers say they want Jesus of Nazareth and he answers "I am he," which is how anyone answers a question about who they are. The Isaiah passages people cite (43.10) are about Yahweh's servant and witness, and in John 8.28, the very verse quoted, Yeshua finishes the sentence with "I do nothing of myself, but as the Father taught me." The soldiers falling back in 18.6 is John showing nobody could arrest him until he gave himself up (10.18, 19.11), the way men fall before angels in Daniel 10.9. The man who wrote all these sayings also wrote John 17.3, where Yeshua calls the Father "the only true God," and John 20.17, where the risen Yeshua says "my God." Whatever ego eimi means, it cannot mean something that makes those verses false.

**(2) The Hebrews 1 version.**

Hebrews 1 is the chapter people use and it is the chapter that answers them. Yes, 1.3 calls the Son the radiance of God's glory and the stamp of his being, which is Wisdom of Solomon 7.26 language for God's wisdom, and a stamp is a copy, not the die. Yes, 1.6 has angels bow to him, but the one they bow to is "the firstborn," the title Psalm 89.27 gives David, and the quoted line is not even in the Hebrew Masoretic text of Deuteronomy 32.43. Yes, 1.10 applies Psalm 102 to him, but the author is quoting the Greek psalm, which reads differently from the Hebrew and can be read as God addressing a second Lord, and he does it to prove the Son outlasts the angels, not that he is the Father. Now read the rest of the chapter. 1.2, God "appointed" him heir. 1.4, he "became" better than the angels, "having inherited" the name. 1.8–9, "God, thy God, hath anointed thee," so the one called God has a God who anoints him. 5.5, "Christ also glorified not himself." 5.8–9, he "learned obedience" and was "made perfect." A being who is appointed, inherits, is anointed by his God, learns, and is made perfect is not the uncreated God. Hebrews 1 is about the exalted Son, and the one exalting him is the Father.

**(3) The "Old Testament Yahweh verses" version.**

Every one of these breaks the same way. Isaiah 43.11 says no saviour apart from Yahweh, and the same Bible calls Othniel, Ehud, and the judges "saviour" (Judges 3.9, 3.15, Nehemiah 9.27) because Yahweh raised them up, which is exactly what Acts 5.31 says about Yeshua, "God exalted him to be a Saviour." Joel 2.32 says call on the name of Yahweh; Peter quotes it in Acts 2.21 and explains it in 2.36, "God hath made him both Lord and Christ." Zechariah 12.10 says "look unto me" in the Masoretic text and then "mourn for him" in the same sentence; the JPS reads it "lament to Me about those who are slain," and John 19.37, the only New Testament quotation, changes it to "look on him." Micah 5.2 says the ruler's origins are "from the days of olam," the same phrase Micah 7.14 uses for the days of Moses, and the ruler comes forth "unto me" and feeds the flock in the name of "Yahweh his God" in 5.4. Genesis 19.24 repeats Yahweh's name the way Hebrew repeats names where English uses pronouns (1 Kings 8.1, Hosea 1.7), and the chapter says the visitors in Sodom were angels. In every case the Hebrew Bible itself has a human agent through whom Yahweh acts, and the New Testament says that is what Yeshua is.

## PART D. HOW THE DOCTRINE WAS BUILT

### D1. The gospels get higher in order of writing

Lay the four gospels in the order scholars date them and watch the claim grow. **Mark**, around 70, opens with the baptism, no birth story, no pre existence, Yeshua is a man on whom the Spirit descends and who does not know the day of the end. **Matthew and Luke**, 80 to 95, add birth narratives and push the Sonship back to conception, and Matthew edits Mark's "why callest thou me good." **John**, 90 to 110, opens with the Logos before creation and has "before Abraham was, I am." The farther from the events, the higher the Christology. Ehrman's *How Jesus Became God* (2014) is the clearest book length statement of this trajectory for a general reader, and Maurice Casey's *From Jewish Prophet to Gentile God* (1991) makes the same case with more technical detail. [Casey at openlibrary](https://openlibrary.org/search?q=casey+from+jewish+prophet+to+gentile+god).

### D3. The scribes kept upgrading the text

These are the places catalogued in Ehrman's *Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* where the manuscripts show the text being moved toward a divine Yeshua or away from an adopted one. Every one is a documented variant, not a theory.

- **1 Timothy 3.16.** "Who" to "God," two strokes added. See C10.
- **John 1.18.** "Only begotten Son" against "only begotten God." See C1.
- **1 John 5.7.** The Comma, inserted. See C11.
- **Matthew 24.36.** "Neither the Son," deleted from many copies. See A3.
- **Luke 3.22.** "Today I have begotten thee," the adoptionist voice, changed to "well pleased." See B3.
- **Luke 2.33 and 2.43.** The early manuscripts read "his father and his mother" and "his parents." Later scribes changed them to "Joseph and his mother" to protect the virgin birth. The KJV follows the altered reading.
- **Mark 1.1.** "The Son of God" is absent from the original hand of Sinaiticus and from Origen's citations, present in Vaticanus. Possibly added.
- **Luke 22.43 to 44.** The angel and the sweat like blood, absent from P75, Vaticanus and others, likely added to counter the view that Yeshua was not truly human, which shows the fight ran both ways.

The point of the list is not any one item. It is that on the single question of who Yeshua was, the people copying the New Testament had their thumbs on the scale, and we can see the thumbprints.

### D4. From Justin to Nicaea

**Justin Martyr, around 155.** The first Christian philosopher. In *Dialogue with Trypho* 56 and 61 he argues that Christ is "another God and Lord subject to the Maker of all things," "numerically distinct," begotten by the Father's will before creation. A second, subordinate God. That is what the most educated Christian of the second century believed, and it is not the Trinity. [Dialogue 56 at New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/01285.htm).

**Theophilus and Tertullian, 180 to 210.** The words *trias* and *trinitas* appear. Tertullian still says in *Against Praxeas* that "there was a time when the Son was not" in the sense that the Word was not yet begotten as Son, a statement that would later be condemned.

**Origen, around 230.** Teaches the eternal generation of the Son but also that the Son is subordinate, a "second God," and that prayer should not be addressed to Christ but to the Father through him (*On Prayer* 15).

**Arius, around 318.** A presbyter of Alexandria who taught that the Son was created by the Father and "there was when he was not." He was quoting Proverbs 8.22 and Colossians 1.15. Half the bishops of the East were with him.

**Nicaea, 325.** The emperor Constantine, not yet baptised, convened some 300 bishops at his summer palace to settle the dispute because it was disturbing the empire. The council adopted *homoousios*, "of one substance," a word not in scripture, proposed by the emperor's adviser Hosius of Cordoba. Eusebius of Caesarea, who was present, wrote to his church afterwards explaining with evident discomfort how he had been brought to sign. The decades after Nicaea saw the Arian side win repeatedly, with emperors on each side exiling bishops of the other, until Theodosius made the Nicene position the law of the empire in 380.

**Constantinople, 381.** Added the Holy Spirit to make the Trinity complete. **Chalcedon, 451.** Defined the two natures, so that the verses in Part A could be assigned to the human one.

> **How to present this.** Do not say Nicaea invented the deity of Christ. Ignatius said it in 110 and John's prologue is older than that. Say that Nicaea settled a dispute that had been open for three centuries, that both sides at Nicaea quoted scripture, that the deciding word is not in scripture, and that the decision was enforced by the state. That is all true and no historian will contest it.

### D5. The scholarship, both sides stated fairly

**For the trajectory view (low to high).** Bart Ehrman, *How Jesus Became God* (2014) and *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* (1993). James Dunn, *Christology in the Making* (1980), argues incarnation Christology is found only in John and that Paul's Christ is the second Adam. Maurice Casey, *From Jewish Prophet to Gentile God* (1991). James McGrath, *The Only True God* (2009), on how the New Testament stays inside Jewish monotheism. Raymond Brown, the Catholic count in C18.

**For early high Christology.** This is the view you will meet from informed opponents and it must be answered, not ignored. Larry Hurtado, *Lord Jesus Christ, Devotion to Jesus in Earliest Christianity* (2003), argues that Yeshua was worshipped alongside God within the first two decades, as shown by prayer to him, hymns, baptism in his name, and the nomina sacra. Richard Bauckham, *Jesus and the God of Israel* (2008), argues that 1 Corinthians 8.6 and Philippians 2 include Yeshua inside the "divine identity" of the one God from the start. [Hurtado at openlibrary](https://openlibrary.org/search?q=hurtado+lord+jesus+christ+devotion), [Bauckham at openlibrary](https://openlibrary.org/search?q=bauckham+jesus+and+the+god+of+israel).

**The answer to Hurtado and Bauckham.** Three points. First, early devotion is not the same as early deity. Hurtado shows that Gentile Christians in Paul's churches honoured Yeshua very early. He does not show that Yeshua claimed it or that the Jerusalem church held it, and the sources for the Jerusalem church (Acts speeches, James, the Ebionites) point the other way. Second, Hurtado's evidence is almost entirely Pauline. Our thesis is that Paul's Gentile churches diverged from the Hebrew believers. Early high devotion in Pauline churches confirms the divergence, it does not refute it. Third, Bauckham's "divine identity" reading of 1 Corinthians 8.6 has to explain why the same letter says "the head of Christ is God" (11.3) and that the Son will be "subjected" to God "that God may be all in all" (15.28). Paul can be read as distinguishing the one God from the one Lord, and the rest of the letter shows that he did.

## The strongest objections, stated fairly, and the answers

**"John 1.1 says the Word was God."** The Word was with the God and was divine, without the article. The Logos is God's own agent in Philo and Proverbs 8 without being a second person. The gospel's stated purpose (20.31) is that you believe he is the Son of God. And John 1.18 shows the scribes moving the text on this very question.

**"Thomas called him my Lord and my God."** Eleven verses earlier Yeshua said "my God and your God." Three verses later John says his purpose is that you believe Yeshua is the Son of God. In Thomas's Bible, men who stood in God's place were called gods (Psalm 82.6, Exodus 7.1).

**"Philippians 2 says he was in the form of God and Isaiah 45's knee bowing is applied to him."** Form of a servant is the same word, so it is about role. God exalts him, God gives him the name, and the confession is to the glory of the Father. On Dunn's reading it is Adam Christology throughout.

**"He forgave sins and accepted worship."** Matthew 9.8, the crowd glorified God for giving such authority to men. John 20.23, he gives the same authority to the disciples. *Proskyneō* is used of kings and in a parable of a servant bowing to a king.

**"Before Abraham was, I am."** The divine name in Exodus 3.14 LXX is *ho ōn*, not *egō eimi*. The healed blind man says *egō eimi* in 9.9. Pre existence is not deity, as the Ebionites' created archangel and John's own Logos show.

**"The limitation verses refer to his human nature."** That is the doctrine of 451 applied backwards to the texts. The texts themselves say the Son does not know, the Son can do nothing of himself, the Father is greater. A theory that assigns every low verse to one nature and every high verse to another cannot be tested against any verse.

**"The early church worshipped him within twenty years, so this was not a Nicene invention."** Agreed that it was not a Nicene invention, and this sheet does not say it was. The early worship Hurtado documents is in Paul's Gentile churches. The Jerusalem church and its heirs are documented by three hostile fathers as denying he was God.

**"The Ebionites were a heretical fringe."** They were the Torah keeping Jewish followers of Yeshua, heirs of James, using Matthew, in Palestine and Syria. The people who called them heretics were the Gentile church of Paul and Ignatius. Who the fringe was depends on which church you think is the original. That is the question this whole set of sheets is about.

**"The Trinity is a mystery beyond logic."** Then it cannot be argued from verses, and the other side should stop citing them. This sheet answers the verses.

**"You are using Muslim or Jehovah's Witness arguments."** The Ebionites made these arguments in the second century, Arius made them in the fourth, and Raymond Brown counted the verses in 1965 for a Catholic journal. Who else reads a verse correctly has no bearing on whether it is read correctly.

## Comment length versions

**Full comment.**

Yeshua said the Lord our God is one (Mark 12.29) and never once in Matthew, Mark or Luke said he was God. He said why call me good, none is good but God (Mark 10.18), and Matthew rewrote the line because it bothered him (Matthew 19.17). He said of the end that the Son does not know, only the Father (Mark 13.32), and scribes deleted "nor the Son" from Matthew 24.36, which is why the KJV lacks it. He prayed to "the only true God" and called himself the one God sent (John 17.3). After the resurrection he said "my God and your God" (John 20.17). Peter's first sermon called him a man approved of God whom God made Lord and Christ (Acts 2.22, 36). The oldest creed in the New Testament says he was appointed Son of God by the resurrection (Romans 1.4). Paul says the head of Christ is God and the Son will be subject to the Father so that God may be all in all (1 Corinthians 11.3, 15.28). The only verse that states the Trinity, 1 John 5.7, is in no Greek manuscript before the 1400s. "God was manifest in the flesh" in 1 Timothy 3.16 reads "he who" in the oldest copies, two strokes were added. Codex Bezae and every church father before 250 read the baptism voice as "today I have begotten thee." The Hebrew followers of Yeshua denied he was God, and Irenaeus, Eusebius and Epiphanius all record it. The word that settled it, homoousios, is in no verse of scripture and was chosen at a council called by a Roman emperor in 325. Show me where he said it.

**Short comment, his own words.**

Mark 12.29, the Lord our God is one. Mark 10.18, why callest thou me good, none is good but God. Mark 13.32, of that day no one knows, not the angels, not the Son, only the Father. John 17.3, thee the only true God, and him whom thou didst send. John 14.28, the Father is greater than I. John 20.17, after the resurrection, my God and your God. God does not have a God. He never said he was God. Find the verse.

**Short comment, the manuscripts.**

The Trinity verse, 1 John 5.7, is in no Greek manuscript before the fourteenth century and no critical edition prints it. "God was manifest in the flesh" in 1 Timothy 3.16 reads "he who" in Sinaiticus and Alexandrinus and the correction is visible on the page. "Nor the Son" was cut from Matthew 24.36 in the manuscripts behind the KJV. The baptism voice in Codex Bezae and in Justin, Clement and Origen says "today I have begotten thee," Psalm 2.7, a coronation. Every one of those moves goes the same direction. Scribes were making him God on the page.

**Short comment, the first believers.**

Acts 2.22, a man approved of God. Acts 2.36, God made him Lord and Christ. Acts 3.13, the God of Abraham glorified his servant Jesus. Romans 1.4, appointed Son of God by the resurrection. Philippians 2.9, God exalted him and gave him the name. 1 Corinthians 15.28, the Son himself shall be subjected, that God may be all in all. The men who knew him preached an exalted man. The Hebrew believers who kept his teaching for three centuries denied he was God, and Irenaeus, Eusebius and Epiphanius all say so. The first person to call him "our God" outright was Ignatius of Antioch in the year 110, from Paul's church.
