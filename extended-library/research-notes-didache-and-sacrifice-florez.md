# Research Notes, The Didache and Sacrifice (Pinned)

Status: pinned. Starting notes only, to be researched further before any of it is used.

### The question

The Didache, also called the Teaching of the Twelve Apostles, is treated as the oldest surviving Christian church manual and liturgy. Why does it say so little about sacrifice, and where it does use the word, what does it mean?

### What the text actually says

**Chapter 14, the Sunday gathering.** The community gathers on the Lord's day, breaks bread and gives thanks after confessing sins, "that your sacrifice may be pure." Anyone at odds with a neighbor may not join until reconciled, "so that your sacrifice may not be defiled." It then quotes Malachi 1:11 and 14, "In every place and time offer me a pure sacrifice." The Greek word is thusia, used twice for the gathering itself. There is no blood, no altar and no victim. The sacrifice is confession, reconciliation and a bread meal. Text and discussion at [CARM](https://carm.org/catholic/does-didache-teach-eucharist-as-sacrifice) and [Steven Nemes](https://snemes2.medium.com/the-eucharist-in-the-didache-d60ce7562887).

**Chapters 9 and 10, the thanksgiving prayers over the cup and bread.** The prayers never mention Yeshua's death, blood or atonement.

### What scholarship says

- Andrew McGowan, a scholar of early Christian meals, notes the Didache can use sacrifice language for the Eucharistic meal "despite the lack of knowledge of, or at least explicit interest in, the death of Jesus or themes of atonement and blood sacrifice in the document," and that it extends cultic language to a "meatless but bread-centred Eucharistic meal." [McGowan, Eucharist and Sacrifice (III)](http://abmcg.blogspot.com/2009/11/eucharist-and-sacrifice-iii-septuagint.html)
- A study titled "Pure Sacrifice in Didache 14 as Jewish Christian Exegesis" reads the Malachi quotation as following Hellenistic Jewish practice, where Malachi served as a proof text that a pure offering made outside the temple, in the Diaspora, brings glory to God's Name. It ties this to the prayer in 10:2 about the Name dwelling in the hearts of the community. [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/277018453_Pure_Sacrifice_in_Didache_14_as_Jewish_Christian_Exegesis)
- Dating and layers. One source places the final form around 125 AD, possibly in Alexandria, assembled from two earlier documents that may date to the apostolic era ([Crossroads Initiative](https://www.crossroadsinitiative.com/media/articles/didache_eucharist_teaching_twelve_apostles/)). Other estimates go as early as 70 to 80 AD ([intertextual.bible](https://intertextual.bible/text/didache-14.3/irenaeus-against-heresies-book-4-17.5)).

### Working theories (Christian's, not yet tested)

1. It is a Hebrew-believer document in which sacrifice had already become prayer, repentance and reconciliation, in line with Hosea 6:6.
1. Its meal comes from before or outside Paul's atonement theology. A first-century liturgy whose Eucharist has no death of Yeshua in it does not match "this is my body, broken for you."
1. Even if the text has later layers, the atonement theology never got written into the meal prayers, and that absence is evidence in its own right.

### Expected pushback

Catholic and Orthodox writers use chapter 14 as early proof that the Eucharist is a sacrifice ([The Eastern Church](https://www.theeasternchurch.com/saints/didache-full-text-with-commentary)). The answer is that the text defines the sacrifice as confession and reconciliation, not an offering of a body.
