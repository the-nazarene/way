## Summary

Wescoe asks whether Mark's Gospel is fundamentally at odds with John's, and he argues that it is. Working through a series of paired passages, he contends that Mark consistently defames Jesus, the apostles, Jesus's family and the wider movement, and he calls Mark an "antichrist" gospel. He argues that the two Gospels give incompatible accounts of how disciples were recruited (John: Bethany beyond the Jordan, disciples of John the Baptist; Mark: Galilean fishermen by the Sea of Galilee). He also contends that Mark reverses John's portrait of the disciples' understanding, faithfulness and power, and of the family, the Baptist, the messianic secret, the demons and the Gentiles.

His positive framework rests on Acts 15, a report attributed to Hegesippus, and Galatians 2:9, which together he uses to argue that James the brother of Jesus led the Jerusalem church with Cephas and John as pillars. He presents Mark's sons-of-Zebedee material as a deliberate distortion of this picture. For the Passover question he compares Mark 14:12 with John 19:14 and argues that Mark keeps Jesus from being slain on the preparation day, which he reads as a sign that Mark rejects the Lamb of God theology.

The talk is a single presenter's monologue with no guest. It ends with the summary claim that Mark's purpose is to spread "lies and deceptions" about Jesus, his apostles, his family and the movement, and it links this to 1 and 2 John's warnings about antichrists. Most of the argument is close reading of selected verses, and the talk offers few sources beyond the texts themselves.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Two incompatible recruitment stories
Wescoe sets John 1 (Andrew and another disciple of John the Baptist follow Jesus at Bethany beyond the Jordan; Jesus then sets out for Galilee) against Mark 1 (Simon, Andrew, James and John are called from their boats on the Sea of Galilee). He notes that John never calls any of them fishermen and that he says the first 20 chapters of John contain no mention of fishing. He rejects harmonization: "these are two completely different conflicting stories." He does not address the possibility that the two accounts are compressed or selective in different ways.

### 2. The pillars, and the sons of Zebedee who want power
Wescoe argues that Acts 15 (James delivering the ruling), a report attributed to Hegesippus, and Galatians 2:9 (James, Cephas and John "those with the reputation of being pillars") show that James led the church and that Peter and John were senior figures. He says this picture appears "throughout most of the earliest literature," though only Hegesippus is named. In Mark, he says, James and John are reduced to "Galilean fishermen, sons of Zebedee," and then appear seeking seats at Jesus's right and left. He reads Jesus's reply ("These seats belong to those for whom they have been prepared") as a rebuke of power-seeking. He presents the disciples in Mark as "power-hungry" and says Mark wants the reader to see the apostles as illegitimate. (Note for checking: the sons-of-Zebedee request is usually numbered Mark 10:35-37 in standard references, while Wescoe cites it as 9:34-35.)

### 3. Peter's confession and the rebuke
Wescoe sets John 6:68-69, where Peter confesses Jesus as "the holy one of God," against Mark 8:29, where Peter says "You are the Christ." In Mark, Jesus then orders silence, predicts suffering, and rebukes Peter, who had himself rebuked Jesus. Jesus answers with "Get behind me, Satan." Wescoe reads this as part of a pattern in which Mark's Jesus is aligned with the demons and "lord of the demons" (he uses the spelling "Be elabub," meaning Beelzebul), while the human disciples are repeatedly rebuked.

### 4. Friends in John, dullards in Mark
Wescoe contrasts John 15:15 ("I have called you friends") and John 16:30 (the disciples say they understand and believe) with a run of Markan rebukes: Mark 4:13, 6:52, 7:18 and 8:17-18. He claims that Mark's disciples also fail at exorcism (Mark 9:18-19) and that Mark's Jesus sometimes fails at healing (the blind man who sees "trees," and the town where Jesus "couldn't heal anyone"). He mentions the common reading that Mark's disciples model patience toward failing followers, then rejects it as the opposite of John's portrait.

### 5. Desertion at the arrest, and the silent women
Wescoe contrasts John 18:8-9 (Jesus says "let these men go" and takes the blame) with Mark 14:46 and 14:50 (the arrest, then everyone flees). He contrasts John 20:18 (Mary Magdalene announces the risen Lord) with Mark 16:8 (the women say nothing "in their fear"). He treats the longer ending as a later addition and reads Mark's shorter ending as the author's intent. He argues that "the women didn't see Jesus" in Mark.

### 6. Family, the Baptist, and "no honor"
Wescoe says that in John 10:20 the charge of insanity comes from opponents, while in Mark 3:21 it comes from Jesus's family. He asks how this fits with James being put in charge by Jesus. He compares John 4:44 (a prophet has no honor in his own hometown) with Mark 6:4, which adds "among his relatives and in his own household," and says Mark also moves the saying to Galilee. He compares Mark 1:6 (John the Baptist in camel's hair with a leather belt, eating locusts and wild honey) with John's omission of that detail. He treats this as part of the same effort to make the movement look like "fur coat" ascetics rather than the "vegan Nazarenes" the earlier evidence supposedly describes. He also cites John 1:21 ("I am not Elijah") against Mark 9:13, where Jesus says Elijah has come. He does not give a source for the Nazarene dietary claim.

### 7. The messianic secret, parables, demons and Gentiles
Wescoe contrasts John 18:20 ("I said nothing in secret") with the many Markan silence commands (1:34, 1:44, 3:12, 5:43, 7:36, 8:30, 9:9). He reads Mark 4:11-12 as saying that parables are used so that outsiders "may be ever seeing but not perceiving," so they will not "turn and be forgiven." He argues that in Mark the true confessors are demons (1:23-25; 5:7-13), whose exorcisms he treats as evidence that Mark's Jesus is aligned with the demonic. He sets the Samaritan village of John 4 against the Syrophoenician woman of Mark 7:27 ("not right to take the children's bread and toss it to the dogs") and John 10:10 against the Gerasene pigs.

He ends with the Passover timeline: John 1:29 and 19:14 (Jesus slain on the preparation day, the Lamb of God) against Mark 14:12, where the disciples prepare the Passover and Jesus eats it. He says Mark has "Simon of Cyrene crucified instead of Jesus," which the transcript does not quote from Mark and which should be verified in Mark 15:21.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Harmonizing John and Mark:** Wescoe says the recruitment, relationship and family accounts cannot be reconciled, and rejects the attempt to combine them.
- **Mark's disciples as patient models:** He rejects the reading that Mark's rebukes of the disciples are a lesson in God's patience with failing followers, arguing it is the "exact opposite" of John.
- **Mark's messianic secret as a literary device:** He treats the silence commands as evidence that Mark wants the messiahship concealed, not as a narrative feature to be explained otherwise.
- **Mark's Jesus as the same figure as John's:** He argues the two Gospels present different Jesuses, and says Mark's Jesus is not God.
- **Mark's Passover timeline as harmless:** He reads Mark 14:12 as deliberately denying Jesus's death as the Lamb of God on the preparation day.
