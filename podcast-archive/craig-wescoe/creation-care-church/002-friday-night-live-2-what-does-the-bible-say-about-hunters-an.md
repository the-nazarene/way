## Summary

The talk asks what the Bible says about hunting and hunters, and whether the practice fits God's design for people. The host, Craig, argues from Genesis, Leviticus, Malachi, Luke and the Psalms. He says God made humans to be gardeners whose food is "every herb that yields seed" (Genesis 1:29), so hunting is a deviation from the original design. He identifies Nimrod and Esau as the only two hunters named in Scripture and reads both as portrayed negatively: Nimrod founds Babel/Babylon, which he treats as the opposite of God's city, and God says of Esau, "I have hated" (Malachi 1:2-3). He then reads the Leviticus 17:13-14 blood rule as a restriction that cannot be fully met, since some blood always remains in meat, and whose penalty is being "cut off" from God's people. He closes with God's compassion (Psalm 145:8-9; Luke 6:36) as the standard for how humans should treat animals.

The second half is a question-and-answer session. Craig answers that Isaac's preference for Esau's game shows a fleshly distortion, not divine approval, and that Jesus' reference to Isaac in the kingdom (Luke 13:28) does not endorse Esau's hunting. He reads Genesis 9 not as a divine concession to meat-eating but as a warning, since Noah acted "by sight rather than faith." He also links animal sacrifice's blood to the blood of Jesus as the effective atonement. He says openly that some points are "debatable," and promises a future talk on Genesis 9. The talk ends with announcements about the church's events, book club and prayer group, and a closing prayer.

## Key Teachings

### Humans were designed as gardeners, and their original diet was plants
Craig starts from Genesis 2:15, where the Lord God puts the man in the garden "to tend and keep it," and argues that "gardening is the task that god designed us for," in contrast to the idea that hunting is the mark of human dominance. He then reads Genesis 1:29 ("every herb that yields seed... to you it shall be for food") as God's original diet, and cites Genesis 1:31 ("very good") as the standard. On this reading, killing animals for food is "a deviation from god's original purpose and design." In the Q&A he says plainly that "scripture is clear on this point" that a garden diet should be eaten whenever possible.

### Nimrod and Esau: the two named hunters are portrayed negatively
Craig says there are "only two hunters that are mentioned by name in scripture." On Nimrod (Genesis 10:8-10), he notes that "the beginning of his kingdom was babel," and then links Babel to Babylon, which Revelation 17:5-6 presents as a city opposed to God. He concludes that the little said about Nimrod "seems to be kind of on the side of not good." He adds that "mighty hunter before the lord" means "in the lord's presence," not that Nimrod worships God, and he compares it to Satan appearing before the Lord in the Job narrative.

On Esau, he reads Genesis 25:27-28 (Isaac "loved esau because he ate of his game") against Malachi 1:2-3 ("jacob i have loved but esau i have hated"). He does not claim the text gives a reason, but argues that because Esau's hunting is one of the few things known about him, it was "at least partly the reason why god did not approve of of him." He reads Isaac's preference as a fleshly distortion: "his desires the flesh were causing him to not see things the way gods saw things."

### Leviticus 17:13-14: a hunting rule that cannot be fully kept
Craig reads Leviticus 17:13-14 as setting two conditions on hunting: the animal must be one of the clean animals, and its blood must be poured out and covered with dust. Breaking this brings the penalty that the person "shall be cut off" from God's people. His central argument is that the blood requirement is impossible to meet in practice: "it's impossible physically to drain all of the blood out" of a carcass. He concludes that the only way to fully keep the rule is to eat no flesh at all, which returns the reader to Genesis 1:29. He presents this as "an impossible restriction" that he thinks points toward the garden diet.

### God's character of mercy and compassion sets the standard for how we treat animals
Craig cites Psalm 145:8-9 ("the lord is gracious and full of compassion... his tender mercies are over all his works") and Luke 6:36 ("be merciful just as your father"). He argues that humans made in God's image should show that mercy to creation, "including animals," and that killing a defenseless animal when other options exist is not consistent with God's character. He allows only one exception, a "dire circumstance where we're just starving to death."

### Genesis 9 is a warning, not a divine concession to meat-eating
In the Q&A, Paula asks whether God "makes concessions to evil" in allowing animal killing. Craig says the reading that God changed his mind is "the common view," but he disagrees. He points to Genesis 8:21, where God says he will not curse the ground again, and to Genesis 6:5 ("the imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth"). He notes that Noah built the altar without a command from God, and reads this as Noah "walking by sight not by faith." Genesis 9:4-5 forbidding the eating of blood is, for him, a foreshadowing of the atonement. He admits this is "a kind of debatable topic" and says he will address it in a future talk.

### Blood, sacrifice and atonement
Answering a question about why blood atones for sin, Craig reads the life-in-the-blood principle (Genesis 9:4-5) as the basis for animal sacrifice, which he calls "a shadow" of Jesus. He says the blood of Jesus was "the actual atonement" that "covered our sins." This is the only place in the talk where Jesus' death is discussed directly.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Humans were given dominion to hunt and use animals as they please.** Craig raises this as the popular view in his opening, then argues that the original design is gardening and a plant-based diet (Genesis 1:29), with dominion read as stewardship under God's character of mercy.
- **Genesis 9 shows God changing his mind to allow meat-eating.** Craig says this is "the common view" and argues instead that Genesis 8:21 and Noah's unrequested altar show displeasure, and that the passage is a warning.
- **Draining the blood makes hunted meat acceptable under Leviticus 17.** Craig argues the blood rule cannot be fully met because some blood always remains in the flesh, so the only complete obedience is to eat no animal flesh.
- **Isaac's approval of Esau's hunting shows God's approval of hunting.** Craig replies that Isaac's evaluation is the reverse of God's, that the text ties it to Isaac's taste for game, and that Isaac's inclusion in the kingdom (Luke 13:28) does not endorse his preference, since righteous biblical figures had shortcomings.
