## Summary

Wescoe asks what it means that humanity was made in God's image and given "dominion" (Genesis 1:26-28), and whether that dominion licenses people to do whatever they want with animals. He answers that dominion is delegated authority to carry out God's will, not permission to exploit. His main evidence is a sequence of readings: Genesis 1:29 assigns a plant-based diet immediately after the dominion statement; Ephesians 1:21-22 and Mark 10:45 present Christ's rule as service, which he offers as the model for human dominion; 1 John 4:16 and 1 Corinthians 16:14 make love the governing character; Genesis 2:15 and 2:20 cast humans as garden keepers who know animals by name; and 2 Corinthians 3:18 and Romans 13:10 describe a transformation back toward God's image that shows up as "doing no harm" to neighbors, animals included.

The second half is a live question-and-answer session. Wescoe answers questions about what happened to fish in the flood (his own speculation), whether factory-farmed animals have names, and whether God changed his mind in Genesis 9. On the last point he argues from Genesis 3, 6, 8 and 9 and from 1 John 4:18 that the fear of animals in Genesis 9:2 is a product of the fall, not a new divine plan, and that the covenant with the animals implies they were never mere food. ## Key Teachings

### Dominion is delegated authority bounded by God's instructions
Wescoe starts from Genesis 1:26-28, which people use to justify treating animals as they please. He rejects that reading. Genesis 1:29, he says, comes right after the dominion statement and assigns fruit, seed and plants as food, so "dominion" cannot mean "eat or kill whatever you like." He calls dominion "authority to do God's will," with God's instructions about "what to do and what not to do." He presents the diet as the first thing God says to do with that authority.

### Christ's dominion as the model for human dominion
Wescoe says Christ is the image of God, "God in the flesh," so Christ's dominion should guide the human kind. He cites Ephesians 1:21-22 (Christ over all principality and power) and Mark 10:45 ("the Son of man did not come to be served but to serve"). The lesson he draws is that human rule over animals and the earth should look like humble service, meaning "taking care of the needs of everyone," not the will of the animals. He adds that this service is submission to God's will, not to whatever the one served wants.

### Love as God's character, and dominion exercised in love
Using 1 John 4:16 ("God is love") and 1 Corinthians 16:14 ("let all that you do be done with love"), he argues that bearing God's image means bearing God's loving character. Dominion over animals should therefore be exercised in love. He then reads Galatians 5:22-23 as a fuller list of God's character (joy, peace, kindness, goodness, gentleness, self-control), and asks whether people's treatment of animals reflects those fruits. He sets this against Philippians 3:19 and the danger of letting "the belly" become one's god.

### The garden keeper, not the tyrant: Genesis 2 and the naming of animals
Genesis 2:15 ("to tend and keep it") and 2:20 (Adam naming the animals) describe the pre-fall relationship that Wescoe takes as the model. Humans are to be keepers and tenders, and animals are to be known "as someone not just something." He quotes Sarah Withrow King that being made in God's image "is not a license to conquer and kill it is a charge to keep." In the Q&A, he explains that farmers tell children not to name livestock because naming creates attachment, and says factory farming is "an opposite example" of the intended relationship.

### Transformation back into the image, and "no harm to a neighbor"
Wescoe reads 2 Corinthians 3:18 as a present process in which believers "are being transformed into the same image," powered by the Spirit and not by human effort. He cites Romans 13:10 ("love does no harm to a neighbor") and widens "neighbor" to include animals, since God made the earth for humans and animals together. He proposes a practical test: "am i harming animals," and if so, "what can i do to stop causing this harm," with a plant-based diet as the main alternative. He calls the Hebrew word for dominion one that "means to rule over," associated with kings, and says rule should follow God's character. The Hebrew word itself is not clearly captured in the captions.

### Genesis 9 is not God changing his mind
Asked about the flood and Genesis 9:1-2 (animals given "into your hand" with fear and dread), Wescoe rejects the reading that God switched from a plant diet to permitting meat after the flood. His arguments are these:
- Fear enters the story only after the fall (Genesis 3:7-10), so fear over animals is a fallen condition, not God's plan.
- 1 John 4:18 says "there is no fear in love," so a loving God would not want fear imposed as policy.
- Genesis 8:21 has God promising not to curse the ground or destroy every creature again, while acknowledging that human imagination is evil, which Wescoe reads as a continuation of the pre-flood pattern (Genesis 6:5, 6:11).
- The covenant in Genesis 9 includes the animals, which he says would make no sense if they were "just menu items."
He backs the principle of divine constancy with Malachi 3:6 and Hebrews 13:8. On the fish question, he offers his own speculation that sea creatures survived the flood in the water and were not taken into the ark.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Dominion means the right to do whatever one wants with animals.** Wescoe says dominion is authority to do God's will, shaped by the diet God assigns in Genesis 1:29 and by Christ's servant model.
- **After the flood God changed his mind and permitted eating animals (Genesis 9).** Wescoe argues the fear and killing language reflects the fall and the pre-flood pattern of human evil, not a new divine plan, and that the animals' inclusion in the covenant argues against treating them as "menu items."

- **Christology:** Wescoe calls Jesus "the image of God," "God in the flesh," and says Christ's dominion is the model for human rule. The argument rests on Pauline and Johannine passages, not on the Gospels' own account of Jesus's self-understanding.
