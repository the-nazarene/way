## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks whether animals will be in heaven, and whether the animals people have loved, such as their dogs and cats, will be seen again. He answers yes, and says the case comes from the whole Bible rather than a few verses. He builds it in stages. Animals are part of God's plan from the start (Genesis 1:24-25), and they appear in the final peaceful kingdom (Isaiah 11:6-9). Ecclesiastes 3:19 says humans have "no advantage over beasts" in their shared breath of life, so he reasons that animals share the same hope. He then argues that the Noah covenant of Genesis 9 includes animals, with "animals mentioned five times," and that Hosea 2:18 gives a further promise to animals. He treats the story of Balaam's donkey (Numbers 22) as his strongest evidence, presenting the donkey as a model of "christ-like selfless love." He finishes with Revelation 5:13, where "every creature" praises God, and concludes that many of the same animals will be resurrected.

In the Q&A, Craig concedes that the Bible does not say which animals will be in heaven or why. He says the practical question is how to show love to animals now. He also says a secular "Rainbow Bridge" idea has no biblical basis, but he links the rainbow of Genesis 9 to God's covenant with animals. He closes by urging a reading of Scripture that treats animals as recipients of God's promises, and he presents his conclusions as his own reasoning ("that's why i believe"), not as settled doctrine.

## Key Teachings

### Animals are part of God's plan from the beginning and at the end
Craig starts with Genesis 1:24-25, where God makes the "living creatures" and calls the creation "very good." He argues that if animals were in God's original plan, they should also be in its completion. He then turns to Isaiah 11:6-9, which he calls his favorite passage on animals, describing the wolf and lamb, leopard and goat, and a child playing by the cobra's hole. He reads this as a picture of the heavenly kingdom with peaceful human-animal relations.

### Ecclesiastes 3:19 and the shared breath of life
Craig cites Ecclesiastes 3:19 ("man has no advantage over beasts") and links it to God breathing life into Adam (he gives no verse for this). His reasoning is that if humans and animals share the same breath and the same fate, animals must share the same hope of an afterlife. He treats this as an inference from the text rather than an explicit promise.

### The Noah covenant includes animals
In Genesis 9:15 and the surrounding verses, Craig argues that God's covenant after the Flood is made with "every living creature," not only with Noah and his descendants. He says the repetition is deliberate and that animals are "mentioned five times" as recipients. He reasons that if God makes a covenant promise to animals, they would also need to exist to receive its fulfillment, just as humans do with the promise of the heavenly kingdom. He also points out that the rainbow in Genesis 9:13 is the sign of this covenant "between me and the whole earth."

### Hosea 2:18 as a further promise to animals
Craig reads Hosea 2:18, where God says he will "make a covenant for them with the beasts of the field" and abolish bow and sword so that "all may lie down in safety." He presents this as a second covenant promise to animals about a future time, which again implies that animals have the same hope as humans.

### Balaam's donkey as the strongest case
Craig calls this "probably one of the most compelling reasons." He summarizes Numbers 22: the donkey turns aside three times to avoid an angel of the Lord, Balaam beats her each time, and then the Lord opens her mouth (Numbers 22:28). The angel says the donkey saved Balaam's life and that Balaam would have been killed otherwise (Numbers 22:32-33). Craig calls the donkey a hero who returns evil with good, "exactly what god calls all of us to do," and says he is looking forward to meeting her in the kingdom.

### Revelation 5:13 and the praise of all creatures
Craig reads Revelation 5:13, where "every creature in heaven and on earth and under the earth and in the sea" gives praise. He argues that the verse names every kind of animal, so it shows that all animals will praise God in the end times. He reasons that if they do so, they should also be in the kingdom. He acknowledges that some may suggest God will create new animals instead, and answers that Balaam's donkey and the animals of Revelation make this unlikely.

### Diet, gentleness and the question of which animals
Craig uses Romans 14:17 ("the kingdom of God is not food and drink but righteousness and peace and joy in the holy spirit") to argue against eating animals for taste. He cites Genesis 1:29 as God's original diet and says that following it avoids taking "that joy from the animals." In the Q&A, he cites Matthew 11:29 ("gentle and lowly in heart") and Galatians 5:22-23 (gentleness as a fruit of the Spirit) and condemns factory farms, slaughterhouses, circuses, zoos and aquariums as places where animals are treated as profit. On which animals are in heaven, he says "we don't know for sure," and he adds that some verses use a word for "wild" that is also used for "evil," which he suggests could complicate the question. He does not resolve this.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Animals do not survive death and have no hope of an afterlife.** Craig argues from Ecclesiastes 3:19 and the Genesis 9 covenant that animals share in the breath of life and in the promise, so they share the same hope.
- **The Noah covenant is only with humans.** Craig says Genesis 9 names animals as recipients and that translation and headings in many Bibles obscure this.
- **Heaven will be filled with newly created animals instead of the animals we knew.** Craig argues, from Balaam's donkey and Revelation 5:13, that many of the same animals will be resurrected.
- **A "Rainbow Bridge" where pets wait for their owners is biblical.** Craig says the Rainbow Bridge has no biblical basis, though he links the rainbow of Genesis 9 to God's covenant with animals.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Kingdom on earth:** Craig reads Isaiah 11 and Romans 14:17 as pictures of a peaceful kingdom that includes animals, and he links this to the prayer "your kingdom come your will be done on earth as it is in heaven."
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** The talk is about afterlife for animals. Craig argues that God's ability to "resurrect and bring back to life" extends to animals, but he does not address hell.
- **Torah, diet and nonviolence:** Craig uses Genesis 1:29 (the original plant diet), Romans 14:17 and the gentleness passages to argue against killing animals for food and against factory farms and similar practices. The talk applies Old Testament texts to diet, not the Torah's ritual law.
- **What Jesus taught:** Craig cites Matthew 11:29 as Jesus' attitude of gentleness and connects it to how believers should treat animals. He does not address Jesus' view of animals directly.
- **How Scripture is read:** Craig's method is to gather texts from across the Bible and treat them as a single witness. He acknowledges the limits of this approach ("we don't know for sure").
