## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks whether believers should judge people who harm animals, and whether the common objection "Jesus said not to judge" settles the matter. He starts with Matthew 7:1 and Luke 6:37, arguing that the Greek *krinō* in these passages means something closer to "condemn" than to "give moral instruction." On his reading, judgment is wishing punishment or harm on someone for a wrong, and forgiveness is its opposite. He supports this with the Sermon on the Mount, Matthew 18 (the unforgiving servant), and Jesus's harsh language toward religious leaders in Matthew 23.

He then separates condemnation from admonishing. Admonishing aims at repentance, and he presents the prophets, Jesus, and John the Baptist as doing this. He cites Luke 15:7 and 2 Peter 3:9 to argue that God delights when a wrongdoer repents, and he uses Saul's conversion in Acts 9 as a model for hoping that someone who harms animals might become an advocate against harm. He grounds the animal question in Genesis 9:15 (God's covenant including "every living creature"), Psalm 145:9, and Isaiah 11:6-9 (the peaceable kingdom), and he reads Jesus's mother-hen image as evidence that animals can love.

The talk ends with a Q&A. Craig says believers should both forgive and admonish, should first examine their own lives, and should welcome animal advocates rather than dismissing their concern. He takes Romans 14:3 as a common misuse, arguing from Romans 14:15 that Paul's actual conclusion is that eating which grieves a brother is "no longer walking in love." He closes by announcing that next week's topic is whether animals have souls, and the session ends in prayer.

## Key Teachings

### Judgment means condemnation, not moral instruction
Craig argues that Matthew 7:1 ("Judge not that you be not judged") does not forbid all evaluation of conduct. He says *krinō* carries the sense of judging someone guilty and assigning punishment, and he suggests "condemn" as a closer English word. He reads Luke 6:37 ("Judge not, and you shall not be judged. Condemn not...") as equating judgment with condemnation, contrasted with forgiveness. He notes that the Sermon on the Mount is itself full of instruction about how to live, so he concludes that "judge" cannot mean "tell people how to live."

### Forgiveness, the unforgiving servant, and divine reciprocity
He cites Matthew 6:14-15 to argue that forgiveness is reciprocal in God's economy, then reads the parable of the unforgiving servant (Matthew 18:21-35) at length. His application: believers stand in the position of the forgiven servant, and failing to forgive others forfeits the forgiveness they have received. He treats this as the main point of the passage.

### Admonishing aims at repentance, unlike judgment
Using Colossians 1:28 (NKJV and NIV readings), Craig distinguishes admonishing from judgment. Judgment wants the wrongdoer repaid; admonishing wants the wrongdoer to stop and turn. He ties this to "repent, for the kingdom of God is at hand," which he attributes to both John the Baptist and Jesus. He counts wishing a person to "burn in hell" as judgment, and he calls rejoicing over a hunter's accidental death judgment too. He draws on 2 Peter 3:9 and Luke 15:7, 10 to say God and the angels rejoice at repentance, not at punishment.

### Saul's conversion as the model for hoping for change
Acts 9:1-2 and 9:20-21 describe Saul persecuting the church and then preaching Christ in Damascus. Craig presents this as the "best case scenario" for anyone who harms animals: not merely that the harm stops, but that the person becomes "just as zealous for preventing that harm" as they once were for causing it. He notes Paul is the author of "half of the New Testament, or about that much."

### Animals in God's covenant, compassion, and the kingdom
Craig argues from Genesis 9:15 that God makes a covenant with animals, which he takes as evidence that God cares about them. He cites Psalm 145:8-9 ("his tender mercies are over all his works") as showing God's compassion over all creation, and Isaiah 11:6-9 as a picture of God's intended peaceable kingdom. He reads Jesus's comparison of his love to a mother hen (the Jerusalem saying, passage not identified in the captions) as proof that animals are capable of love, and therefore should not be treated as mere commodities.

### Romans 14 is not a "don't judge my diet" text
Craig reads Romans 14:2-3 as commonly quoted to shut down diet discussion, then argues that Paul's conclusion comes at 14:15: "if your brother is grieved because of your food, ... you are no longer walking in love." He treats this as a reversal of the popular reading. He says he has a separate talk on Romans 14 in preparation.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus's "judge not" forbids telling people how to live.** Craig says judgment in Matthew 7:1 and Luke 6:37 means condemning someone to punishment, and that the Sermon on the Mount itself is full of instruction.
- **Judgment just means being impolite or harsh.** He argues that Jesus himself used harsh language when situations required it, so "judgment" is not simply about tone; it is about wishing punishment on someone.
- **God doesn't care about animals, so animals don't matter.** Craig cites Genesis 9:15, Psalm 145:9, and Isaiah 11:6-9 to argue the opposite.
- **Romans 14 means "don't tell me about my diet."** He says Paul's conclusion at 14:15 is that causing a brother to be grieved over food is a failure of love.
