## Summary

Craig Wescoe, presenting a "Friday Night Live" session for Creation Care Church, asks whether animals have a soul. He frames the question as one for the Bible rather than personal opinion, and asks listeners to follow along with their own Bibles. He carries the whole 25-30 minute talk himself, then takes questions from the chat for about 30 minutes.

His main argument rests on word study. He says the Hebrew *nephesh* (translated "living being" or "soul" in Genesis 2:7) is used of animals in Genesis 1:30, and that the Greek *psyche*-equivalent in 1 Corinthians 15:45 maps onto the same concept. He cites Theodore Hiebert's *The Yahwist's Landscape* for the claim that English translators have "muddled" a distinction between humans and animals that the Hebrew does not make. From there he moves to Ecclesiastes 3:19-20, which he reads as saying humans and animals share one breath and one fate, and to God's covenant with the animals in Genesis 9, Isaiah 11, and Romans 8:19, which he reads as evidence that animals have a hope of the kingdom. He concludes that humans should "regard the soul of the animal" (Proverbs 12:10), which he applies to zoos, circuses, horse racing, factory farming, and meat-eating.

In the Q&A he admits uncertainty on whether animals need to repent or will be judged, and on which animals will be in the kingdom. He treats consciousness as not the criterion for having a soul, and ends by previewing next week's talk on whether God changed his mind at the flood and the diet of Genesis 1:29 versus Genesis 9.

## Key Teachings

### 1. "Nephesh" and "soul" are the same word across animals and humans
Craig argues that *nephesh* is "consistently translated as either being, life, soul, something like that" (03:06). He cites Genesis 2:7 for humans, then Genesis 1:30 (read from the JPS translation) for animals, which describes "a living soul." He says the Greek word in 1 Corinthians 15:45 (*psyche*, which he spells "suche") corresponds to the Hebrew, and that Matthew 22:37 quoting Deuteronomy 6:5 shows the same mapping ("with all your soul"). He reads Ezekiel 33:5 as using the same word for humans, which he takes to show "there's no distinction here about a human or an animal when it comes to a soul" (07:02). He quotes Hiebert on translators creating a human/animal distinction "where none exists in the Hebrew text" (07:43).

### 2. Shared breath and shared fate in Ecclesiastes
Craig reads Ecclesiastes 3:19 (NIV): "Humans have no advantage over animals," and "All have the same breath." He takes this to mean the animal soul "evidently has the same fate" as the human soul (12:33). In the Q&A he links Ecclesiastes 3:20 ("All go to one place... all return to dust") with Genesis 3:19, and says that apart from God's breath "we're just dust" (27:11). He concludes that this shared breath means animals should not be deprived of the breath of life, and that "love your neighbor as yourself" extends to "our animal neighbors" (28:04).

### 3. God's covenant with animals and the hope of the kingdom
Craig argues from Genesis 9:9-10 that God makes a covenant "with every living creature," which he says is stated five times. He reasons that you do not make promises to objects, so animals must be "someones" to God (15:38). He pairs this with Isaiah 11:9 and Isaiah 11:6-8 (animals at peace, a child leading them) to argue that animals share a hope of living with God in the kingdom (16:22, 17:10). He reads Romans 8:19 ("the earnest expectation of the creation eagerly waits") to mean all creation, not just humanity, is waiting for the revealing of the children of God (18:48).

### 4. Practical application: "regard the soul of the animal" and "live peaceably with all"
Proverbs 12:10 is central here: "A righteous man regards the life of his animal" (he prefers the BSB's "regardeth the life, nephesh, of his animal"). Craig asks listeners whether zoos, aquariums, circuses, horse racing, and factory farms "regard the soul of the animal" (10:57). He uses Romans 12:18 ("so far as it depends on you, live peaceably with all") and notes that the word "people" is not in the Greek (20:30). He asks whether eating animals is "living peaceably" when Genesis 1:29 gave fruit and vegetation as food (21:21). He cites Psalm 36:6 (God "preserve[s] man and beast") as a call to imitate God's preservation of animals (22:04).

### 5. Animals, repentance, and judgment: tentative answers
Craig says he does not know whether animals must repent or be "blameless," but has held "for several years" that they do not. His reasoning: Genesis gives humans the added command to keep the garden and avoid the tree, so humans brought sin into the world, while animals "did nothing wrong" and were simply told to be fruitful (37:09). He acknowledges that some verses call certain animals "evil" and that he cannot yet make sense of them (37:54). On Genesis 9:5 ("I will demand an accounting... from every animal"), he calls it "debatable" and says he has "not seen compelling evidence" that animals will be judged (52:07).

### 6. Consciousness is not the criterion for having a soul
Responding to a question about infants who never become conscious (a comment from a children's doctor), Craig argues that sentience is not a prerequisite for having a soul. He cites Jeremiah (God knew him "while you were still in the womb") and the Luke account of John leaping in Elizabeth's womb when Mary arrives, to argue the unborn can already have a soul (29:02). He adds that animals "do exhibit these levels of consciousness" (pigs, cows, chickens, squirrels), but that the question is not "are you as smart as me?" but "can you suffer?" (30:02). On oysters and mussels, he says the argument "they can't feel pain" does not settle things because "it's about their desire to live," and he rejects a magnitude view of souls: "I think it's yes or no, do they have a soul?" (44:42, 45:26).

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Animals don't have a soul, so we don't need to care about them."** Craig says the Bible describes animals with nephesh, so "we can't just say, 'they don't have a soul, and that's why they don't matter'" (23:56, 53:53).
- **"Translations show that only humans have a soul."** Craig says English translations (including those using "people" or "life") impose a distinction the Hebrew does not make (07:43, 08:30).
- **"Sentience or intelligence determines who has a soul."** He argues consciousness is not a prerequisite, citing unborn children and saying the question is "can you suffer?" rather than "are you as smart as me?" (29:02, 30:02).
