## Summary

Craig Wescoe, leading a live Friday-night session for Creation Care Church, asks whether God changed his mind about eating animals after the flood. The popular reading he sets out is that Genesis 1:29 gives humanity a plant diet, and that Genesis 9:2-3 then adds animals to the menu. He argues against that reading mainly from the surrounding text. Genesis 9:9-17 makes a covenant with the animals as well as with humans, which he says is strange if animals are only food. Genesis 8:21 has God speaking of the "imagination of man's heart" as evil, which Craig contrasts with the "very good" of Genesis 1:31. He ties the post-flood situation back to the curse in Genesis 3:16-17 and the violence in Genesis 6:13. He considers a "temporary concession" reading and rejects it on the basis of the dove and olive leaf in Genesis 8:11. He also considers a "permission because of hardness of heart" reading and compares it to Jesus's comment on divorce in Matthew 19:8. His preferred conclusion is that God issued a warning about the consequences of doing things humanity's own way, and that the Genesis 1:29 diet remains the ideal.

Most of the talk is Craig speaking, with a live Q&A from the chat in the second half. He answers questions on the word "shall," whether the blood-draining rule shows God accommodating people, the meaning of "pleasing aroma" in Genesis 8:21, Jerome and Matthew Henry's views, and why God blessed Noah. He ends by saying he tends toward being a raw fruitarian and at least vegan, and that the next session starts a series on Old Testament animal sacrifice. The talk closes with prayer.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Genesis 9:2-3 is not a simple "menu change"
Craig reads Genesis 9:2-3 and concedes it "kind of references" Genesis 1:29 when God says "I have given you all things, even as the green herbs." He calls the common reading ("God's basically changing his mind") too straightforward. His first counter is the covenant in Genesis 9:16. He says the covenant is extended to "every living creature" and that animals are mentioned as covenant recipients "five times in chapter nine," "arguably a sixth." His question: if animals were only food, why would God make them covenant partners?

### 2. The post-flood state is evil, not approved (Genesis 8:21, 6:13, 3:16-17)
Craig argues that Genesis 8:21 ("the imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth") contrasts with Genesis 1:31's "very good." He reads the animals' fear of humans (Genesis 9:2) as resembling Adam and Eve hiding after the fall. He links God's promise not to "curse the ground for man's sake" to the curse in Genesis 3:16-17, which he reads as following humanity's failure to "keep the garden" (Genesis 2:15). He links the promise not to destroy "every living thing" to the violence that prompted the flood in Genesis 6:13. On this reading God is responding to corruption and promising not to repeat his earlier judgments, not endorsing a new arrangement.

### 3. Two rival readings, both rejected or ranked lower
- **Temporary concession:** God allowed animal-eating only until plants regrew. Craig rejects this because Noah's dove returned with a "freshly plucked olive leaf" (Genesis 8:11), showing that vegetation had already returned. He adds that God could have supplied manna or multiplied the ark's stores (Numbers 11; Exodus 16).
- **Permission because of hardness of heart:** Craig says this is "the next best reading" after his own, and he uses Jesus's remark on divorce (Matthew 19:8, citing Deuteronomy 24:1 and Genesis 2:24) as a model. Jesus says Moses permitted divorce "because of the hardness of your hearts, but from the beginning it was not so." Craig presents this as a concession that falls short of God's ideal. He then reads Ezekiel 36:26 ("a new heart") as the remedy for hard hearts.
- **His preferred reading:** God is warning of consequences. The Genesis 1:29 diet is still God's will. The post-flood provisions describe what happens when humans act on their own judgment.

### 4. The "pleasing aroma" does not mean God was pleased (Genesis 8:20-21)
Craig notes that the text says God "smelled" the pleasing aroma, not that God "was pleased." He cites Ezekiel 6:13, where the same phrase is used of offerings to idols, and Psalm 115:4-6, which says idols have noses but "cannot smell." From these he argues the aroma is "pleasing to the flesh" and the senses, as in Genesis 3:6 ("pleasing to the eyes"), and not pleasing to God. He says this is the first explicit altar and burnt offering in Scripture, and that Abel's offering is "questionable" and reserved for a later talk.

### 5. Ideal diet, dominion and the practical ladder (Genesis 1:29-30; Isaiah 11:9; Q&A)
Craig says that the Genesis 1:29 diet is the ideal. He argues that animals are "outside the scope" of that diet, and that this includes their milk, eggs and honey. He presents a ladder: vegan as a "huge jump," with raw fruitarianism as the ideal, because fruit-eating and herb-pruning are, in his view, symbiotic and do not kill the plant. He uses Isaiah 11:9 for the peaceable kingdom. On the Q&A he takes the following positions: "shall" in Hebrew is ambiguous between "is" and "ought" (he cites the Ten Commandments as clear moral directives); the blood command in Genesis 9:4 shows God accommodating a stubborn people, and draining blood is a "crutch" toward the ideal; and "humane slaughter" still counts as gluttony. He concedes that God "gives us over" to some non-ideal things, and that this is temporary.

- **Belief:** God was pleased by Noah's burnt offering. **Correction:** Craig says the text says God "smelled" the aroma, not that he was pleased, and that the same phrase used of idol offerings in Ezekiel 6:13 and Psalm 115 argues against that reading.
