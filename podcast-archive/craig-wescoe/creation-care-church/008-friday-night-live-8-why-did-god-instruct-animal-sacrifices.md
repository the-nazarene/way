## Summary

Craig asks why God instructed animal sacrifice in the Old Testament, and whether those sacrifices will return (he defers that second question to the following week). He answers that God accommodated a people already immersed in sacrificial worship. He draws on Richard Schwartz (that sacrifice was the general practice among all nations in Moses' time), on Clement of Rome (that Moses allowed sacrifice but only to God, to cut away "one half of the deeply ingrained evil"), and on Ryan Hicks (that God's end goal was to wean people off violence). On this reading, the Mosaic sacrifices were a partial, temporary step away from idolatry. Craig then lists the five offering types (burnt, meal, peace, sin, trespass) and matches each to a New Testament fulfillment. His main textual support is Hebrews (7:11, 9:9, 10:4, 10:5-9), where he argues that the animal blood could not take away sins and that Jesus "takes away the first" to establish God's will. He says this makes Jesus' sacrifice once-for-all, and that the 70 AD destruction of the temple ended the practice for Christians.

The second half turns to "spiritual sacrifices" drawn from 1 Peter 2:5, Romans 12:1-2, Hebrews 13:15-16 and Ephesians 5:2: living as the temple, offering praise, doing good and sharing, and walking in love. Craig's Q&A answers extend the argument to animal research (which he calls vivisection and an unjustified practice), factory farming (which he calls the closest modern parallel to sacrifice), Kaparot chicken rituals, and Isaiah 1:10-20. The talk ends with a prayer and a teaser for next week's question about the future temple sacrifices in Ezekiel 43:19 and Isaiah 56:7.

Craig presents this as "the view that I think is correct" and says it is a long-standing view, citing Tertullian (read by a questioner) as supporting evidence. He does not treat the question as settled. He repeatedly describes the historical claims as uncertain, especially whether any Jewish group avoided sacrifice.

## Key Teachings

### God accommodated a people already practicing sacrifice
Craig argues that Moses' laws on sacrifice were given into an existing practice. He cites Richard Schwartz (a "Jewish scholar," per Craig) that sacrifice was the general worship practice among nations in Moses' time, and says the Israelites had been around Egyptian animal sacrifice. He then reads Clement of Rome, quoting a translation by Thomas Smith from the Ante-Nicene Fathers series. In Craig's reading, God permitted sacrifice only to himself as a first step, so that worship of other gods would stop first, and the laws would correct the remaining wrong later. He describes the sequence as "first things first": worship God alone, then the rest follows. He says Jesus was the one who later removed the sacrifices.

### Five offerings, each fulfilled in Jesus
Craig lists the five Levitical offerings (burnt, meal, peace, sin, trespass) and gives a proof-text pair for each: the Old Testament offering law and a New Testament passage he treats as its fulfillment. He says the animal offerings "were all pointing to Jesus," who "could not themselves actually accomplish" what the offerings symbolized. He presents the list as a quick reference, not a full study ("that could basically be a whole life talk study on its own"). He does not argue the mapping from the texts themselves in detail.

### Hebrews: Melchizedek, insufficiency, and Jesus as priest and sacrifice
Using Hebrews 7:11, Craig says the Levitical (Aaronic) priesthood was earthly and inferior, and that Jesus comes from the "order of Melchizedek," which predates Aaron. He ties this to Genesis 14:18 (Abraham's tithe to Melchizedek, the priest of God Most High), and argues that Levi was "in the loins" of Abraham when he tithed. From Hebrews 10:4 ("it is not possible that the blood of bulls and goats could take away sins") and 9:9 he argues that animal sacrifice was "symbolic" and insufficient for the conscience. He then reads Hebrews 10:5-9 as words Jesus speaks on entering the world ("a body you have prepared for me"), and says the sacrifice is "once and for all," so that no sacrifice need be repeated. He says the temple's destruction in 70 AD ended animal sacrifice for Christians.

### Spiritual sacrifice: the temple of living stones, a living sacrifice
Craig argues that after the temple's fall, believers replace it. From 1 Peter 2:5 he says "we are the temple" and "a holy priesthood." He cites Romans 12:1-2, which he reads as presenting one's body as a living sacrifice, marked by transformation and not conformity to the world. Hebrews 13:15-16 gives "the sacrifice of praise" and "to do good and to share." Ephesians 5:2 ("walk in love as Christ also has loved us") serves, for Craig, as the summary of what God wants. He says Paul's letters are where "the early church" develops what these spiritual sacrifices are. Craig does not discuss how Paul's letters relate to the teaching of Jesus.

### Animals were never meant to be sacrifices (the Old Testament prophets)
Responding to a question about Jewish groups who avoided sacrifice, Craig argues that God's rejection of sacrifice is not a new idea in the New Testament. He cites Isaiah 1:10-20 ("I do not delight in the blood of bulls or lambs and goats"), and says the same message appears in the Psalms and in Jeremiah, "thousands of years before Jesus." In his view, obedience (following God, loving mercy and kindness) is what God actually wants. He says God "didn't design animals just to be these objects of sacrifice," and that the kingdom vision shows animals and people living peacefully together.

### Application: animals, slaughter, and diet
In answer to questions, Craig argues that the logic against sacrifice extends to animal research and factory farming. He says animal testing is "archaic," that double-blind studies work better, and that profit is the main reason it continues. He calls factory farming the closest modern analogue to sacrifice and draws on Genesis 1:29 (the original diet) and Philippians (his phrasing "your God is your belly"). He also condemns Kaparot, saying chickens are nowhere in scripture as sacrificial animals, and that chickens are sometimes discarded alive. He frames the ethical point as stewardship that Christians also fail at by paying others to slaughter animals. He admits that "it's not super clear" why specific procedures were commanded, such as the knife to the throat, and says the purpose may have included hygiene or ethics but that he doesn't know of specific evidence.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That all Jews at Jesus' time were required to offer animal sacrifices.** A questioner (Michelle) raised this, citing non-vegan Christians. Craig says he knows of a group, the Essenes, who reportedly did not sacrifice or eat animal flesh. He is explicit that the evidence is thin, and adds that other Jews probably held the same view.
- **That God wanted animal sacrifices in the first place.** Craig's correction: "it's not like God wanted them." In his account, they were a concession to existing practice, and the prophets (Isaiah, Jeremiah, the Psalms) state that God does not delight in them.
- **That the rejection of sacrifice is a New Testament innovation.** Craig says the Old Testament prophets and Psalms already say God does not desire animal sacrifice, "thousands of years before Jesus," so the idea is not a later Christian invention.
