## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks why God seems to tell Israelites in Deuteronomy 12:20 that they may "eat as much meat as your heart desires," and why Numbers 11:18 has God promise meat to a complaining people. He argues that the phrase is not a general endorsement of eating animals. He reads Numbers 11 as the story of the first generation out of Egypt, who craved meat, were given quail "until it comes out of your nostrils," and died of a plague while the meat was still in their teeth, a place he says was named the "grave of craving." Deuteronomy 12, addressed to their children, is for him a merciful, gradual accommodation to the same craving, not a sign of approval. His main supports are the surrounding narrative, 1 Corinthians 10:5-6, Galatians 5:17 and 5:24, Micah 6:8, Genesis 1:29, and Proverbs 23:20, read with the claim that the Hebrew *basar* means "flesh" rather than "food."

Wescoe is careful to frame the talk as pastoral. He says the argument is not about judging people who eat meat, and he allows that some people in desperate circumstances may eat it. He presents the "ideal" diet of Genesis 1:29 as the standard believers should aim at. The Q&A adds further points: the "halfway" logic of permission leading toward full obedience (he compares this to the sacrifice question), his claim that blood cannot be fully removed from meat, and a short answer on why Jesus is said to have eaten fish after the resurrection (he says this proves the resurrection and that the Greek word *ichthyon* is debated). The talk ends with a prayer and announcements for the next week's topic, "a land flowing with milk and honey" (Exodus 3:8 as homework), and a forthcoming study on Jesus and fish.

## Key Teachings

### The "grave of craving" in Numbers 11
Wescoe reads Numbers 11:18-20 and 11:33-34 as God giving the complaining first generation quail "for a whole month, until it comes out of your nostrils and becomes loathsome to you." He reads the plague "while the meat was still between their teeth" as divine displeasure, and the place name Kibroth-hattaavah as "the grave of craving." He reads 1 Corinthians 10:5-6 as the New Testament's own commentary, with the "evil thing" being the lust for animal flesh. He presents this as the clearest evidence in the story of what God actually wants.

### Deuteronomy 12:20 as mercy to the second generation
He argues that Deuteronomy, a retelling of the law, is addressed to the children of the Numbers 11 generation. The line "you may eat as much meat as your heart desires" is, in his reading, conditional ("if you long to eat meat") and describes a people God is weaning rather than a command or blessing. He says God shows "grace" to the second generation where he had destroyed the first. In the Q&A, a listener (Cathy/Rich exchange) suggests God is drawing Israel away from surrounding cultures and their treatment of sacrificial animals; Wescoe agrees with the general frame of accommodation.

### Flesh versus Spirit (Galatians 5:17, 5:24)
Wescoe uses Galatians 5:17 to describe an internal conflict between "the flesh" and "the spirit," and Galatians 5:24 ("have crucified the flesh with its passions and desires") to describe the believer's task. He ties the Israelites' craving to the flesh side of that conflict and presents the "fruit of the Spirit" (love, peace, joy, mercy, compassion) as the other side. He does not argue the Pauline wording in depth; he uses it as a frame for the Torah narrative.

### Micah 6:8, Genesis 1:29 and the designed diet
He cites Micah 6:8 ("to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God") to argue that serving the flesh's cravings pulls against walking humbly with God. He then cites Genesis 1:29, "every herb that yields seed... To you it shall be for food," as the diet God "designed" and called "very good." He draws a link to modern disease, saying that heart disease, strokes, cancers, and diabetes are "highly correlated with consuming animal products." He names no studies, so this is an unsourced assertion in the talk.

### Proverbs 23:20 and the meaning of *basar*
He argues that Deuteronomy 12:20 cannot mean "gluttony is fine," because Proverbs 23:20 warns against "gluttonous eaters of meat." He says the Hebrew is *basar*, which he claims is always "flesh" (living creatures or carcasses), not general "food," and that King James "meat" is therefore not the best rendering here. He reads the verse as a direct prohibition on gluttony in flesh-eating. He quotes the Amplified translation and does not discuss Hebrew scholarship beyond this.

### Blood, "halfway" obedience and sacrifice (Q&A)
In answer to a question about blood, Wescoe says that eating flesh cannot be done without consuming some blood, so a person who wants to fully obey the prohibition on blood must abstain from flesh. He calls God's permissions a "halfway" step that exposes a person's shortfall and leads toward full obedience. He draws a parallel to sacrifice: God first moved Israel from worshipping other gods with animal sacrifice to worshipping God alone, then to worshipping him in the way he wants, without animal sacrifice. He says "we talked about this a few weeks ago," so the sacrifice argument is partly in an earlier talk.

### Jesus, fish and the resurrection (Q&A)
Asked why Jesus ate fish, Wescoe says he knows of one gospel passage where Jesus is "explicitly recorded as consuming an animal product," after the resurrection, when he eats broiled fish to show the disciples he is not a ghost. He presents this as a justified exception that proves the resurrection and the hope of resurrection in the kingdom. He acknowledges that the Greek *ichthyon* is debated, with some arguing it means seaweed, and says he will treat that in a later talk. He does not name the gospel or verse in the transcript.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Deuteronomy 12:20 is God's blanket permission to eat as much meat as you want.** Wescoe says the verse is conditional ("if you long to eat meat") and addressed to a second generation God is weaning, not a command or approval.
- **Numbers 11 shows God approving meat-eating.** He reads the story as displeasure: the quail is given in anger, and the people die of a plague with the meat in their teeth.
- **"Meat" in older translations means food in general.** He argues *basar* means flesh, and that Proverbs 23:20 targets gluttony in flesh specifically.
- **Jesus' eating of fish endorses eating animals.** He says the single post-resurrection instance is a proof of the resurrection, not a general dietary teaching.
