## Summary

Craig Wescoe, leading a Creation Care Church "Friday Night Live" sermon, asks what it means for a land to be "flowing with milk and honey," and whether the phrase supports eating dairy and honey. He argues it is an idiom for abundant, flourishing life rather than a literal promise of food. His main evidence is Numbers 13: the spies sent into Canaan brought back grapes, pomegranates and figs, not milk or honey, which Craig takes as the signal that the phrase means fruitfulness. He adds a reductio from Ezekiel 20:6: literal rivers of milk and honey would spoil and become sticky, so the phrase cannot be read literally. He then reads milk as a sign of thriving mammals and honey as a sign of thriving bees, flowers and water.

Craig links the promised land to God's future kingdom. Moses, barred from entering, stands for the Law, which brings people to the edge; Joshua, who leads them in, stands for Jesus (Craig says the names share the meaning "salvation"). He argues that in the kingdom honey belongs to bees and milk to calves, so people should steward creation rather than consume its products, and he ties this to the Genesis 1:29 diet, Genesis 2:15 and Isaiah 11. The talk closes with a prayer and a live chat Q&A, where Craig answers questions read aloud from viewers (including Philip, Timothy and Kathy), touching on dairy harms, bee decline, Bible translations, the meaning of names, dominion, and plant-based health claims. He ends by previewing a talk on stewardship and says later talks will turn to Jesus and the early church.

## Key Teachings

### Milk and honey as an idiom for abundance, not literal food
Craig's central claim is that the phrase signifies flourishing. He builds this from Numbers 13:20-27, where the spies sent to view the land return with a cluster of grapes, pomegranates and figs and report that the land "truly flows with milk and honey." If the phrase meant literal dairy and honey, he reasons, they would have brought back milk and honey as proof. He presents this as "your first indication" that the phrase points to something else, with fruit as the signifier. He also cites Ezekiel 20:6, which calls the land "the glory of all lands," and argues that literal rivers of milk would rot and literal rivers of honey would make everything sticky, so no sensible reading is literal.

### What milk and honey signal ecologically
Craig reads the two products as indicators of a healthy ecosystem. Milk exists because mammalian mothers feed their young, so abundant milk implies abundant reproducing animals, which implies abundant grass and herbs and therefore water. Honey implies bees, which implies abundant flowers and vegetation for pollination. The combination, in his reading, describes conditions "conducive to flourishing" for humans too. He sets this against the opposite term "desolate" wasteland, citing Ezekiel 33:29, and presents it as a choice between blessing and drought and famine.

### Moses, Joshua and the limits of the Law
Drawing on Numbers 13-14, Craig says Joshua and Caleb alone gave a faithful report (Numbers 14:6-8) and were the only first-generation Israelites allowed in (Numbers 14:23-24). He explains Moses' exclusion as a consequence of striking the rock rather than speaking to it, citing Numbers 20:12 and Deuteronomy 32:52, and reads Numbers 11 as the record of craving and murmuring. He then reads Moses as representing the Law: it "can get us to the edge of the promised land... looking in." Joshua represents Jesus, since the Hebrew names are "yahushua" and "yeshua," which he says both mean salvation. He supports the need to follow Jesus, not only keep the commandments, with John 14:6 and the rich young ruler of Mark 10:17-21. Note that the etymology and the typology are Craig's own readings; the talk gives no source for the name meanings beyond his assertion.

### The kingdom as a restored design, with milk for calves and honey for bees
Craig says the promised land symbolically represents God's heavenly kingdom, though he concedes it "wasn't the heavenly kingdom" itself. In that kingdom he expects the Genesis design to be restored. He cites Isaiah 11:6-9 for a peaceable creation, then argues that honey is designed for bees' winter survival and milk for a mother's offspring, so neither is intended for human consumption. Genesis 2:15 (tend and keep the garden) and Cain's question in Genesis 4 ("am I my brother's keeper") frame humans as preservers. He concludes that in the kingdom people will cultivate habitats rather than take what belongs to animals.

### Stewardship, dominion and the critique of exploitation
Craig reads Genesis 1:26 (image and dominion) as a responsibility to uphold God's way, not a license to do as one pleases. In Q&A he says dominion does not mean eating animals, since the very next verse (Genesis 1:29) gives fruit and vegetation as food, and calls the desire to dominate the same impulse as the Fall. He uses John 10:10-11 (the thief steals, kills and destroys; the good shepherd lays down his life) and Matthew 24:46 (the faithful servant found working when the master returns) to frame the dairy industry as theft and destruction. He also says the dairy industry separates calves from mothers, and that some of the harms he describes were presented from a general perspective and not sourced in the talk. In answer to a viewer, he urges seeing animals as "someone not something."

### Health and diet claims
In answer to a question on why humans can tolerate meat, Craig says diseases such as cancers, strokes, heart disease, high blood pressure and diabetes correlate with animal-product consumption, and that whole-food plant-based diets have reversed some of these in months. He points to the Physicians Committee for Responsible Medicine and to Loma Linda as a "blue zone" linked to a largely vegetarian Seventh-day Adventist diet. These are offered as supporting "scientific evidence" and are not documented in the talk beyond these pointers.

### Honey as grape or date syrup (a question he leaves open)
Responding to a viewer who cited the NRSV Oxford Bible's suggestion that the honey was a thick syrup from grapes and dates, Craig says he has "heard this before," has not studied it in depth, and that it "wouldn't surprise me" if it were correct. He says the context (the spies bringing fruit) supports a non-bee reading either way. Notably, this openness does not change his conclusion about the dairy and honey diet.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Milk and honey promise literal dairy and honey to eat.** Craig argues the phrase is an idiom for abundant flourishing; the spies brought back fruit, not milk and honey, and literal rivers of milk and honey would spoil or make everything sticky (Numbers 13; Ezekiel 20:6).
- **The "land flowing with milk and honey" undermines a plant-based diet.** Craig says the objection fails because the promised land is a symbol of the kingdom, where honey is for bees and milk for calves, and the Genesis 1:29 diet is restored.
- **Dominion means humans may use animals as they please.** Craig reads Genesis 1:26 as a responsibility to uphold God's design, pointing to the diet given in Genesis 1:29 as the next instruction.
- **Keeping the Law is enough to enter the kingdom.** Craig says Moses could only lead people to the edge; following Jesus (Joshua) is required (Numbers 20:12; Deuteronomy 32:52; John 14:6).
