## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks why Christians should be good stewards of the earth if God will destroy it and replace it. He takes that objection on two levels. First, he argues that the apocalyptic passages usually cited for destruction (Revelation 6:14, 2 Peter 3:10, Revelation 21:1-2) could be read symbolically, as a refining and renewal of creation rather than its annihilation. He backs this with Genesis 2-3 (the Fall), Genesis 9:11 (the post-Flood covenant), and Matthew 5:5 (the meek inheriting the earth). He says this evidence is "not definitive proof" either way.

Second, he argues that stewardship matters even if the earth is destroyed. Revelation 11:18 says God will destroy "those who destroy the earth," and Genesis 2:15 and Luke 12:42 present humans as stewards whom the master finds at work when he returns. He says the steward is accountable to God, the owner, and that the proper stewardship includes a plant-based diet, rejecting materialism (1 Timothy 6:6-10), and caring for animals as God's creatures. His method is a verse-by-verse sermon with no outside historical or critical scholarship.

The talk ends with about thirty minutes of live Q&A and closing prayer. In the Q&A Wescoe answers questions on animals' place in creation, whether trees communicate, the risk of the environmental movement being co-opted, and whether Christians should be vegan. He previews next week's topic, whether Jesus "declared all foods clean" (Matthew 15:11; Mark 7:15).

## Key Teachings

### Apocalyptic imagery may be symbolic renewal, not annihilation
Wescoe reads the sky "rolled up like a scroll" (Revelation 6:14) as a symbol of "the completion of something," namely the end of the reign of sin and death. He reads the earth "melted with fervent heat" (2 Peter 3:10) as a metaphor for God refining human character, using the image of metal being melted and reshaped. He reads the new Jerusalem descending (Revelation 21:1-2) as renewal, not replacement: the world "renewed to the way it was originally designed." He concedes that "it's not made clear" whether these are literal, but says the symbolic reading "should cause us to rethink" the case against stewardship.

### Genesis 2-3: a restoration, not a better-than-Eden future
Wescoe argues that if the coming kingdom were "something better than a newly restored earth," then humanity would be "better off having trusted the serpent rather than god." He rejects that reading. Citing Genesis 2:16-17 and 3:4, he notes that God is "the author of truth" and the devil "the father of lies," so the Fall was a mistake to be undone. On this reading, God is restoring creation "to that very good state it was in the beginning," with death as "the last enemy to be defeated."

### Genesis 9:11 and Matthew 5:5
He reads God's post-Flood covenant with "every creature" (Genesis 9:11) as a promise not to destroy the earth by flood again. He says that if the earth will be burned, the promise amounts to "fire insurance now not flood insurance," which he finds unconvincing. Matthew 5:5 ("blessed are the meek for they shall inherit the earth") then implies that the meek inherit a restored earth, not a "burned up uninhabitable" one. He says this makes "the kingdom on earth as it is in heaven."

### Even if the earth is destroyed, stewardship still matters
Wescoe's second line of argument assumes the worst case. Revelation 11:18 says God will "destroy those who destroy the earth," so the reader must choose which group to be in. Genesis 2:15 ("to tend and keep it") is then read as God's original purpose for humanity, and Luke 12:42 is taken to mean the faithful steward is found working when the master returns. He says the believer's part is to prepare, and that God does the rest "through us," citing Mark 1:3 ("prepare the way for the lord's coming").

### Stewardship as God's ownership and human responsibility
Using Psalm 24:1 ("the earth is the lord's") and Isaiah 45:18 ("formed it to be inhabited"), Wescoe argues that humans hold creation in trust, not as owners. He defines a steward as "someone who essentially manages someone's estate while they're gone." He compares this to pet-sitting: the owner's instructions must be followed, and the animal must be cared for. He uses this to reject the idea that human "dominion" (Genesis 1, cited without a verse) means a license to do as one likes.

### Diet and the rejection of materialism
Wescoe says God's first instruction to humans about animals was to eat "a diet that consists entirely of fruit and vegetation." He argues that eating animals contradicts the restored kingdom in Revelation 21:4, in which there is "no more death." He also argues that a "frugivore" diet would not harm plants, because eating fruit assists the tree's reproduction. He ties this to 1 Timothy 6:6-10 (the love of money as a root of evil), and to the temple cleansing (he quotes Jesus: "do not make my father's house a house of merchandise," with no reference given). He argues the earth is "god's house" and should not become a marketplace.

### Animals as creatures God cares for
Citing Genesis 2:19 (Adam naming the animals) and Proverbs 12:10 ("a righteous man regards the life of his animal"), Wescoe argues that humans should relate to animals as "someone not something." He says animals are part of the restored creation, and that "the kingdom of god is not a matter of eating and drinking but of righteousness peace and joy in the holy spirit" (Romans 14:17). He says that the welfare of animals is part of stewardship, not a separate question.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"God will destroy the earth, so there is no point in caring for it."** Wescoe argues the destruction texts may be symbolic (refinement, renewal), and that stewardship matters even if literal destruction is coming, since Revelation 11:18 condemns those who destroy the earth.
- **"Salvation is the only thing that matters, so stewardship is irrelevant."** He argues stewardship is part of the original human calling in Genesis 2:15 and is tied to the restored kingdom on earth.
- **"Humans are the pinnacle of creation, so animals do not matter."** He grants that humans have a higher role but argues that this carries responsibility (God is still the owner), and that animals are creatures God cares for (Proverbs 12:10).
- **"Human dominion means permission to do whatever we want."** He argues that dominion is a trust, like a steward looking after an owner's property, and that the owner's instructions still apply.
