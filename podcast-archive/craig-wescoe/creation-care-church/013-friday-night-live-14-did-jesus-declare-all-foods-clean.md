## Summary

This is a livestreamed "Friday Night Live" talk from Creation Care Church, in which Craig Wescoe takes up the question "Did Jesus declare all foods clean?" The question arises from Jesus's saying that what goes into a person does not defile them (Matthew 15:11; Mark 7:15), which many readers take to abolish the Mosaic food laws. Wescoe argues that the answer is no. His main evidence is textual: the phrase "thus purging all foods" in Mark 7:19 is a translator's addition in the NIV, with a parenthetical "thus all foods are clean," and the original Greek has no such parentheses. He also argues that the Mark 7 and Matthew 15 controversy is about eating with unwashed hands, a tradition of the elders, not about what animals may be eaten.

He supports this with later New Testament texts he reads as still prescribing food restrictions (Acts 15:29, Revelation 2:20), and with an ethical argument from love and mercy toward animals (1 Corinthians 16:14; Proverbs; Micah 6:8). He ends by urging a diet like the one he reads in Genesis 1:29. The talk closes with a Q&A from viewers (Timothy, Kathy, Angel, Tim Zona, and others, whose questions are read aloud), an invitation to the Discord fellowship, and a preview of next week's talk on 1 Timothy 4:1-3 and a later talk on Jesus and fish and lamb.

## Key Teachings

### The NIV parenthetical in Mark 7:19 is a translation choice, not the text
Wescoe reads Mark 7:19 in the NIV ("because it does not enter his heart but it goes into the stomach and then is eliminated and then in parentheses thus all foods are clean"). He says the original Greek has no parentheses, so the translator supplied the interpretation. He compares the King James Version ("purging all meats") and argues that "purging" refers to the body expelling food after digestion. In his account, the NIV's "thus all foods are clean" is a mistranslation that gave rise to the doctrine that Jesus abolished the dietary laws. He makes the same point about Matthew 15:17, which he says uses the same "eliminated" language with no claim about cleanness.

### The dispute is about unwashed hands
Wescoe reads Mark 7:1-5 and states that the Pharisees and scribes from Jerusalem object that Jesus's disciples eat with defiled, unwashed hands, contrary to "the tradition of the elders." He notes the passage's further list of ceremonial washings (cups, pitchers, kettles, couches). He concludes that the controversy "had nothing to do with the kinds of food that was being eaten." He argues that building a food-law abolition on this passage is "an extremely flimsy foundation."

### The heart-based reading (Matthew 15:18-20; Luke 6:45)
He reads Matthew 15:18-20, where evil thoughts, murders, adulteries, thefts and false witness are said to defile a man, and treats this as Jesus's teaching tool aimed at the Pharisees. He pairs it with Luke 6:45 ("out of the abundance of the heart his mouth speaks") and the "good tree / good fruit" saying, which he takes as showing that inner condition, not food, is the issue. He describes the Pharisees as looking for loopholes and as "religious politicians."

### Later New Testament texts still restrict food (Acts 15:29; Revelation 2:20)
He argues that if Jesus had declared all foods clean, the early disciples would not keep prohibiting food. He cites Acts 15:29 (abstain from things offered to idols, from blood, from strangled things, from sexual immorality). He cites Revelation 2:20 as a warning against a "Jezebel" who would teach eating food offered to idols. He also uses the cannibalism question: if all food is clean, human flesh would have to be permitted, and he says murder's inclusion in the list of defilements (Mark 7:21) rules that out, which he takes to show that the "all foods clean" reading produces contradictions.

### Ethical argument: love, violence and mercy toward animals (1 Corinthians 16:14; Proverbs; Micah 6:8)
Wescoe argues that even if eating is not defiling, what comes out of a person can be. Citing 1 Corinthians 16:14 ("let all that you do be done with love"), he asks whether eating animal flesh shows love to the animal, and answers no when the eating is unnecessary. He cites Proverbs 3:31 (do not choose the ways of a man of violence), Proverbs 12:10 (the righteous care for the needs of their animals), and Micah 6:8 (do justly, love mercy) as applying to animals. He says that someone who eats unclean animals such as pigs and shellfish is disregarding God's instructions, the same fault he attributes to the Pharisees.

### Genesis 1:29 as the ideal diet; the "impossible restriction"; Genesis 9
He says that Leviticus and Deuteronomy regulate clean and unclean animals (citing Deuteronomy 14:8 on the pig), but that these were given to Israelites who were "chasing after other gods" and "craving meat instead of manna," so God was "meeting them where they were at." He argues that the ideal is Genesis 1:29 (fruit and vegetation as food, which he says will also be food "in the kingdom"). He calls the prohibition on blood the "impossible restriction," since no flesh can be eaten without some blood. On Genesis 9, he argues that God did not change his mind about diet after the flood, and that the reference to "evil imagination" (Genesis 8:21) and the covenant with the animals (Genesis 9, mentioned five times) show that animals are not simply added to the menu. He cites 1 John 4:16 and 4:18 to link fear with the Fall.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus declared all foods clean (Mark 7:19 / Matthew 15:11).** Wescoe argues the passage is about unwashed hands and that the NIV's "thus all foods are clean" is an interpolation, not part of the Greek text.
- **Jesus's teaching abolished the Mosaic dietary laws.** He argues that Acts 15:29 and Revelation 2:20 show ongoing food prohibitions, and that Deuteronomy 14:8 remains in force.
- **Leviticus allows eating clean animals.** He argues that the blood prohibition makes this impossible, so the only consistent path is no animal flesh.
- **Genesis 9 added animals to the human diet.** He argues that God's words in Genesis 9 are a covenant with animals and that God did not change his mind.
- **Christians who eat lamb or fish are following Jesus.** He argues that Jesus is recorded eating animal products only once (after the resurrection), and that the Passover lamb pointed to Jesus's own sacrifice, so eating lamb is unnecessary now. He admits that whether Jesus ate Passover lamb is unknown.
