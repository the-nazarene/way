## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks whether 1 Timothy 4 suggests that vegans are following a demon, and whether animal flesh was created by God to be eaten with thanks. He notes that the passage is often quoted against people who advocate for animals or eating something other than meat. He reads 1 Timothy 4:1-3, which describes people who will "prohibit marriage and require abstinence from certain foods," and argues that the verse does not condemn plant-based eating.

His main line of argument has four parts. First, he reads the surrounding context: the qualifications for church leaders in 1 Timothy 3 (gentle, not violent, self-controlled) and the remark on "bodily training" in 1 Timothy 4:7-8. Second, he argues that abstaining from marriage and from certain foods is attested elsewhere in Scripture as legitimate (Jesus and Paul on celibacy, Leviticus and Acts on food restrictions), so neither practice can be the "irreverent, silly myth" the passage condemns. Third, he claims that the phrase usually rendered "every creature" in 1 Timothy 4:4 is a mistranslation, pointing to the same Greek phrase in Mark 16:15 and to its rendering as "the whole creation" in Romans 8:22. On this reading the verse means "everything created by God and declared good should not be forbidden." Fourth, he reads Genesis 1:29 as God's original plant-based diet, which he sets beside Genesis 1:31 ("very good") and Genesis 2:24 (marriage), and concludes that the verse warns against those who forbid what God called very good. He supports this with Daniel 1, the fruit of the Spirit in Galatians 5, and James 4:3 about asking God to bless what the flesh desires.

The talk ends with a long Q&A in the live chat. Craig answers questions on Leviticus 11 (fish with fins and scales, blood drainage), whether fish feel pain (citing Jonathan Balcombe's book *What a Fish Knows*), whether Jesus ate fish (deferred to a later talk), the image of God and dominion, and Galatians 5. He closes by previewing next week's talk on Acts 10 and ends with a prayer for the kingdom "on earth as it is in heaven," extended to all creation.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The context of 1 Timothy 3 and 4 points away from condemning vegans
Craig reads the list of overseer qualifications in 1 Timothy 3:2-3 (ESV), picking out "self-controlled" and "not violent, but gentle." He argues that a person who chooses not to harm animals meets these qualities and is no less qualified. He then links 1 Timothy 4:7-8 ("bodily training is of some value") with 1 Corinthians 9:27 (Paul disciplining his body) to argue that the "silly myths" of 4:7 are not self-discipline. He treats the 1 Timothy 4:1-3 prohibitions as belonging to the discussion of church leadership.

### 2. Celibacy and food abstinence are both attested as legitimate
Craig's test is whether a practice the verse condemns can also be endorsed elsewhere in Scripture. For marriage, he cites Matthew 19:11-12 (Jesus on those who make themselves eunuchs "for the kingdom of heaven's sake") and 1 Corinthians 7:6-9 (Paul preferring the unmarried state). He concludes that "saying not to marry" cannot itself mark someone as a demon-follower, or Jesus and Paul would be included. For food, he cites Genesis 2:17, Leviticus 11:8, Acts 15:29 and 1 Corinthians 10:28 as examples of food abstinence that God or the apostles commanded. He flags Acts 15 and 1 Corinthians 10 as passages he will treat in more depth later, so he does not develop them here.

### 3. "Every creature" as a mistranslation
This is the speaker's most distinctive argument. He reads 1 Timothy 4:4 ("every creature of God is good and nothing is to be refused if it is received with Thanksgiving") and objects that the literal reading would put humans "on the menu." He says that the Greek phrase he renders as "pasay ktise" (he adds that he may be mispronouncing it) also appears in Mark 16:15 ("preach the gospel to every creature") and that the New King James renders the same phrase as "the whole creation" in Romans 8:22. He concludes that the phrase is being translated differently to fit the context, and that the verse should be read as "everything created by God and declared good." He presents this as an argument from translation consistency. He does not cite a Greek lexicon, a commentary, or a Greek text on screen, so this step rests on his own reading of the phrase.

### 4. Genesis 1:29 as the "very good" plant-based design
Craig reads Genesis 2:24 (marriage as "one flesh") and Genesis 1:29 ("every herb that yields seed... to you it shall be for food") as two institutions God established and called "very good" (Genesis 1:31) before sin entered the world. On this reading, 1 Timothy 4 warns against people who forbid those two things. He draws a further conclusion that the plant-based diet is the one God intended, and that the verse's reference to food "received with Thanksgiving" should be applied to fruit and vegetation rather than to animal flesh. He reads Genesis 1:29 as a settled command, which the talk does not argue for against other readings of Genesis 1 and 9.

### 5. Discernment, wisdom and the "works of the flesh"
Craig's proposed purpose for 1 Timothy 4 is discernment: the church leader must discern which spirits are "of God," as in 1 John 4:1. He uses Daniel 1:12-20 (Daniel and his companions eating vegetables and water for ten days and appearing healthier and "ten times wiser") as an example of blessing for following God's diet. He reads 1 Corinthians 6:19 (the body as a temple) and James 4:3 (asking "amiss") as objections to thanking God for flesh one desires. He then sets the works of the flesh (Galatians 5:19-21) against the fruit of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22-23) and asks, of a person with a knife and one without, which one shows love, peace, kindness and self-control to the animal. He concedes that the Galatians list does not name eating meat, but says the fruit of the Spirit describes the choice.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That 1 Timothy 4 condemns vegans as followers of demons.** Craig argues the passage concerns prohibiting marriage and certain foods within a discussion of church leadership and discernment, and that the same practices are endorsed elsewhere in Scripture.
- **That "every creature" in 1 Timothy 4:4 means every animal is fit to eat.** Craig argues this is a mistranslation, since it would also include humans. He reads the phrase as "everything created by God and declared good."
- **That Galatians 5 permits eating meat because meat is not named among the works of the flesh.** Craig concedes the list does not name meat, but argues the fruit of the Spirit (love, peace, kindness, self-control) describes the choice to avoid killing animals.
- **That Jesus ate fish and so approved of fish-eating.** Craig defers this question to a later talk, saying there is "a whole lot more to it," and rejects the idea that it endorses factory fishing or overfishing.
- **That "some will prohibit foods" means the verse forbids all food restriction.** Craig notes that Leviticus, Acts 15 and 1 Corinthians 10 all describe food restrictions, so the verse cannot be a blanket condemnation of food abstinence.
