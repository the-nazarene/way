## Summary

Craig Wescoe, host of Creation Care Church's "Friday Night Live," takes up the question of why, in Acts 10, a voice tells Peter "Arise, Peter, kill and eat" when Jewish law forbids unclean animals. Before the teaching, he briefly announces the death of a congregation member, Timothy J. Varett, and offers hope in resurrection to eternal life in God's kingdom. The chapter is read aloud in order (Acts 10:9-17), and Craig notes that Peter refuses three times and puzzles over the vision.

Craig argues that the vision's meaning is stated in the text itself: Peter says God has shown him not to call any man "common or unclean" (Acts 10:28), and then that God shows no partiality (Acts 10:34-36). On his reading, the vision concerns salvation for Gentiles, not a license to eat unclean animals. His evidence includes the Spirit falling on Cornelius's household (Acts 10:44-47), Romans 3:29 as Paul restating the point, and his claim that no disciple is recorded eating unclean animals. He also argues, in a side note referring to an earlier talk, that Jesus did not declare all foods clean, because Peter's refusal would make no sense if he had.

Craig then explains God's "pedagogical style" as testing, citing Deuteronomy 8:2, the binding of Isaac (Genesis 22) and Hebrews 11:17-19. Peter's vision is, in his view, a test that makes him reason toward the meaning. He extends the argument to animals through Joel 2:28 (his reading of "all flesh" as including animals), Revelation 5:13, and Genesis 9, and calls for kindness toward animals as "step one." The talk ends with chat questions (answered by Craig; the questioners do not speak on the recording), a preview of next week's Matthew 8 pig story, invitations to Discord fellowship, and a request for donations to fund a podcast.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Acts 10 is about the opening of salvation to Gentiles, not a change in the menu
Craig reads Acts 10:9-16 as a trance in which a sheet of animals, which he identifies as ones Jewish law calls unclean, descends with a voice saying "rise, Peter, kill and eat." Peter refuses each of three times. Craig's key evidence is Peter's own explanation in Acts 10:28 and Acts 10:34-36, which he treats as the "aha" moment. He reads the vision as concerning people and salvation, and says that Peter's later action (Acts 10:44-47) confirms this, since the Spirit came on the Gentiles without any dietary change. He says the Holy Spirit falling on the Gentiles is "clear evidence that peter understood the vision correctly."

### 2. Dietary law remains in force, in his reading
Craig cites Leviticus 11:11 and Deuteronomy 14:3 as clear prohibitions, and Leviticus 20:24-26 as the reason God separated Israel by clean and unclean. He says there is "no scriptural basis" for the view that the vision made all animals edible, and that there is no evidence in Acts 10 "or anywhere else in scripture" of a disciple eating unclean animals or teaching that it is acceptable. Romans 3:29 ("is he not also the God of the Gentiles") is his Pauline corroboration for the salvation reading. He presents Romans 12:1-2 as linking the "good and acceptable and perfect" will of God to a plant-based original design in Genesis 1:29 and to dominion in Genesis 1:26.

### 3. Against the claim that Jesus declared all foods clean
Craig refers to an earlier talk on the unwashed-hands dispute ("what goes into your mouth doesn't defile you"). His argument is that if Jesus had already declared all foods clean, Peter's refusal in Acts 10 would be inexplicable, so the vision is "further evidence" that Jesus did not make that declaration. He says Jesus was only saying that eating with unwashed hands is not sin. The transcript gives no verse for the saying, and Craig does not engage the Gospel text in this talk.

### 4. God's teaching method is testing
Drawing on Deuteronomy 8:2 ("to know what is in your heart"), Craig argues that God often gives instructions that seem contradictory so that people must "grapple" until they understand. He compares Peter with Abraham: God promises a son through Isaac, then commands his sacrifice (Genesis 22:1-2). Craig reads Hebrews 11:17-19 as showing that Abraham reasoned God could raise the dead, and he reads Genesis 22:8 ("God will provide for himself the lamb") as typological of Jesus, linked to John 1:29 ("behold the lamb of God"). He says Peter's vision "kind of shows all the signs of being a test," though it is not explicitly called one. Craig says God is patient and "not genocidal," and that the instructions are meant to reveal the heart, not change God's mind.

### 5. Animals as "someone," and the Holy Spirit's reach to "all flesh"
This is the argument Craig treats as hardest for listeners. He reads Joel 2:28 ("I will pour out my spirit on all flesh") and says the Hebrew phrase *kol basar* refers to all people and animals in every use in scripture, and that Pentecost (Acts 2) was only a partial fulfillment. He cites Revelation 5:13 (all creatures praising God) and Genesis 1:30 (the Hebrew *nephesh* applied to animals) as support for animals having a form of soul or spirit. He says he is "not trying to necessarily convince somebody that animals can receive the holy spirit," and his practical aim is that people "choose to show love to animals." He credits Matthew King's book *And I Will Abolish the Bow* for the idea that animals are "someone not some thing." Craig also appeals to Genesis 9:3, 9:8-9 and the post-flood covenant, which he reads as including animals, and asks why God would repeat the covenant with animals if they were only menu items.

### 6. Animals should be treated as commodities no longer
Craig concludes that "step one is stop treating animals as commodities to serve the desires of your flesh," and to start "treating them with love and dignity." He ties this to Romans 12:1-2 and to a kingdom way of life that is "peacefully and not violently." He says this is a "much smaller ask" than full acceptance of animal Spirit-reception.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Acts 10 permits eating pork or other unclean animals.** Craig argues the vision concerned Gentile salvation and that Peter refused the food each time; no disciple is recorded eating unclean animals.
- **God contradicted himself by reversing Leviticus.** Craig says God does not change his mind; the apparent contradiction is a deliberate test that makes Peter reason to the true meaning.
- **Jesus declared all foods clean (from the unwashed-hands dispute).** Craig argues that Peter's reaction in Acts 10 shows otherwise, and that Jesus was only addressing ritual handwashing.
- **God is a violent or child-killing deity in these stories.** Craig says God's testing of Abraham and Peter reveals a merciful and patient God who does not actually want harm done.
- **Animals lack souls or any role in God's redemptive plan.** Craig cites Genesis 1:30, Joel 2:28 and Revelation 5:13 to argue that animals have *nephesh*, are covenant recipients, and may be part of the Spirit's reach.
- **Dominion over animals means permission to use them however one likes.** Craig argues that dominion carries responsibility and that the proper use of dominion is love and dignity, not exploitation.
