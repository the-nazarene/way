## Summary

The talk asks why God respected Abel's offering in Genesis 4 but not Cain's. Wescoe opens by describing the standard reading: Cain brought fruit, Abel brought an animal, and God therefore wants animal sacrifice rather than a plant diet. He says this reading is shaped by the later Levitical sacrificial system and by reading Moses-era practice back into Genesis before any altar appears. He sets out to test whether a better explanation exists in the text.

His answer has three parts. First, the difference lies in the manner of the gift, not its kind. Genesis 4:4 describes Abel's gift as the firstlings and fat of his flock, which Wescoe reads as "his best," while Genesis 4:3 gives Cain only "some of the fruit of the soil." He reinforces this with Hebrews 11:4 ("by faith"), Genesis 4:7, Matthew 10:37-38, Deuteronomy 10:12, and Acts 5:1-5 (Ananias and Sapphira, who held back part of their gift). Second, he argues that Abel's gift was a living lamb (or possibly its milk, following Josephus's reading) presented to a God who was physically present in the garden, not a slain sacrifice. He cites Genesis 1:26-29 and 2:15 to say that humanity's original jobs were tilling and "dominion," and that the original diet was plants, so Abel's role as keeper of flocks was a faithful task, not a deviation. Third, he suggests that the lamb-symbolism of Jesus (John 1:18, John 15:4, Luke 23:33-34) still works if the lamb is living rather than slain.

The talk ends with a question-and-answer segment. Viewers raised Josephus's milk reading, an early group he called the "moltenists" (transcript spelling) who used cheese in love feasts, whether Abel drank the milk himself, and how dominion relates to diet. Wescoe answered each in turn, announced that the January series will open with "Jesus and fish," and closed with prayer. Throughout, he presents his reading as the view he finds more consistent with the evidence, and he acknowledges that the slain-lamb reading is "plausible" but "less likely."

## Key Teachings

### 1. Abel's gift was "his best," offered by faith; Cain's was partial
Wescoe reads Genesis 4:3 ("some of the fruit of the soil") against Genesis 4:4 ("the firstlings... of their fat"), arguing that the missing qualifier on Cain's side matters. He compares this with Hebrews 11:4, which says Abel offered "a more excellent sacrifice" "by faith" (he reads the Greek-derived word as "offering," and uses the NIV). He ties "by faith" to giving one's best, and then to Genesis 4:7 (Cain is not "doing well") and to Matthew 10:37-38 (following Jesus must come before all other loyalties). He ends with Deuteronomy 10:12 and the command to serve God "with all your heart and with all your soul," and with Acts 5:1-5, where Ananias and Sapphira keep back part of the proceeds and die.

### 2. The original human jobs were tilling and dominion; diet was plants
Wescoe argues that Cain's tilling cannot be the problem, since God assigned humanity the task of tilling the ground in Genesis 2:15. He then turns to Genesis 1:26-29, which gives humanity "dominion" over animals, and Genesis 1:29, which gives "every herb that yields seed" and "every tree whose fruit yields seed" as food. He reads "dominion" as stewardship and keeping, not as permission to kill, and concludes that Abel's task of keeping the flock was the commanded work of a steward. He argues that Genesis 1:29 sets the diet before any sacrifice and that the text should be read in that light, not in light of later Mosaic practice.

### 3. Abel's gift was probably a living lamb, not a slain one (and possibly milk)
Wescoe makes three arguments. He notes that the phrase translated "firstlings... of the fat" appears only once in the Hebrew Bible, so it must be interpreted from context. He observes that no altar is mentioned before Noah after the flood (he points to the end of Genesis 8 or Genesis 9), and that God was physically walking with humans in the garden "in the cool of the day," so an offering did not need to be killed to "reach" him. He suggests a comparison with Exodus 19:3, where God speaks to Moses from a cloud and burning bush as an intermediary, which he presents as a different relationship. He likens the gift to a baby dedication: a healthy newborn lamb presented as the product of faithful stewardship.

### 4. Josephus's reading: "milk" rather than "fat"
Wescoe cites Flavius Josephus (*Antiquities* 1.2.1, which he says he read in William Whiston's translation) as saying that Abel brought milk. He explains that the Hebrew *khaleb* ("fat") and *halab* ("milk") were written identically in consonantal Hebrew, so the reading depends on context. He suggests that a suckling newborn lamb with its milk would fit the picture of a faithful keeper presenting flourishing flocks. He presents this as evidence, not as a settled reading, and says Josephus is a view "you often won't hear in church."

### 5. Symbolism of Jesus as lamb survives a living-lamb reading
Against the objection that the lamb symbolizes Jesus's death, Wescoe argues the living lamb still foreshadows Jesus "as the lamb of god," but of his eternal life "in the bosom of the father," citing John 1:18 ("in the bosom of the father"). He contrasts this with Luke 23:33-34, where Jesus says "father forgive them for they do not know what they do," and argues that a God who accepted executioners' acts would not be "good job you faithful servant." He concludes that the crucifixion is not the paradigm for Abel's gift.

### 6. Offerings in scripture are not always sacrificial
Wescoe lists non-animal offerings: Exodus 35:21 (people bring materials, jewelry and labor for the tabernacle) and Exodus 25:8 (God's dwelling place). He uses the tabernacle to argue that in Eden God dwelt among humanity, and that the tabernacle was built later because humanity had moved far from that closeness. He says the same Greek or Hebrew word for "offering" can describe such gifts, so the word does not require an animal sacrifice.

### 7. Caution about reading the present or later Israelite practice into Genesis
Wescoe warns against projecting later practice backward: "we want to be careful not to interpret historical events in terms of the present." He says reading Leviticus into Genesis 4 is one of the most common historical mistakes, and that the text should be read against the creation narrative of Genesis 1-2 instead.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **God rejected Cain's fruit and wants animal sacrifice.** Wescoe says the difference is the wholeheartedness and "by faith" of the gift, not that it was fruit rather than animal.
- **Abel's gift was a slain lamb.** Wescoe says the text does not require a slaughtered animal; a living lamb or its milk is a plausible reading, and the altar-based picture comes from later Levitical practice.
- **Sacrifice was built into the story from the beginning.** Wescoe argues that no altar appears until after the flood and that the early human relationship with God was direct, so sacrifice should not be read back into Genesis 4.
- **God's design includes eating animals because of human dominion.** Wescoe reads "dominion" as stewardship and says the diet in Genesis 1:29 was plants and fruit.
- **The lamb symbolism of Jesus requires a slaughtered lamb.** Wescoe argues the symbolism can point to Jesus's eternal life "in the bosom of the father," not only to his death.
