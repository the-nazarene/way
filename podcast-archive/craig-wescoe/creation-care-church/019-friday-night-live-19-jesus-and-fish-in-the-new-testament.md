## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks why Jesus eats fish, feeds crowds with fish, and tells Simon Peter to catch fish, and whether these episodes support eating animals. He answers with a harmonizing reading of the Gospels, treating each episode as a deliberate act with a purpose. The fish eaten before the disciples in Luke 24:42-43 proves the resurrection is real and not a ghost. The fishing miracle in Luke 5 recruits fishermen. The feeding miracles (Mark 8, Matthew 14, John 6) display God's power and compassion. The third post-resurrection appearance in John 21 recalls the Luke 5 catch. Craig says the lesson for believers is mercy toward animals, and he cites Jonathan Balcombe's *What a Fish Knows* for the claim that fish have lives and feelings. He does not treat the comparison as a demonstration that Jesus endorsed a vegetarian or animal-rights ethic.

Craig explicitly sets aside textual doubt. He says he assumes Scripture is "authentically preserved" and reads "fish" literally, though he acknowledges a fish-versus-seaweed translation debate and manuscript discrepancies. The talk ends with the closing prayer and a preview of next week's topic, whether animals are depicted in Scripture as capable of giving and receiving love.

Much of the second half is a Q&A where Craig answers chat questions. These include whether Jesus was moving the fishermen out of their trade, why the feeding accounts mention only loaves in some recountings (Lily's point), the fish as an early identifying symbol, and whether any disciple spoke up for the fish.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The fish in Luke 24 proves the resurrection
Craig reads Luke 24:37 (the disciples think they are seeing a ghost) and Luke 24:42-43 (they give him broiled fish, and he eats it before them). His reasoning is that ghosts were believed unable to eat, so eating proves a bodily resurrection. He ties this to John 6:54 and the hope of resurrection to eternal life. He then argues the meal was needed only because the disciples lacked faith, and that it was "just what was on hand" rather than a choice of fish over fruit.

### 2. Thomas and the demand for evidence
Using John 20:25-29, Craig argues that Thomas's insistence on touching the wounds, and Jesus's "blessed are those who have not seen," show the whole pattern is about building faith in people who lacked it. He explains "doubting Thomas" as the name for this episode.

### 3. The fishing miracle recruits fishermen
Luke 5:4-11 is read as a staged recruitment. Craig says the fishermen had worked all night with nothing, so the huge catch that breaks the nets and sinks the boats shows "the miraculous power of god," and this leads Peter to leave everything. He says Jesus "met them where they were at" in a fishing village. He links this to Mark 1:17 ("fishers of men") and the Great Commission in Matthew 28:19-20.

### 4. The feeding miracles: compassion and faith
Craig reads Mark 8:2-3 (Jesus's compassion, since the crowd would faint on the way home) and Matthew 14:13-20 (the five thousand, with the disciples telling Jesus to send the crowd away). He notes that the crowds likely included women and children, perhaps fifteen to twenty thousand people. He says the purpose of the two feedings was to show that trust in Jesus meant the disciples had enough to provide. He cites Matthew 16:9-10 and Mark 8:19-20 as Jesus himself recalling the loaves, and he treats the fish as present in the original event even though those recollections mention only bread.

### 5. The third appearance in John 21 as a callback
Craig argues John 21:4-14 deliberately echoes Luke 5: the disciples catch nothing, a net-filling catch follows, and Jesus serves bread and fish. He reads the chapter as a reminder of the earlier miracles and of Peter's future martyrdom (John 21:18-19). He suggests the disciples did not recognize Jesus at first but knew him through the miracle, and he admits he cannot say exactly why they did not recognize him.

### 6. Symbolism and parables
Matthew 13:10 is used to argue Jesus taught through parables, meeting audiences (fishermen, shepherds, bankers) in terms they understood. In the Q&A, Craig suggests the fish was an identifying symbol for early Christians (an acronym he gives as "Jesus Christ savior son of god") and compares it to the pigs in the Gerasene demoniac episode, which he says some scholars read as a symbol of the Roman legion. He says he prefers a literal reading unless the text signals a symbolic one, citing Acts 10.

### 7. From Jesus's example to mercy toward animals
Craig's practical conclusion uses Luke 6:36 ("be merciful just as your father is merciful"), Philippians 4:13, and John 14:12 ("greater works"). He quotes Balcombe that fish "are not merely alive they have lives they are not just things but beings." He concludes that believers should emulate Jesus's compassion, not his particular actions, and that the circumstances (a desert, fainting crowds, a resurrection proof) do not apply to ordinary life today.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- The popular belief that Jesus ate fish, so believers may eat animals or disregard animal welfare. Craig says this is "not the conclusion we should draw," because the meal was a resurrection proof given to doubting disciples, not a dietary teaching.
- The implied view that Jesus's fish were a choice of food: Craig says the fish was "what was on hand" and what the disciples were eating, not a preference for fish over fruit.
- The idea that the miracles' circumstances (desert, fainting crowds, a proof of resurrection) apply to ordinary believers. Craig says we are not in those situations and so should not use them to justify ignoring God's instructions on mercy.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Resurrection:** The talk's main theological claim. Luke 24 and John 20 are read as proof of bodily resurrection and as a basis for believers' hope of resurrection to eternal life.
