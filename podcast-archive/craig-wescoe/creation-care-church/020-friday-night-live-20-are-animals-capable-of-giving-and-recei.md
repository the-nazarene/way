## Key Teachings

### 1. Love is God's essential character, so animals share in it
Craig reads 1 John 4:8 ("He who does not love does not know God, for God is love") and 1 John 4:16 as establishing that love is God's "highest or most holy attribute." His chain of reasoning is: if animals have the capacity to give and receive love, they have the capacity to "embody God's most holy attribute." He then says that humans who fail to love show that God is not in them. The argument is an inference from the Johannine statements. Craig does not argue that 1 John means animals are included.

### 2. The hen and the chicks, and Psalm 91:4
Craig takes Jesus' lament over Jerusalem in Matthew 23:37 ("as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings") as an illustration that only works if animals really love their young. He adds Psalm 91:4 ("He shall cover you with his feathers"), which he reads as the same image of God's protection. He argues that the wider point is that the church is meant to love in the way animals do. He links this to John 15:12 ("love one another as I have loved you").

### 3. Balaam's donkey as a model of love
This is the longest argument. Craig retells Numbers 22:22-33: the donkey sees the angel with a drawn sword and turns aside three times; Balaam beats her each time; she finally lies down; the Lord opens her mouth; and the angel says he would have killed Balaam and spared her. Craig's reading:
- The donkey knew the angel's intentions because she could see him and Balaam could not, and "common sense" supports this.
- Her repeated rescue is a kind of enemy-love of the sort Jesus describes (the "love your enemy" teaching, which Craig says without giving a verse) and matches 1 Peter 3:9 ("not returning evil for evil").
- Her speech ("What have I done to you...?") speaks for all mistreated animals, which Craig applies to animal agriculture, entertainment, experimentation and the clothing industry.

Craig's reading that the donkey "saved" Balaam is his interpretation. The text says she turned aside, and the angel's speech is the only explicit statement of intent.

### 4. Creatures praising God: Psalm 150:6, Isaiah 66:23, Revelation 5:13
Craig reads Psalm 150:6 ("Let everything that has breath praise the Lord") as including animals. He cites Isaiah 66:23 and says the Hebrew *kol basar* ("all flesh") "always refers to all animals and all people," contrasting it with *kol adam* ("all people"). He presents this as a fact about the word. He gives no lexicon or source for it. Revelation 5:13 ("every creature which is in heaven and on the earth...") is read as a prophecy that every animal will praise God. Craig concludes that animals can love God, each other, people, and be loved in return, and that there is "no restriction" on the love they can give.

### 5. Animals can receive love: the ewe lamb and God's care
Craig reads 2 Samuel 12:3 (Nathan's parable of the poor man's ewe lamb, which "lay in his bosom... and was like a daughter to him") as presupposing that David understood animals can receive love. He draws on common experience with pets and farm animals (cuddling, being fed and protected). He adds Psalm 145:16 ("you open your hand and satisfy the desire of every living thing") and says God's love extends to animals and that "if animals can receive our love, they can certainly receive God's love."

### 6. Humans as shamar, keepers of animals
Craig reads Genesis 2:15, where Adam is placed in the garden "to tend and keep it," and glosses the Hebrew *shamar* as "keep, preserve, protect, watch over, or care for." He pairs this with Psalm 36:6 ("you preserve both man and animals," NIV) and with Cain's question in Genesis 4 ("Am I my brother's keeper?"), which he answers "Yes." His conclusion is that humans are to preserve, not kill, both people and animals, and that animals should not be treated "as commodities to be exploited."

### 7. Revelation 18 and "Babylon" (Q&A)
In answer to Fred, Craig and the chat locate Revelation 18:3, 11, 14, which describe merchants growing rich from Babylon's luxury and then weeping at her fall. Craig reads Babylon as a symbol of a system that treats everything, including animals, as "merchandise," and expects it to "come crashing down in an hour." He ties this to a Jesus saying about the temple as a "house of merchandise" (the saying is not located by verse in the talk). He sees the fall as the arrival of God's kingdom.
