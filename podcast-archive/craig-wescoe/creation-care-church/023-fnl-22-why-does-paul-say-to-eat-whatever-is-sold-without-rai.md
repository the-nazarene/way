## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks why, in 1 Corinthians 10:25-27, Paul tells believers to eat whatever is sold in the meat market or served at an unbeliever's table "asking no questions for conscience sake." He notes that some Christians cite the passage to defend eating animal products without checking ingredients, and he wants to test that use against the chapter's context.

His answer is that Paul is addressing idolatry, not diet. He reads Psalm 24:1 ("the earth is the Lord's") as Paul's scriptural base, then ties the chapter to Exodus 32 (the golden calf feast), Acts 15:29, and Revelation 2:14 and 2:20 to argue that eating in an idol's cultic meal is idolatry and is forbidden. The line he draws is temporal: food eaten during the act of worship is idolatry, but food sold afterward in the market or served at a home is not, unless the eater is told it was offered to idols. Even then, Wescoe reads Paul as asking believers to abstain "for conscience sake" to protect weaker believers and their witness. He presents this as a dispute between a more sensitive and a less sensitive group that Paul tries to reconcile.

Wescoe then argues that the principle cannot be stretched to cover animal products. Paul is not discussing the moral acceptability of eating animal flesh, and Acts 15:29 itself forbids blood, so the "eat anything" latitude only concerns whether food was offered to idols. He draws an application instead: a believer who knows the cruelty involved should be given the same consideration Paul asks of the sensitive conscience, and Paul's own example ("I will never again eat meat") is what he recommends. The talk closes with a live Q&A (chat questions were read aloud and answered by Wescoe) and a preview of next week's topic, whether God created animals to be eaten, which he tied to Genesis 1:29.

## Key Teachings

### The line is drawn at the worship meal, not at the market
Wescoe says the question is not whether animal flesh is acceptable but "is it idolatry to eat food that was offered idols." His reading: during the ritual the sacrificial meal is communion with the idol, which is forbidden. Afterward, when the meat is sold or given to a guest, it is not idolatry. The exception is if someone explicitly says the food was offered to idols, in which case he says abstaining is required for conscience's sake, not because it is idolatry.

### The scriptural and cultic background
He builds the case through Exodus 32:4-6 (Paul's quotation of "the people sat down to eat and drink and rose up to play" is treated as a reference to the golden calf feast), Acts 15:29, and Revelation 2:14 and 2:20, which he reads as three witnesses that eating food offered to idols is idolatry. He reads 1 Corinthians 10:20-21 ("the table of the lord and the table of demons") as the core point: a believer cannot commune at both tables. He also cites Adam Clark's commentary on the economics of sacrificial meat sold in the shambles.

### Exodus 20 and the jealous God
Wescoe links 1 Corinthians 10:22 ("do we provoke the Lord to jealousy") to the second commandment in Exodus 20:4-6. He says the "jealous God" language is not a controlling-partner image but a demand for exclusive devotion, which he links to Deuteronomy 6:4-5 and Mark 12:29-30 as the "greatest commandment."

### The two consciences and the "weaker brother" principle
He argues that the Corinthian community was divided: a sensitive group that avoided all food that might have been offered to idols, and a less sensitive group that did not. He reads 1 Corinthians 8:13 and 10:23-24 as Paul asking the less sensitive to give up their freedom for the sake of others, and 1 Corinthians 11:1 as Paul's own example ("imitate me") of abstaining. He reads 1 Corinthians 10:32 as a warning against causing others to stumble, especially those who are being evangelized.

### Application to veganism
Wescoe rejects the reading that Paul licenses eating anything regardless of animal welfare, calling that "clearly the wrong interpretation." He argues instead that the principle that governs conscience (prioritizing others over freedom, "not all things are beneficial") applies to a believer or potential believer who objects to eating animal products because of cruelty. On that reading, a believer should consider that conscience rather than insist on their own freedom. He says he personally avoids the question by declining food, and that he uses dinners to talk about "god's very good will his original diet his kingdom ideal." He cites Genesis 1:29 only in passing here, with the full diet question deferred to the next talk.

### Q&A points
In answer to chat questions he says: (a) the greatest commandment is exclusive devotion to God; (b) "Satan is the same as Christ" is not an acceptable belief; (c) God has many names, and Yeshua/Jesus, Yahweh and Lord can all refer to the God of Scripture; (d) the New Testament's "sharing with Jesus" language (Galatians 2:20; Colossians 2:20; Romans 6:4; Ephesians 2:6; Colossians 2:13; 3:1) makes idol-feasting spiritually significant.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Paul permits eating anything sold in the market, whatever its animal content.** Wescoe says the latitude applies only to whether food was offered to idols; the chapter does not address the morality of eating animal flesh, and blood is forbidden regardless.
- **The "jealous God" of Exodus 20 is a controlling or jealous partner.** Wescoe reads "jealous" as a demand for exclusive devotion, not a personal insecurity.
- **Believers today do not face idolatry.** In the closing remarks Wescoe says Christians still "bow down to statues inside churches and cathedrals," which he presents as a live application of the warning.
