## Summary

Wescoe asks why more Christians, and especially church leaders, are not concerned with "creation care," meaning the humane treatment of animals and care for the earth. He opens from Proverbs 12:10 ("the righteous care for the needs of their animals") and argues that God's character of love and mercy makes this a matter of obedience. He answers with five reasons: ignorance, tradition, deception, temptation, and, for church leaders only, fear that teaching creation care is bad for business because congregants will leave and take their money and volunteer labor with them. Each reason is supported by a sequence of proof-texts, mainly from the NIV and ESV, with Genesis 1:29 (the plant diet) as the anchor for the claim that eating animals departs from God's original design. Wescoe makes several empirical claims without citing any study. He says cow's milk is not needed by adults, that people who drink it have "higher incidences of osteoporosis," and that "humane" slaughter labels are deceptive.

The second half is a question-and-answer segment in which Lily reads questions from viewers. Wescoe answers on sin, repentance and pastoral humility, on whether leaders are held to a higher standard (citing an unnamed verse about teaching "little ones"), and on why some Christians resist veganism (he says they "twist scripture," citing 1 Timothy 4 without quoting it). He closes by previewing a future live talk on whether eating animals is sin and next week's talk on whether creation care belongs to the gospel message. The talk ends with a prayer.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The five-reason framework
Wescoe lists five reasons Christians neglect creation care: (1) ignorance, because teaching is incomplete or "backwards," supported by Romans 10:3 and Luke 6:39 ("can the blind lead the blind"); (2) tradition, with Mark 7:8 and Luke 6:36 set against inherited practices; (3) deception, with Proverbs 3:31, 2 Corinthians 11:3 and Romans 12:2 set against "the ways of the world"; (4) temptation, with Galatians 5:17 and 5:24 set against "the desires of the flesh"; and (5) for church leaders alone, fear of losing congregants and money, with John 6:66-67 and Zechariah 11:5. The talk's structure is essentially a list of proof-texts under each heading. It does not engage the historical context of any passage.

### 2. Genesis 1:29 as the baseline
Wescoe reads Genesis 1:29 as God's original diet of "fruit and vegetation," and treats any tradition of eating animals as a departure from that. He presents the serpent's line in Genesis 3 ("didn't God give you dominion over them") as a deception that Christians now repeat, which implicitly reads dominion as no license to kill animals. This is the talk's most important interpretive move, and it is offered without discussion of other readings.

### 3. Deception in animal agriculture
Wescoe argues that industry marketing is a form of deception. He calls "humane slaughter" a contradiction ("there's no humane way to kill someone who doesn't want to die") and says the claim that adults need cow's milk is false ("why would anyone other than a baby cow need cow's milk"). He also says shoppers who pay premiums for "humane" meat are "doing two wrongs instead of one": violence plus disguise. He ties the argument to environmental harm, saying cattle grazing drives rainforest clearance. No sources are named for these claims, though the talk briefly quotes an unnamed person on whether a practice would be acceptable if done to you.

### 4. Church leaders and the business conflict
This is the one argument Wescoe says applies specifically to pastors. He describes a conflict of interest: teaching creation care may make congregants leave for other churches, which costs tithes, donations and volunteer work. He acknowledges the financial pressure, especially for full-time pastors with families, and says leaders must "trust in God" and teach the truth anyway. He argues that omitting creation care is a larger mistake than losing members, invoking Zechariah 11:5, where the shepherds "have no compassion."

### 5. Sin, ideal, and the gospel (previewed)
In answering questions, Wescoe says that even if one doesn't call animal treatment a sin, it "falls short of his ideal." He previews a future talk on whether eating animals is sin, and says that next week he will argue that a gospel message without creation care is "incomplete." This is a teaser only, and the talk does not develop it.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Dominion means permission to use animals as one wishes.** Wescoe treats the serpent's suggestion in Genesis 3 ("didn't God give you dominion over them") as a deception, and argues that God's original diet in Genesis 1:29 is plant-based.
- **"Humane" meat is an ethical compromise.** He says "humane slaughter" is a contradiction and that buying "humane" products adds deception to violence.
- **Adults need cow's milk for bones and health.** He says this is a marketing deception and that people who drink it have more osteoporosis, a claim he does not source.
- **Creation care is peripheral to the gospel.** He says a gospel without it is "incomplete," though he defers the argument to next week's talk.
- **Church leaders who omit creation care are simply ignorant.** He argues that fear of losing money and members is a distinct, leader-specific motive.
