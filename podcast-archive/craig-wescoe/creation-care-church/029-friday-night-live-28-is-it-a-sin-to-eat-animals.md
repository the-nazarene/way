## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks whether eating animals is a sin according to the Bible, and answers yes. He argues in three stages: first, that Scripture explicitly calls eating certain animals a sin (mainly Leviticus 11 and Deuteronomy 14); second, that three further principles (Genesis 1:29-31 with James 4:17, the commands to love and be merciful, and Paul's concern about causing a brother to stumble and about evangelism) make eating animals sinful in general; third, that the apparent exceptions, chiefly Jesus eating broiled fish in Luke 24 and the feeding of the crowd with loaves and fish, should not be used to override those principles. He also says the animal sacrifices of the Old Testament were fulfilled in Jesus' death, so they do not justify eating animals now.

The talk is a live session with Craig carrying nearly all of it. Chat questions are read aloud and answered in between, and they cover humane-slaughter labels, the Sabbath and the Ten Commandments, milk, covenant sacrifices, factory-farm footage, and "willful blindness." Craig ends by announcing next week's topic, how to overcome the temptation to eat animal products, and closes with prayer.

Craig's sources are almost entirely the biblical text as he reads it aloud. Several of his textual claims (about the Greek word in Luke 24 and about manuscript variants) are stated without naming a manuscript or scholar, so the group should check them before relying on them.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Explicit prohibitions in the Law
Craig reads Leviticus 11 as a list of animals whose eating is "sin": pigs, camels, rabbits, and hares (cloven hooves and cud, 11:4-8); shellfish and anything without fins or scales (11:12); birds of prey and bats (11:13-19); flying insects that walk on four legs (11:20); animals that walk on paws, including dogs and cats (11:27); and small ground creatures (11:29-30, 11:42). He says the list is not exhaustive because the stated criteria cover further creatures. He adds Deuteronomy 14:21 (no eating of what "dies of itself," and no boiling a kid in its mother's milk) and Genesis 9:4 with Leviticus 17:10 (no eating flesh with its blood). He argues that draining blood "mostly" does not satisfy this. He also argues that Acts 15:29 forbids "strangled" meat, and that slaughter by gas (which he describes as asphyxiation) counts as strangling. He frames the Old Testament restrictions as still binding and says a pastor's claim that they are "done away with" is wrong.

### 2. Known good and the very good diet (Genesis 1:29-31 and James 4:17)
Craig reads God's diet in Genesis 1:29-31 (seed-bearing plants and fruit) as "the very good diet" God gave. He pairs this with James 4:17 ("to him who knows to do good and does not do it, to him it is sin") and concludes that eating animals, knowing this diet, is sin. The argument depends on Craig's reading of Genesis 1 as a prescription rather than a description, which he does not discuss.

### 3. Love and mercy as the governing principle (1 Corinthians 16:14, Luke 6:36, Matthew 5:46-48, Psalm 36:6)
Craig argues that "do everything in love" (1 Corinthians 16:14), "be merciful just as your father is merciful" (Luke 6:36), and "be perfect" as God is perfect (Matthew 5:48, which he reads as "complete," showing love indiscriminately) apply to animals. He cites Psalm 36:6 ("you preserve both man and animal") as showing God's care for animals. He concludes that killing animals when one does not need to is contrary to love and mercy.

### 4. Causing a brother to stumble and the duty to evangelize (1 Corinthians 8:12-13, 9:22)
Craig reads Paul's "if food makes my brother stumble, I will never again eat meat" as applying to people whose consciences are wounded by animal killing. He then argues that Christians who eat animals will be seen as hypocrites by secular animal advocates, which undermines evangelism. He reads 1 Corinthians 9:22 ("I have become all things to all men") as Paul giving up ordinary comforts to reach others, and says putting diet above reaching the unsaved is sin. He presents these as the strongest in-house Pauline argument and invites the chat to affirm being "offended" by animal cruelty.

### 5. Exceptions and the "Jesus ate fish" objection (Luke 24:42-43; feeding of the crowd; John 14:12)
Craig's main reply is that the Luke 24 broiled-fish passage is textually unclear. He says some scholars read the Greek word "ichthyon" as something other than a dead sea creature (he suggests seaweed), and that a comparison of two documents shows variation (one adds honeycomb, one does not). He does not name the scholars or manuscripts. On the feeding of the crowd, he says that when Jesus later refers to the miracle, the reference mentions only loaves, so "fish" may have been added later. He grants that a starvation situation could be an exception. He then reads John 14:12 ("greater works") as Christ working in believers, and ties this to Revelation 21:4 and the kingdom: "we will have to stop killing and eating animals to make ourselves ready for this kingdom." He argues that using these exceptions to override the broader principles is "blaming Jesus" for one's own choices.

### 6. Sacrifice, covenant, and Eden
Craig says the animal sacrifices and "covenants" in the Old Testament were fulfilled in Jesus' voluntary death, and that the "blood of the new covenant" ends that system. He points out that Eden has no altar or sacrifice until after the flood (Genesis 8), arguing that God did not create animals to be sacrificed. He refers to earlier and upcoming talks on Abram's animal-halving covenant (Genesis 15) and Noah's covenant, without quoting those texts in this talk.

### 7. Humane slaughter, milk, and the Sabbath
- **Humane labels:** Craig calls "humane slaughter" a deceptive marketing scheme that lets buyers keep the purchase while avoiding guilt, so it adds deception to cruelty.
- **Milk:** He argues that drinking milk meant for a calf is covetousness (desiring what is intended for another), and cites the commenter's reading of Rav Cook.
- **Commandments:** He argues that "thou shalt not kill" (Exodus 20) includes animals, because the Sabbath commandment in the same chapter includes animals, and that the commandment against coveting a neighbor's donkey shows animals are among those addressed.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Nowhere in the Bible does it say eating animals is sin."** Craig says Leviticus 11, Deuteronomy 14, Genesis 9, and Acts 15 explicitly name certain animals or practices as sinful.
- **"The Old Testament food laws were done away with (e.g., Jesus' "unwashed hands" teaching)."** Craig says Jesus did not abolish these prohibitions, and that treating them as abolished would mean God allows dogs and cats.
- **"Jesus ate fish, so eating animals is fine."** Craig argues the Luke 24 and feeding passages are textually uncertain and, even if accepted, do not override the broader principles of love and mercy.
- **"The Ten Commandments only apply to people."** Craig reads the Sabbath commandment as including animals, so the command against killing also includes them.
- **"Humane slaughter is a real option."** Craig calls the label a deception that adds sin to cruelty.
- **"Animal sacrifices were God's purpose for animals."** Craig says Eden had no sacrifice, and that the sacrificial system was fulfilled in Jesus.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Torah:** Craig's method is to apply Leviticus 11 and Deuteronomy 14 as binding law for Christians. This is the most direct overlap with the repo's question about what Jesus and his earliest followers would have kept, though Craig does not discuss Jewish practice or Jesus' own attitude toward the food laws in any depth.
- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Craig leans heavily on Paul (1 Corinthians 8-9) for his conclusion, and uses Jesus' eating of fish as an exception to be set aside. This is a case where the talk resolves a Jesus-vs-Paul tension by letting Paul's letters govern.
- **Kingdom on earth:** Craig ties the Lord's Prayer ("your kingdom come") and Revelation 21:4 to a present obligation to stop killing animals.
- **Sacrifice:** Craig's argument that Jesus' death ended animal sacrifice is a Christian reading of the sacrificial system, not a historical one. The repo may want to compare it with other readings.
- **Gospels and canon:** Craig says Luke 24 and the feeding texts are uncertain because of manuscript variation and possible later additions. He does not name the manuscripts or the scholars, so these claims should be checked before being relied on.
- **Resurrection:** The Luke 24 broiled-fish passage is one of the few places the talk touches the resurrection, and Craig's handling of it depends on a disputed reading.
