## Summary

Wescoe asks whether animals will be judged by God on Judgment Day, and if so, whether they can be saved and how. He frames two views: animals are innocent and so are not judged, since humans brought sin and death into the world (Romans 5:12; Habakkuk 2:17); or animals are judged, because they share some responsibility and agency. He says he has long held the first view but now takes the second seriously, and he sets out the evidence for it. His main lines are: Revelation 20:12-14 says "the dead" are judged and mentions the sea; the Bible treats animals as accountable (Genesis 9:5; Exodus 21:28-29) and as capable of "evil" (the Hebrew *ra*); the serpent in Eden was a real animal and not only Satan (Genesis 3); Balaam's donkey reasons and makes moral choices (Numbers 22); and animals appear in the ark (1 Peter 3:20-21), in praise (Revelation 5:13) and in the promised Spirit (Joel 2:28).

He does not settle the question. He says he doesn't know which view is true, that both are defensible, and that the practical duty to be good stewards is the same either way. The talk is a live Q&A: after the prepared argument, he reads viewer questions (from unnamed participants) on animal relationships in Eden, abuse of animals, whether the serpent is Satan, animals and the Spirit, and Romans 8. He answers each from the same texts, then previews a next talk on whether defending animals or defending people's freedom to harm them is "more christ-like."

## Key Teachings

### 1. The two views and the stewardship conclusion
Wescoe presents the "animals are innocent" view as straightforward, resting on Romans 5:12 and the fall of Adam and Eve. The "animals are judged" view, he says, needs more defense. He argues that either way the practical implication is the same: humans should show mercy and stewardship, and "it's more of like a theoretical kind of topic." He contrasts this with diet and treatment of animals, which he calls practical. He returns to this in the closing and the Q&A, where he calls it an issue on which "two believers" can agree to disagree.

### 2. Revelation 20: "the dead" and the sea
In Revelation 20:12-14 the dead are judged "according to what they had done," and Wescoe notes the text says "the dead," not "the dead people." He says this could include dead animals, though he calls it a large assumption on its own. He then argues the mention of the sea ("the sea gave up the dead who are in it") is odd if only humans are meant, since humans do not live in the sea. He links this to a pattern in Genesis 9:2 and Genesis 1:26 in which God lists animals by habitat (earth, air, sea) when speaking of them collectively. Death and Hades being thrown into the lake of fire leads him to ask what happens to animals if the graves are emptied. He presents this as the view's main textual basis.

### 3. Animal accountability: Genesis 9:5, Exodus 21:28-29, and "evil beasts"
- Genesis 9:5 says God will require the lifeblood of an animal that kills a person. Wescoe notes that some read this as a physical execution of the animal, others as a sign of spiritual judgment. He says either way it shows animals held accountable.
- Exodus 21:28-29 (the goring ox) shows a limited human responsibility for animal actions: an ox that kills someone is stoned, and an owner who knew of the ox's habit and did not confine it is also put to death. Wescoe reads this as animals sharing some responsibility.
- Ezekiel 34:25 (King James Version) speaks of "evil beasts." He says the Hebrew word *ra* is the same word used for "evil" in the prohibition about the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, and that it appears in the phrase "evil beasts" in Genesis 37:20, Leviticus 26:6, Ezekiel 5:17 and Ezekiel 14:15. He says the context in these verses points to an inclination toward violence and harm, and that animals can therefore act from an "evil inclination."

### 4. The serpent was a real animal, not only Satan
Wescoe grants that Satan is involved (Revelation 12:9 calls the serpent of old "Satan"). He uses Matthew 16:23, where Jesus calls Peter "Satan" but means the thought was Satan's, to argue that Genesis 3 can mean a real serpent motivated by Satan. Evidence he gives: Genesis 3:1 says the serpent was "more cunning than any beast of the field"; Genesis 3:14 curses it "more than all cattle and more than every beast of the field," with the crawling on the belly; and Isaiah 65:25 says dust will be the serpent's food in the kingdom, so the serpent is again described as an animal among animals. He says this made him revise his earlier reading.

### 5. Animal reason and moral agency: Balaam's donkey and the serpent
Numbers 22:30 and 22:33 show Balaam's donkey reasoning with Balaam and choosing, he argues, a morally good act (turning aside to save his life). He calls this a "christ-like" action. Genesis 3:4-5 shows the serpent using reasoning to deceive Eve, a morally evil use of reason. Wescoe concludes animals can reason and make moral choices, so they could in principle be held to account.

### 6. Salvation, the ark, the gospel to "every creature" and the Spirit
- 1 Peter 3:20-21: Wescoe reads the ark as a picture of salvation through Christ, noting that the ark held many animals as well as the eight people.
- Psalm 36:6 (English Standard Version) says God "saves" or "preserves" man and beast.
- Mark 16:15 has Jesus say to preach "to every creature."
- Romans 10:9 (confess Jesus as Lord) is the main objection: animals cannot declare Jesus is Lord, so they cannot be saved. Wescoe answers with Revelation 5:13, where "every creature" blesses the one on the throne and the Lamb, which he reads as animals declaring Jesus Lord.
- Joel 2:28 promises the Spirit on "all flesh" (*kol basar*), which he reads as including animals. He points to Isaiah 11:6-9 as animals bearing the fruit of the Spirit (peace, absence of fear, a non-violent diet), tied to Galatians 5:22-23 and 1 John 4:18.

### 7. Human and animal fates: Ecclesiastes 3:19 and Romans 8
Ecclesiastes 3:19 says humans have no advantage over animals, which he takes as evidence for shared fate. In the Q&A on Romans 8:19-22 (creation "groans" and waits for the children of God), he argues the groaning creation includes animals, and that the "children of God" are those who exercise a Christlike, not violent, dominion (citing Proverbs 12:10). He ends by saying that all of creation's liberation depends on taking part in Christ's salvation.
