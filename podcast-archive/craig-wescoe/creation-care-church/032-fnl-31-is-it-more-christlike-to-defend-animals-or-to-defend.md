## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks whether it is "more Christlike to defend animals or to defend people's freedom to hurt animals." He answers that defending animals is the more Christlike position. He builds the case mainly from character lists. He starts with humility (James 4:6, Philippians 2:3, Mark 10:45), then moves through love, the fruit of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22-23), mercy (Luke 6:36, Matthew 18:33-35), and care for the vulnerable (James 1:27, Proverbs 12:10, Proverbs 31:8). For each trait he asks which side, defending animals or defending the freedom to harm them, better embodies it.

His most specific evidence is the temple cleansing (John 2:15-16 and its Synoptic parallels). Wescoe argues it was an act of animal liberation directed at the sale of animals for sacrifice, not mainly at the money changers. He leans on the Luke 19 account, which does not mention money changers, and on the quotation of Jeremiah 7:11, which he links to the chapter's critique of animal sacrifice (Jeremiah 7:22). He then argues that the believer's duty to preach the gospel (Luke 4:43, Mark 16:15) outranks personal freedom, citing Romans 14:15 and Galatians 5:13. In his telling, a Christian who defends harming animals undermines their witness to animal advocates who are not believers.

The talk is a live Friday session with an open chat. Craig answers questions read aloud: whether dominion means service (Genesis, Psalm 36:6), how to respond to Christians who refuse animal advocacy, and whether welfare reform counts as a step. He says reform is "less bad" but should not be the end goal. He closes by announcing next week's topic, whether Jesus ate lamb at Passover, and ends with prayer.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The character of Christ as the test
Wescoe frames the question as one of "character." He takes a sequence of virtues and asks, for each, which position embodies it. Humility comes first (James 4:6, Philippians 2:3, and Mark 10:45, where Jesus says he "did not come to be served but to serve"). Love follows (1 John 4:16, 1 John 4:19). He then uses the fruit of the Spirit list in Galatians 5:22-23 and the wisdom list in James 3:17 ("full of mercy and good fruits"), and concludes that kindness, peace, and self-control point toward defending animals. He presents the argument as a reading of text, not as a separate ethical theory.

### 2. Caring for the vulnerable and speaking for the voiceless
Wescoe argues that pure religion (James 1:27) means caring for those whom society neglects. He sees widows and orphans as the model, since they lack a provider and "fall through the cracks." He then contrasts this with the scribes who "devour widows' houses" (Luke 20:46-47). He applies the category to animals, calling them the most vulnerable beings in animal agriculture. He cites Proverbs 12:10 ("a righteous man cares for the needs of his animal") and Proverbs 31:8 ("speak up for those who cannot speak for themselves... appointed to die"). He ties the second to his claim that calves in agriculture are given a number and a kill date at birth. That is his own description of the practice and is unsourced in the talk. He also reads Isaiah 58:6 as a call to "loose the bonds of wickedness" and "break every yoke," and says that the yoke image describes animal exploitation most directly.

### 3. The temple cleansing as animal liberation
This is the central historical argument. Wescoe says the event probably happened twice, once at the start of John's gospel and again near the end of Jesus's ministry in the other accounts. He reads John 2:15-16 as Jesus driving out "the sheep and the oxen," releasing animals, and overturning tables. He argues that the Luke 19:45-46 version omits money changers entirely, so "those selling animals were the primary antagonists." He asks why Jesus would quote Jeremiah 7:11 ("a den of robbers") instead of verses on dishonest scales, which he says Proverbs contains. He reads the Jeremiah context as God disclaiming animal sacrifice (Jeremiah 7:22). He concludes that critics who say the event was only about money "are missing a huge part of what Jesus was doing." The argument depends on the wording of the Luke text and on Wescoe's reading of Jeremiah. He does not cite any commentary or scholarship on the event.

### 4. Gospel witness takes priority over personal freedom
Wescoe argues that Jesus's mission was preaching the kingdom (Luke 4:43) and that the Great Commission extends it to "every creature" (Mark 16:15). He then reads Romans 14:15 as a principle that one's freedom should not override the ability to keep fellowship and preach the gospel. He describes conversations with non-believing animal advocates who say Christians who eat animals are hypocrites, and he claims this blocks evangelism. He reads Galatians 5:13 ("do not use your freedom to indulge the flesh") as the frame for this. The argument is strategic as well as moral: the gospel message is hindered if Christians look hypocritical about animals.

### 5. Dominion as servant stewardship, not license
Wescoe reads dominion (Genesis) as a call to serve rather than to exploit. He cites Genesis 2:15 for the garden to be "tended and kept" and the Genesis diet passage in the form "eat the fruit of the tree and the green plants of the ground that shall be your food." He argues that this diet is "truly godly dominion," which implies limits on killing animals. He uses the shepherd image (a good shepherd who lays down his life and leaves the ninety-nine) to suggest that humans should guard animals rather than use them. He reads Psalm 36:6, read from chat, as showing that God "preserves man and beast." He does not discuss how to reconcile this with the Genesis passages that permit animal food after the Flood.

### 6. Welfare reform as partial, not final
Asked about reducing consumption and moving to higher-welfare sourcing, Wescoe says that "less harm is better than more harm." He warns that a stopping point at welfare improvement falls "well short of the goal," which he says is to liberate animals completely and change how people see them. He treats "humane slaughter" as a "nonsensical term." He gives no scripture for this section, and it is his ethical judgment.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The temple cleansing was only about corrupt money changers.** Wescoe argues the animal trade was the main target, citing the Luke omission and the Jeremiah 7 quotation.
- **God doesn't care about animals.** Wescoe says Proverbs, Isaiah, and Psalm 36:6 show God's concern for animals, and that a Christian who thinks otherwise is misreading the text.
- **Dominion means the right to use animals however one wishes.** He reads dominion as servant stewardship modeled on Christ (Mark 10:45) and on the Genesis garden.
- **Freedom in Christ permits any conduct.** He cites Galatians 5:13 and Romans 14:15 to argue that freedom should not indulge the flesh or hinder witness.
- **Reducing harm to animals is enough.** He says lesser harm is "a step in the right direction" but warns that stopping there "falls kind of well short of the goal."
- **"Humane slaughter" is a meaningful term.** He calls it "a nonsensical term."
