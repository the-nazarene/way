## Summary

Wescoe asks whether Jesus ate lamb at Passover, and whether his followers should eat lamb today. He opens by conceding that no verse says Jesus ate lamb. The only direct evidence he finds is the account of Jesus eating fish after the resurrection, which he calls questionable as a translation. He describes the case that Jesus ate Passover lamb as an inference: if Passover required eating the lamb, and Jesus was without sin, then Jesus must have eaten it each year. He calls this reasoning open to question and looks for alternatives.

His main lines of evidence are the Old Testament Passover instructions (Exodus 12, Deuteronomy 16), the pilgrimage references (Luke 2:41-42, Mark 14:12), and the claim that Jewish groups such as the Essenes, as described by Philo and an unnamed historian, did not sacrifice animals. He also points to the Last Supper, where he says only bread and wine are mentioned, and to John's chronology, which places the crucifixion on the day of preparation for Passover. He treats the New Testament's language of Jesus as "the lamb" (John 1:29, 1 Corinthians 5:7, 1 Peter 1:19) as the basis for his conclusion that Jesus himself was the Passover sacrifice. Ending with a viewer Q&A, he concludes that whatever Jesus ate, Christians today need no animal sacrifice, and that eating a plant-based diet and "remembering" Jesus through unleavened bread is the fitting way to observe the feast.

Wescoe presents his conclusions as "I think" and "it would make more sense" throughout, and he invites the group to weigh the circumstantial evidence. He explicitly says the question is not settled in scripture, and he acknowledges that the case about James being vegetarian has no source he could cite on air.

## Key Teachings

### The inference chain, and its weak point
Wescoe states that there is "no scripture verse that says jesus ate lamb." Those who argue he did rely on an inference: if God required every believer to eat the Passover lamb each year, and Jesus was sinless, then Jesus must have eaten it. He says he will test whether this reasoning is valid, and he proposes that other lines of reasoning may make as much or more sense.

### Passover origins and the Jerusalem sacrifice
He reads Exodus 12:12-14 (the blood on the doorposts and the memorial feast) and Deuteronomy 16:5-6 (the Passover to be sacrificed at the place God chooses). He argues that the sacrifice came to be made only at the temple, because the tabernacle became a fixed temple. He ties this to Luke 2:41-42 (the family going up to Jerusalem each year, and Jesus at age twelve) and Mark 14:12 ("customary to sacrifice the passover lamb"). He reads the pilgrimage as showing Jesus observed Passover annually, but says this is "not definitive evidence that he ate lamb."

### The Essenes and the rejection of animal sacrifice
Wescoe cites Philo, a first-century Jewish philosopher and historian, saying the Essenes were "not sacrificing living animals." He also cites an unnamed historian who said they renounced private possessions, and a source he attributes to Vermes (p. 47) on the "rejection of animal sacrifice." He notes the Dead Sea Scrolls may relate to one Essene group. He argues that Jesus, who harshly criticized the Pharisees and Sadducees but said nothing negative about the Essenes, may have shared their view on animal sacrifice. He links this to Acts 4:32 (believers holding all things in common), which he reads as Essene-like. He says this is "just the first piece of evidence" and acknowledges that the Essene connection is uncertain.

### The Last Supper: bread and wine, and Jesus as the lamb
He observes that the Last Supper mentions bread and wine but no lamb. He reads 1 Corinthians 5:7, 1 Peter 1:19 and John 1:29 as calling Jesus the Passover lamb, and argues that if Jesus is the lamb sacrificed at that Passover, there would not also have been a second lamb. He points to 1 Corinthians 11:24 ("this is my body which is broken for you") to argue that if a lamb had been on the table, Jesus would have used it to make his point. He notes that Luke's mention of the customary lamb may be explanatory, written for a non-Jewish audience.

### John's chronology and the preparation day
He reads John 19:14 ("the preparation day of the passover") to argue that Jesus was crucified on the day the lambs were slain, so he was not eating lamb with his disciples. A viewer raised the Greek word *protos* in Mark 14:12, suggesting the Last Supper may have been the day before Passover; Wescoe calls this "certainly a possibility" and says he would need to examine it more carefully. He also grants that the Last Supper could have been the preparation day, when only unleavened bread was eaten after leaven was removed from the house.

### Once-for-all sacrifice and the ethics of diet
Wescoe reads Hebrews 10:4, 8 and 10 to argue that Christ's single offering ended the need for animal sacrifice. He concludes that anyone still sacrificing animals shows "lack of faith in christ." He reads John 6:56-57 and John 15:9-10 as teaching that "eating the flesh" of Jesus means keeping his command to love, and he argues that "sparing their lives" and eating the plant-based diet of Genesis 1:29 is the way to live this out. He says the unleavened bread can still be eaten in remembrance, as communion, but no animal needs to be killed.

- **"Jesus must have eaten lamb because he was sinless."** He calls this inference open to question, since the premise (that the Passover lamb was obligatory) is itself an assumption.
