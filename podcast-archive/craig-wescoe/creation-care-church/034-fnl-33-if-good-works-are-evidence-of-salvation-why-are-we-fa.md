## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks, in this live Friday session of "Creation Care Church," why people who believe they are saved fail to do good works toward animals, given that good works are supposed to be evidence of salvation. He first defines salvation as deliverance from "eternal destruction separated from the presence of the lord" (2 Thessalonians 1:9), arguing it is permanent and not merely rescue from temporary enemies. He then argues that salvation comes "by grace through faith" (Ephesians 2:8-10), is not earned, and produces visible fruit. He cites James 2:17-18 on dead faith, John 3:16, Romans 10:9, Matthew 7:21, Matthew 12:33, Galatians 5:22-23, 2 Peter 1 and 2 Corinthians 13:5 as a set of "tests" a believer can apply to themselves. The fruit of the Spirit includes kindness, and Craig concludes that food choices which depend on animal suffering (meat, dairy, honey) are a daily failure of kindness.

Most of the talk is a sequence of verse readings followed by his explanation of each. The second half is a question-and-answer session with chat participants on faith versus belief, baptism, sanctification, disability and salvation, and whether animals are saved. Craig repeatedly says salvation is not earned and that works are evidence, not a cause. He treats "that's not a salvation issue" as a trap and says believers should aim at what is best in God's eyes regardless of their own benefit. The talk closes with a prayer for God's kingdom "on earth as it is in heaven" and a preview of next week's session on the aroma of Noah's burnt offering (Genesis 8).

Craig does not engage any historical source, scholar or textual variant. His positions rest on his own reading of the verses and on the general sense of the passages. Readers should treat his animal-ethics conclusion as his inference from the fruit-of-the-Spirit lists, since he does not cite a text that addresses food directly.

## Key Teachings

### Salvation is by grace through faith, and works are its evidence
Craig reads Ephesians 2:8-10 and says salvation "isn't something that we earn through our own works," but that once it occurs it "will give rise to a sort of transformation." He pairs this with James 2:17-18, which he takes to mean that faith without works "is dead." He treats James and Paul as making the same point, so the two do not conflict in his reading.

### Belief alone is not enough (John 3:16, James 2:19, Romans 10:9, Matthew 7:21)
He argues that belief is necessary but insufficient, using James 2:19 ("even the demons believe") as the reductio. He adds Romans 10:9 (confession with the mouth plus belief in the resurrection) and Matthew 7:21 (doing "the will of my father") to show that confessing Jesus as Lord must be joined to doing God's will. He summarizes the "evidence of salvation" as believing, declaring Jesus as Lord, maintaining faith, and doing God's will.

### The fruit test (Matthew 12:33, Galatians 5:22-23, 2 Peter 1:5-8, 2 Corinthians 13:5)
Craig reads the tree-and-fruit saying as a parable about people, and sets the fruit of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22-23) beside the list of virtues in 2 Peter 1:5-8. He says the qualities "will keep you from being ineffective and unproductive," and that believers should "test" themselves against them, as 2 Corinthians 13:5 instructs. He says growth is gradual, so failing some of the tests does not prove a person is unsaved, but it signals a need for prayer.

### Kindness to animals as a failed test (Titus 3:4-5, Galatians 5, Romans 12:1-2)
He argues that God saves "not because of righteous things we had done but because of his mercy" (Titus 3:4-5), so kindness and mercy are divine attributes that the saved reflect. Applying Galatians 5, he says that "every time we sit down to eat" believers can either show kindness or "withhold kindness," and names the consumption of animal flesh, milk meant for their young, and honey stored for winter as examples. He concludes that believers should abstain from things that "cause harm to animals." This is his own application; he does not cite a passage that addresses animal food.

### Sanctification and growth (2 Peter 3:18, 2 Timothy 2:21, Romans 12:1-2, Matthew 25)
He describes salvation as "the beginning" of a process of growth. He reads Romans 12:1-2 as calling believers to be a "living sacrifice," meaning a life lived for God, not literal self-destruction, and to separate from "the ways of the world." For the uneven growth of believers he uses the parable of the talents, warning against comparing oneself to others with more gifts.

### Ignorance, disability and who is saved
In answer to a chat question about children, animals, and people with disabilities, Craig says they are not automatically lost. He offers ignorance and growth as explanations for uneven fruit and says God alone is "the discerner of hearts." He cautions against concluding one is unsaved because one cannot do what others do.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"We are saved by the blood of Jesus no matter what, so we may eat animals since grace is not through works."** Craig answers, using James 2, that faith without works is dead, so a faith that is content with cruelty is not the living faith the New Testament describes. He notes this is a question raised in outreach by a chat participant (Tanya).
- **"Works don't matter because salvation is by grace."** He says works are not the means of salvation but the evidence of it, and that dismissing them is itself a "trap."
- **"Salvation is earned by good works."** He insists Ephesians 2:8-10 rules this out, and that no one is deserving.
- **"Belief alone is enough."** He argues from James 2:19 and Matthew 7:21 that belief must be joined to works and to the doing of God's will.
- **"Faith and belief are the same thing."** He says they overlap but that belief is more conceptual and faith more like devotion or courage. He encourages viewers to check the Greek and Hebrew terms themselves.
- **"A person who cannot grow in Christ, such as a young child or someone with a disability, is lost."** He says no, citing ignorance, growth and God's role as the discerner of hearts.
