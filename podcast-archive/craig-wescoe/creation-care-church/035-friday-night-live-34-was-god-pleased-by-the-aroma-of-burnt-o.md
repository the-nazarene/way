## Summary

Craig, host of this Creation Care Church live Q&A, asks whether God was actually pleased by the "pleasing aroma" of burnt offerings. The phrase appears first in Genesis 8:20-21, after Noah's sacrifice following the flood. The standard reading, as Craig describes it, is that God enjoyed the smell and therefore blessed the sacrificer. He argues that this reading does not fit the surrounding text or the rest of Scripture.

His main evidence has three parts. First, Genesis 8:21 pairs the aroma with God's statement that human hearts are evil, as Genesis 6:5 said before the flood, and with a promise not to curse the ground, which echoes the curse after the fall in Genesis 3:17. Second, the phrase is applied to offerings made to idols in Ezekiel 6:13. Since Psalm 115:4-6 says idols cannot smell, Craig concludes the aroma cannot mean pleasing to the recipient. He reads it instead as pleasing to the senses, like the fruit that was "pleasing to the eye" in Genesis 3:6, which the flesh-versus-Spirit contrast in Galatians 5:17 sets against God's will. Third, he cites Hosea 6:6 ("I desire mercy not sacrifice") and Ephesians 5:2 to argue that Christ's single sacrifice is the only one pleasing to God and that it ended animal sacrifice.

The talk ends with a question-and-answer segment. Craig explains that animal sacrifice was not instituted by God in Genesis, that its first explicit instruction comes in Genesis 22, and that it was given through Moses as a way of redirecting worship from other gods. He closes by announcing that next week's talk will address why God blessed Noah and made a covenant with him in Genesis 9, which he expects to be read as God's approval by the "standard interpretation."

## Key Teachings

### Genesis 8:20-21 is framed by the flood and the fall
Craig reads Genesis 8:20-21 (NIV) and notes that the sentence containing "the Lord smelled the pleasing aroma" continues with "never again will I curse the ground because of humans" and "every inclination of the human heart is evil from childhood." He points out that Genesis 6:5 uses almost identical wording for the wickedness that led to the flood, and that Genesis 6:13 describes God's decision to destroy the earth. He says the curse on the ground echoes Genesis 3:17. From these parallels he reads three "indicators" that God is displeased rather than pleased, and says the aroma is the only element that might suggest approval.

### "Pleasing aroma" cannot mean pleasing to the recipient
Craig cites Exodus 29:18 as a typical use of the phrase for burnt offerings to Yahweh. He then cites Ezekiel 6:13 (ESV), where the phrase describes offerings made to idols. If the phrase meant pleasing to the recipient, it would mean idols were pleased. Craig reads Psalm 115:4-6 (NIV) to argue that idols have noses but cannot smell. He says the New Testament makes "essentially the same" point through Paul, though he gives no reference. He concludes that the aroma must be pleasing in some other sense, and he raises the question "who is pleased by the aroma if not the recipient." His answer is that the phrase describes the offerer's or author's perspective.

### Sense pleasure versus God's pleasure: Genesis 3:6 and Galatians 5:17
Craig points out that in Genesis 3:6 the forbidden fruit is "pleasing to the eye," yet God is clearly displeased by the eating. He proposes that "pleasing aroma" should be read the same way, as a sensory pleasure that runs against God's will. He links this to Paul's contrast in Galatians 5:17 between "the flesh" and "the spirit," and he applies it to modern meat-eating, asking whether people burn animals "because it's pleasing to the nose" or are showing mercy to God's creatures. He says God's displeasure at the offerings is what stands behind the Genesis 8 wording, and he claims that the animals' fear of humans described in Genesis 9 is a result of that displeasure.

### What God desires: Hosea 6:6 and Ephesians 5:2
Craig reads Hosea 6:6 (NIV): God desires "mercy not sacrifice and acknowledgement of god rather than burnt offerings." He treats this as an explicit statement of divine preference. He then reads Ephesians 5:2 (New Living Translation), which says Christ "offered himself as a sacrifice for us a pleasing aroma to God." He argues that Christ's once-for-all sacrifice is the only one that was pleasing to God and that it ended the earlier animal sacrifices, which he calls foreshadowings.

### Origins and purpose of animal sacrifice (Q&A)
In response to viewer questions, Craig says the first explicit mention of an animal sacrifice is Noah's, though he notes the text does not say God instructed it. He says the first explicit divine instruction to sacrifice is Genesis 22 (Abraham and Isaac), which he reads as a foreshadowing of God's "only begotten son." Animal sacrifice was codified under Moses. Craig argues that sacrifice was instituted in a world where people already sacrificed to other gods, so God used it to redirect worship toward himself, in a gradual process ("a refining process") that moved toward the "greatest commandment" of wholehearted devotion to God and love of neighbour.

### Everything, including the bad, points to Christ
Asked whether the "pleasing aroma" points to Christ, Craig agrees that "everything in scripture" points to Christ, but argues this does not make the foreshadowed thing good. He gives the examples of the murder of Jesus and of Judas's betrayal, both of which he says were "necessary to fulfill scripture" without being good. He reads 2 Corinthians 2:14-16 to argue that Christians are "the aroma of Christ," an aroma of life that comes through Jesus, in contrast to an aroma of death that comes through Adam.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The standard reading: God is pleased by the smell of burnt animals and so blesses the sacrificer.** Craig argues the phrase cannot mean pleasing to the recipient, since it is also used for idol offerings, and reads it as sensory pleasure opposed to God's will.
- **If an event points to Christ, it must be good.** Craig says the murder of Jesus and Judas's betrayal point to Christ without being good. Things that foreshadow Christ are not thereby approved.
- **God instituted animal sacrifice from the start.** Craig says Genesis 8 does not record God instructing Noah to sacrifice, the first explicit instruction is in Genesis 22, and sacrifice was later regulated through Moses in a world already sacrificing to other gods.
