## Summary

Wescoe takes up the problem of evil: if God is all-good and all-powerful, how can human and animal suffering exist? He frames it as a challenge going back to Epicurus and says it was the problem that led Darwin to become an atheist. He explicitly does not claim to "solve it once and for all," and he works from Scripture rather than from philosophical argument. He opens by citing Psalm 145:9 ("the Lord is good to all") as the premise that God is entirely good.

His answer has three main parts. First, he argues that the possibility of evil must exist for goodness to exist, and reads the Eden story (Genesis 2:9, 2:17, 1:31, 3:17) as God creating a very good world with the forbidden tree as a "potential" for evil, which humans then actualized by disobeying. Second, he ties this to free will and the image of God: humans were made as children who could act against their loving nature, not as programmed robots (1 John 4:16, Genesis 1:26, 1 John 3:2, 1 Peter 2:16). He adds a satanic deceiver (Genesis 3:13, Revelation 12:9). Third, he argues that suffering is temporary and that God's plan of restoration is already built in: Revelation 21:4 and 22:2, Isaiah 11:9, 2 Chronicles 7:14, and 2 Peter 3:8-9, which he uses to argue that a few thousand years is "a blink of an eye" from the vantage of eternity. On animal suffering, he cites Darwin's 1860 letter to Asa Gray about ichneumon wasps and argues from Genesis 1:30 that animals were originally created to be herbivorous and at peace.

The talk ends with a chat Q&A (on eternal suffering, Christian attitudes toward medicine and animal testing, whether science has been "hijacked," and suffering in the Christian life, drawing on Matthew and Philippians 4:13), a teaser for next week's talk on Romans 14:2-3 and "weak faith" in relation to vegetarianism, and a closing prayer. The evidence throughout is scriptural and largely cross-referential; the speaker offers no independent historical or textual analysis of the passages.

## Key Teachings

### 1. God's goodness as the starting premise
Wescoe treats the claim that God is wholly good as settled by Scripture. He cites Psalm 145:9 and 1 John 4:16 ("God is love") and says that "if you're accepting the authority of scripture," the goodness of God should be "kind of indisputable" (about 03:07 and 10:31). The challenge he takes up is therefore how to reconcile this premise with the observed existence of evil, not whether the premise holds.

### 2. Evil as a possibility required for good; the "best possible world"
Reading Genesis 2:9 and 2:17 with Genesis 1:31 ("very good"), he argues that the forbidden tree of knowledge of good and evil represents "the potential for evil." He says that "the possibility not the actuality but the possibility of evil existing" is necessary for good, and that God therefore "did actually create the best possible world" in which evil was potential but not actual (about 04:42 and 08:54). Humans then disobeyed (Genesis 3:17) and "brought evil into the world." He stresses that this makes the suffering "our own fault," and that God had warned them. Note that the "best possible world" framing is the speaker's own gloss on the Genesis narrative; the text itself does not use that phrase.

### 3. Free will and the image of God
Wescoe argues God did not create "robots" who "cannot do otherwise than to do good." Citing Genesis 1:26 (image and likeness), 1 John 3:2 ("beloved, now we are children of God"), and 1 Peter 2:16 (read from the BSB: "live in freedom but do not use your freedom as a cover-up for evil"), he argues that God's children must have the freedom to act contrary to their loving nature, and that this freedom is the necessary condition under which evil became actual (about 10:31 to 14:59). He also cites Genesis 6:12 ("the earth was corrupt") as evidence that humans corrupted what God had made very good.

### 4. The deceiver and the fall of Satan
He adds a second cause alongside human choice. Reading Genesis 3:13 ("the serpent deceived me") with Revelation 12:9 (the "serpent of old called the devil and satan"), he identifies the serpent with a "fallen angel," "lucifer," who "coaxed us into not believing in God" (about 19:14 to 21:38). This is a secondary cause in his account, not the primary one; he says humans were "unfortunately" persuaded by the deceiver.

### 5. Temporary suffering, restoration and the perspective of eternity
This is the central answer. Wescoe argues that suffering is a temporary state and that God built a safeguard into creation from the start (the tree of life, Revelation 22:2; Romans 8:18). He reads 1 Peter 5:10 ("after you have suffered for a little while") as a limit on the duration of suffering, and uses 2 Peter 3:8-9 to argue that a period of thousands of years is "a blink of an eye" from the vantage of eternity. Revelation 21:4 ("no more death nor sorrow nor crying"), 2 Chronicles 7:14 ("I will heal their land"), and Isaiah 11:9 ("they shall not hurt nor destroy") are presented as God's promise of restoration. Wescoe identifies the resurrection with "giving us that eternal life that he designed for us from the beginning."

### 6. Animal suffering: Darwin's letter and Genesis 1:30
Wescoe quotes Darwin's May 22, 1860 letter to Asa Gray, reading the passage about "too much misery in the world" and the ichneumon wasps (transcribed in the captions as "icumenidai" and "ichnumenodai") that lay their eggs in caterpillars (about 33:52 to 35:25). He then argues from Genesis 1:30 that "god didn't design animals to be this way": all animals were originally "created to be at peace and herbivores not killing and destroying each other," and predation is a result of the corruption that came with human sin (about 37:00). This is the speaker's own theological reading of Genesis 1:30 and is presented as the answer to the animal-suffering part of the question.

### 7. Suffering in the Christian life: Jesus, Paul and the Kingdom "at hand"
In the Q&A, Wescoe argues against what he calls a worldly church attitude that tries to eliminate suffering. He cites Matthew 4:17 (Jesus preaching "repent for the kingdom of heaven is at hand") and John 6:54 (Jesus as the "tree of life," the one who eats his flesh "has eternal life"). He says Jesus told followers they would face persecution, and presents Paul's account of shipwrecks, beatings and hunger as a model of joy in suffering. He reads Philippians 4:13 ("I can do all things through Christ who strengthens me") in its context of contentment and suffering (Philippians 4:10-13), and argues that the verse means enduring hardship, not achieving success. He also argues that modern science was "hijacked" by atheistic methodology; this is his own argument and is not tied to any named source.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That God's design includes animal predation and parasitism.** Wescoe argues, against the Darwin-style argument, that animals were created herbivorous and at peace (Genesis 1:30), and that predation is a result of the corruption that followed human sin.
- **That the existence of suffering proves God does not exist or is not good.** He argues that the premises of the problem of evil (God is all-powerful and all-good) are answered by the Fall narrative and the promise of restoration, and that suffering is temporary from the perspective of eternity (2 Peter 3:8-9).
- **That Philippians 4:13 means believers can achieve anything.** He reads it in context as the ability to endure hunger, shipwreck and imprisonment through Christ's strength.
- **That following Jesus means an easy, suffering-free life.** He argues Jesus said followers would face persecution and that the suffering-free kingdom comes later.
- **That the problem of evil is a neutral or purely philosophical objection.** He presents it as one of the main reasons people leave belief, and frames it as a pastoral challenge to address through Scripture.
- **That modern science is religiously neutral.** He argues it was "hijacked" by atheistic methodology and that its "meta-narratives" are unfalsifiable. This is his position and is not supported by a named source in the talk.
