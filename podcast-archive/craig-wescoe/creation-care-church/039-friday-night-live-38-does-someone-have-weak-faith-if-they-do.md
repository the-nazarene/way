## Summary

Craig Wescoe takes up the question in the title: does Romans 14 mean that someone who avoids animal flesh has weak faith? He argues that it does not. His case has three parts. First, he offers Daniel and his three companions as counterexamples, since the book of Daniel shows them eating only vegetables while displaying strong faith (Daniel 1:11-13; Daniel 3; Daniel 6). Second, he reconstructs the debate Paul is addressing. Following Acts 15:29, he says the dispute was about food offered to idols, not about meat as such. He also argues that the NIV's "weak faith" is a poor rendering, and that "sensitive conscience" (as in the NLT) or "weak conscience" (as in 1 Corinthians 8:12) fits better. Third, he applies Paul's principle that the burden falls on the person whose eating causes a brother to stumble. Craig concludes that abstaining from animal flesh is not a sign of weak faith, and that a scripturally grounded reason to abstain is a reason of faith (Genesis 1:29 is his example).

The talk ends with a question-and-answer segment, read from live chat. Topics include how to talk with non-vegan Christians, the liberation pledge, taking notes, and Matthew Scully's "show grace" line. Craig closes by previewing next week's question about whether believers should live the kingdom diet now or wait for Jesus to return.

## Key Teachings

### The Daniel counterexample
Craig begins with Daniel 1:11-13, where Daniel and three companions (Hananiah, Mishael, Azariah, later named Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego) ask for vegetables and water instead of the king's food and wine. He then cites Daniel 3:15 and 3:18, where the three refuse to worship Nebuchadnezzar's statue and are thrown into the furnace, and Daniel 6:7 and 6:16, where Daniel is thrown into the lions' den for praying to his God. His argument is that people who eat only vegetables have shown some of the strongest faith in Scripture, so the Romans 14 passage cannot mean they are weak. He adds: "something else must be going on." One caution for checking: the Daniel 6 passages he quotes do not mention diet. The vegetable detail is in Daniel 1, and his claim that "these four characters only ate vegetables" as a single settled point rests on combining the two chapters.

### The actual debate: food offered to idols
Craig reads Acts 15:29 (abstain from "things offered to idols," blood, strangled things, and sexual immorality) as the apostolic baseline. He says the dispute in Romans 14 concerns meat sold in the market or served at an unbeliever's table after it had been part of an idol-sacrifice, and that "it wasn't a debate about... being a vegan." In his reading, Paul acts as a mediator between a group that avoids such food entirely and a group that eats it when not told otherwise. He cites 1 Corinthians 8 as the parallel discussion of the same issue, and he refers listeners to a separate live talk on food that is sold or served.

### Translation: "weak faith" or "sensitive conscience"
Craig says the NIV is the only major translation that uses "weak faith" in Romans 14:2. The NLT renders it as "a believer with a sensitive conscience." In 1 Corinthians 8:12, the NIV has "weak conscience," which he takes as evidence that "weak faith" is a translation choice and not the Greek sense. He concludes that "sensitive conscience" is the best rendering, because the issue is caution about idolatry, not a deficiency of faith. (Readers should check his claim about the NIV against other translations and the Greek. The transcript does not show that check.)

### Two groups, two instructions
Craig argues that Paul does not give both groups the same advice. He uses Greek words to show the difference. Romans 14:3 and Romans 14:21 use exoutheneo ("treat with contempt"), which he links to Luke 23:11, where Herod and his men "treated him... with contempt" and mocked him. Romans 14:3 uses krino ("judge"), which he links to Luke 6:37. For the less cautious group (those who eat), Paul gives a specific instruction to stop: "it is good neither to eat meat nor drink wine" if it causes a brother to stumble (Romans 14:21) and "do not destroy with your food the one for whom Christ died" (Romans 14:15). For the cautious group, Paul gives no instruction to change. Craig reads this as showing that the "don't judge" line is not a blanket permission to abstain from criticizing others' food.

### The principle applied to animal flesh
Craig draws a principle from Romans 14:19 ("the things which make for peace and... mutual edification") and Romans 14:17 ("the kingdom of God is not food and drink"). He argues that the burden falls on the person whose eating causes division. Applied to animal flesh, he says eating meat hinders people who care about animal suffering from feeling in fellowship with the church, and may hinder new believers who care about animals from coming to faith. So, he says, "Stop eating animal flesh." He carries this into a claim about the "kingdom" priority: the kingdom is "not food and drink," but righteousness, peace and joy.

### Genesis 1:29 as a positive scriptural reason
Craig gives two reasons someone might avoid animal flesh that are faithful, not weak. One is the Romans 14 principle of not causing a brother to stumble. The other is following the diet God gave "from the beginning" in Genesis 1:29 (seed-bearing plants and fruit-bearing trees "for food"). He concludes: "there is no good scriptural evidence to suggest it has anything to do with weakness of faith."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Romans 14 condemns vegetarians as weak in faith.** Craig says the passage is about food offered to idols and Christians' consciences, and that abstainers are not weak. He points to Daniel's companions as strong examples.
- **Romans 14 is about vegans or meat-eating as such.** Craig says the debate concerned idolatry and food offered to idols, "nothing to do with that."
- **"Weak faith" is what Paul wrote.** Craig says the phrase is an NIV choice; the NLT and 1 Corinthians 8 point to "sensitive" or "weak" conscience.
- **Romans 14:3 means "don't judge anyone's food."** Craig says Paul gives specific instructions to the eating group to stop, so the passage is not a blanket ban on advising others about food.
- **Abstaining from meat is only a matter of preference or diet.** Craig argues that abstaining can be a faithful act grounded in Romans 14 (not causing a brother to stumble) or in Genesis 1:29.
