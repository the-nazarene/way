## Key Teachings

### The seven appointed times and the Sabbath
Craig says Leviticus 23 is the place where the seven appointed times are gathered together. The weekly Sabbath (Friday sunset to Saturday sunset) is the first, and it is paired with six annual feasts. Three are in spring (Passover/Unleavened Bread, First Fruits, Pentecost/Feast of Weeks) and three in autumn (Trumpets/*Yom Teruah*, Day of Atonement, Tabernacles/Booths). He stresses that the Sabbath also includes animals, citing the Ten Commandments in Exodus 20.

### The feasts are part of creation's original design
Craig's central interpretive claim is that the *moadim* predate the Fall. He reads Genesis 1:14 in the New King James and NIV, arguing that *moadim* means "sacred times" or "appointed times" rather than "seasons" in the sense of spring and summer. He then cites Genesis 2:3 (God blesses and sanctifies the seventh day) as the start of the Sabbath pattern. In his words, "unlike a lot of things that come after sin enters the world those things were established as part of god's very good design in the beginning." The argument is that the feasts are not merely Israelite law but part of the created order.

### Spring feasts as Jesus's death, resurrection and the Spirit
Craig maps the three spring feasts onto Jesus's ministry. Passover (Exodus 12, the lamb and the doorposts, with 1 Corinthians 5:7-8 as the New Testament parallel) corresponds to Jesus's death. First Fruits corresponds to his resurrection, which Craig links to 1 Corinthians 15:19-24 and Acts 26:22-23. Pentecost, counted fifty days from First Fruits (a "week of weeks," the Feast of Weeks), corresponds to the giving of the Holy Spirit in Acts 2 (Luke 24:49 is the bridge). He argues the pattern shows the feasts being "fulfilled precisely" on their appointed days, and that the same pattern will recur at Christ's return and the final resurrection. He also notes that Passover and Unleavened Bread are the same appointed time, while acknowledging that some people read them as two. Craig does not establish the historical date of the crucifixion or resurrection, only the scriptural pattern.

### Pentecost, Joel 2:28, and "all flesh"
Craig reads Acts 2:17 (Peter quoting Joel 2:28) as the start of a fulfillment. He argues that the Hebrew *kol basar* ("all flesh") is used consistently in Scripture for both people and animals, citing the Flood. From this he concludes that Joel's promise is about "all creatures" receiving the Spirit. He reads Revelation 5:13 (every creature in heaven, earth and sea praising the Lamb) as the final stage of that outpouring, which he calls "the final fulfillment uh just before the kingdom."

### The fall feasts and Jesus as high priest "forever"
For the autumn feasts, Craig takes the Feast of Trumpets (Revelation 11:15, with the king's announced arrival and Daniel 2:44) as the announcement of the kingdom, the Day of Atonement as the picture of judgment and repentance, and Tabernacles as joyful anticipation of permanent dwelling in God's kingdom (Hebrews 13:14, Philippians 3:20-21). On Atonement, he relies on Hebrews 6:20 and Hebrews 10:10 to argue that Jesus is high priest "forever" and that his self-offering was "once for all," so no further animal sacrifices or successor priests are needed. Observance today, in his view, means remembering this rather than sacrificing. He notes that Atonement is the only feast where fasting is explicitly commanded.

### Speaking in tongues as "the language of love"
In the Q&A, Craig reads Acts 2 as a reversal of Babel: the disciples were understood by people from many nations, so tongues were a way of "breaking through language barriers, not creating new ones." He says not everyone receives the same gift (citing Paul's lists of gifts), and that unintelligible tongues without an interpreter should not be used in public worship. He argues the future fulfillment is a universal language, which he identifies as love, understood by humans and animals alike, and he ties this to Isaiah 11:6 and the peacemakers of Matthew 5:9.

### Why observe the feasts, and the contrast with pagan holidays
Craig argues that humans have an "innate need to observe holy days," which he says is why the feasts appear before sin. When these are neglected, he claims, people adopt substitutes such as national donut day, Saturnalia, or "star wars day," which he treats as other gods' holy days. He cites Romans 12:2 ("do not be conformed to this world") and concludes that believers should compare what the world observes with what the Bible prescribes. He concedes that this will "probably not gonna happen overnight for most people."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Genesis 1:14's 'seasons' means the seasons of the year."** Craig argues the Hebrew *moadim* means sacred, appointed times, and that the translation "seasons" misleads.
- **"The feasts were done away with at the Cross."** Craig says Jesus fulfilled the feasts but did not abolish them. He says they still point to past acts and future ones, and that most of the Old Testament was "restated and not done away with."
- **"Every day is holy, so special days don't matter."** Craig argues that this view leads people to substitute secular or pagan holidays for God's appointed times.
- **"Every believer must speak in tongues."** Craig says Paul describes different gifts, so not everyone speaks in tongues.
- **"Speaking in tongues is ecstatic speech that no one understands."** Craig says the Acts 2 gift was intelligible to hearers in their own languages, and that unintelligible tongues without an interpreter should not be used in worship.
- **"Animal sacrifice and priestly succession are still required."** Craig argues Hebrews 6 and 10 show Jesus as high priest forever and the once-for-all sacrifice, so neither is needed.
