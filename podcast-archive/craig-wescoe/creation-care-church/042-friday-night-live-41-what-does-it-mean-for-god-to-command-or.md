## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks what it means for God to "command or instruct," using two Hebrew words, *torah* (usually rendered "law") and *savah* ("command"). He argues that the two words mean essentially the same thing, that they are used interchangeably in Scripture, and that "law" should be read as "instruction." He treats *torah* as a broader category than the Mosaic code, pointing to Genesis 26:5, where Abraham is said to have kept God's *torah* centuries before Sinai.

His main answer is that God's instructions are for human benefit, are meant to be kept from love rather than fear, and reduce to two commands from Jesus: be wholly devoted to God and love your neighbor as yourself (Mark 12:29-31, quoting Deuteronomy 6:4-5 and Leviticus 19:18). He reads Romans 13:10, 1 Corinthians 7:19 and Galatians 5:6 as saying the same thing in other words. He argues that the animal sacrifices of the Mosaic system were a foreshadowing that Jesus' once-for-all offering (Hebrews 10) fulfilled, so they no longer continue. He also extends "neighbor" to animals and argues that Christians should be vegan, with an emphasis on the welfare of slaughterhouse workers and on changing the system rather than blaming individual workers.

### Key Teachings

### 1. Torah and savah are near-synonyms, and torah predates Moses
Craig reads Malachi 2:6 ("his true instruction was in his mouth") and Exodus 24:12 (tablets "on which I've inscribed the instructions and commands") to show that *torah* means instruction in general. He says the word first appears in Genesis 26:5, where Abraham "obeyed me and did everything I required of him, keeping my commands my decrees and my instructions." He notes that Galatians 3:17-18 places the Law 430 years after the promises to Abraham, and concludes that Abraham's *torah* cannot mean the later Mosaic laws. He takes *torah* to be a wider category that includes the Mosaic instructions and the Ten Commandments, which are subsets of it. His first *savah* example is Genesis 2:16-17, the prohibition on eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, which he reads as the first command to humans.

### 2. Instructions as delight, and testing as refining
Craig argues that God's instructions should be a "delight" and not a burden, citing Psalm 119:174 (NLT). He reads Exodus 16:4, the manna test, as a sign that God tests people to see whether they will walk in his instructions, including the Sabbath arrangement and the failures recorded in the wilderness. He compares this testing to the refining of silver and gold, removing contaminants so that a person is restored to the image of God. He uses David's prayer ("test me, make me more like you") as a model attitude, and says he will return to refining "in some future live talks."

### 3. Some instructions weigh more than others
Using Matthew 23:23, Craig argues that Jesus ranked the commandments. He reads justice, mercy and faith as "the weightier matters," with tithing (including the mint, anise and cumin) as less important. Jesus' phrase "without leaving the others undone" means that the lesser commands still apply. Craig extends this principle to other commands, such as the doorpost and wrist reminders, which exist to point toward the weightier ones.

### 4. The two great commands, and a wide reading of "neighbor"
Craig reads Mark 12:29-31 as Jesus' answer to the question about the greatest commandment: love God with all your heart, soul, mind and strength, and love your neighbor as yourself. He says these two "hang all the law and the prophets" and summarize everything else. He reads "neighbor" as applying to animals, since Genesis presents humans and animals together as creatures to be fruitful, and he uses the Good Samaritan parable to argue that neighborly conduct means showing mercy to those in distress, not classifying people by lineage.

### 5. Love fulfills the law, and the motive changes under the new covenant
Craig reads Romans 13:10 ("love does no harm to a neighbor") as Paul's statement that love fulfills the law. He matches the Ten Commandments to this: the first three concern devotion to God, and the last seven concern harm to neighbors, such as adultery, theft and murder. He argues that Jeremiah 31:33 (the law written on the heart) and 1 John 4:18 ("perfect love casts out fear") mark a change in motive rather than a change in the commands. Obedience out of fear of punishment gives way to obedience from love, which Craig describes as the light dispelling darkness. The commands are still kept. He adds that the Holy Spirit enables this change, citing the fruits of the Spirit in Galatians 5:22-23 in the Q&A.

### 6. Sacrifice and the Mosaic system end with Jesus, and faith expressed through love remains
Craig argues that the Kingdom's "no death" condition (Revelation 21:4) makes animal sacrifice and capital punishment incompatible with the final state. He reads Hebrews 10:4 ("it is not possible that the blood of bulls and goats could take away sin") and Hebrews 10:10 (the once-for-all offering of Jesus' body) to show that sacrifices foreshadowed Jesus and became unnecessary. He links this to Hebrews 7:27 and the Melchizedek priesthood, and says the temple's destruction in 70 A.D. "kind of ended that practice" (his statement, with no source named). On circumcision he cites 1 Corinthians 7:19 ("keeping the commandments of God is what matters"), Galatians 5:6 ("faith working through love"), and 1 John 4:16 and 2 John 1:6 to define the essence of the commands as faith expressed through love.

### 7. Animals as neighbors, and a vegan conclusion
Craig reads Genesis 1:29 as a kingdom ideal in which humans eat plants, and argues that translations of "world" (John 3:16), "flesh," and "all flesh" have often been rendered to exclude animals, which he calls "anthropocentric" readings. He argues that to love one's neighbor includes not turning a blind eye to the treatment of animals. He says slaughterhouse workers often have PTSD at high rates and are often people with felony records or undocumented immigrants with few other options. He argues that the right response is to change the demand, to be vegan, and to address the system rather than blame the workers. He also cites pollution and asthma in low-income areas near factory farms as a reason to go vegan "even if you don't care about animals."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Torah" means only the Mosaic Law.** Craig argues it means instruction in general, and that Abraham kept God's *torah* before Sinai (Genesis 26:5).
- **"Law" and "command" are different things.** Craig argues that *torah* and *savah* mean essentially the same thing and are used interchangeably.
- **Jesus' new covenant abolished the commands.** Craig argues that the commands are still kept, but the motive changes from fear to love, and that sacrifice ended with Jesus' once-for-all offering.
- **"Neighbor" means only other humans.** Craig argues that God created humans and animals together, so animals are also neighbors.
- **Translations of "world," "flesh" and "all flesh" are neutral.** Craig argues they have often been rendered to exclude animals.
