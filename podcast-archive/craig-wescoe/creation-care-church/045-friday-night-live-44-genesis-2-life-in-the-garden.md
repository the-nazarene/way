## Summary

This is the second part of Creation Care Church's Friday Night Live series on Genesis, following a talk on Genesis 1. Craig Wescoe asks what life in the Garden of Eden was meant to be: how humans and animals related to God and to each other before the fall. The talk opens with a short Live Mercifully video, "We Are All Herbivores," which argues from Genesis 1:29-30 that the original diet was plant-based and that the Edenic peace is God's permanent will. Craig then works verse by verse through Genesis 2, using cross-references from Exodus, John, Jeremiah, Matthew, Proverbs, Revelation and 1 John to argue that the Sabbath is a covenant sign rooted in creation, that the "breath of life" links humans and animals, and that Adam's task of *shamar* (keeping, protecting) is a model of loving care.

His main method is to read the Hebrew terms (*shamar*, *ezer*, *ruach*, *nefesh*) in light of later Jesus and apostolic texts, treating words attributed to Jesus as authoritative guides to the meaning of the "beginning." He presents these readings as his own reasoning and invites objections; he does not cite outside scholarship. The talk ends with a Q&A stretch (a viewer question on whether Adam and Eve were ever commanded to keep the Sabbath), a closing prayer, and a preview of next week's topic: how and why God tests people. ## Key Teachings

### Sabbath as creation pattern and covenant sign
Craig reads Genesis 2:1-3 as showing that God's final "day" involves no creating, only rest, and that this sets a weekly pattern for human work. He ties this to Exodus 20:8-11, noting that the Sabbath command extends rest to children, servants, animals and sojourners, and to Exodus 31:16-17, which calls the Sabbath a "perpetual covenant" and "a sign between me and the children of Israel forever." He describes the Sabbath as a blessing and a time of communion and remembrance of the Creator, not a salvation mechanism. He adds that Jesus said the Sabbath was made for man and that Jesus is "lord of the Sabbath," using these as grounds for doing good on it (though he gives no verse for the Sabbath-for-man saying). In answer to a viewer's question about whether Adam and Eve were ever commanded to keep the Sabbath, he argues that the text does not say either way, but that the commandment is grounded in what was "established in the beginning," so some form of keeping was presumably present then.

### Breath of life and the human as living being
Reading Genesis 2:7, Craig argues that humans are not "just matter," because God breathed into Adam's nostrils the *ruach* that made him a *nefesh* (living being). He links this to John 20:21-23, where Jesus breathes on the disciples and says "receive the Holy Spirit," reading it as a new creation echoing Adam, and he links the "Word" of John 1 to the Creator of Genesis 1. He also notes that Noah's ark instructions describe animals as "every animal that has the breath of life" (he gives no verse; the context is Genesis 6-7), arguing that the breath of life is shared by animals. He cites Lazarus (John 11, no verse given) as an example of resuscitation versus the eternal life he expects in resurrection.

### Tree of life as divine wisdom and healing
Craig takes Genesis 2:8-9 and the warning about the tree of knowledge (Genesis 2:16-17) as setting up a contrast between life and death. He reads the tree of life as a riddle answered by Proverbs 3:13-18, where wisdom is "a tree of life to those who hold her." He then cites Revelation 22:2, where the tree's leaves are "for the healing of the nations," and reads the post-fall guarding of the tree (Genesis 3, cherubim; no verse given) as preventing an eternal life in sin. He concludes that access to the tree returns only once sin and death are finally removed, which he associates with the defeat of death and Hades in the lake of fire. He uses the Hebrew word *Beershet* for "river head" to argue that Eden is the elevated source from which the other rivers flow (Genesis 2:10-14).

### *Shamar*: keeping and protecting, not only gardening
Genesis 2:15 says Adam was placed in the garden "to tend and keep" it. Craig argues that *shamar* means more than farming: it means to maintain, preserve and protect, like a parent caring for a child or a good shepherd watching a flock. He sets this against Cain's reply in Genesis 4:9 ("Am I my brother's keeper?") as a failure of this calling, and he calls Jesus "the embodiment of this Shamar." He ties it to 2 John 1:5, where the command to love one another is described as "from the beginning," which he reads as the command given at creation. He concludes that humans are meant to watch over both other humans and animals.

### The helper (*ezer*), naming the animals, and covenant with animals
Genesis 2:18 says it is "not good that man should be alone," and Craig reads this as a call to community and fruitfulness, as well as a need for a partner to fulfil God's command. He argues that *ezer* does not imply subordination, citing John 14:26, where the Holy Spirit is the helper who teaches and reminds. He presents Adam's naming of the animals (Genesis 2:19-20) as more than taxonomy: naming signals a personal relationship and a covenant-type placement under human dominion. He compares this to God renaming Abraham (Genesis 17:5), Jesus renaming Peter (Matthew 16:18), and Saul becoming Paul (no verse given). He also says that after the flood God makes covenant with animals (he says "five times," citing Genesis 9 in general terms).

### Marriage, nakedness, fear, and diet
Craig reads Genesis 2:21-25 as the origin of marriage and cites Jesus' use of it in Matthew 19:3-8, where Jesus appeals to "from the beginning" over Moses' allowance for divorce because of hardness of heart. He applies the same logic to diet: the Mosaic permission to eat animals is a concession, and the original diet of fruit and plants (Genesis 1:29-30) is the ideal. He reads the nakedness of Genesis 2:25 against Genesis 3:9-11, where shame and fear lead to hiding from God. He then contrasts this with Genesis 9:2, where animals come to fear humans after the flood, and cites 1 John 4:18 ("perfect love casts out fear") to argue that a life of love involves neither human fear of God nor animal fear of humans. He presents this as a case for not killing animals, though he does not spell out the dietary conclusion in detail beyond the opening video's claims.

### Commandments written by love, not fear
Craig argues that the Ten Commandments were given on stone, but that Jeremiah 31:31-33 promises a new covenant with the law written on hearts and minds. He says the new covenant is fulfilled in Jesus, so people keep the law "not because we're afraid of punishment" but out of love. He summarizes this with Jesus' reduction of the commandments to loving God with all one's heart and loving one's neighbour as oneself (no verse given). A viewer's comment during the Q&A that the Sabbath no longer applies after the cross is presented as a commenter's view, which Craig does not endorse; he instead argues that Jesus fulfilled the law and that the Sabbath remains a blessing.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That God never commanded the Sabbath before Sinai, so it was only for Israel.** Craig argues that the Sabbath was established at creation (Genesis 2:1-3) and that the Ten Commandments refer back to it. He concedes the text does not say whether Adam and Eve were told to keep it.
- **That the Sabbath was abolished at the cross.** Craig argues that Jesus is "lord of the Sabbath," that the Sabbath is a perpetual sign and blessing, and that the law is fulfilled in love rather than abolished. (A viewer's comment in the Q&A makes the opposite claim, which Craig does not adopt.)
- **That humans are only physical matter.** Craig uses Genesis 2:7 to argue that the breath of God makes humans living beings in a spiritual sense.
- **That *shamar* simply means "gardener."** Craig argues it means protect, maintain and keep, with a broader sense of care.
- **That *ezer* ("helper") implies a subordinate.** Craig cites John 14:26 to argue the helper is indispensable, not inferior.
- **That Adam's naming of animals was only taxonomy.** Craig argues it signals personal relationship and covenantal placement under human dominion.
- **That the Ten Commandments were a fear-based law.** Craig argues that the new covenant writes the commandments on the heart so they are kept from love.
