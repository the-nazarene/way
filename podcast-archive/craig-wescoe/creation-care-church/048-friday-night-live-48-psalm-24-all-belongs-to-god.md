## Summary

Craig Wescoe, host of Creation Care Church's "Friday Night Live" series, works through Psalm 24 to ask what follows from the claim that "the earth is the Lord's." His answer is that God owns all creatures, including animals, so humans are stewards rather than owners. He argues that this ownership implies merciful treatment of animals and a plant-based diet. He supports this mainly by chaining verses across the Psalms, Genesis 1-2, Matthew, Revelation, John, and several Pauline letters, reading them as one canonical whole. The talk opens with an embedded clip by Live Mercifully arguing that Psalm 24:1 supports veganism, and Craig says he agrees with the clip's main points.

Most of the talk follows Psalm 24 verse by verse. Craig reads "clean hands and a pure heart" (Psalm 24:3-4) alongside Matthew 5:8 and Psalm 51:16-17, arguing that God prefers a "broken spirit" to animal sacrifice. He then treats Genesis 1:29 as God's original food instruction and cites 1 Timothy 4 against those who call abstaining from foods a doctrine of demons.

Throughout, the argument is presented as Craig's reading of the text rather than as established scholarship. ## Key Teachings

### 1. God owns all creatures; humans are stewards, not owners
Craig reads Psalm 24:1 in the New King James Version ("The earth is the lord's and all its fullness, the world, and those who dwell therein") and argues that "every animal, every creature, everything" belongs to God. He supports this with Psalm 50:10-12 ("every beast of the forest is mine") and Psalm 89:11-14. He argues that human dominion in Genesis 1:26-28 is a stewardship role, not a license for ownership. He also draws on Genesis 2:15, where Adam is placed "to tend and keep" the garden. He reads the Hebrew verb *shamar* as "take care of, protect," so the human role becomes preserving animals as God preserves them (Psalm 36:5-6, "you preserve man and beast"). He presents the Live Mercifully clip as making the same point and says he likes how it "talks about a topic that people usually gloss over."

### 2. Diet: plants as God's original food
Craig cites Genesis 1:29 ("the fruit of the tree and the green plants of the ground, that shall be your food") to argue that God gave humans plants, not animals, as food. He says this should shape how Christians treat animals. He adds Genesis 1:31 ("very good") and argues that the fruit of the Spirit in Galatians 5:22-23 (love, joy, peace, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control) reflects the "goodness" God saw in creation. Later, in answer to a chat question, he reads 1 Timothy 4:1-5 as condemning people who forbid foods God created "to be received with thanksgiving," and he says that a Christian who abstains from animal products and teaches it is not speaking the lies the passage describes. He also notes that the Old Testament forbids certain animals and blood, and that Paul's letters mention foods offered to idols. He uses this to argue that dietary abstention alone does not make someone a follower of demons.

### 3. Clean hands, pure heart, and a contrite heart rather than sacrifice
Craig reads Psalm 24:3-4 ("he who has clean hands and a pure heart") as requiring exclusive devotion (no idols) plus inner purity, and he pairs it with Matthew 5:8 ("blessed are the pure in heart"). He reads Psalm 51:10-17 ("create in me a clean heart") as the model for inner renewal and says that "the sacrifices of God are a broken spirit, a broken and contrite heart" (Psalm 51:16-17). He argues that the Psalm explicitly rejects burnt offerings and that "the god the creator of the universe" is not pleased by animal sacrifice, which he contrasts with "a lot of ancient religions." He also links Psalm 51:14 to the blood of Christ and forgiveness, and he links the broken heart and righteous action to loving animals as neighbors.

### 4. Seeking God's face and dwelling in God's family
Craig reads Psalm 27:4-6 ("one thing I have desired of the Lord") and says that seeking God's face means dwelling in the house of the Lord and beholding his beauty. He treats the whole earth as God's house and believers as part of God's family, and he argues that animals are also invited into the covenant family (he says he will return to covenants with animals in a later talk). He describes sacrifice in Psalm 27 as "a sacrifice of joy and of song," which he reads as praise rather than blood offering.

### 5. The "king of glory" is Jesus, who conquers by the tongue, not the sword
This is the talk's central Christological claim. Craig reads Psalm 24:7-10 ("the Lord strong and mighty... mighty in battle") and asks whether the "king of glory" is a violent warrior god. He answers with 1 Corinthians 2:7-8 (the "Lord of glory" whom the rulers crucified), John 1:1-14 ("the word was God" who "became flesh"), and Revelation 19:11-16 (a rider on a white horse, "clothed with a robe dipped in blood," whose name is "the word of God"). He treats the robe dipped in blood as the crucifixion and the white horses as the righteousness of the saints (Revelation 19:8, 14). He reads the sword from the mouth (Revelation 19:15) as the tongue, which he links to Paul's language about spiritual armor and to Jesus's statement "my kingdom is not of this world" (the speaker does not give the source reference). He concludes that the kingdom comes through preaching and conversion rather than "bloodshed and violent conquest." He is explicit that he is not reading the sword as literal.

### 6. Materialism and "treasure in heaven"
In the Q&A, Craig argues that "God owns everything" should loosen attachment to temporary things. He cites Matthew 6 ("where your treasure is, that's where your heart is"; moth, rust, thieves) and "you can't worship two masters... God and mammon." He cites Matthew 24:45-46 (the "faithful and wise servant or steward" whom the master finds doing his work when he returns) to argue that the steward's task is to follow God's instructions. He treats salvation as the only possession one can take to the grave, because it is what brings one out of the grave to eternal life. He says a sentimental attachment to small things is not materialism, but that the test is whether one pursues wealth, luxury and fame or "the righteousness of God."

### 7. Hunting: Nimrod, Esau and Babylon
Responding to a chat question about someone who calls caring for animals "unchristian" or "pagan," Craig identifies two hunters in the Bible: Nimrod ("the great hunter before the Lord," the founder of Babylon) and Esau (whom he says God "hated"). He argues that the Bible presents both as opponents of God, and that Babylon in Revelation represents everything contrary to God. He admits that no verse says "thou shalt not hunt" and frames the question as "do I want to model my lifestyle after these characters." He says hunting for sport is especially contrary to God's character of mercy.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Animals were given to humans to do with as they please.** Craig argues that Psalm 24:1 and Psalm 50:10-12 show God's ongoing ownership of animals, so human dominion is stewardship and must be exercised in God's way.
- **God wants animal sacrifice.** Craig reads Psalm 51:16-17 as God rejecting burnt offerings in favor of a broken, contrite heart, and says ancient religions wrongly assumed the opposite.
- **Revelation's rider wins by violent conquest.** Craig reads the sword from the mouth as the tongue, so the "war" is proclamation, not bloodshed.
- **Abstaining from certain foods is demonic.** Craig argues that 1 Timothy 4 condemns forbidding God-created foods, not diet choices motivated by faith, and notes that God himself forbade certain foods in the Old Testament.
- **You can take your possessions with you.** Craig says the only possession one takes to the grave is salvation, which is what brings one out of the grave to eternal life.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Jesus vs. Paul:** Craig reads 1 Corinthians 2, Galatians 5 and 1 Timothy 4 as authoritative scripture alongside the Gospels and Revelation, without distinguishing the authors or comparing Jesus's teaching with Paul's. The talk is a useful example of the canonical harmonizing approach the repo's question is designed to test.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet and nonviolence:** The talk's strongest bearing on the repo is here. Craig reads Genesis 1:29 as God's original plant-based diet, Psalm 51 as rejecting sacrifice, and Revelation 19 as nonviolent conquest by the tongue. None of these readings is compared with other interpretive traditions, including Jewish readings of Genesis 9 or Psalm 51.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Craig says the king will "come to establish his kingdom," that "all the kingdoms of the world will no longer exist," and that the coming kingdom is established by preaching. He treats the earthly kingdom as future and tied to Jesus's return.
- **Resurrection and afterlife:** Craig says salvation is the one possession one can bring to the grave and that it leads to resurrection and "eternal life." He also speaks of joining God's family "one day" in the kingdom. He does not discuss hell.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Craig identifies Jesus with the "word of God" in John 1 and the "king of glory" in Psalm 24 and Revelation 19, and he treats Jesus as the Lord of glory crucified in 1 Corinthians 2. He does not address whether Jesus himself claimed this identity.
- **Gospels and canon:** Craig quotes Matthew, John and Revelation without discussing dates, authorship, or how the books came together. He treats the Bible as a unified text in which "scripture will interpret itself."
