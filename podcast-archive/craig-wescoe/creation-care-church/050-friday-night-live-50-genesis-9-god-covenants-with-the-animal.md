## Summary

Wescoe's Friday Night Live talk asks why God includes animals in covenants, and what Genesis 8-9 implies for how people should treat animals. He opens by playing a short video by Lily of Love Mercifully, which argues that Genesis 9:9-17 shows God covenanting with animals and that Christians overlook this because of anthropocentrism and pride. Wescoe then goes through Genesis 8:16 to 9:17 verse by verse. He counts five places in Genesis 9 where God says the covenant includes "every living creature" or "all flesh," and treats this as the central point.

Wescoe's main argument is that Genesis 8-9 describes a fallen, post-flood world that falls short of Eden. He reads the "pleasing aroma" of Noah's burnt offering (8:21) as pleasing to the senses rather than to God, using Ezekiel 6:13 and an aside on idols. He takes God's statement that the human heart is "evil from youth" (8:21, compared with 6:5) as God's verdict on Noah's violence and sacrifice. He reads the animals' "fear" and being "given into your hand" (9:2) through Job 1:12 and 2:6 as permitted power, not divine approval. He reads the blood prohibition (9:4-6) as a partial step toward abstaining from flesh altogether, and cites Acts as carrying that principle forward. He ends by linking Hosea 2:18 and Isaiah 11 to a future of peace for animals, and closes with prayer and a preview of John 1:17 for next week.

The talk is mostly Wescoe's own exegesis, with the Lily video as a short overture, and it is framed as a devotional and advocacy talk. Several of its key readings (the aroma, the blood prohibition as a step toward veganism, the reading of "dominion") are his interpretive choices and are presented as such. The group should test them against the Hebrew text and mainstream commentary before relying on them.

## Key Teachings

### God's covenant includes animals (Genesis 9:8-17)
Wescoe's core claim is that God's covenant with Noah is also a covenant with "every living creature" and "all flesh." He notes that God repeats this in verses 9, 10, 12, 15-16 and 17, and calls the repetition a sign of importance. He says the covenant's content is that God will never again destroy the earth by flood, and that the rainbow is its sign. He cites Exodus 20:8 to show that the Sabbath, the sign of the Sinai covenant, also applies to cattle and "your stranger," which he presents as a parallel. He also reads the phrase "between me and the earth" (9:13) as not explicitly naming animals and so does not count it in his tally.

### The pleasing aroma is not God's pleasure (Genesis 8:20-21)
Wescoe argues that "the Lord smelled the pleasing aroma" should not be read as God enjoying the sacrifice. He cites Ezekiel 6:13, where a "pleasing aroma" is offered to idols, to argue that the phrase can describe something pleasing to the senses. He adds that "Paul reiterates" that idols "have noses but cannot smell," though he gives no reference. His reasoning is that God's next words, "every imagination of man's heart is evil," would contradict pleasure. He sets the sacrifice against God's stated purpose in 8:17, to let the animals go out and be fruitful and multiply.

### Eden, dominion and diet (Genesis 1:28-29 vs. 9:1-4)
Wescoe contrasts the post-flood commands with Genesis 1. In Eden, humans have "dominion," and are given plants as food. In Genesis 9, God repeats "be fruitful and multiply," but the animals now come under "fear" and "dread," and every moving thing is given as food. He says this is not an improved Eden but a "much worse version," a fallen world that God will work to bring back toward Eden. He reads dominion as stewardship (care) and not as permission to kill, and he treats the shift in 9:2-3 as a concession to human hardness of heart.

### Fear, "into your hand," and violence (Genesis 9:2; Job; Habakkuk 2:17)
Wescoe reads "given into your hand" in 9:2 through Job 1:12 and 2:6, where Satan is given power over Job within limits. He argues that the phrase signals power granted, not God's approval of how it is used. He links the animals' fear to Genesis 3:10, where Adam's fear after the fall is the first sign of disobedience, and to 1 John 4:18, where perfect love "drives out fear." He also cites Habakkuk 2:17 to say the animals' fear comes from human violence. He reads this as continuity with the violence in Genesis 6:11-13 that led to the flood.

### The blood prohibition and abstaining from flesh (Genesis 9:4-6)
Wescoe reads the rule against eating flesh "with its life that is its blood" as a partial instruction. He argues that draining blood is never complete, so the only full obedience is to abstain from flesh, which he says is what God wanted in Eden. He traces the blood rule through the Mosaic law and into Acts, which he says lists abstaining from blood among the "elementary principles" of following Jesus. He treats God's "meeting people where they are" as a pattern, and compares Genesis 9 to the Abraham covenant (Genesis 15), where he says Abraham's animal-cutting is a human custom that God did not require.

### The future peace of the animals (Hosea 2:18; Isaiah 11:6-9)
Wescoe reads Hosea 2:18 as God making a covenant with "the beasts of the field" and "the birds of the air," with the result that all creatures "lie down in safety." He says this restores the Noah covenant's promise of safety and will be fully realized in Isaiah 11:6-9. He ties this to the closing prayer's hope that God's kingdom will come "on earth as it is in heaven," with all creatures at peace.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **God covenants only with humans.** Wescoe says Genesis 9 names animals as recipients of God's covenant and repeats it several times.
- **God was pleased by Noah's burnt offering.** He argues "the Lord smelled the pleasing aroma" describes something pleasing to the senses, and that God's next words ("every imagination... is evil") show disapproval.
- **"Dominion" licenses killing animals.** He reads dominion as stewardship and says the Eden diet was plant-based.
- **"Given into your hand" means God approves of human power.** He compares it to Job 1-2, where power is granted within limits.
- **Noah is simply one of the good Bible characters.** He says almost every character in the Bible is complex, and that Noah's sacrifice goes beyond God's instruction.
- **The blood prohibition is a narrow ritual rule.** He reads it as a partial step toward abstaining from all flesh, traced through Moses to Acts.
