## Summary

Craig Wescoe, host of the Creation Care Church Friday Night Live talk, asks what Jesus meant by the second greatest commandment, "love your neighbor as yourself," and who counts as a neighbor. He opens with Matthew 22:34-40, where Jesus pairs Deuteronomy 6:4-5 with Leviticus 19:18 and says "all the law and the prophets hang on these two commandments." Craig argues that the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20) can be sorted under these two headings: the first three concern exclusive devotion to God, and the rest concern neighbor relations. He then takes up the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25-37), reading it as a lesson that neighborliness is shown by mercy and character rather than by ethnicity or status.

The talk's central move is to extend "neighbor" beyond humans. Craig points to Genesis 1, where humans and animals share the created world, and to Exodus 20:10 and 20:17, where animals appear in the Sabbath and covetousness commands. He concludes that loving one's neighbor means loving every creature one shares the earth with, and he applies 1 Corinthians 13:4-7, Romans 13:10 and Matthew 5:43-48 to how people should treat animals, especially in factory farming, dairy, eggs and hunting. He cites Isaiah 11:6 as a picture of peaceable creation. The evidence is almost entirely scriptural cross-reference and moral reasoning; Craig offers no historical or textual-critical argument.

The second half is an audience Q&A. Viewers (Heidi, Donna, Dave, Tanya, Cathy and others) ask about animal rescue, righteous anger toward abusers, whether one should rejoice at a hunter's death, and how to read "believes all things" in 1 Corinthians 13:7. Craig answers each, recommends holding anger in check, and closes by announcing next week's topic, "God can change hearts," and a prayer.

## Key Teachings

### The two greatest commandments as the summary of the Law
Craig reads Matthew 22:34-40 and ties Jesus's first commandment to Deuteronomy 6:4-5 and the second to Leviticus 19:18. He draws on Luke 24:27 to argue that Jesus claimed the whole of Moses and the Prophets "is all pointing to me." On this reading, all of God's commands reduce to devotion to God and love of neighbor.

### Sorting the Ten Commandments under the two headings
Using Exodus 20, Craig assigns verses 3-7 (no other gods, no images, not taking God's name in vain) to the first commandment, and verses 8-11 (Sabbath) to exclusive devotion as well, since Sabbath is "a way of bringing the two greatest commandments together on a weekly basis." Verses 12-17 (honor parents, no murder, adultery, theft, false witness, covetousness) he treats as the neighbor commandments. He notes that Exodus 20 names animals (verse 10, the Sabbath rest includes cattle; verse 17, an ox or donkey) as part of the household that is to be included.

### Who is my neighbor? The Good Samaritan and mercy as the mark
Craig reads Luke 10:25-37 and notes that Jesus does not directly answer the lawyer's question about the limits of "neighbor" (he does not say only Israelites, nor only humans). Craig explains that the priest and Levite were mediators between God and man who passed by, while the Samaritan, considered unclean, showed mercy. He argues the point is character, not title or lineage. Jesus's instruction "go and do likewise" makes mercy the defining trait of a neighbor. Craig says the better question is "how can I be a neighbor" rather than "who is my neighbor."

### Creation as shared habitation makes animals neighbors
From Genesis 1:21-26 (creatures, then humans given "dominion"), Craig argues that people and animals "inhabit this world together" and that "when you're inhabiting a space together with someone, what does that make you? It makes you neighbors." He adds Isaiah 11:6 (wolf and lamb, leopard and young goat, "a little child shall lead them") as the kingdom picture in which humans model peaceable neighborliness toward animals. He presents current animal practices (habitat destruction, killing for meat, taking calves' milk, taking honey) as failures of this stewardship. He also cites Matthew 5:43-48 to argue that God's impartial love should be extended to animals, and he says Jesus's remark about being "more valuable" than animals is arrogance, referring to a prior live talk for the fuller case.

### How to love: 1 Corinthians 13, Romans 13:10 and Matthew 5 applied to animals
Craig reads Romans 13:10 ("love does no harm to a neighbor") and 1 Corinthians 13:4-7 line by line, applying "long suffering," "kind," "does not envy," "not puffed up" and "does not rejoice in iniquity" to how humans treat animals. He cites factory-farming conditions (battery cages, gestation crates) and fur and leather as "vanity and this is evil." He uses honey as an example: a bee's lifetime of work is "not intended for us," and says the milk of a mother's baby and animal flesh likewise belong to the animals. He refers to 1 John 4:8 and 4:16 (captioned as "first John 48 and sixteen") for "God is love" and "love never fails."

### Interpreting "believes all things" (1 Corinthians 13:7)
In answer to Heidi, Craig argues that "believes all things" does not mean credulity. He contrasts Genesis 3, where the serpent contradicts God's word, and quotes Matthew 4:4 ("man shall not live by bread alone but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God") to say love believes what comes from God's word and rejects deception.

### Righteous anger, rejoicing and the hope of repentance
On a viewer's question about anger toward abusers, Craig distinguishes "righteous anger," citing Jesus overturning the temple tables and calling the Pharisees a "brood of vipers," from anger that becomes displaced. He says Jesus showed anger toward people who repeatedly ignored his teachings. Craig argues that believers should not rejoice at anyone's suffering, including a hunter killed by an animal, and that the only proper object of rejoicing is repentance. He advises keeping the goal of the other person's repentance "in focus."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That "neighbor" means only fellow Israelites or only fellow humans.** Craig argues Jesus does not narrow the term, and that the Genesis and Exodus texts make animals part of the neighborhood.
- **That animals are commodities put here to serve, entertain or feed humans.** Craig calls this "unfortunately one of the biggest misconceptions" and argues God's design gives animals their own jobs, such as pollination, seed-dispersal and pest control.
- **That "believes all things" (1 Corinthians 13:7) means being gullible.** Craig says it means believing what proceeds from God's word, and rejecting the serpent's deception.
- **That it is fitting to rejoice at an enemy's suffering, such as a hunter killed by an animal.** Craig says the only proper object of rejoicing is repentance.
- **That all anger is sinful.** Craig distinguishes righteous anger, citing Jesus in the temple and against the Pharisees, from displaced anger.
