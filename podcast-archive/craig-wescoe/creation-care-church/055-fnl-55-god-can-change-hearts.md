## Summary

Wescoe, host of Creation Care Church's "Friday Night Live" live talk (this is episode 55), asks whether God can change hearts, including the hearts of people who seem far from following Jesus. He frames the talk for two audiences: Christians who have not yet accepted that they should show love to all of God's creatures, and vegans who are still on the fence about accepting Christ. He carries the talk throughout, reading and commenting on the texts himself, with a chat Q&A from about 35:47 to the end.

His main argument rests on a sequence of passages. He reads the rich young ruler (Matthew 19:16-22) as a sad story that may still have a hopeful reading. He then proposes, explicitly without calling it proof, that the naked young man in Mark 14:51-52 could be the same person, using the descriptors "young man," "running," and a linen cloth, along with Revelation 19:7-8 and Genesis 2:25. He adds the parable of the two sons (Matthew 21:28-32) and Jonah, to argue that repentance is shown in present action rather than past claims. He then argues, using Jeremiah 17:9, Jeremiah 24:7, Psalm 51:10 and Romans 12:2, that the human heart is unreliable and must be changed by God rather than followed. Finally he applies this to animals: Exodus 20:13 ("do not kill") should be read to cover animals, and Genesis 1:29 sets a plant diet. He says that the change of heart he has in mind is both becoming a Christian and becoming vegan.

The talk ends with a Q&A, in which Wescoe answers questions about Exodus 20:13, prison reform, Paul's conversion, Jonah's anger, Proverbs 3:5, the "forbidden fruit," and the lost sheep. He closes with a prayer and previews the next talk on Colossians 1:6, which he describes as being about animals created for God and not for us.

## Key Teachings

### The rich young ruler: goodness, the commandments, and the cost of discipleship
Wescoe reads Matthew 19:16-22 closely. Jesus's reply that "no one is good except one, that is God" means that a person's own goodness cannot earn entry into the kingdom. The young man says he has kept the commandments from his youth. Jesus's answer, to sell everything and follow him, is the one thing he lacks, and the young man goes away sorrowful. Wescoe calls this "one of the most disheartening stories" in scripture. He notes that Matthew 19:23-26 follows, with the saying about the camel and the needle's eye and "with God, all things are possible." He reads the story two ways: as a final refusal, or as a man who may have wrestled with the cost and later changed. He compares the second possibility to Moses giving up royal privilege to lead Israel out of slavery.

### A possible second appearance in Mark 14:51-52
Wescoe proposes that the young man who flees the garden naked (Mark 14:51-52, which he notes appears only in Mark) could be the rich young ruler. His indicators are: the man is young and "running" (in one translation "ran to him"), the rich young ruler also ran to Jesus, and the linen cloth suggests wealth. He adds a spiritual reading from Revelation 19:7-8, where fine linen is "the righteous acts of the saints," so that a man wrapped in linen follows Jesus as a disciple. Leaving the linen behind and fleeing naked, in his reading, means he has given up his own righteousness and kept only faith in Christ. He ties this to Philippians 3:8-9, where Paul counts all things as loss to gain Christ and rejects righteousness "from the law" for righteousness through faith. He also reads Genesis 2:25 ("they were both naked... and were not ashamed") as a sign of covenant faithfulness. He repeatedly says this is "not rock solid evidence" and that he is choosing the hopeful reading.

### Repentance is shown in present action: the two sons and Jonah
In Matthew 21:28-32, the son who first refuses and then goes is the one who does the father's will, and Jesus says tax collectors and harlots enter the kingdom before the religious leaders. Wescoe reads this as applying to people who claim to be atheists or who think their past does not matter. What matters, he says, is "what happens henceforth." He backs this with Jonah 1:1-3 and 3:1-3, where Jonah's refusal and eventual obedience show that the change is what counts, and with the saying that the angels rejoice over one sinner who repents. He connects this to his general claim that "God does change hearts."

### The deceitful heart and the need for God to change it
Wescoe argues from Jeremiah 17:9 ("the heart is deceitful above all things") that "follow your heart" is a recipe for disaster. Trusting one's own sense of being "good" is also dangerous, given Jesus's words that only God is good, so people should check whether they are bearing the fruit of the Spirit. He offers Jeremiah 24:7 ("I will give them a heart to know me") and Psalm 51:10 ("create in me a clean heart") as God's solution, and connects them to Romans 12:2 (transformed heart). He notes that Jesus calls the greatest commandment love God with the whole heart. He says we should follow God even when our desires point elsewhere, "even if it contradic[ts] the desires of our heart," and names eating animals as one such desire. Trust in God is also tested in the Q&A: he argues that Proverbs 3:5 ("lean not on your own understanding") means trusting God's wisdom over desire, and he warns against proof-texting.

### Discipleship as friendship, and the foundation of Christ
Wescoe reads Matthew 7:24-27 (the house on rock versus sand) to say that good values with no foundation in Christ will fall under trial. He then reads John 15:15-17 ("no longer do I call you servants... I have called you friends") and asks whether people treat animals as servants or friends. His answer is that "we want to see animals as friends, not food," and that "we want to love our neighbor as ourselves, both human and non-human."

### Exodus 20:13 and Genesis 1:29: applying the commandments to animals
He reads "You shall not murder" (in some translations "do not kill") in Exodus 20:13 as meaning "do not kill any creature," not just humans. He argues that the Sabbath commandment (Exodus 20:8-11) already names animals resting, so it is not a "sketchy" reading to extend the sixth commandment to them. He also says scripture should "interpret itself," and that the Mosaic death penalties belong to an earlier stage before Jesus's death. On diet, he cites Genesis 1:29 ("the fruit of the tree and the green plants to the ground") as God's design, and argues against "I really like eggs," honey, meat or cheese. He acknowledges that some people identify the forbidden fruit as a non-meat food, and says he finds that "not outside the realm of possibility," which is a point of his own speculation.

### Not writing people off
Wescoe applies the heart theme to others. He cites Paul's conversion, which the disciples trusted through the Holy Spirit, and Jonah and the Ninevites as examples that people who seem "a lost cause" can change. He says the right attitude is to pray for repentance and not to "wait to see them... in hell." He allows that one may "shake off the dust of your sandals" with people who will not listen, but says one should not give up hope or wish harm. On prisons, he cites Jesus's instruction to visit the imprisoned (Matthew 25 is not named; he says only "visit those in prison"), and argues that prisoners should not be assumed guilty.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"I'm good, so I'll get into the kingdom."** Jesus's answer "only God is good" means that one's good works cannot earn entry, and the rich young ruler's record of keeping commandments does not settle the question.
- **"Follow your heart."** Wescoe says Jeremiah 17:9 shows the heart is deceitful, so one should trust God's instructions instead, even against one's desires.
- **"Exodus 20:13 only forbids killing humans."** He argues the commandment, read with the Sabbath commandment's mention of animals, should be applied to all creatures.
- **"Someone who rejects God, or who is an atheist or a hunter, is a lost cause."** He says God changes hearts and that the right attitude is prayer for repentance, not condemnation or wishing hell on them.
- **"Open your Bible to a random verse and apply it."** He warns that this is proof-texting, which can take scripture out of context, as Satan did in his temptation of Jesus.

- **Jesus's teaching versus Paul's:** Wescoe reads Philippians 3:8-9 and Romans 12:2 as Paul agreeing with Jesus's call to give up everything, and presents Paul's conversion as a model of change. The talk treats Paul as an authority, not as a figure whose teaching is compared with Jesus's.
- **Torah, diet and nonviolence:** The talk's main concrete applications are reading Exodus 20:13 to cover animals and Genesis 1:29 as God's diet for humans. Both are presented as following the Torah's wisdom.
- **The kingdom on earth:** His closing prayer looks to a kingdom "where there is no death and no killing and no crying," and he treats the kingdom as the goal of present obedience.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** He rejects wishing for people to "burn in hell," and says the proper hope is that they repent. He also refers to the reward "in the afterlife," without elaborating.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** He calls Jesus the foundation and the vine, the Messiah, and the good shepherd. He does not address the question in historical terms.
- **Gospels and canon:** He notes that the Mark 14:51-52 passage appears only in Mark, and he uses Matthew, John, Mark, and Revelation together without discussing how they were formed.
