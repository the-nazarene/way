## Summary

Craig Wescoe's Friday Night Live talk asks what Colossians 1:16 ("all things were created through him and for him") means for how humans should treat animals. He opens by playing a Live Mercifully video, narrated by Lily, which argues that animals "are here with us, not for us." The video rejects the objection that animals were "put here for us" as a resource, calling it man-centered and contrary to Colossians 1:16 and Romans 1. Craig then reads Colossians 1 verse by verse, treating it as the letter of Paul and Timothy to the Colossian church, and ties its themes to other texts: Genesis 1:26-29 on dominion, Genesis 9 on the covenant with animals, Mark 16:15 on preaching "to every creature," and Matthew 5:43-48 on love of enemies.

His answer is that dominion over animals, which Genesis gives humans, is "a Christ-like dominion" that belongs to Christ, the image of God, and so must be exercised in love, peace and self-control rather than by killing, experimenting on, or skinning animals. He presents the gospel of the kingdom as good news for all creation, with reconciliation extending to animals through the covenant. He grants that the practical steps (sanctuaries, enclosures, predators, feeding carnivores) are complex, and says the key is changing people's hearts and attitudes. The talk ends with a read-through of viewer comments, a note that Lily's video is part of a series on Bible verses supporting veganism, and a preview of the next talk on the fall moedim (Leviticus 23).

The talk is pastoral and devotional rather than historical. It does not address the date, authorship or setting of Colossians, and it relies on the English text and on Craig's reading of Hebrew and Greek words, which he says are sometimes translated inconsistently.

## Key Teachings

### Created for God, not for human use
Both the Live Mercifully video and Craig take Colossians 1:16 as decisive: "it's unequivocally clear here that god created everything for himself. It wasn't for us." The video says the "animals for us" view is "selfish and man-centered" and calls for a "theocentric" rather than "anthropocentric" mindset. Craig adds that animals are "valuable to God" apart from their usefulness to people, and that God "enjoys the animals." Romans 1 is cited for the idea that God's power is perceived "from the things he has made."

### Dominion as Christ-like love
Craig links Colossians 1:15-17 to Genesis 1:26. Humans are made in God's image, and Jesus is "the image of the invisible god." Because humans received dominion over the animals (Genesis 1:26), and because all things were created "for him," that dominion still belongs to Christ. Craig reads Genesis 1:29 (plants as food) as showing that the dominion "is not to kill them and eat them, to experiment on them, to turn them into fur coats." Instead it should be a "dominion of love" that bears the fruit of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22-23).

### The gospel is for every creature
Craig reads Colossians 1:23 ("preached to every creature under heaven") alongside Mark 16:15 ("preach the gospel to every creature") to argue that the good news of the kingdom is not only for humans. He says creation is "groaning" for restoration, and that the gospel means "no more tears, no more pain, no more killing." He notes that Paul's "Gentile" simply means non-Jew, so the message is for all peoples, and then extends that openness to animals.

### Reconciliation and the covenant with animals
Colossians 1:19-22 is read as reconciliation of "all things," including the cosmos. Craig cites Genesis 9:15, saying the covenant is "with the animals as well," and that the text says this "five times." He reasons that since God made all creatures for covenant family relationship with him, and humans broke it, animals are "rejoined into that covenant family relationship" when humans are reconciled. He connects this to the kingdom "on earth as is in heaven" and to eternal life in God's kingdom, with John 3:16 as a reference point.

### Fruit of the Spirit, perfection and love of enemies
Galatians 5:22-23 defines the "fruit" that the Colossian believers are to bear. Craig reads Colossians 1:28 ("present every man perfect") through Matthew 5:43-48. "Perfect" means complete or mature, not flawless, and it requires loving enemies and those who harm animals, while still opposing the harm and standing up for God's creatures. He says one should not wish harm or death on abusers, and should pray for their repentance. James 1:2-3 is cited for the idea that trials produce endurance.

### Translation bias and the nefesh
Craig argues that translators "intentionally downplay" animals by rendering the same Hebrew or Greek term differently depending on whether humans or animals are meant. He gives examples of "all flesh" in Genesis (the ark, and receiving the spirit) and says this reflects "intentional anthropocentrism." He urges people to check the original languages and invokes the Hebrew nefesh chayah ("living soul"), which he says animals share with people. He also attributes the church's historical neglect of animals partly to Aquinas and Augustine, whose views he says "steered a lot of theologians" away from seeing animals' place in God's plan. He admits this is a guess about historical origins.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Animals were put here for us, so we can use them as we like."** The video and Craig argue that Colossians 1:16 makes all things created "for him," so animals have value apart from human use, and human dominion is a Christ-like love rather than license.
- **"Dominion means the right to kill and eat animals."** Craig reads Genesis 1:26-29 as a dominion of love, pointing to the plant diet in Genesis 1:29.
- **"The gospel is only for humans."** Craig reads Colossians 1:23 and Mark 16:15 ("every creature") to include animals in the good news of the kingdom.
- **"'Perfect' means flawless, so no believer ever errs."** Craig says "perfect" (Colossians 1:28; Matthew 5:43-48) means complete or mature, meaning love that is not withheld from anyone.
- **"Translations are neutral."** Craig says some translations are shaped by anthropocentrism, distinguishing humans and animals where the Hebrew and Greek do not.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** The talk takes Colossians as Pauline throughout and treats its theology as settled. It does not compare it with Jesus' own sayings except through Matthew 5 and Mark 16, which it reads as continuous with Paul. Its readings are therefore relevant to the Paul question but do not test it.
- **Torah, diet, nonviolence:** Craig reads Genesis 1:29 (plants as food) and Genesis 9 (covenant with animals) as foundations for a non-killing ethic. He cites a Leviticus "appointed times" passage without engaging its dietary or sacrificial laws. Diet and nonviolence are treated through a veganism frame, with the caveat that practical application is complex.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** The talk presents the kingdom as the restoration of creation, with "no more killing" and peace among animals, citing Isaiah 11:9 and Colossians 1:19-20. This is a substantial kingdom-on-earth reading.
- **Resurrection and afterlife:** Craig ties John 3:16 and Colossians 1:11-14 to resurrection to eternal life in the kingdom, which he says includes animals reunited with the people and animals one knew. He does not discuss hell.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Craig identifies Jesus with the image of God and the Word of John 1, the Christological basis for the argument.
- **Gospels and canon:** Craig's main method point is translation: which Hebrew and Greek words are rendered how, and how that shapes the English reader. He does not address how the canon formed.
- **Apparent gaps:** No engagement with the historical setting of Colossians, its authorship debate, or the James/Jerusalem community. The James reference is only to James 1:2-3.
