## Summary

Wescoe asks what the Fall Moedim (the autumn feasts of Leviticus 23) are and how Jesus fulfills them. He treats Trumpets, the Day of Atonement and Tabernacles as one countdown: Trumpets begins a ten-day count to Atonement, and Tabernacles falls on the fifteenth day of the seventh month. He reads each feast as pointing to a future judgment and kingdom. Jesus is the once-for-all sacrifice and high priest, so believers observe the feasts in remembrance and hope rather than by offering animal sacrifices.

### Key Teachings

### The fall feasts as one countdown
Wescoe reads Leviticus 23 as listing seven feasts, three spring and three fall. He says the fall feasts form a count: Trumpets (the seventh month, first day) begins ten days to Atonement, and Tabernacles starts on the fifteenth day. He treats Atonement as "a precursor to the day of judgement" and Tabernacles as the celebration of the kingdom that follows.

### Rejecting Rosh Hashanah and rabbinic tradition
He argues that Rosh Hashanah ("head of the year") rests on "an oral tradition" and a rabbinic belief that this was the day God created humanity. His reasoning is textual: Leviticus 23 calls this the seventh month, so it cannot be the first. He concludes that "we want to put our faith in the word of God," not in a human tradition. He does not engage the sources of that tradition beyond this description.

### Atonement, Jonah and Jesus as high priest and victim
Atonement is presented as a day of fasting, repentance and reconciliation, a time for "crying out to God." Wescoe uses Jonah 3:5-10 as a model: the Ninevites fasted, put on sackcloth, and "God relented." He then argues that Jesus is both the high priest and the sacrificial victim (Romans 5:11, plus the claim that Hebrews presents Jesus as "once for all"). He says Acts 27:9 refers to the Day of Atonement as "the fast," showing that Paul and the apostles observed it, and that the feast still points to a future judgment and so should not be ignored.

### Tabernacles as a reminder that this world is not home
Sukkot is described as dwelling in temporary booths, recalling the Exodus wilderness and the belief that "our home is not in this world." He links it to the marriage of the Lamb, to Hebrews 13:14 ("we seek the one to come"), and to Colossians 1:20 on reconciling all things. He reads John 7:37-39 as Jesus crying out on the last day of the feast, and uses Ephesians 5:19-20 to frame the feast as joyful thanksgiving.

### Against a literal Third Temple
Wescoe answers a viewer's question about red heifers and a Third Temple by arguing that the temple has progressed from the tabernacle, to Solomon's temple, to the torn veil, to believers. He says the Second Temple, destroyed in 70 AD, "was never rebuilt" because "that's not what the temple is about," and that "I think that the temple is is now ourselves we are that temple." He cites Revelation's "no temple" in the kingdom, and says a literal rebuilding reflects "a real lack of understanding."

### Animals, Jonah, diet and nonviolence
He reads Jonah 3:7-8 as the animals joining the fast and the Ninevites turning "from the violence that is in his hands," and Jonah 4:11 as God's concern for "many animals." He concludes that Atonement is a good time to commit to non-violence toward animals, and that the feasts are "designed with the kingdom in mind." He argues from Genesis that God gave humans "the fruit of the tree and the green plants" as food, and presents veganism as the better practice, also recommending it to people who ask why he doesn't eat meat.

### Kaporos and sin transferred to animals
Wescoe describes Kaporos, in which a chicken's throat is cut, the bird is swung over the head, and it is discarded, with the belief that sin passes to the animal. He calls the practice a reason to keep living in sin, and contrasts it with Jesus, who "already did pay that price." He notes that his own protests against Kaporos "haven't gone very far."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Rosh Hashanah is the first day of the year.** Wescoe says Leviticus 23 calls it the seventh month and the first day of that month, and that the New Year label comes from a human tradition.
- **The Third Temple must be rebuilt with animal sacrifice for prophecy to be fulfilled.** He argues the temple has been fulfilled in the tabernacle-to-believers progression, and that Revelation describes no temple in the kingdom.
- **Kaporos transfers sin to an animal so the sinner is not punished.** He calls this a misreading and says Jesus "already did pay that price."
- **Because Jesus fulfilled the feasts, they can be ignored.** He says Atonement still represents a coming judgment and "it's a good idea to do it."
- **God gave animals to humans to eat.** He argues from Genesis that God gave plants as food, and so a plant-based diet is what God designed.
