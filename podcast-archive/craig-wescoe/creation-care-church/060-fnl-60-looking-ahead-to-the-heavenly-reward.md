## Summary

Craig Wescoe, speaking in a "Friday Night Live" live-stream from his Creation Care Church, asks what Hebrews 11:26 means: that Moses regarded "disgrace for Christ" as greater riches than "the treasures of Egypt" because he "looked to the reward." He asks what that reward is, how it is obtained, and how it applies to human lives and to animals. Wescoe's answer is that the reward is the coming kingdom of God and eternal life, which is worth more than worldly wealth and status. He argues that faith is the basis of salvation and that living faith necessarily produces works. He builds the case mainly by stringing together passages from Matthew, Paul's letters, James, Revelation and Isaiah, reading them together with little discussion of their original settings. His most distinctive move is to argue that creation as a whole, including animals, is "groaning" for restoration (Romans 8:19-23), and that the restored kingdom of Isaiah 11 is a world without killing, which he uses to argue for a vegan ethic.

The second half is an audience question-and-answer session drawn from chat comments. Wescoe answers questions on the Ten Commandments and the two greatest commandments, the parable of the talents, the Sabbath (which he says is Friday sunset to Saturday sunset and that the Catholic Church changed it in the 300s AD, a claim he states without a named source), prayer, and whether the victory over evil is already won. He ends by announcing the next week's topic, Philippians 3:19 ("should our eternal perspective include animals"), and closes in prayer.

## Key Teachings

### Treasure in heaven versus the treasures of Egypt
Wescoe reads Matthew 13:44 (the hidden treasure, sold-all-to-buy-the-field) and Matthew 6:19-21 (treasure in heaven, where moth and rust do not destroy) as teaching that the kingdom relationship with Christ is the highest priority. He says worldly goods, "prestige, fame, things of that nature," are fleeting, while "heavenly things" are "our character," meaning kindness, goodness and love. He then uses Philippians 3:7-8 to describe Paul as a former Pharisee who counted his educational and social position as "rubbish or garbage" compared with knowing Christ. Wescoe treats Paul's own renunciation as the model the talk is recommending.

### Faith first, works as its fruit
Wescoe reads Matthew 16:27 (the Son of Man rewarding each "according to his works") and immediately qualifies it: reward is not earned by works but comes from faith, and "living faith" gives rise to works, while "dead faith" does not. He says this is repeated in Revelation 22:12 and, later, cites James 1:12 (the crown of life) and says the book of James "talks a lot about" how repentance and works of faith fit together. His position is that works are the thing "we'll be rewarded for," but that the ground of salvation is faith.

### The creation groaning, including animals
This is the talk's central argument for applying the reward to animals. Wescoe reads Romans 8:19-23 and says that "creation" includes animals. He claims humans have "corrupted" animals by changing their DNA, breeding them to be "vicious killers," and destroying their habitat, and that the animals are therefore awaiting the day when humans will act as God's children. He treats this as the basis for his view that humans should not harm animals.

### The restored kingdom: no death, no killing
Wescoe reads Revelation 21:4 (no more death, mourning or pain) and Isaiah 11:6-9 (the wolf and lamb, the lion eating straw, the child by the viper's den, "they shall not hurt nor destroy"). He argues this vision is a world without violence, with humans as "good stewards" who meet animals' needs. He cites Genesis 1:29-30 (the plant-based diet given at creation) to argue that God's original intention was for animals not to eat each other, and that humans were given plants for food. He describes the restored bodies of the redeemed as incorporating no disease or death, and admits the details of the kingdom (for example, what tapeworms will be) are not fully specified in scripture.

### Persecution as part of the road
Wescoe reads Matthew 5:11-12 and says many Christians avoid this passage because it promises reviling and persecution for Jesus' sake. He says the prophets were stoned and beheaded, and that following Jesus "comes at a price," which must be accepted with joy because the reward is greater.

### Use your gifts: the parable of the talents
Wescoe reads Matthew 25:21 ("well done, good and faithful servant") and explains the parable of the talents. He says the servants who doubled their money received the same reward despite different amounts, because the issue is faithfulness and willingness, not quantity. He says God gives "these gifts for the sake of his kingdom" and expects them to be used. In answering a question from Heidi, he adds that God "gives us the strength himself" and that righteousness "only comes from Christ," so Christ is the one doing the work through the believer.

### Ten Commandments and the Sabbath
In answer to a question about whether "keep my commandment" refers to the Ten Commandments or the two great commandments, Wescoe says both, because the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20) are an expanded form of the two greatest commandments. He says the first four concern loving God and the last six concern loving one's neighbor, with the Sabbath linking the two. He also says the Sabbath commandment includes animals, and that the Sabbath is Friday sunset to Saturday sunset, a view he says is "not a universally accepted position."

### The victory already won
In answer to Heidi, Wescoe says the victory "was achieved on the cross 2000 years ago" and is "a done deal," but its fullness is not yet visible. He illustrates this with the delay between seeing lightning and hearing thunder: the lightning has struck, but the thunder (the second coming) has not yet arrived.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Salvation is earned by works.** Wescoe says it is not: it comes from faith, and works are the fruit of living faith.
- **Vegans think they are better than others.** He says the vegan motive is the opposite: a recognition that humans are not entitled to harm animals for the sake of desire, and that those who harm animals while "just not caring" are the ones who presume superiority.
- **Eating animals is necessary for human health.** He says humans can get all nutrients from plants, and that dairy and flesh are a matter of desire rather than necessity.
- **The Sabbath is Sunday.** He says the Sabbath is Friday sunset to Saturday sunset and that most churches meet on Sunday because of a later change; he acknowledges this is contested.
- **God requires quantity of achievement.** In the talents parable, the reward is for faithfulness, not for the amount produced.
