## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks whether "our eternal perspective" should include animals, working from Philippians 3:19 ("whose god is their belly"). He reads Philippians 3:17-21 as a contrast between Paul's example and those who walk as "enemies of the cross," then argues that the "earthly things" versus "heavenly things" dichotomy extends to how people treat animals. His main support comes from Paul's letters: Romans 8 (creation "groans" and awaits liberation), Galatians 5 (the fruit of the Spirit and "love your neighbor"), Romans 12:2 (transformation rather than conformity), and 1 Corinthians 6:19 and 15:25-28 (the body belongs to Christ, and God will be "all in all"). He also uses the Hebrew Bible: Genesis 9:16 as a covenant that includes animals, Psalm 136:25 and Proverbs 12:10 on God's and the righteous person's care for animals, Genesis 1:29 as the original diet, and the quail episode in Numbers 11 as a warning that craving can become a "grave." Wescoe concludes that animals belong in the eternal perspective and that Christians should not eat them.

The talk is a live Friday Night Live session with a question-and-answer segment. Wescoe reads chat comments aloud and answers them. Among the topics are whether eggs from backyard hens are acceptable (he says no animal product should be seen as food), whether ketogenic or carnivore diets are healthy (he says weight loss does not prove health and points to the Physicians Committee for Responsible Medicine), and how to respond to hostile social media comments. The talk ends with a closing prayer and a reminder of the group's schedule. It is a devotional argument, not a historical reconstruction, and Wescoe does not engage with historical questions about the early Jesus movement.

## Key Teachings

### Eternal perspective means valuing what God values
Wescoe reads Matthew 6:22-23 as being about the "spiritual eye" rather than physical sight. He ties it to the surrounding verses on storing up treasure in heaven and not serving both God and money. For him, the "eternal perspective" means treating heavenly things as more valuable than earthly ones, which he then applies to animals.

### God's covenant and care include animals
Wescoe argues from Genesis 9:16 and the surrounding verses (9:10 and others), where God makes a covenant "with every living creature." He treats animals as "fellow recipients of this covenant" and sets this against a "worldly perspective" that is "just about fulfilling the desires of the flesh." He adds Psalm 136:25 ("who gives food to all flesh") and Proverbs 12:10 (a righteous person "regards the life of his animal") as evidence that God and the righteous both care for animals.

### The Numbers 11 quail story as a warning against craving
This is the most detailed argument. Wescoe reads Numbers 11:18-34 as a story of Israelites who wanted meat and despised the manna. He rejects the reading that God's "you shall eat meat" was a permission, arguing that the outcome (a plague and the "grave of craving," Kibroth-hattaavah) shows God was judging their craving. He warns against people who "look for passages in the Bible" to justify fleshly desire, and he suggests that a "carnivore" diet would be the same kind of rationalization.

### Philippians 3 and the flesh/spirit contrast
Wescoe reads "enemies of the cross" (Philippians 3:18), "their end is destruction," "their glory is in their shame," and "earthly things" as a contrast with the citizenship in heaven (Philippians 3:20). He links this to Galatians 6:14 (being "crucified to the world"), Romans 8:5 (minds set on the flesh versus the Spirit), Colossians 3:1-4 (set minds "on things above"), and Galatians 5:13-23 (the Spirit's fruit, including peace and self-control). Animal cruelty and meat-eating become, in his reading, examples of living by the flesh.

### Creation's hope and the Kingdom "on earth as it is in heaven"
Wescoe reads Romans 8:18-23 as saying that creation itself, including animals, eagerly waits for "the glorious liberty of the children of God." He links this to 1 Corinthians 15:25-28 (Christ reigns until death is destroyed and God is "all in all") and concludes that the restored Kingdom will include peace among all creatures. He describes the goal as "recreating Eden," in which everyone walks by faith and "we're taking care of the animals."

### Diet, health, and Genesis 1:29
Wescoe cites Genesis 1:29 as God's original diet, and Daniel 1 as a case of Daniel and his companions thriving on vegetables and water. He says the Physicians Committee for Responsible Medicine (PCRM) promotes a whole-food plant-based diet, but he says "I'm not a doctor" and offers the site as a pointer rather than as his own expertise. He also says that "long-term effect of ketosis is not even known" (in response to a chat comment).

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Numbers 11 shows God approved eating meat.** Wescoe argues the opposite: the story is about craving and ends in God's judgment, so it is not a license to eat animal flesh.
- **Eating meat is fine because Jesus and the Bible allow it.** He briefly raises the objection "Jesus ate fish that died" and sets it aside with an appeal to "God's perspective" rather than engaging the texts directly.
- **Keto or carnivore diets are healthy because people lose weight.** He says weight loss does not show the diet is healthy and warns of arterial and heart risks, citing PCRM and his own observations of people's diets.
- **Animal products from backyard chickens are acceptable.** He argues that animals should not be seen as commodities at all and that the sale or slaughter of older hens shows the same pattern.
