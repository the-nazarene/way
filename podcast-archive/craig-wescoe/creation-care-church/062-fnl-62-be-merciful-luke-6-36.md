## Summary

This is a live Creation Care Church talk by Craig Wescoe on Luke 6:36, "Be merciful, just as your father is merciful." It opens with a short video by Lily, who argues that the command to mercy covers animals as well as humans, since God's mercy extends to all creation and humans have been given dominion over animals. Craig then goes through Luke 6 (the "Sermon on the Plain"), comparing it with Matthew 5-7 (the "Sermon on the Mount") and reading the surrounding verses (loving enemies, not retaliating, generosity to the needy, the Golden Rule, avoiding hypocrisy) as the content of that mercy.

Craig's answer is that mercy in this sense is active and sacrificial, and that it must extend to animals. He applies the Golden Rule to laboratory use, factory farming, and the dairy, egg and wool industries, asking whether the listener would want those things done to himself. He treats the Pauline and other epistle passages he cites (Romans 12:20, 1 Peter 3:9, 2 Corinthians 9:6-7, Galatians 5:22-23, 1 John 2:6, James 5:1-6) as confirming the same ethic. His evidence is almost entirely the scripture texts themselves, read side by side; he cites no historical scholarship by name.

The second half is a response to live-chat questions: the meaning of "heaping coals of fire" (he offers two possibilities, which he says come from his research without naming sources), how to handle people who keep harming animals, how to correct fellow believers without hypocrisy, and how to persuade Christians who exclude animals from the Golden Rule. He closes with a prayer and previews next week's talk on Matthew 20:25-28 and human superiority to animals.

## Key Teachings

### Mercy as the content of Luke 6, extended to animals
Craig reads Luke 6:20-36 as a unit in which the command "be merciful" is spelled out as loving enemies, not returning evil, giving to those who ask, and treating others as you would be treated. He argues that if God's mercy covers all creation (the Lily video's claim), then human mercy should too. He ties this to the Golden Rule in Matthew 7:12, which he calls "the law and the prophets," meaning the Tanakh in summary, and asks the listener whether he would want the treatment that animals receive in laboratories, factory farms and the wool and dairy industries done to himself.

### Non-retaliation and loving enemies
On Luke 6:27-29 and 1 Peter 3:9, Craig argues that the believer must not retaliate ("We don't want to retaliate"), and must pray for those who harm him. He presents this as active, not passive: returning evil with good does not mean enabling bad behavior, but refusing a vindictive heart. He links it to Jesus's prayer on the cross (Luke 23, as he gives it without a verse) and to Romans 12:20 quoting Proverbs 25:21-22, which Craig reads as an instruction to do good to enemies so as to lead them to repentance.

### Against the rich and for the poor: Luke 6:20-26 and James 5
Craig reads the Beatitudes and woes in Luke 6 as condemning rich people who are indifferent to the needy, and he uses James 5:1-6 to reinforce the point. He reads the poverty in view as material and not merely spiritual, contrasting Matthew 5:3 ("poor in spirit") with Luke 6:20 ("poor"). He draws the lesson that the believer should check whether he is "living too comfortably and ignoring my neighbor," using John the Baptist's instruction in Luke 3:11 to share two tunics. He says the disciples "lived communally" and that this is the model, but cites no passage for that claim in the talk.

### Mercy without hypocrisy or show
Using Luke 6:39-42 (the blind leading the blind, the plank and the speck) and Matthew 6:1-2, Craig argues that charity done for display is "virtue signaling," and that correction should be done privately, not publicly. He cites Luke 6:43-45 (a tree known by its fruit) and Galatians 5:22-23 (the fruit of the Spirit, "against such there is no law") to argue that the fruit of the Spirit must be shown to animals as well as to people, since "that's just not how the spirit of God works" if it is turned off for some creatures.

### Obedience versus confession: Luke 6:46-49 and Matthew 7:21-23
Craig reads "Why do you call me Lord, Lord, and do not do the things which I say" as a warning that calling Jesus Lord is not enough without doing his commands, and that the commands include mercy toward animals. He reads Matthew 7:21-23 as the same point and adds 1 John 2:6 ("ought himself also to walk just as he walked"). He treats "lawlessness" in Matthew 7:23 as failure to practice mercy.

### Patience and the limits of the believer's role (Q&A)
Answering chat questions, Craig argues that people take time to change (he says he went vegan in his twenties and "spent half my life" eating animal products), so mercy includes patience. He draws on Jonah and the Ninevites: the prophet's job was only to deliver the message, and God does the work of changing hearts. He describes the believer's task as speaking the truth boldly and "leaving it up to God."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Loving enemies means enabling their evil.** Craig says returning evil with good means "your heart isn't vindictive," not being "an enabler of bad behavior."
- **The Golden Rule and the command to mercy apply only to humans.** Craig argues that "treat others as you would be treated" and "love your neighbor" extend to animals, since God's mercy covers all creation.
- **Giving to the poor can be done for show.** Craig reads Matthew 6:1-2 as condemning charity for display ("virtue signaling") and calls for giving without public recognition.
- **Saying "Lord, Lord" is enough.** Craig reads Luke 6:46 and Matthew 7:21-23 as saying that professed faith without practicing the commands is the house built on sand.
- **Christians who exclude animals from scripture's mercy are simply following scripture.** Craig answers that the texts they cite (Psalm 145:9) and the fruit of the Spirit point the other way, and that a heart that is "really in the right place" would not restrict mercy this way.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Craig does not distinguish between the Gospels and the epistles. He reads Romans 12:20, 1 Peter 3:9, 2 Corinthians 9:6-7, Galatians 5:22-23, and James 5:1-6 as the same ethic as the Sermon on the Plain. This is a harmonizing reading and the talk makes no historical argument about development.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Craig cites James 5:1-6 (against rich oppressors) and says the disciples "lived communally" in a way that shared bare necessities (Luke 3:11 and the "two tunics" example). He cites no passage for the communal claim, and does not address the Jerusalem community as a historical group.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Craig reads "Blessed are you poor, for yours is the kingdom of God" (Luke 6:20) and "not everyone who says to me, Lord, Lord, shall enter the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 7:21) as conditioned on practice, and says the kingdom "isn't about eating and drinking" but about righteousness, peace and joy.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet and nonviolence:** The Golden Rule, understood as the summary of "law and prophets," is applied directly to animal diet and farming. Nonviolence is developed as non-retaliation and prayer for enemies. Craig does not discuss Torah ritual law or sacrifice in its original sense; "sacrifice" appears only in his summary of Jesus's death and in the closing prayer.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** Only in passing. Craig reads Luke 6:23 ("your reward is great in heaven") and says God's kingdom is coming, but does not develop a theology of the afterlife.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Craig calls Christ "that expression of that divine mercy" and says believers should "follow Christ's character," without a historical argument about Jesus's self-understanding.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Not addressed beyond the comparison of Matthew 5-7 and Luke 6 as parallel sermons, which Craig treats as complementary without discussing sources or dating.
