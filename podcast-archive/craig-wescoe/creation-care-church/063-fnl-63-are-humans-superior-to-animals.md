## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks whether humans are superior to animals and, if so, what that superiority requires. He opens with Genesis 1:26-29, the passage most often cited for human dominion over animals. He argues that dominion is a responsibility to carry out God's will, not a license to do as one pleases, and points to Genesis 1:29, where the original diet is fruit, seed, and vegetation.

He then works through several sayings of Jesus that people use to claim human superiority: Matthew 6:26, Matthew 10:28-31, and Matthew 12:11-12. In each, Craig argues the "you are more valuable" point depends on God caring for animals, so the sayings presuppose animal worth rather than dismissing it. He adds Ecclesiastes 3:19 and Job 12:10 to argue that humans have no intrinsic advantage over animals, and that any superiority is functional and tied to responsibility. His main textual anchor is Matthew 20:25-28, where Jesus says that kingdom greatness means serving. Craig reads Proverbs 12:10 and Isaiah 11:6-9 as descriptions of what that servant leadership toward animals looks like, and he closes with 1 John 4:8 and 4:16 (God is love) and with warnings against pride (Proverbs 16:18, 29:23).

The second half is a live Q&A. Craig discusses why pastors often do not teach plant-based eating (tradition and ignorance, in his account), the history of Seventh-day Adventist vegetarianism (crediting Ellen G. White), whether eating animal flesh is sin (he says "in most cases" yes but declines to say every instance is sinful), and what Passover and communion mean in this context. The talk ends with a closing prayer and a preview of the next week's topic, 2 Corinthians 4:4.

## Key Teachings

### Dominion is a responsibility to God's will, not a license
Craig says the phrase "have dominion" in Genesis 1:26-28 is often "read backward." In his reading, humans "are put in charge to do what God wants," and the moral weight is on duty. He pairs this with Genesis 1:29, which gives fruit and seed as food, arguing that if dominion meant killing and eating animals, the text would not stop at plants. He describes superiority, if any, as "a sort of responsibility."

### Matthew 6:26 and 10:28-31: "more valuable" is about role, and it presupposes God's care for animals
In Craig's reading, these sayings address worried disciples. The "you are of more value" comparison only works if God cares for birds and sparrows. He uses an airline pilot analogy: the pilot is the most important person on the flight because of role, not because passengers are inferior. If God did not care about animals, the argument "God cares for sparrows, so he will care for you" would fail, so the logic depends on animal worth.

### Matthew 12:11-12: the Sabbath example assumes care for animals
Craig reads Jesus's sheep-in-a-pit example as an argument from a premise everyone accepts: one would rescue an animal on the Sabbath. The lesson "how much more is a man than a sheep" only works if the rescue of an animal is treated as good. He concludes that the passage assumes God cares about animals.

### Intrinsic value and function: Ecclesiastes 3:19 and Job 12:10
Craig argues that "man has no advantage over beasts" (Ecclesiastes 3:19) and that all life is in God's hands (Job 12:10). If both humans and animals are valued by God, then any superiority must be functional, tied to responsibility, not intrinsic worth. He says this humbles the reader and that human gifts exist to be used to treat animals well.

### Matthew 20:25-28 as the model: servant leadership, the opposite of worldly authority
This is Craig's "key passage." He contrasts "the rulers of the Gentiles" who "exercise authority over them" with Jesus's instruction that "whoever desires to become great among you, let him be your servant." He says that "kingdom authority works the exact opposite way," and that Christ's superiority over his followers is the pattern for human superiority over animals. Proverbs 12:10 ("A righteous man regards the life of his animal") and Isaiah 11:6-9 (the peaceable kingdom) are offered as descriptions of what this leadership looks like.

### Pride, deception, and the test of love
Craig warns that using these passages to justify mistreating animals is "prideful deception." He cites Proverbs 16:18 and 29:23 on pride and humility, and 1 John 4:8 and 4:16 ("God is love") to argue that a loving relationship to animals is how one "knows god" and bears God's image. He says the image of God is "not just, you have the authority, do whatever you want with it."

### Sin, diet, and the Passover lamb (Q&A)
In response to audience questions, Craig says that the Jesus-ate-fish episode and other texts show "it must not always be sinned to eat animals," but that dozens of passages make some animal eating sinful. Citing James (he gives no reference), he argues that knowingly departing from the "very good diet" of Genesis is sin, and that most meat eating is done to satisfy appetite, which is sin in most cases. He allows that some cases (such as desperation) may not be sin. On the Passover lamb, he says the only command to eat animal flesh he knows is the Passover lamb, which he reads as fulfilled in Christ, so Christians now observe it through communion.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Dominion means a license to exploit.** Craig argues dominion in Genesis 1 is a God-given duty, and that Genesis 1:29 gives a plant-based diet as the original design.
- **"More valuable" means animals don't matter.** Craig argues that Matthew 6, 10, and 12 presuppose God's care for animals; the comparisons would fail otherwise.
- **Humans are intrinsically superior to animals.** Craig argues from Ecclesiastes 3:19 and Job 12:10 that any superiority is functional and comes with responsibility.
- **God created animals for us to eat.** Craig says this is a claim pastors make without checking scripture, and he counters it with Genesis 1:29 and the Passover lamb (which he reads as fulfilled in Christ).
- **Vegans think they are superior to non-vegans.** Craig says the opposite: veganism comes from not believing one is superior to animals.
- **Any eating of meat is sin.** Craig explicitly declines to say every instance is sinful, citing Jesus eating fish and allowing for cases like desperation.
