## Summary

This is a live Friday-night talk by Craig Wescoe of Creation Care Church, titled around John 1:14 ("the Word became flesh"). He asks what it means that the Word, which John identifies as God, became flesh and "dwelt among us," and how a congregation should think about celebrating the incarnation around Christmas. Most of the talk is exegesis: he argues that John 1 deliberately mirrors Genesis 1, that Jesus is the "author" (archēgos) of life and faith, that Matthew 1:23 and Isaiah 7:14 identify Jesus as "God with us," and that Isaiah 43:11 ("besides me there is no savior") makes Jesus God incarnate. He then walks through Luke 2 (birth, circumcision, Simeon, the boy in the temple) to argue that Jesus developed like a human child while remaining without sin.

The second half turns to practice. Wescoe says celebrating the incarnation is fine, but advises separating commercialism, untruthful stories to children (Santa Claus), and pagan-derived customs, which he traces to Yuletide, Saturnalia and Sol Invictus. He supports this with Deuteronomy 12 and does not cite sources for the origin claims, saying he has "done a lot of research" and wants listeners to do their own. The talk closes with a long live-chat Q&A (commenters including Fred, Cathy, Heidi, Sharon and Donna) on sinlessness, the Holy Spirit as one God, the forbidden fruit, the name Emmanuel versus Jesus, and animal care. Wescoe's answers are his own positions, presented as teaching for his fellowship, not as settled scholarship.

## Key Teachings

### The Word as God and as author in the story
Wescoe reads John 1:1-3 and says the Word "is God" and that all things were made through him, which "should immediately elicit" Genesis 1. He contrasts John's opening with the birth narratives: John places Jesus's beginning at creation, not at birth. He then uses Hebrews 12:2 and Acts 3:15, where the Greek word archēgos ("author," "prince," "captain") is applied to Jesus, to offer an analogy. God is the author who writes himself into the story as a character, and a child who writes themselves into a story is still one author. He presents this as a way to "conceptualize" the incarnation without positing two persons.

### God with us, and the savior
From Matthew 1:21 he notes that the name Jesus (Yeshua) means salvation, so Jesus is "the savior." He ties Matthew 1:23, quoting Isaiah 7:14, to Emmanuel ("God with us"), and then to Isaiah 43:11, where the LORD says there is no other savior. His conclusion: if God alone is savior and Jesus is the savior, Jesus is "God in the flesh." He links this to the sin and death narrative (Genesis 2-3, 1 Corinthians 15 is not cited directly) and says the resurrection makes Jesus the "first fruit" who assures followers of resurrection to eternal life. He treats the Emmanuel-versus-Jesus question as a question about names carrying purpose: "god with us" points to the tabernacle and temple, and the name Jesus indicates his purpose.

### A fully human, sinless child (Luke 2)
Wescoe reads Luke 2:1-7 (birth in Bethlehem, the manger), Luke 2:21 (circumcision on the eighth day, Simeon), and Luke 2:39-52 (growth, the twelve-year-old in the temple, Mary and Joseph's anxiety). He argues that Luke 2:40 ("grew and became strong, filled with wisdom") shows Jesus developed as a child, rather than arriving as a fully formed adult with miraculous powers. He holds that Jesus never sinned at any age, and that this matters because the sacrifice must be unblemished: "the wages of sin is death" (Romans 6 is quoted without a verse), and only a sinless death could pay it. He says a "gospel" depicting the child Jesus harming another child was rightly left out of the canon, though he names no text.

### One God, not a committee of three
In the Q&A he says God is spirit (John 4:24), and that the Holy Spirit is not a separate person beside God but God "pouring himself out" and breathing life into creation (the Hebrew ruach, "breath"). He cites Deuteronomy 6:4 ("the LORD our God, the LORD is one") and the greatest-commandment context. The point, he says, is that the Word, the Spirit and the Father are "one God," and that the father-son language describes God's self-revelation rather than a plurality of gods.

### Celebrating the incarnation: commercialism and values
He says any day is a fine day to celebrate, but that certain attachments should be separated out. Commercialism conflicts with Matthew 6 ("lay up your treasures in heaven"; he paraphrases, no verse given), and he contrasts the apostles' simple living. He asks what values children learn from lists of desired toys.

### Santa, honesty, and what children are told
Wescoe argues that telling children a Santa Claus story who knows whether they are good or bad risks teaching them later that adults lie. He reasons that if children later discover Santa is not real, they may ask whether the claims about God and eternal life are also untrue. He frames this as a question for each family to pray over and research, not as a rule for all.

### Pagan origins and Deuteronomy 12
He says the incarnation festival has been merged with Yuletide, Saturnalia and Sol Invictus. He suggests Santa's sleigh descends from Odin's eight-legged horse (the reindeer detail is his), and that Yuletide carols and log-burning come from pagan worship. He states that a Christmas tree, wreaths and mistletoe have origins "more complicated than we thought." He cites Deuteronomy 12:2-4 and 12:31 ("you shall not worship the LORD your God in that way") and Psalm 37:11 and Leviticus 18:3 to argue against adopting pagan worship forms. No scholarly source is given for the origin claims.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus arrived as an adult with miraculous powers.** Wescoe says Luke 2:40 shows Jesus grew and developed like any child, filled with wisdom and grace, rather than appearing as a fully formed adult with superpowers.
- **Holy Spirit as a separate person, making three gods.** He says God is spirit and the Spirit is God pouring himself out, and that Deuteronomy 6:4 ("the LORD is one") rules out a "committee of three gods."
- **"Satan made me do it" as an excuse for sin.** He says blame rests with Satan, but believers should not use that as an excuse; Jesus overcame the same temptations through the Spirit's power.
- **Christmas customs are harmless and purely Christian.** He claims the Santa figure, carols and some decorations have pagan origins (Odin, Saturnalia, Sol Invictus). These origin claims are presented without sources.
- **The forbidden fruit was flesh.** In the Q&A he calls this "a perspective" with "some evidence" but says the main issue was distrust of God, and admits he is not sure what the fruit was.
