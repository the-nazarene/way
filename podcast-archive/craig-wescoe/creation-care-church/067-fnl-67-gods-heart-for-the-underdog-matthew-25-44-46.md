## Summary

Wescoe asks what God's "heart for the underdog" looks like, using Matthew 25:31-46 (the sheep and goats) as the main text. He reads the six needs the king names (hunger, thirst, being a stranger, nakedness, sickness, imprisonment) as the test of a person's standing with Jesus: whoever meets these needs does so "for me," and whoever does not is excluded from the kingdom. He treats the parable as a call to practical charity and extends it to animals, arguing that factory farming, habitat destruction, lab confinement and captive display are failures of the same duty.

For each of the six needs he pairs the parable with other passages: Romans 12:20 and Proverbs 25 on feeding enemies, Isaiah 58:10, John 6:35, Hebrews 13:2, Leviticus 19:34, James 2:15-17, 1 Timothy 2:9, James 5:14-16, Hebrews 13:3 and Isaiah 61:1. His argument is that God looks at the heart rather than outward appearance (1 Samuel 16:7), and that faith has to show up in works. Much of the second half answers live chat questions. Against a commenter who read the goats literally, he argues from context that the parable is not about animals. He also notes that Revelation 12:9 identifies the serpent of Genesis with Satan, and that animal imagery in scripture is often symbolic.

The talk closes with Revelation 21:4 and a prayer for a future without tears or death. Wescoe presents his positions as his reading of the text, not as settled conclusions. He acknowledges that some questions, such as whether Samuel sacrificed a heifer, "would be a long answer" and are better answered in the text.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The sheep and goats parable as a test of love in practice
Wescoe reads Matthew 25:31-46 as Jesus setting out the standard for the righteous: care for the hungry, thirsty, strangers, naked, sick and imprisoned counts as care for Jesus himself. He notes that the parable first describes those who did these things and then, "in contrast," those who did not. He presents the exclusion as the consequence of neglect, not as a separate teaching. He describes this as the reason he wants his listeners "to be one of this... group that does the things for those in need."

### 2. Cross-references for each category of need
He takes the six needs one by one, with a supporting text for each:
- **Hungry:** Romans 12:20 (feeding one's enemy) and Isaiah 58:10. He argues that not being able to feed everyone is no reason to feed no one.
- **Thirsty:** John 6:35. He reads "bread of life" as also a call to share the gospel and to treat the marginalized as human beings, not just to give physical water.
- **Stranger:** Hebrews 13:2 (entertaining angels unawares) and Leviticus 19:34 (treating the stranger as a native). He cautions that he is not urging people to open their homes to any stranger, but argues against dismissing people because one does not know them.
- **Naked:** James 2:15-17 (faith without works) and 1 Timothy 2:9 (modest dress, with surplus given to the needy). He argues that expensive clothing signals misplaced priorities.
- **Sick:** James 5:14-16 (elders praying and anointing). He adds practical help, such as doing someone's chores, and says the church should not support industries that make animals sick.
- **Prison:** Hebrews 13:3 and Isaiah 61:1. He argues that many people in prison are unjustly held (citing plea deals and "prison for profit"), that everyone is a sinner forgiven by Jesus, and that prison ministry can be "sowing seeds for the gospel."

### 3. Extending the duty to animals
Wescoe argues that the same principle covers animals: feeding hungry wildlife (including a squirrel and a peanut), putting out water in hot climates, building shelters for feral cats in winter, and refusing to pay for animal confinement, including factory farms, labs, aquariums and "orcas" in tanks. He says human-caused habitat destruction causes animal hunger. He cites Genesis dominion as a stewardship responsibility. He describes the Save Movement, which gives water to thirsty pigs on transport trucks for about two minutes before slaughter, as an example of mercy "showing where your heart is."

### 4. Heart, not appearance (1 Samuel 16:7 and David)
He opens by recalling David and Goliath as a story of an underdog who wins because he has God. He then uses 1 Samuel 16:7 ("the Lord looks at the heart") to argue that people should not judge by appearance, including the appearance of animals. He says this applies to people who judge animals as "just a four-legged creature with a tail," and that God breathed the same "breath of life" into animals as into humans.

### 5. Objections and how he answers them
He answers several chat questions. He argues that giving money can be wise discernment, but that people should not refuse help because of a fear the money will be misused, since donors also cannot control where their money goes. He suggests offering to buy food directly as a "test." He says long-term food solutions matter, but gives the example of a community garden in a food desert that city ordinances cut down. He also describes siege and embargo as deliberate starvation of a population, contrary to loving one's neighbor.

### 6. Spiritual imprisonment and the "voice for the voiceless"
Wescoe extends "prison" to people held by sin, to be visited in prayer and ministry. He then turns to Proverbs 31:8 (for next week's talk), arguing that animals do cry out (he describes dairy cows separated from calves) but lack the political representation that would let their cry be heard. He frames the human role as using the power humans have to act on their behalf.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"God loves sheep but not goats; real goats are satanic and unworthy of love."** Wescoe says the parable is not about literal animals, since the examples given are all about people, and points to Isaiah 11 as a passage showing a goat at peace.
- **Animal imagery in scripture is always literal.** He argues that the serpent, the lion and similar images are often symbolic, and that Revelation 12:9 identifies the Genesis serpent with Satan.
- **Giving money to a person in need enriches a drug dealer, so one should not give.** He grants the risk but says every dollar given to a business carries similar uncertainty, and that discernment, not refusal, is the answer. He suggests offering to buy food directly.
- **Prisoners are necessarily guilty and deserving.** He says many are unjustly held, and that all people are sinners who can be forgiven.
- **Food shortages are solved by charity alone.** He argues that long-term food access matters, but that laws and systems can block local food projects.
