## Summary

Craig Wescoe opens with 1 Peter 3:15 and asks how a believer should give a "defense" (Greek *apologia*) of the hope within them, with a focus on defending Christian veganism when others object. He breaks the verse into parts: the basis of the defense is Scripture (John 17:17, 2 Timothy 2:15), the hope is God's promises of a future kingdom and resurrection (Jeremiah 29:11, Hebrews 11:1, Romans 8:24-25, 1 Peter 1:3), and the manner is "meekness and fear" or "gentleness and respect," which he ties to 2 Timothy 2:24-26, James 3:17, Titus 3:2 and Psalm 37:11. Apologetics, in his account, is a form of evangelism carried out through "reason and evidence."

His main example is 1 Corinthians 15:12-23, where Paul answers people who deny the resurrection of the dead. Wescoe reads the passage as a chain of reasoning: if Christ is not raised, faith is empty, and if Christ is raised, believers have hope of resurrection to eternal life in God's kingdom. He presents this as sound, evidence-based argument that a Christian can imitate.

In the Q&A, Wescoe takes questions from the live audience. He answers that suffering in the world can be met with Romans 8:18-22, Revelation 21:4 and Genesis 1:31, and that the solution is God's future redemption rather than God being the cause. For the question of whether God's original plan was vegan until Jesus returns, he uses Luke 12:42 and Genesis 1:26-30 to argue that humans were given stewardship and should act now. For Genesis 9:1-3 he argues that the permission to eat flesh is not God's approval, citing Habakkuk 2:17, Job 1:12 and 2:6, and Genesis 8:21, and he says the passage is better read as a warning about the human heart. The talk ends with a preview of the next talk on 2 Chronicles 7:14 and animal cruelty, and an invitation to a Creation Care Church School of Apologetics.

## Key Teachings

### 1 Peter 3:15 as a method: basis, hope, manner
Wescoe reads 1 Peter 3:15 as three instructions. The defense should rest on Scripture ("sanctify them by your truth. Your word is truth," John 17:17). The hope is the content of God's promises, which are seen but "not seen" and sustained by faith (Hebrews 11:1, Romans 8:24-25, 1 Peter 1:3). The manner is gentleness and respect, which he ties to Christian character and to 2 Timothy 2:24-26, the aim of which is repentance and release from the "snare of the devil." He insists that being "willing to yield" does not mean conceding the truth, but meeting the questioner where they are, citing Paul's "I become all things to all people."

### Paul's apologetic in 1 Corinthians 15
Wescoe sets up the context: some (he names "sadducees and some other groups") deny the resurrection of the dead. He reads 1 Corinthians 15:12-23 and summarizes the argument: if God does not raise the dead, Christ was not raised, and then the hope is "in vain." Since Christ was raised, the "first fruits," believers who belong to Christ will be raised "at his coming." He compares the structure to "hypothetical syllogisms," mentioning modus tollens and modus ponens, though he does not develop them. He presents the passage as a model of reasoned defense grounded in evidence (the resurrection of Christ) and in Scripture.

### Suffering, creation and God's promised redemption
Responding to a listener (Heidi) on the "ongoing cruelty," Wescoe says the concern itself shows the heart is "in the right place." He then uses Romans 8:18-22 (creation "eagerly waits" and "groans") to argue that the animals share in the hope of redemption. Genesis 1:31 shows creation was good at the beginning, and Revelation 21:4 gives God's promise to wipe away tears. He links James 1 (evil desire gives rise to sin, which gives rise to death) to explain the cause of suffering, so that God is presented as the solution, not the author of the problem.

### Genesis 1:26-30 and the stewardship argument
Answering a question about whether God's original plan was vegan, Wescoe builds from the parable of the faithful and wise steward (Luke 12:42, within the parables of Luke 12:35-48). He reads this with Genesis 1:26 (dominion), 1:29 (the human diet is plants and fruit), and 1:30 (green herbs for food for the beasts). His reasoning: humans were made stewards who give each creature its food "in due season," and Jesus commands readiness, not waiting until his return to get ready. He adds that the reply is one of several valid approaches.

### Genesis 9: "given into your hand" is not approval
This is the main exegetical argument in the talk. Wescoe concedes that Genesis 9:2-3 ("they are given into your hand," "every moving thing that lives will be food for you") is "one of the toughest objections." He argues that:
- The fear of humans among animals is the result of violence, not God's will (Habakkuk 2:17: "the violence done to Lebanon").
- The phrase "into your hand" is used of Satan's power over Job, which was limited ("do not lay a hand on his person," Job 1:12; "spare his life," Job 2:6), so it describes authority within restrictions, not approval.
- Genesis 8:21 shows God describing man's heart as evil, so the text is a warning about fallen human imagination.
- 1 John 4:18 ("there's no fear in love") is used to argue that a loving God would not design animal fear as part of his will.
He also says the text is better read as a warning, a reckoning of blood, than as God changing his mind, though he allows that "permission" might apply in the way Jesus speaks of Moses and divorce, which he attributes to "the hardness of your hearts."

### Freedom in Christ is not license to sin
Answering Heidi's memory of Paul, Wescoe cites Galatians 5:13 ("do not use liberty as an opportunity for the flesh") and Romans 14:19 ("pursue the things which make for peace"). He argues that grace does not give a "credit card" to sin against animals or people, and that salvation is evidenced by good fruit.

### The Q&A on evangelism, theology and apologetics
In the closing question Wescoe distinguishes theology (grounded knowledge of Scripture's message), apologetics (defending a position using evidence and reason), and evangelism (sharing the hope, which often but not always involves apologetics). He gives the School of Apologetics as the place to learn the method, and the theology talks as the place to learn content.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Apologetics means arguing to win.** Wescoe says the defense should be gentle and respectful, aimed at repentance and freedom from the devil's snare, not at humiliating the other person (2 Timothy 2:24-26, Titus 3:2).
- **One correct answer exists for every question.** He says there are many valid ways to defend the hope, and his answers are examples, not the only option.
- **Genesis 9 gives God's permission to eat animals.** He argues the phrases "given into your hand" and "every moving thing... food for you" describe authority within limits (Job 1:12, 2:6) and follow from human violence, not from God's approval.
- **God's plan was meat-eating, and believers should wait until Jesus returns to change.** He answers that Jesus commands readiness now (Luke 12:42) and that humans were given stewardship in Genesis 1.
- **Suffering in creation disproves God.** He answers that God is the solution, with redemption promised in Romans 8:18-22 and Revelation 21:4.
- **Freedom in Christ permits any behavior.** He answers that grace does not license sin against animals or people (Galatians 5:13).

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Wescoe uses Paul as the model apologist and reads Paul's teaching on resurrection and freedom alongside Jesus's parables and sayings (Luke 12:42, Matthew 5:5, "believe for the sake of the works"). The talk does not distinguish Jesus's teaching from Paul's; it treats them as one body of authority.
- **Torah, diet and nonviolence:** The central exegetical argument is Genesis 1:29-30 (plant diet) and Genesis 9:1-3 (permission read as a warning), with Habakkuk 2:17 on violence as the cause of animal fear. This is the most direct bearing on the repo's diet and nonviolence questions.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Wescoe describes the hope as an eternal life "in his kingdom," with Isaiah 11 (animals at peace) as part of the vision, and the resurrection as the entry point. He does not discuss whether the kingdom is on earth or in heaven beyond the Isaiah and Psalm 37 imagery.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** The resurrection is central to the 1 Corinthians 15 example. He says that those who reject God are "under the snare of the devil" and that the goal is repentance, but he does not describe hell in detail; he rejects the idea of wishing people "burning for all eternity."
