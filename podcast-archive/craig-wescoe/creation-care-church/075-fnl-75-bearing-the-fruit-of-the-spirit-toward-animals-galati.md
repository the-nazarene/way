## Summary

The host opens a Friday Night Live session of the Creation Care Church by asking what it would mean to "bear the fruit of the Spirit" toward animals, using Galatians 5:22-23 as the organizing text. He treats the nine fruits (love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control) as a practical checklist for how believers should treat both people and animals. His main support comes from Paul's list and from Jesus's sayings in John 15:16 and Matthew 12:33 ("a tree is known by its fruit"). He then links love to the whole law, citing Galatians 5:14 and Leviticus 19:18, and to the greatest commandment in Matthew 22:36-40 and Deuteronomy 6:5.

Each fruit is then tied to a verse and to the treatment of animals. Peace, for example, is linked to Romans 12:18 and Matthew 5:9, and the host argues that a "peacemaker" must actively intervene rather than stay silent. There he argues that animals share the "breath of life" and the same fate as humans (Ecclesiastes 3:18-21), that Balaam's donkey (Numbers 22) suggests animals may be in the Kingdom, and that the "dogs" outside the city in Revelation 22:15 are metaphorical. He also argues that animal sacrifice was not God's real desire, citing the prophets and Hebrews, and offers a literary-critical theory (the "P source") as one way such sacrifices entered Scripture.

The talk ends with further questions on righteous anger (he points to Jesus cleansing the Temple), on the difference between joy and happiness, and on the reference to Jesus as "good." It closes with an announcement of next week's talk on John 10:10-11 and a prayer. The host presents all of this as his own reading of Scripture; he does not engage critical scholarship beyond a brief, hedged mention.

## Key Teachings

### Love as the root of the fruit and the fulfilment of the law
The host argues that love is the first and primary fruit, and that Paul in Galatians 5:14 says "all the law is fulfilled in one word, even in this: love your neighbor as yourself," quoting Leviticus 19:18. He links this to Jesus's statement in Matthew 22:36-40 that the greatest commandment is to love God and the second is like it. His conclusion is that "everything that God is instructing us to do can essentially boil down to" love, and that loving animals is part of loving one's neighbour. He also uses 1 John 4:16 ("God is love") to argue that love is God's fundamental nature, not an abstract concept.

### Walking through the nine fruits as practical tests for animal treatment
Each fruit is given a short treatment with an animal application:
- **Peace:** Romans 12:18 uses the word *pantōn* ("all"), which he reads as including animals. Killing or frightening animals is therefore failure to live at peace with them. Matthew 5:9 is used to argue that a peacemaker must actively act against animal suffering, not merely stay quiet.
- **Kindness:** Proverbs 11:17 and 12:10 are read as contrasting kindness with cruelty. He says that if an animal is "chopped up and put on your dinner plate," that is cruelty.
- **Goodness:** Psalm 31:19 and Genesis 1 are used. He says God pronounced creation "very good," and that the diet God gave, plants and fruit (Genesis 1:29 as he describes it), is the good diet believers should follow.
- **Faithfulness:** Luke 16:10 is read as meaning that small, everyday choices, including what one eats, matter.
- **Self-control:** 2 Timothy 1:7 is used to argue that God's Spirit gives believers power to resist appetite, including desire for animal products he describes as "a product of animal suffering."

### Animals, the "breath of life," and the afterlife
In Ecclesiastes 3:18-21 the host argues that humans and animals share the same fate and the same breath of life (citing Genesis 1). He argues that the Hebrew *nephesh* is used of both animals and humans, which he treats as a rebuttal to a "Platonic" or Greek view that only humans have a soul. He then argues that if animals share the same fate and hope as humans, they may also share the hope of resurrection to eternal life. He cites Numbers 22 (Balaam's donkey) as "the best piece of evidence" that animals may be in God's kingdom, because the donkey returns good for beatings, which he takes to be Christ-like. He uses Revelation 5:13 (every creature praising God) to argue the same point.

### Revelation 22:15 and the metaphor of "dogs"
A listener asked about "dogs" being outside the kingdom in Revelation 22:15. The host argues this cannot be literal because Genesis 9 repeats God's everlasting covenant with all animals, including dogs. He reads "dogs" as a metaphor for people who "turn and tear you to pieces" when they hear the gospel, pointing to Matthew 7:6 ("give not what is holy to the dogs"). He also refers to Isaiah 11's "peaceable kingdom" as a picture of the restored creation.

### Animal sacrifice: his answer and the competing theories
Asked why God requires blood sacrifice from innocent animals, the host gives a short answer ("he doesn't") and offers several explanations:
- Hebrews (he says chapter 9, with the hedge "I believe") presents Jesus as the "once for all" sacrifice, making animal sacrifice obsolete.
- The prophets (he names Jeremiah, Isaiah, the Psalms and Hosea, "I desired mercy not sacrifice") are cited to argue God did not desire sacrifices.
- He describes a "P source" theory in literary criticism, using the Noah narrative (two of each animal versus seven of the clean animals) as an example of the kind of redaction that might have inserted sacrificial material. He says this is "entirely possible" but that he does not need to settle it.
- His preferred reading is that sacrifice was a "temporary concession to our hard-heartedness," a way of weaning people from sacrificing animals to other gods.

### Righteous anger, patience, and the Temple cleansing
In answer to a question about whether anger can be righteous, he points to Jesus cleansing the Temple and liberating the animals sold for sacrifice as an example of righteous anger. He says such anger is rare, and that the default should be self-control and patience. He suggests putting oneself in the position of someone new to veganism, recalling his own first 20 years of eating animals, and praying as Jesus did on the cross ("forgive them... they don't know what they're doing").

### Joy versus happiness
Responding to a question about "Biblical Joy," the host argues that joy in John 15:11 and Philippians 4:4 is "not dependent on your circumstances," unlike happiness. He cites Paul's suffering (stoning, shipwreck, flogging) and James's call to "count it all joy" as examples, and says believers should also let animals be joyful and not take their joy away.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Believing all things means believing whatever you're told."** The host says 1 Corinthians 13:7 refers to believing God's words and promises, not everything people say.
- **"Animals have no soul; only humans do."** The host argues this is a Greek/Platonic doctrine and that the Hebrew *nephesh* is used for both.
- **"Dogs in Revelation 22:15 are literally shut out of heaven."** He argues the verse is metaphorical, since animals are included in God's covenant (Genesis 9) and in the praise of Revelation 5:13.
- **"God commanded animal sacrifice."** He says God "doesn't" require it, citing Hebrews, the prophets, and a possible literary explanation. He acknowledges the manuscripts suggest otherwise and does not claim certainty.
- **"I'm a good person, so I don't need Christ (or veganism)."** He reads Jesus's "why do you call me good?" as teaching that only God is good and that goodness comes through God's Spirit.
- **"Joy is the same as happiness."** He argues joy in the Lord does not depend on circumstances.
