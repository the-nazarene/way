## Summary

Craig asks what John 10:10-11 means for how we treat animals: if Jesus is the Good Shepherd who gives life, are we acting as the thief who "steals and kills and destroys," or as a shepherd? He reads "the thief" as a set of practices toward animals: stealing their milk, eggs, honey, babies and "breath of life," and killing them for food, clothing or sport. He treats the Good Shepherd as the model for human care of animals.

His evidence runs mostly through the Hebrew Bible and the Gospels. He cites Isaiah 40:11 and Psalm 23 for God as shepherd, Genesis 1:28-31 for humans as stewards given a plant diet, and the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:13, 15, 17) for prohibitions on killing and stealing, which he argues apply to animals by default. He reads Jesus' temple cleansing (Matthew 21:13) as a protest against the slaughter and sale of animals for sacrifice, and he argues that the money-changer reading is a later overlay. He also uses Isaiah 11:6-9 as a picture of a peaceable kingdom and Revelation 5:13 for creatures praising God.

The talk is mostly Craig's exposition, with a long chat segment near the end in which he answers viewer questions. Those answers cover the temple incident, the meaning of "dominion," whether animals are property, a "plants rights" objection, the Hebrew word *shamar* (Genesis 2:15), and the Jerusalem community's sharing of possessions. He ends by announcing next week's talk on Matthew 24:45 (the faithful and wise steward) and closes with prayer. He presents all of this as his own position, not as a settled reading of the texts.

## Key Teachings

### The thief in John 10 is a pattern of exploitation of animals
Craig reads the three verbs in John 10:10 (steal, kill, destroy) as concrete acts against animals. Stealing covers their milk, babies, eggs, honey and "breath of life." Killing covers slaughter for food, fur or leather. Destroying is tied to the Isaiah 11 vision of "no hurting and no destroying." He links the thief's motive to covetousness (Exodus 20:17), which he says leads to stealing.

### Dominion means stewardship, and the text gives a plant diet
Craig argues that "dominion" in Genesis 1:28 is stewardship, not license to kill. He points to Genesis 1:29-30, where humans and animals are given plants for food, and says the verse that follows the dominion command contradicts a reading that permits killing animals. He concludes that God's "very good" created order had no animal killing in it.

### The Ten Commandments apply to animals by default
He reads Exodus 20:13 ("do not kill") and 20:15 ("do not steal") as unqualified, so they should be assumed to cover animals unless the text limits them to humans. He cites the Sabbath command (Exodus 20:8-10), which names "your cattle," as evidence that animals are included in the covenant. He adds Exodus 23:7 ("do not kill the innocent"), arguing that innocent animals qualify.

### The temple cleansing was about animal sacrifice, not only money-changing
Craig argues that Jesus' quotations ("my house shall be called a house of prayer"; "a den of Thieves" or "a Marketplace") come from prophetic passages against sacrifice (he names Hosea, Jeremiah, the Psalms and Isaiah without verses). Since Jesus quotes those passages, he says the protest was against the sale and slaughter of animals. He notes that the money-changers appear in only three of the four Gospel accounts, while the release of the animals appears in all four. He says Hebrews presents Jesus as the once-for-all sacrifice, which ended animal sacrifice, and asks why Christians draw that conclusion from the cross but not from the temple.

### Animals belong to God, not to owners; Jesus calls his followers friends
Craig cites Psalm 24:1 ("the earth is the Lord's") and the Caesar coin passage to argue that animals are not property and belong to God. From John 15:12-17 he argues that Jesus' "I no longer call you servants... I call you friends" should shape how humans see animals: as companions, not servants or food. He ties this to the fruit of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22-23) as the means by which people become good shepherds, and to Jesus' words about the rich man, "with God all things are possible."

### Responding to objections: dominion, plants, and "animals are property"
In the chat segment Craig answers three objections. (1) Dominion permits killing: he reverses it with Genesis 1:29. (2) Concern for plants: he argues that a "fruitarian" diet best fits the concern (it does not kill the plant), that eating animals means eating more plants, and that the objection is often insincere. (3) Animals are property: he says God's ownership undercuts it and that animals are individuals, not objects. He also argues from the Hebrew *shamar* (Genesis 2:15; Genesis 4:9's "my brother's keeper") that humans are keepers of animals and each other.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Dominion" licenses killing and eating animals.** Craig argues the verse after the dominion command (Genesis 1:29) gives plants and fruit as food, so dominion is stewardship.
- **The temple cleansing was about money-changers and dishonest trade.** Craig argues it was about the sale and slaughter of animals for sacrifice, since Jesus quotes prophetic passages against sacrifice and the animal element appears in all four Gospels.
- **The Ten Commandments' "do not kill" and "do not steal" apply only to humans.** He says the commands are unqualified, and the Sabbath command names cattle as part of the covenant.
- **Animals are property to be used however owners like.** He answers that God owns all creatures (Psalm 24:1) and animals are individuals, not objects.
- **Concern for plants is a reason not to eat animals only if one also rejects eating plants.** He argues this is a disingenuous objection, and that a fruitarian diet would be the consistent response.
