## Summary

Craig Wescoe, host of Creation Care Church's Friday Night Live stream, asks whether believers are being "faithful and wise stewards over God's household," using the parable in Matthew 24:45-51. His central claim is that the "food in due season" the steward gives should be read as food for animals, so that stewardship over God's household means caring for animals, and that God's intended human diet is plant-based. He builds this by chaining cross-references: Psalm 145:15-17 (God opens his hand to feed every living creature), Proverbs 12:10 (the righteous care for animals), Genesis 1:26-31 (dominion, with a plant-based diet for humans and animals), and Isaiah 11:6-9 (the peaceable kingdom). He then argues that eating animals is a post-Fall arrangement, citing Genesis 9:2-3 and Habakkuk 2:17 as descriptions of fear and violence toward animals, and that this differs from God's "very good" design.

Craig's method is almost entirely intra-scriptural and devotional. He cites no ancient manuscripts, dates, authors, or modern scholars, and he does not discuss how the parable's original audience would have heard it. He reads James 1:5, 1:22-25 and 3:17 as calls to wisdom and peacemaking, and he reads Proverbs 3 and "the greatest commandment" as an ethic of love. He also answers objections, such as Genesis 9:3 being used to justify meat-eating, by pointing to Genesis 1:29-31 and to the wider passage.

The second half is a live question-and-answer session with chat participants on topics including fear of God (he cites 1 John 4:18), the house-sitter analogy for stewardship, gardening and abundance, the dangers of "misanthropy" in secular veganism, and how to respond to church members who cite Genesis 9:3. The talk ends with a closing prayer that frames the parable as primarily about animals, and with an invitation to join the next Friday session.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The parable's "food in due season" refers to animals
Craig reads Matthew 24:45 as drawing on Psalm 145:15-17, where God "gives them their food in due season" and "satisfy[ies] the desire of every living creature." He argues that since God feeds all creatures graciously and this counts as righteousness, the faithful steward imitates this by feeding and caring for animals. He ties this to Proverbs 12:10 ("a righteous man regards the life of his animal"). He also says "a person is only righteous if it's God's spirit acting through us."

### 2. Genesis 1 sets a plant-based design; dominion is care, not killing
From Genesis 1:26-31 Craig takes "dominion" as stewardship, not license. He stresses that God gives "every herb that yields seed" and "every green herb for food" to both humans and animals, and concludes that "this ruling over does not involve killing and eating them." He presents Genesis 1:29 and 1:31 ("very good") as the design God intended, which he later uses against the view that Genesis 9:3 permits meat.

### 3. Genesis 9:2-3 and Habakkuk 2:17 describe the post-Fall condition, not God's ideal
Craig reads the fear and dread of animals in Genesis 9:2 as a consequence of violence, not as God's will. He links this to Habakkuk 2:17 ("the violence done to Lebanon... the plunder of beasts which made them afraid"), and compares animal fear to Adam and Eve hiding after the Fall (Genesis 3). In his view, "fear is of the fall." In the Q&A he returns to Genesis 9:3 with an argument that Genesis 1:29 and 1:31 should be followed over the post-Flood permission.

### 4. The peaceable kingdom and the tree of life as the picture of restoration
He cites Isaiah 11:6-9 as the vision of a harmonious creation led by "a little child," and calls it "the kingdom" that God wants his household to live in. He ties this to 2 Chronicles 7:14 (humble, pray, turn from wicked ways, and God will "heal their land") and Revelation 22:1-2 (the tree of life "for the healing of the nations"). He reads Proverbs 3 as wisdom being "a tree of life," and identifies this wisdom with "paths of peace." In his reading, healing and restoration of the land are the goal.

### 5. James on wisdom, doing the word, and peacemaking
Craig reads James 1:5 (ask God for wisdom, who gives "generously"), James 1:22-25 (be doers and not hearers; the "mirror" of being made in God's image), and James 3:17 ("the wisdom that is from above... full of mercy... peaceable") as instructions for embodying God's peace. He ties the "fruit of righteousness is sown in peace" to Matthew 5's "blessed are the peacemakers," and says this peace includes animals. He reads "fear of the Lord" in Proverbs 1:7 as trust and worship, and Ephesians 5:15-17 ("redeeming the time") as a call to active obedience.

### 6. The master's return and the call to exceed the minimum
Craig identifies the master in Matthew 24 as Jesus, "Lord of Lords, King of Kings." He argues that believers should not look for the minimum needed to get into heaven or ask "how much can I serve my flesh." He uses the house-sitter analogy: the steward keeps the house "in the order that I gave it to you." He also cites Luke 3 (the "two shirts" saying) for sharing necessities, and says the goal is an "A plus," not a "D minus passing grade."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Dominion means license to use or kill animals.** Craig says dominion is stewardship and care, and that Genesis 1 excludes killing and eating animals.
- **Genesis 9:3 permits eating meat as God's design.** He says Genesis 9 describes a post-Fall condition and that Genesis 1:29-31 is "very good."
- **God is a bloodthirsty God who wants animal sacrifices and is pleased by pleasure.** He says this is a projection of human desires and that God regards animals as beloved creatures.
- **"Fear of God" means terror.** He cites 1 John 4:18 and Proverbs 1:7 to argue that fear in this sense means trust, honor, and worship, and that perfect love casts out fear.
- **Faith requires only the minimum obedience to get into heaven.** He argues that believers should aim to please the master fully rather than look for a "D minus."
- **Secular vegans hate humanity ("misanthropy") and want humans to disappear.** He says this is an error that results from lacking "that biblical understanding and that faith," and argues that humans should become the world's "biggest caretaker."
