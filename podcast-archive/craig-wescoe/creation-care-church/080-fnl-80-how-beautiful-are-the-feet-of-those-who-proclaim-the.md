## Summary

Wescoe, presenting a live Friday Night Live message for Creation Care Church, asks what "the kingdom of God" is that the feet of the messenger in Isaiah 52:7 are running to proclaim, and what it means for those feet to be "beautiful." He rejects a literal reading (he jokes about painted toenails) and treats the phrase as the image of a runner bringing good news. He notes an ancient example, a messenger announcing the outcome of a battle, and says Bible writers used the image for the good news that God will restore his "very good world" through Jesus.

His main answer is that the good news of peace means a kingdom with no violence, in which humans and animals alike are not harmed or killed, and that the diet of that kingdom is plant-based. He supports this with Genesis 1:29-30, Isaiah 11:6-9, Hosea 2:18, Isaiah 2:4 and Revelation 21:3-5, and he reads Jesus' Isaiah 61 mission (Luke 4:18) as including liberation of animals, including an account of Jesus in the temple court that Wescoe presents without a verse reference. He argues that Christians who cite Genesis 1 "dominion," Peter's vision in Acts, or the teaching about unwashed hands to justify eating animals are misreading those texts, and that this pushes secular vegans away from the church. He calls his listeners to be vegan, to plant the message in their own churches, and to "preach the gospel to every creature" (Mark 16:15).

The second half is a Q&A with viewers in the live chat. Wescoe answers comments on Psalm 119:105 and Jesus as "the lamp," on dominion, on the early believers' practice (promised for a future talk), and on the danger of trusting political authority over Scripture. He closes by previewing next week's talk on Romans 14:15 and how to win over one's church.

## Key Teachings

### 1. "Beautiful feet" means a messenger bringing good news, not outward appearance
Wescoe reads Isaiah 52:7 as the image of a runner bringing news of victory, using a battle analogy (two kings, one surrenders, and a messenger carries the word back). He ties this to 1 Samuel 16:7 (God looks at the heart, not appearance, as in David's anointing) and 1 Peter 3:3-4 (the "hidden person of the heart" and a "gentle and quiet spirit"). He argues that the beauty God values is inward and lies in putting the proclamation of the kingdom first, citing "seek first the kingdom" (Gospels, unspecified). He also reads Psalm 119:105 and John 1 together, arguing that the "word" that is a lamp to the feet is Jesus, who is the "light" that shows the way.

### 2. The gospel of peace is the end of violence, for humans and animals
Wescoe defines the "good news of peace" in Romans 10:15 as a world where "nobody's being violent," at war, killing or harming. He reads Isaiah 61:1 and Luke 4:18 as Jesus's mission statement, pointing to "liberty to the captives" and "the opening of the prison" as applying to animals in factory farms, cages, zoos and experiments. He says Jesus fulfilled this by liberating animals sold for slaughter in the temple court and by ending the system of animal sacrifice, and he cites Luke 4:43 and Luke 9:1-2 to argue that Jesus commissioned his disciples to proclaim the kingdom and heal, a purpose he says applies to followers today. He also says "proclaim" (Isaiah 61:1) carries the sense of calling out or declaring something into effect, citing a Strong's number (transliterated in the captions as "Hebrews seven 1 two one" and "La Croe").

### 3. The kingdom is a restoration of Eden, pictured in Revelation 21
Wescoe reads Revelation 21:3-5 ("I make all things new") as God restoring the order of Genesis 1-2, "before sin and death came into the world." He links the Holy of Holies in the tabernacle to Eden, a holy dwelling place where God lives with his creation, and describes heaven and earth being joined again. He argues the two great commandments (love God, love neighbor) sum up the law and the prophets, and that the kingdom is their realization, with no killing, death or suffering.

### 4. Creation's diet is plant-based, and the prophets picture a peaceable kingdom
Wescoe's central textual argument for a vegan diet is Genesis 1:29-30: humans are given "every herb that yields seed" and "every tree whose fruit yields seed" for food, and animals are given "every green herb." He reads Isaiah 11:6-9 (wolf and lamb, lion eating straw, the child leading the animals) and Hosea 2:18 (a covenant with "the beasts of the field" so all "lie down safely") as the restored kingdom. He adds Isaiah 2:4 (swords into plowshares) as a picture of growing food rather than making weapons, so that "neighbors, humans and animals" have their needs met in abundance. He also cites Revelation 5:13, where he says animals praise God alongside people.

### 5. "Dominion" means stewardship, and Genesis 2:15 shows the garden role
Against the reading that Genesis 1:26-28 gives humans license over animals, Wescoe argues that the very next verse (Genesis 1:29) gives plant food only, so "dominion" cannot mean eating animals. He reads Genesis 2:15 (the man placed in the garden "to tend and keep it") as the human role of keeping God's order. He says the "vegan" lifestyle includes not destroying anyone, "not just for food."

### 6. Objections he rebuts: naturalism, Peter's vision, and the unwashed-hands passage
Wescoe contrasts biblical hope with "metaphysical naturalists" who say carnivory is "just the way of nature," and says "killing is a symptom of a fallen world" that God will restore. He cites Genesis 6:5 and 6:11 (a world "filled with violence") as the wickedness that led to the flood, and says Enoch is cited in "other ancient works" on the same theme. Against Christians who use Peter's vision (in Acts, no reference given) to justify eating animals, he says the vision was about Gentile inclusion in salvation. Against those who cite the teaching that Jesus "declared all food clean," he says the passage concerns eating with unwashed hands. He also flags questions about creation chronology as eroding trust in Scripture.

### 7. Romans 14:17 and 1 Corinthians 8: don't use food to hurt a brother, and act in joy
Wescoe reads Romans 14:17 (the kingdom is "righteousness and peace and joy in the Holy Spirit") as the frame for diet, and ties it to 1 Corinthians 8 (a parallel passage on causing a brother to stumble), which he reads as a warning against using liberty to "kill my animal neighbors." He cites Proverbs 12:10 ("the righteous will care for the needs of their animals") and argues that giving animals joy, not taking it, is part of the kingdom. He says the early believers "were known for caring enough to not use animals," but defers that topic to a later talk.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Beautiful feet" means nice shoes or painted toenails.** Wescoe says the phrase refers to a messenger running to bring good news, and that God values the inward heart, not outward appearance (1 Samuel 16:7; 1 Peter 3:3-4).
- **Genesis 1 gives humans license to kill and eat animals ("dominion").** Wescoe argues that Genesis 1:29 gives only plant food, so dominion means stewardship and keeping the garden (Genesis 2:15).
- **Jesus declared all food clean, so animals may be eaten.** He says the passage concerns eating with unwashed hands and does not defile, not the status of animal foods.
- **Peter's vision in Acts justifies eating animals.** He says the vision was about salvation being open to Gentiles.
- **Killing is just the way nature works (carnivory as natural and hopeless).** He says killing is a symptom of a fallen world that God will restore, and that the Bible offers hope, not naturalist resignation.
- **Vegan food is bland, plain tofu, and unappealing.** He says apples, rice and beans are vegan, and that well-made vegan dishes (empanadas, cakes, cookies) can be welcomed at church potlucks.
- **Worship means singing and feeling devoted.** He argues that worship includes acting in accordance with God's will, including on animals.
