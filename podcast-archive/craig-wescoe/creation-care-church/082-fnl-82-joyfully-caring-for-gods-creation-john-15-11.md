## Summary

Craig Wescoe, presenting a Friday night live message for Creation Care Church, asks what John 15:11 ("these things I have spoken to you, that my joy may remain in you, and that your joy may be full") means for how Christians should treat animals. He argues that joy is a gift from God that does not depend on circumstances, and that the same fruit of the Spirit that governs human relationships (love, joy, peace, Galatians 5:22-23) should govern human conduct toward animals. He supports this mainly by reading a long chain of passages: John, Luke, 1 John, James, Hebrews, 1 Peter, 3 John, and especially Paul (1 Thessalonians, 2 Corinthians, Romans, Ephesians), plus Psalms and Revelation 5:13. He also cites Genesis 1:30 and 2:15 and Proverbs 12:10 for human stewardship of animals, and draws on his own experience at Toronto Pig Save and on animal sanctuaries.

The talk is less a textual argument about Greek or manuscript questions than a devotional reading. Craig's central move is to connect the peaceable, joyful Kingdom of Romans 14:17 and the renewed creation of Romans 8:18-19 to present duties toward animals. The talk ends with a closing prayer, a preview of the next talk on Revelation 11:18, and a note that Craig will answer chat questions in text afterward. ## Key Teachings

### 1. Joy as God's gift, independent of circumstances
Craig reads John 15:11 as joy that "comes from the lord" rather than from the world, and ties it to John 16:22 ("your joy, noone will take from you"). He cites Hebrews 12:2 to suggest Jesus endured the cross "for the joy that was set before him," and 1 Peter 1:8 for "joy inexpressible and full of glory." He describes this joy as a property of God that existed before creation and that God gives to his creatures, so that it is "unshakable in its foundation."

### 2. Joy in trials, not pleasure
Craig explicitly rejects the view that the meaning of life is maximizing pleasure, describing it as a "utilitarian sort of view." He says the biblical pattern often involves "choosing the path where you're choosing trials," citing James 1:2 ("count it all joy when you fall into various trials") and 2 Corinthians 7:4, where Paul says he is "exceedingly joyful in all our tribulation." He also says Paul was "stoned" and still counted joy in his sufferings. He does not give a chapter for the stoning reference.

### 3. Joy in repentance, love and gratitude
Using Luke 15:10 (joy among the angels over one sinner who repents), Craig defines repentance as turning from "the opposite of love" toward "up building." He pairs 1 Thessalonians 5:15-18 (rejoice always, pray without ceasing, give thanks) with a critique of consumer culture, which he says teaches people they are never beautiful, rich, or successful enough. He contrasts this with contentment and thankfulness for basic needs such as water, food and shelter. He ties these basics to God's provision and says forgetting them leads to forgetting joy.

### 4. The peaceable kingdom and animals
This is the talk's central application. Craig reads Romans 12:10-21, including "live peaceably with all," and argues that the Greek word for "all" (pantone) covers animals as well as people. He reads Jesus' call to be "perfect" as extending love to enemies as applying to animals. He then cites Proverbs 12:10 (the righteous care for their animals), Genesis 2:15 (keeping the garden), and Genesis 1:30 (giving animals their food) as establishing human stewardship from the beginning. He ties this to Romans 14:17 ("the kingdom of god is not food and drink but righteousness and peace and joy in the Holy Spirit"), arguing that in the kingdom "there is no killing, there is no death." Craig does not discuss how this passage was read historically; he presents the reading as the plain sense.

### 5. Hope and "biblical veganism" versus secular veganism
Craig says the Christian hope in a restored creation (Romans 8:18-19, creation "eagerly waits for the revealing of the children of god") is what separates "biblical veganism" from "secular veganism," which he says lacks grounds to expect the whole world to become vegan. He describes Christian hope as a reason to take the lead in advocacy. The argument is presented as a contrast with secular advocates, not as a claim about their actual beliefs in any detail.

### 6. Psalms and the song-book
Craig says the Psalms are "literally a song book" and that joy is a main focus of many of them. He cites Psalm 30:5 ("weeping may endure for a night, but joy comes in the morning"), Psalm 118:24, Psalm 16:11 ("in your presence is fullness of joy"), Psalm 71:23, Psalm 51:12, and Psalm 126:5 ("those who sow in tears shall reap in joy"). He applies Psalm 126:5 to his experience of sitting with pigs at Toronto Pig Save and argues that such exposure is what "emboldens" advocacy. He closes the Psalms section with Psalm 150:6 ("let everything that has breath praise the lord") and Revelation 5:13, which he reads as a final vision of every creature praising God.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Happiness or pleasure is the purpose of life.** Craig says the biblical view centers on joy that often comes through choosing trials, not on maximizing pleasure.
- **Joy depends on circumstances or on acquiring more.** Craig says joy comes from God and that the consumer cycle (more beauty, money, status) is a worldly program that cannot satisfy.
- **Animals do not suffer; their responses are only instinct.** Craig describes this as a common assumption among people who have never met farm animals, and presents sanctuary contact as correcting it. He is describing the belief rather than citing any study.
- **Christian love applies only to people.** Craig argues that the fruit of the Spirit and Jesus' command to love enemies, read through Romans 12:18's "all," extend to animals.
- **Secular and Christian vegans differ mainly in ethics.** Craig argues the difference is hope: Christians have grounds to expect a restored creation.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** The talk treats Paul as a direct authority on joy, suffering and creation care, and reads Jesus' sayings and Paul's letters as one consistent teaching. It does not distinguish them.
- **Torah:** Craig's stewardship arguments rest on Genesis 1:30, Genesis 2:15 and Proverbs 12:10, but he does not discuss sacrifice, dietary law or Torah observance as such.
- **Diet:** Craig argues Christians should avoid eating animals, presenting it as "biblical veganism," but the talk does not make an extended scriptural case for the diet itself.
- **Nonviolence:** Romans 12:14-21 (repaying no one evil for evil) and the peaceable kingdom are central. Craig applies them to animal rescue and advocacy, including a description of a driver killing an activist at Toronto Pig Save.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Romans 14:17 and Revelation 5:13 are read as a future renewed creation of peace, joy and no killing, including animals. Craig treats this as a present hope, not only a heavenly one.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** Not discussed in these terms; the "joy" passages are read as present and future joy, with no treatment of an afterlife or hell.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Briefly. Craig reads Jesus as "Yeshua" (salvation) and cites John 16:22 and Hebrews 12:2 to present him as the source and model of joy.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Not discussed. Craig treats the canon as a single collection of letters and psalms without addressing dating or authorship.
