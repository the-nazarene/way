## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks how God feels about those who destroy the earth, taking Revelation 11:18 ("destroy those who destroy the earth") as his starting point. He reads the verse as part of the book's end-time pattern of judgment, in which the people do not repent and further calamity follows. He then argues that God's displeasure is rooted in the created order. Starting from Genesis 2:15, he says humans were placed in the Garden to be *shamar* (keepers, guardians) of the earth as well as its animals. From there he builds a case through Matthew 7, John 2, Luke 12 and 16, Isaiah 45 and 65, Ezekiel 16, and 2 Chronicles 7:14 that God made the world to be inhabited and sustained, and that human greed, pride and neglect are what damage it.

Most of the talk is a verse-by-verse devotional reading, not a historical or source-critical study. Craig's practical conclusions are that humans should steward land, water, air and animals; that animal agriculture is a central form of this destruction; and that the remedy is repentance, a simpler life lived as a witness, and trust that God will restore the earth. He also cites Isaiah and Revelation to suggest that the earth's restoration and the end of killing are part of God's coming kingdom.

The talk ends with a long live Q&A in which Craig answers chat comments (from Fred, Ellen, Cathy, Heidi, Lily, Chris and others), a discussion of prayer options, and a closing prayer. Craig carries the entire talk. The chat participants contribute only questions and examples.

## Key Teachings

### Revelation 11:18 and the cycle of non-repentance
Craig places the verse at the "last days," when God brings judgment and establishes his kingdom. He describes a repeated pattern across Revelation: calamities occur, people "did not repent," and more calamities follow. He reads the "destroyers of the earth" as part of this pattern. He then asks what it means to "destroy the earth" and proposes that this is a list of human actions: waste, pollution of air and water, fracking, consumption, plastics, habitat loss and animal agriculture (with palm oil and cattle clearing of rainforest as examples).

### Stewardship, shamar and dominion (Genesis 1:26, Genesis 2:15, Luke 12:42-48)
Craig argues that "tend and keep" (Genesis 2:15) uses the Hebrew *shamar*, meaning to protect, maintain and watch over, so humans are guardians and not simply gardeners. He extends the same stewardship to the earth itself, citing Genesis 1:26, which gives dominion over fish, birds, cattle and "over the earth." He reads Luke 12:42-48 (the faithful steward whom the master finds at work) as applying to the whole world. He argues that because humans have been given "much," "much will be expected of you," so humans carry more responsibility than the animals. Craig draws the conclusion that veganism is "part of that" stewardship: "I think being vegan is part of that" (24:25).

### Good fruit and doing the will (Matthew 7:16-27, Matthew 28:18-20)
Craig reads the Sermon on the Mount's "by their fruits" passage as a test of whether one has God's Spirit. He emphasizes Matthew 7:21 ("not everyone who says to me, lord, lord"), and he stresses that doing God's will ("my law is love," tying in Matthew 22:37-39) is the test, not works-based merit. He reads Matthew 28:18-20 as Jesus giving authority to disciples, which Craig says Jesus exercises "through us," so Christians act as Jesus's "viceroys" on earth.

### The temple cleansing as a critique of the whole world (John 2:15-16, Luke 16:12-13)
Craig reads John 2:15-16 ("Do not make my father's house a house of merchandise") as a critique not only of commerce in the temple but of a world turned into a marketplace, where everything becomes a resource. He ties this to the idea that "the whole earth is God's dwelling place" (no reference given), so that the temple's dwelling-place theology extends to creation. Using Luke 16:13 ("you cannot serve both God and mammon"), he argues that money-worship is incompatible with maintaining God's good creation. He also suggests that withdrawal from society is not the only option, and that God calls people "where we're at" to be witnesses who reduce their impact.

### Pride, Sodom, and healing the land (Ezekiel 16:49, Proverbs 19:17, 2 Chronicles 7:14)
Craig reads Ezekiel 16:49 as listing Sodom's sins as pride, excess food, idleness and neglect of the poor, and he argues that humanity today is guilty of the same. He treats pride as the root of the other failings, including the belief that animals are mere commodities. He reads Proverbs 19:17 as a call to give to the poor, and 2 Chronicles 7:14 ("humble themselves... and heal their land") as the remedy for pride and wickedness. He says God is "willing to forgive us and to heal our land," so the earth's healing remains possible even if "scientists say" it is beyond repair.

### Restoration: new heavens and new earth, and the peaceable kingdom (Isaiah 45:18, Isaiah 65:17-25, Isaiah 11:6-9, Revelation 21:1-4)
Craig argues that God "did not create it in vain" and "formed it to be inhabited" (Isaiah 45:18), so destruction is contrary to God's design. He reads Isaiah 65:17 and Revelation 21 as God making "new heavens and a new earth," which he equates with renewal and restoration, not replacement. He links Isaiah 65:25 ("the lion shall eat straw like the ox") to Isaiah 11:6-9 and to the Garden of Eden, where Craig says all animals (including humans) were herbivores. He presents this as a sign that the kingdom will involve "no killing."

### Economic practice and the Jerusalem community (Acts, Paul, mention only)
In the Q&A, Craig says the Jerusalem community around Jesus's apostles (which he calls the "Ebionim" or "poor ones") shared possessions and lived communally according to need, and he presents it as a model for reflection, not a mandatory requirement. He says Paul refers to "the poor in Jerusalem" (no reference given). He also argues that possessions create burdens, that consumption must be limited, and that people should be "thankful for what little we do have."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Genesis dominion as license to exploit.** Craig argues that dominion in Genesis 1:26 is stewardship over God's creation, not ownership, and that humans bear more responsibility than the animals.
- **"Shamar" means only gardener.** He argues that *shamar* means guardianship and protection, which covers the earth and its animals as well as the garden.
- **"God is going to destroy it anyway, so it doesn't matter."** He challenges the fatalism and says God forgives and heals the land (2 Chronicles 7:14). He also says God did not create the world to be destroyed (Isaiah 45:18).
- **"God will fix everything when he comes back, so I don't have to act now."** He says the faithful steward must be found working when the master returns (Luke 12).
- **"What I eat is my decision and no one else's business."** He argues that subsidies, taxes, pollution and worker harm make diet a shared concern.
- **Salvation by works.** When discussing Matthew 7, he denies that the "by their fruits" test is a works-based way of earning entry to the kingdom.
