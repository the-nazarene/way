## Summary

Wescoe asks whether the sixth commandment, "Thou shalt not kill" (Exodus 20:13), applies only to humans or also to animals, and what the commandments as a whole mean. He opens with a contextual argument. The Sabbath command in Exodus 20:8-11 names the cattle as included in the rest, and the coveting command in Exodus 20:17 lists the ox and donkey as objects. From this he argues that animals are both subjects and objects of the Decalogue, so the unspecified "do not kill" should be read the same way. He then answers the usual objections. Texts where God commands killing, such as Leviticus 24:16, 1 Samuel 15:3, Genesis 22:2 and the animal sacrifice laws, would by the same logic remove the prohibition from humans too. He sets these against Jesus' teaching on loving enemies (Matthew 5:44-45) and a "beginning" ideal drawn from Genesis 1:29-30 and Isaiah 11:6-9.

His main move is to argue that Torah permissions were concessions "because of the hardness of your hearts," citing Jesus' divorce teaching in Matthew 19:3-8. Prophetic texts such as Jeremiah 7:22, Psalm 40:6 and Hosea 6:6 are used to argue that God did not really desire animal sacrifice. The talk ends with the Decalogue read as an expansion of the two great commandments (Matthew 22:37-40), and with the claim that "thou shalt not kill" applies to animals in God's ideal world. Wescoe presents this as his reading. He does not present it as the only possible one. In the Q&A he admits that some questions are open, for example how the Genesis 9 and Pentateuch passages arose, and he says the "lying pen of the scribe" and divine accommodation to human hardness amount to the same practical conclusion for him.

The talk closes with a prayer and a note about the next weeks' schedule. The Q&A has also been used to point viewers to his earlier live talks on related passages.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Context: the Decalogue includes animals as subjects and objects
Wescoe argues that the commandments do not name their audience in every case. The Sabbath command (Exodus 20:8-11) explicitly includes "your cattle" and "your stranger," and the coveting command (Exodus 20:17) lists "his ox" and "his donkey." He concludes that animals are both subject and object of the Decalogue, so "do not kill" should be read with the same audience. He calls this "basic reading comprehension" and says the contextual evidence "does suggest that it would also be pertaining to animals."

### 2. Killing texts cut both ways
He presents the usual objection (God commanded killing animals, so the command cannot cover animals) and then says the same logic would apply to humans. He cites Leviticus 24:16 (stoning for blasphemy), 1 Samuel 15:3 (the Amalek command, which includes "ox and sheep, camel, and donkey") and Genesis 22:2 (Isaac as burnt offering). He also cites Deuteronomy 12:31 against child sacrifice. He notes the textual issue with John 8:3-11 (the adulterous woman) but uses it anyway to show Jesus offering an alternative to the Mosaic penalty. He says Jesus "seems to be teaching something different" (citing Matthew 5:44-45).

### 3. Genesis 9 permission versus the Genesis 1 ideal
Wescoe reads Genesis 9:2-6 as the post-Flood grant of animals as food, with the blood prohibition and the "reckoning" for shed human blood. He says that fear of humans among the animals is a result of the fall, not of God's plan, and links it to Genesis 8:21 and Habakkuk 2:17. He sets this against Genesis 1:29-31, where humans and animals are given plants as food, and then reads the Sabbath rest and the Decalogue as consistent with that "ideal." He says the passages that permit killing are "inconsistent with god ideal" and often accompanied by language like "your own evil imagination."

### 4. Animal sacrifice is not God's will
Wescoe argues that Exodus 20:24 ("your sheep and your oxen") is not the last word, citing Jeremiah 7:22 (God "did not speak... concerning burnt offerings or sacrifices" at the Exodus), Psalm 40:6 and Hosea 6:6 ("I desire mercy, not sacrifice"). He says the "best reading" is either corruption by "the lying pen of the scribe" or a divine meeting-people-where-they-are accommodation. In the Q&A he cites Amos 5:25-27 (raised by Chris) and mentions the documentary hypothesis and a "priestly source" dated by him to the time of Hezekiah or Ezra, while saying he is not settling the question. He also reads the Noah altar scene (Genesis 8:20-21) as a case of people reading "only the parts that they want to read," with God's "although the imagination of man's heart is evil" meaning the sacrifice did not change God's disposition.

### 5. Jesus as interpreter: the beginning takes precedence
The central hermeneutical claim is that when Scripture conflicts, the earlier ideal governs. Wescoe reads Matthew 19:3-8 as Jesus saying that Moses permitted divorce "because of the hardness of your hearts" but "from the beginning, it was not so." He generalizes this: "we shouldn't look to certain passages in Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy, as god's eternal ideal." He backs this with Malachi 3:6 ("I do not change") and a paraphrase of Jesus' "I am the same yesterday, today, and forever," arguing that God's will at the beginning and at the end is the same.

### 6. The Decalogue as expansion of the two great commandments
He reads Matthew 22:37-40 (Deuteronomy 6:5 and Leviticus 19:18) as summarizing the Decalogue: the first four commandments concern loyalty to God, and the rest concern the neighbor. Sabbath is the bridge that includes animals. Isaiah 11:6-9 is then used as the picture of the restored creation where "They shall not hurt nor destroy in all my holy mountain," and Wescoe concludes that "thou shalt not kill also applies to animal[s]," since otherwise "we're falling short of both of these ideals."

### 7. Meaning of "murder" and the animal question
In answer to Lily's question, Wescoe says the Hebrew word is "tressa," which he glosses as "to dash into pieces" (he cites Strong's Hebrew 7523 as a lexicon entry). He distinguishes legal murder from manslaughter under US law, and notes that under current US law animals are not "persons," so animals cannot be murdered in that legal sense. He argues that God's view is not bound to human legal definitions and that factory farming (forced impregnation, cages, pre-stamped kill dates, transport and processing) fits the premeditation he says the legal definition requires. He points to Matthew King's book *I shall abolish the bow* on animal personhood in the Bible.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Thou shalt not kill" applies only to humans.** Wescoe argues the Sabbath and coveting commands name animals as subject and object, so the bare "do not kill" should be read the same way.
- **God commanded animal sacrifice, so killing animals is allowed.** He says the sacrifice texts are contradicted by Jeremiah 7:22, Psalm 40:6 and Hosea 6:6, and that such laws reflect concession or scribal alteration.
- **Genesis 9 gives humans permission to kill and eat animals.** He reads Genesis 9 as a post-Flood concession tied to human hardness, and treats Genesis 1:29-31 as the ideal.
- **Killing commands elsewhere show that "do not kill" only refers to humans.** He argues the same logic would then remove the human prohibition, because God also commanded killing of humans in Leviticus 24:16 and 1 Samuel 15:3.
- **The Torah is God's eternal, unchanging ideal.** He argues that Jesus, in Matthew 19, prioritizes the "beginning," and that God's unchanging nature means the beginning and end show the true will.
- **"Murder" means only what the legal system says.** He argues that legal definitions are not the final authority, and that factory farming fits the premeditation and killing elements he thinks define murder.
