## Summary

Craig Wescoe, host of this Creation Care Church live talk, takes up a question from a viewer, Christy Lynn: are vegans causing division by advocating kindness toward animals? He builds on Matthew 10:34-35, where Jesus says he came to bring "a sword" and to set people against their families. Wescoe argues that the unity Scripture asks for is not unity for its own sake. It is unity "in a particular way," defined by one mind, love, compassion and the "bond of peace," drawn from 1 Corinthians 1:10, 1 Peter 3:8, Ephesians 4:3, Philippians 2:2 and 2 Corinthians 13:11. Division is legitimate when someone refuses God's will, and Wescoe reads Jesus's sword saying as a call to separate from those who reject his kingdom, not as a license for violence, citing Matthew 26:52-53 for that reading.

His main evidence for the claim that vegans are not the divisive party is a chain of verses that moves from correction to diet. He cites Luke 17:3-4, Galatians 6:1 and Revelation 3:19 for a gentle, private, escalating process of correction. He then cites James 4:17 ("to him who knows to do good and does not do it, to him it is sin") and applies it to Genesis 1:29-31, which he reads as God's original plant-based diet. On that basis he concludes that the people who keep eating animals, not the vegan who points this out, are the ones who need to change (Romans 14:15 and Philippians 3:19 are used here too). He acknowledges that his argument rests on his own reading of these passages, and that the harder case is when a church leader accuses an advocate of causing division.

The talk ends with a Q&A in which Wescoe gives practical advice: take a stand without humiliating people, start with accommodation (the "peanut allergy" analogy), try a trial "Daniel diet," and point accusers to his earlier talks. He then previews the next live talk, on evidence for veganism in the pre-Nicene church, and closes with prayer.

## Key Teachings

### Unity is defined by its content, not by togetherness
Wescoe reads a run of Pauline and Petrine verses (1 Corinthians 1:10, 1 Peter 3:8, Ephesians 4:3, Philippians 2:2, 2 Corinthians 13:11) and argues that each ties unity to love, compassion and peace. His conclusion is that a unity "contrary to love" or "contrary to peace" is not the unity the apostles promote. He uses the Tower of Babel (Genesis 11) as a counterexample: the people were unified, but in acting against God's will, so God scattered them. The point is that a group can be united and still wrong.

### Jesus's "sword" is a division between followers and rejecters, not a call to violence
Wescoe reads Matthew 10:34-35 against Matthew 26:52-53, where Jesus tells Peter to put his sword away. He says Jesus is "clear that his kingdom is a kingdom of peace," and that the division Jesus causes is like a sword's cut: it separates those who accept his way of peace and love from those who reject it. He applies this to 2 Corinthians 6:14 ("unequally yoked") and 1 Corinthians 10:21 ("cup of the Lord and the cup of demons") as places where division is necessary because the two loyalties are mutually exclusive.

### A process of correction: private first, then the congregation
Drawing on Luke 17:3-4, Galatians 6:1 and Revelation 3:19, Wescoe describes a sequence: go to the person privately, bring one or two others if they refuse, then bring it before leadership and the congregation. The aim is restoration, not humiliation or cancellation. He warns against pride in the one correcting, citing the "speck and plank" image without giving a verse. Revelation 3:19 ("as many as I love, I rebuke and chasten") is used to argue that correction is a sign of God's love.

### Diet as the test case: James 4:17 applied to Genesis 1:29-31
This is the core argument. Wescoe reads James 4:17 as a formula: if you know what is good and do not do it, it is sin. He then reads Genesis 1:29-31 (herbs and fruit for food, "very good") as the good that God has named, and concludes that eating animal flesh is a known departure from God's will. He supports this with Luke 6:36 ("be merciful just as your father"), Romans 12:2 (transformed by the renewing of your mind), and Philippians 3:19 ("whose god is their belly"), arguing that defending meat-eating is "serving our belly." He also cites Galatians 5:17 and 5:22-23 (flesh versus spirit, the fruit of the Spirit) and Romans 14:15 (do not grieve your brother with food). He says Paul's Romans 14 teaching is that the eater of meat, not the vegan, is the one "not walking in love." Wescoe frames these as his own reading and says he has argued the full case in earlier talks.

### Practical application: accommodation, a trial diet, and Isaiah 11
In the Q&A, Wescoe answers a viewer whose church serves bacon and eggs. He suggests starting with accommodation, as one would for a peanut allergy, then moving to a food-coordinator role, then proposing a trial "Daniel diet" for a month, and only then introducing the scripture case, including Isaiah 11:6-9 on peaceable animals. He also advises a humble private reconciliation if the friendship has been strained. He tells another viewer that unity should be "welcoming" and that an inclusive table is compatible with this view.

### Responding to accusations and to injustice in the church
Asked about leaders who accuse advocates of causing division, Wescoe says the answer is "simple but not easy": take a stand, anchored in the God of love and peace, and risk being "unfriended" or "not invited to the party." He points to Isaiah 1:17 (seek justice, reprove the oppressor) as a reason to name injustice, and he links racism, poverty and animal treatment as parallel issues. He admits that political affiliation is a source of division he wants to avoid, and says the question of who must change "can get really complicated."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Christian unity means avoiding all conflict or going along with the group.** Wescoe argues that unity is only good when it is unity in love, peace and God's will; a unity built on acting against God's will is the Tower of Babel error.
- **Jesus's "sword" in Matthew 10:34 licenses or endorses violence.** Wescoe reads it as a sword that divides (between those who follow and reject his kingdom), and points to Matthew 26:52-53 as the clarification.
- **The person who raises an ethical issue is the one causing division.** Wescoe says the one who refuses correction and keeps doing what God has named as wrong is the source of the division.
- **A minority position should give way to the majority's customs.** He rejects a majority-based norm: "It's not about a majority thing," and "we say everyone needs to change because we all need to be led by Christ."
- **Eating all foods is clearly permitted for Christians.** Responding to a viewer (Lily), Wescoe says most Christians assume all foods are clean and may not realize they are departing from what God called good. He refers viewers to an earlier talk titled "Did Jesus declare all foods clean?"
