## Summary

The talk asks whether there were vegans in the early church, and in particular among the apostles and members of Jesus' family. The presenter argues yes. He first sets out four names for Jesus' followers in the New Testament (Christians, Nazarenes, "the Way," and Ebionim), and he identifies the Ebionim as the Jerusalem community of "poor saints" led by James. He then reads Acts, Paul's letters and Epiphanius to argue that this community pooled its property, held a special place for Paul, and was known for abstaining from meat, wine and animal sacrifice.

His evidence for diet comes mainly from later writers: Epiphanius (Nazarenes and Ebionites), Hegesippus as quoted by Eusebius (James), Clement of Alexandria (Matthew), the Acts of Thomas (Judas Thomas), the Clementine Homilies (Peter), the Protoevangelium of James (Mary), Eusebius's *Demonstratio Evangelica* (the disciples in general) and Theophilus of Antioch (on Genesis 1:29-30). He adds Paul's statements in 1 Corinthians 8 and Romans 14 as Paul's own abstention. He also argues that Luke 24:42-44 (the fish) is a later addition, and that Jesus' "law and the prophets" phrasing reflects an earlier canon than the one used in the late second century.

The talk ends with a Q&A on fish, locusts, honey, wool, grape juice and animal sacrifice, followed by a prayer. The presenter says that anyone claiming there is no evidence for early Christian veganism is "wrong," but he also concedes that some Bible passages "seem to suggest otherwise," which he defers to later talks. Many of his claims rest on sources he reads from memory or on his own readings, and several verse references in the captions are garbled.

## Key Teachings

### Four names for Jesus' followers, and what "Ebionim" means
Using John 1:41, Mark 8:29 and Luke 4:41, the presenter identifies Jesus as the Christ, and reads Acts 11 ("the disciples were first called Christians in Antioch") to identify the Christians. He reads John 18:7, John 19:19 and Acts 24:5 to identify the Nazarenes, and John 14:6 with Acts 9:2, 22:4 and 24:14 to identify "the Way." He presents "Ebionim" (the poor ones) as a narrower term for the Jerusalem church of the apostles and their disciples, drawing on 2 Corinthians 8:9 and Luke 6:20. He says the group was also called "the Jerusalem Church," "the Apostolic Community," and "the Poor Saints in Jerusalem."

### The Jerusalem community: pooled property and the cost of joining
The presenter argues that the Ebionim held all things in common. He reads Luke 14:33, Acts 2:44-45, Acts 4:32-35 and Acts 5:1-3 (Ananias and Sapphira, who kept back part of the money) to argue that entry required giving everything to the apostles. He reads Mark 10:21 (and the parallel in Luke 18:22) as Jesus telling the rich young man to sell everything and join the community. He calls James the head of this community and reads James 2:5 and Acts 21:17-19 to support that.

### Paul's relation to the Ebionim
The presenter reads Paul's collection for "the poor saints" in Jerusalem (Romans 15:26-27, 1 Corinthians 16:1-3, 2 Corinthians 8:1-5) and Galatians 2:9-10, where James, Peter and John ask Paul to "remember the poor." He reads Galatians 2:1-2 as Paul seeking the Jerusalem leaders' approval of his gospel, and Acts 15:1-7 as the Jerusalem assembly ruling on circumcision. He uses these to argue that the Jerusalem community was the recognised authority that Paul deferred to.

### The Nazarenes and the Ebionites, according to Epiphanius
- **Nazarenes** (Epiphanius, *Panarion* 18.1.4): the presenter reads that they "would not offer sacrifice or eat meat," and that they said these customs "were not instituted by the fathers." He reads a "two ways" teaching (love God, love your neighbour, the golden rule) and a line about food: "bear what you are able," and avoid food sacrificed to idols.
- **Ebionites** (*Panarion* 30.17.1-2, 30.16.5): Epiphanius says they were called poor because they sold their possessions and laid them at the apostles' feet. The presenter reads a saying "I came to abolish the sacrifices" and links it to Jesus cleansing the temple and to Hebrews. He reads *Panarion* 30.18.7-9 as saying they rejected parts of the Pentateuch (on eating meat, Abraham, Noah, Isaac and Jacob), and he notes that Epiphanius is hostile to them.

### Individual figures who "were vegan" (as the presenter reads the sources)
- **James** (Hegesippus, quoted in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 2.23.5-6): "he drank no wine, nor strong drink, nor did he eat flesh."
- **Matthew** (Clement of Alexandria, *Paedagogus* 2.1.16): ate "seeds and nuts and vegetables without flesh."
- **Judas Thomas** (Acts of Thomas): fasted, ate "only bread with salt," drank water. The presenter says he is unsure whether Judas was the twin of Simeon or of James.
- **Peter** (Clementine Homilies 12.6 and 7.4, attributed to Clement of Rome): ate "only bread and olives and rarely pot herbs." The presenter links this to Acts 10:14 and argues that "common or unclean" meant anything outside that plant diet.
- **Paul** (1 Corinthians 8:13, Romans 14:21): the presenter reads Paul's own words as a personal vow to avoid meat and wine for the sake of others.
- **Mary** (Protoevangelium of James ch. 6): her parents kept "nothing common or unclean" from her, and she was fed by an angel, which he links to "primary Adam literature" (unnamed).
- **The disciples in general** (Eusebius, *Demonstratio Evangelica* 3.5): the apostles abstained "from wine and meat."

### Theophilus of Antioch and Genesis 1:29-30
The presenter links Theophilus, the addressee of Luke 1:1-4, to Theophilus's *To Autolycus* 2.16, where Theophilus says those who keep God's law eat "the seeds of the earth" and do not injure weaker creatures. He reads this as a reference to Genesis 1:29-30 and to the law of loving one's neighbour, and argues that Theophilus was teaching his congregation at Antioch.

### Textual arguments: Luke 24 and the "law and the prophets" phrase
The presenter argues that Luke 24:42-44 (Jesus eats "broiled fish") is a later addition, on three grounds: manuscripts differ on whether honeycomb is included; the episode seems aimed at Marcion's claim of a non-bodily resurrection; and "the law of Moses and the prophets and the Psalms" implies a three-part canon that he dates to the late second century. He notes that elsewhere the New Testament says only "the law and the prophets." He also argues that the Greek "locusts" for John the Baptist may come from a Hebrew word for a sweet cake, which he links to a manna passage in the Gospel of the Ebionites (his claim that Matthew was originally in Hebrew).

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"There was no veganism in the early church."** The presenter says this is wrong and points to the patristic and New Testament texts he has listed.
- **"Jesus ate fish, so eating fish is fine."** He argues that Luke 24:42-44 is a late addition and that the "fish" episode is not original.
- **"Locusts and honey" means insects and bee honey.** He argues that the Greek may be a mistranslation of a Hebrew term for a sweet cake or manna, and that "honey" in the feast context may mean date paste.
- **"Animal sacrifice was mandatory for Jews of Jesus' day."** He says this "depends on your interpretation," that there was no uniform canon or practice, and that Jesus, as a "true prophet," opposed sacrifice according to Hebrews and some extra-biblical sources.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** The talk uses Paul's letters as evidence for the Jerusalem community and for Paul's own abstention (1 Corinthians 8:13; Romans 14:21). It does not contrast Paul's teaching with Jesus' on diet or sacrifice, and it treats Paul as deferring to James and the Jerusalem assembly.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** The presenter identifies James as head of the Jerusalem "Ebionim," bases this on Acts 21 and James 2:5, and uses Hegesippus to describe James's diet.
- **The Nazarenes, Ebionites and Essenes:** The Nazarenes and Ebionites are central, drawn from Epiphanius. The presenter treats the Ebionites Epiphanius describes as the same group as the Jerusalem "poor saints," an identification the talk assumes rather than argues. Essenes appear only in the Q&A, as one of several competing scriptural groups.
- **Torah, sacrifice and diet:** The talk argues that Genesis 1:29-30 and Pentateuchal teaching support a plant diet, that Jesus opposed animal sacrifice, and that some Ebionite-type groups rejected parts of the Pentateuch. The Pentateuch-alteration claim is attributed to Epiphanius's hostile report.
- **Nonviolence:** The presenter links the "do not hurt" principle in Isaiah 11:9 and the Acts of Thomas to nonviolence toward animals, and mentions *ahimsa*.
- **Resurrection:** The presenter's Luke 24 argument is that the bodily-resurrection detail was added to answer Marcion. He does not make a positive argument about the resurrection itself.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** The "law and the prophets" phrasing and the Psalms argument are offered as evidence that Luke 24:44 reflects a late second-century canon. The presenter also says many early writings were destroyed as heretical, which limits the evidence.
