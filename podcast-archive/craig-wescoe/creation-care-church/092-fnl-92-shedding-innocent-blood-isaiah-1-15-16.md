## Summary

Craig Wescoe, host of Creation Care Church's "Friday Night Live" series, asks what it means to "shed innocent blood" in Isaiah 1:15-16, why God hates it, and whether shedding animal blood counts. He answers mainly by building a concordance-style case across the prophets, Proverbs, Psalms and the gospels. He reads the phrase as tied to violence, oppression, covetousness and pride, set against a "path of peace" tied to God's wisdom. He argues that "innocent" extends to animals, citing the Passover lamb, the David and Nathan parable in 2 Samuel 12, and Genesis 1 and 9. He also argues that some translators soften "sacrifices" in Jeremiah 19:4 and that the Passover lamb and Jesus' blood are linked as the paradigm of innocence.

Craig then moves to repentance and the kingdom (1 Peter 3:11, Acts 17:30, Matthew 4:17) and presents Genesis 9:3-5 as a covenant text in which the diet for humans is plant-based. In the Q&A he takes up Leviticus 11, offering a "permissive will versus ideal will" reading as one option and a documentary-hypothesis (critical source) reading as another, and he says he leans toward the first while remarking that critical scholars favor the second. He ends with a prayer and a preview of next week's talk on Philippians 4:13.

Throughout, the talk presents its positions as Craig's own readings. It rests on his translation choices and on the reading of Genesis 9 that he attributes to a critic's article, and it does not engage the scholarly debates in detail.

## Key Teachings

### 1. What "shedding innocent blood" means: violence, oppression, covetousness, pride
Craig builds the definition by cross-reading passages. Isaiah 59:7-8 sets shedding innocent blood against "the way of peace," with "destruction" in its paths. Jeremiah 22:3 links it to an oppressor who does "violence to the stranger the fatherless or the Widow." Jeremiah 22:17 links it to covetousness, and Proverbs 6:16-19 lists "hands that shed innocent blood" alongside "a proud look." Craig treats covetousness and pride as the motives behind the act: wanting "something that's not intended for you" and believing "I matter" more than the victim.

### 2. God's hatred and the refining process
Craig reads Psalm 11:5 ("his soul hates") as the strongest statement in the chapter. He uses the image of a metalsmith's refiner's fire, and of God as potter, to argue that God removes what is "not supposed to be there" so that people reflect his image. The talk treats hatred of violence as God's refining purpose, not a separate theme.

### 3. The "path of peace" and the Beatitudes
Craig argues that Proverbs 3:13-17 (wisdom's "ways of pleasantness" and "paths are peace") is the direct contrast to the path of destruction. He links this to Matthew 5:5-9 (the peacemakers "shall be called children of God") and to Romans 12:18 ("live peaceably with all"). He notes that the Greek word for "all" (pantōn) carries no word for "people," so the instruction covers everything around the believer, not just humans. Proverbs 3:31 ("do not envy the oppressor") closes this line.

### 4. Do animals count? Sacrifice, the Passover lamb and 2 Samuel 12
Craig argues that animals fall within "innocent blood." He cites Exodus 20:13 ("thou shall not kill," with a reference to an earlier live talk on animals) and Exodus 12:5 (the unblemished Passover lamb). He suggests Jeremiah 19:4's "sacrifices" was softened to "incense" by translators unwilling to imply that God opposes animal sacrifice. He reads 1 Peter 1:18-19 and Matthew 27:3-4 as the lamb-without-blemish image of Jesus' innocent blood. He calls the 2 Samuel 12 ewe-lamb parable a case where David immediately recognizes the wrong done to an animal, which Craig takes as evidence that the moral intuition about animals was shared in the biblical world.

### 5. Repentance and the kingdom
Craig reads 1 Peter 3:11 ("turn away from Evil and do good... seek peace and pursue it") and Acts 17:30 (God "commands all men everywhere to repent") as calls to turn from violence. He distinguishes confession from repentance as the act of turning. He then ties Matthew 4:17 ("repent for the kingdom of God is at hand") to bringing the kingdom into the world through peacemaking.

### 6. Diet in Genesis 1 and 9
Craig reads Genesis 1:29-30 as giving humans seed-bearing herbs and fruit, and animals "every green herb." He reads Genesis 9:3-5 ("every moving thing that lives will be food for you... even as the green herbs") as spoken to animals, not humans, since the human diet was already given in Genesis 1. He reads "flesh with its life, that is its blood" as a prohibition, and "surely for your life blood I will demand a reckoning" as the consequence for taking human or animal life. He adds Genesis 9:15, where God makes a covenant with "every living creature," as an argument that animals are covenant partners, not menu items.

### 7. Leviticus 11 and the Q&A on scriptural reading
Responding to a viewer question, Craig offers two readings of Leviticus 11. The first is a "permissive will" framework in which God meets people where they are, weaning them from worship of other gods, blood-drinking and flesh-eating (he cites Numbers 11 on the desire to return to Egypt). The second is the documentary hypothesis: he says that "most like critical Scholars" favor a view that the Pentateuch was redacted at the time of Hezekiah or during Ezra-Nehemiah, perhaps incorporating Babylonian concepts from the captivity. He says he personally tends toward the first reading, taking the text "in its current form," and holds that God's "ideal will" never changes. He also uses the analogy of addiction and weaning (comparing cheese to casomorphine), which the talk presents as an explanation for slow change rather than as a scriptural claim.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Shedding innocent blood means only human murder.** Craig argues it includes violence and oppression broadly and that animals fall within "innocent" through Exodus 20:13 and the 2 Samuel 12 parable.
- **Jeremiah 19:4's "sacrifices" should read as "incense."** Craig argues the translation is softened because translators are reluctant to imply God opposes animal sacrifice.
- **Genesis 9:5 makes animals "menu items."** Craig argues that God's covenant with the animals (Genesis 9:15) and the "reckoning" language undercut that reading.
- **Covetousness just means wanting too much.** Craig says it is wanting what is "not intended for you," and applies this to drinking cow's milk.
- **Leviticus 11 reflects God's ideal will.** Craig offers the "permissive will" and the redaction readings as alternatives, and says his personal lean is toward the first.
