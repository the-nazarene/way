## Summary

Wescoe asks what the story of Balaam's donkey in Numbers 22:21-29 is meant to teach. He reads the donkey as a hero: she turns aside from an angel with a drawn sword three times, and each time Balaam beats her, yet she keeps saving him. He argues that this is the pattern of long-suffering, self-sacrificing love that God shows to sinners, and that Jesus embodies it. Balaam's repentance at the end (Numbers 22:34) is presented as the outcome of that patient mercy.

To support the reading, Wescoe builds a chain of cross-references. He cites Jonah 3-4, Isaiah 55:7, 2 Peter 3:9, Romans 5:8, 1 Peter 2:21-25 and Luke 23:33-34 for God's patience and forgiveness. He then reads animal imagery in Scripture as a contrast: the serpent, wolves and lion stand for the adversary, while the lamb, dove and mother hen stand for Christ and the Spirit. He ties this to John 1:29, John 3:16-17, John 10 and Luke 13:34, and applies it to how Christians should treat animals. He does not cite any historical scholarship or ancient source beyond Scripture.

The second half is a Q&A where viewers ask about Jonah, about the law, about goats and predators, and about quail in Numbers 11. Wescoe answers each with his own reading of Scripture (for example, that Jesus did not abolish the law's essence and that animal sacrifice is done away with). The talk ends with a prayer for God's kingdom and a preview of next week's topic, "My kingdom is not of this world" (John 18:36).

## Key Teachings

### The donkey as a model of long-suffering love
Wescoe reads Numbers 22:21-29 closely. The donkey's question, "What have I done to you that you have struck me these three times?", is treated as the central moral point. He argues against the reading that she acted only from self-preservation, citing the angel's words in Numbers 22:33 ("I would have killed you and not harmed the donkey") as proof that her motive was to save Balaam. He says the story is "a hero story" in which the donkey shows the love that Christ has for sinners.

### God's patience and the call to repent
Wescoe moves from the donkey to God's character. He cites Jonah 3:10 and 4:11 (the Ninevites turn from evil and God relents), Isaiah 55:7 (let the wicked forsake his way), and 2 Peter 3:9 (God is "long-suffering toward us, not willing that any should perish"). His argument is that God delays final judgment so that people can repent. He applies this to readers who may be harming animals, personally or through purchases, and urges them to turn away from that harm.

### Christ as the self-sacrificing, innocent victim
Romans 5:8, 1 Peter 2:21-25 and Luke 23:33-34 are read as showing Jesus returning good for abuse, forgiving his executioners, and bearing sin "in his own body." Wescoe compares this directly to the donkey. He also draws on John 1:29 (the lamb of God), describing the lamb as "the most innocent type of creature" and Jesus as the slain lamb who is without sin.

### Animal imagery: adversary versus Christ and the Spirit
Wescoe sorts biblical animal images into two groups. In the first, the serpent (Genesis 3:4-5, which he links to Revelation as Satan), the wolves (Matthew 7:15) and the roaring lion (1 Peter 5:8) represent deception and devouring. In the second, the lamb (John 1:29), the dove at Jesus' baptism (Luke 3:22) and the mother hen (Luke 13:34; also "Psalms" for the image of protection under wings) represent innocence, peace and love. He reads Matthew 10:16 ("wise as serpents and harmless as doves") as a command to know the adversary's tactics without using them to harm others.

### Jesus' purpose and the rejection of mercy
Wescoe reads John 3:17 as saying the Son came to save, not condemn. He then reads John 10:25-33 and 14:10-11, arguing that the people who picked up stones against Jesus were, like Balaam, acting in ignorance of the mercy being offered to them. He adds that the rejection is by "the world as a whole," not any particular group. He closes this section by applying the model to readers: they should repent and receive Christ's mercy, and live out his love toward creation.

### The law, sacrifice and the principle of reading Scripture from the beginning (Q&A)
In answer to Shirley, Wescoe says the question of whether Jesus fulfilled or abolished Leviticus "is very complicated." He cites Paul's statements that believers are "not under the law" alongside Matthew 5:17 ("I have not come to destroy the law... but to fulfill it"). He holds that the essence of the law, love of God and neighbor, remains, and that animal sacrifice is "certainly done away with." In answer to Fred, he uses the divorce passage (Jesus says "in the beginning, it was not so") as a hermeneutic: read Scripture back to creation. On that basis he reads Genesis 1 (the plant diet) as God's original will, which he says Isaiah 11 and Revelation 21:4 also reflect.

### Diet and the quail (Q&A)
Asked about the quail in Numbers 11, Wescoe argues that the people were "serving the desires of your flesh," and that God gave them the meat as judgment. He cites Numbers 11:19-20 ("until it comes out your nostrils") and 11:33 (the plague) and calls the episode a warning against craving, not an endorsement of eating animals.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The donkey acted only out of self-preservation.** Wescoe says the text rules this out, since the angel states that the donkey would not have been harmed.
- **Animal images in Scripture (wolves, snakes, lions, goats) license harming those animals.** Wescoe (Q&A, responding to Nicole and Heidi) says these are metaphors for character traits, that predators in the kingdom are transformed (Isaiah 11), and that the problem is "fallen behaviors," not the animals.
- **God hates goats, so farmed-animal sanctuaries should not rescue them.** Heidi reports this claim from another page. Wescoe rejects it as bad exegesis that reverses God's will.
- **The quail in Numbers 11 shows God approving of eating meat.** Wescoe argues the passage shows God giving the people over to their craving, with deadly consequences.
- **Jesus abolished the laws in Leviticus.** Wescoe (answering Shirley) says the matter is complicated, that the moral essence of the law (love of God and neighbor) stands, and that animal sacrifice is done away with. He does not endorse the Bible-study claim that Jesus "fulfilled" Leviticus in a way that keeps its laws.
- **God will destroy the wicked now and establish his kingdom immediately.** Wescoe answers with 2 Peter 3:9: God delays so that people can repent.
