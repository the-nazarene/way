## Summary

Wescoe asks whether a "vegan awakening" is happening in the church, using Romans 13:11 ("the hour has come for you to wake up from your slumber") as the frame. He argues that Paul is addressing believers, not unbelievers, and that the "waking up" means becoming watchful, prepared and faithful to God's will. He builds this by reading a chain of passages: Romans 13:11-12, Ephesians 6:11-18 (the armor of God), Ephesians 5:14-16, Matthew 5:14-16, Matthew 26:36-45 (Gethsemane), Matthew 4:17, 1 Peter 5:8, the parables of the ten virgins (Matthew 25:1-13) and the faithful steward (Luke 12:42-47). He then ties the "breastplate of righteousness" to Proverbs 12:10 ("the righteous care for the needs of the animals") and reads the steward parable through Genesis 1, where humans are given dominion and food is given as plants (Genesis 1:29).

His evidence for an awakening is mostly observational and anecdotal. He points to plant-based milk taking up most of a grocery dairy case, more documentaries, podcasts and YouTube channels, and vegan products becoming familiar to ordinary people. He also says "God is planting a vegan in every church," and he describes a church leader who reportedly defended animal sacrifice when challenged. He presents no survey or statistics.

The talk ends with a live chat Q&A (on robots, the parables' audience, persuading people, and whether Christians will come to accept veganism), a preview of next week's talk on the Book of Enoch, and a closing prayer.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Romans 13:11-12 is addressed to believers who must wake up
Wescoe says he had not noticed before that Paul is writing to believers ("he's talking to Believers and saying Believers need to wake up," 04:34). He reads the "waking" in Romans 13:12 ("cast off the works of darkness... put on the armor of light") as a call to spiritual vigilance. He then uses Ephesians 6:11-18 to explain the armor: the spiritual enemy is not flesh and blood but "principalities" and "powers," so the battle is spiritual (06:07). He identifies the belt of truth, breastplate of righteousness, gospel shoes, shield of faith, helmet of salvation and sword of the Spirit (the word of God), citing Jesus' use of Deuteronomy in the temptation (07:40 to 12:22).

### 2. Watchfulness and preparedness in the parables
Wescoe reads the Gethsemane narrative (Matthew 26:36-45) as a warning that even the disciples fell asleep, and that "the spirit indeed is willing but the flesh is weak" (18:54). He treats the ten virgins as a parable about being prepared with oil (26:30-29:39), and he uses the faithful steward in Luke 12:42-47 to argue that Jesus will return and expect the steward to be doing what he was commanded (32:09). For him, the "return" is the coming of the kingdom, which he says Jesus will establish "on Earth as it is in heaven" (29:39).

### 3. Righteousness includes care for animals
Wescoe connects the "breastplate of righteousness" (Ephesians 6:14) to Proverbs 12:10: "the righteous care for the needs of the animals but the kindest acts of the wicked are cruel" (08:? through 23:32). He reads cruelty to animals as part of the devil's deception. He lists practices he calls cruel: slaughter, taking calves' milk, fur and leather, experimentation, captivity for entertainment (33:42). He says the steward parable makes us accountable for the animals, since God "put us in charge over the animals" (33:42).

### 4. Genesis 1 and the vegan reading of diet and dominion
He reads Genesis 1 as giving humans dominion as stewards, and Genesis 1:29 as giving plants for food, so "that's our diet" (32:09). In the chat section he says God "blessed the animals first before he even blessed the humans," so animals are not merely for human use (39:52). He makes an argument from nature about milk: "what other animal is drinking the breast milk of some other animal" (47:40). This is a rhetorical argument, not an exegetical one, and no ancient source is cited for it.

### 5. Evidence of an awakening
The "signs" are observational: the grocery milk aisle is now mostly plant-based milk; documentaries, podcasts and YouTube channels are growing; and vegan products and restaurants are more visible (35:14 to 46:06). He concludes that "God is planting a vegan in every church" and that each vegan in a congregation is "shining that light" to others (36:47). He presents this as a sign of hope rather than a measured trend.

### 6. Reading the parables for their original audience
In reply to a question about why Jesus' parables sound harsh, Wescoe argues that Jesus spoke "in a way that his audience will understand," meaning rural, largely illiterate farmers in Galilee (42:59). He presents this as an interpretive claim about Jesus' audience, without citing any source for the literacy rate or the setting.
