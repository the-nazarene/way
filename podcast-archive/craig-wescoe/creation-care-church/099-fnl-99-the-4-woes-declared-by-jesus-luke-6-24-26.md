## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks why Jesus pronounced four woes in the Sermon on the Plain (Luke 6:24-26), which pair with the four blessings in Luke 6:20-23, and how those woes relate to eating animal flesh. He notes that Matthew's Sermon on the Mount has the blessings without the woes, and that many people skip Luke's woes when they quote the Beatitudes.

His answer is that the woes are warnings to people who are "satisfied with the ways of the world" and who will lose what they have when the kingdom comes. He reads the rich, the full, those who laugh now and those well spoken of as people indulging in animal flesh, and the poor, hungry, mourning and reviled as people who reject that indulgence. His main evidence is a long chain of proof texts. The Daniel 1 account of the king's delicacies and the vegetable diet is read as a precedent (with Genesis 1:29 as the base diet). Craig argues that the Hebrew and Greek word for "flesh" in Proverbs 23:20 ("baser," "sarx") means flesh in general, so that the gluttony warned against is the eating of flesh itself. Isaiah 58:6 is read as a call to end animal exploitation, Ezekiel 16:49 as the sin of Sodom (pride, overfeeding, neglect of the needy), and Romans 14:17 and 14:15 as Paul's rejection of meat-eating for the sake of love. He ends by reading the warnings as a call to repentance rather than a prediction of punishment Jesus wants to inflict.

The talk closes with a chat Q&A (including a reply that Daniel 1 is "the first double-blind study recorded in history," which Craig affirmed in jest), a closing prayer, and a preview of next week's talk on praying for animals (Matthew 6:9).

### Key Teachings

### 1. The woes are warnings to those conformed to the world

Craig reads the four blessings and woes as a contrast between people who set their minds on the kingdom and people who set their minds on the things of the world. He says "the general idea is that those who are satisfied with the the ways of the world" will lose everything when the kingdom is established, and that the woes are not Jesus wishing harm but warning people who are on a path to destruction and should repent (turn around and be transformed). He ties this to Romans 12:2 and Philippians 3:19 (whose "God is their belly"), and reads "belly" as serving the desires of the flesh.

### 2. Daniel 1 as the model of rich food versus Genesis 1:29

Daniel and his companions refuse "the portion of the king's Delicacies" and the wine, and eat vegetables and water for ten days, after which they look healthier and are found "ten times better" in wisdom than the court magicians. Craig reads the king's delicacies as luxuries of the rich (the "woe to the rich" in Luke 6:24), and says the story shows that living by God's diet (Genesis 1:29, fruit and plants) brings better health and wisdom. He links the wealth theme to exotic animal dishes and aged wine, and adds Revelation 11:18 ("destroy those who destroy the Earth") to argue that animal agriculture is destructive and therefore under divine judgment.

### 3. "Gluttonous eaters of meat" and the meaning of "flesh"

Craig's most specific linguistic argument concerns Proverbs 23:20 ("do not mix with wine bibbers or with gluttonous Eaters of meat"). He says the common church reading, "don't eat too much meat," misses the word. He says the Hebrew word *baser* (and Greek *sarx*) "always means one thing and one thing only it means flesh," covering humans, animals and corpses alike, and cites Genesis (Noah's Ark) as an example. On this reading the warning is against eating flesh at all, not against overeating. In the chat he extends this: "for this verse in particular it's talking about don't eat animal flesh," and he says he does not think the verse concerns portion size. He grants that eating healthily and not overeating are good principles.

### 4. Isaiah 58 and the "fast" of ending animal cruelty

Isaiah 58:6 ("loose the bonds of wickedness," "undo the heavy burdens," "break every yoke") is read as using imagery of working animals (yokes, burdens, chains on camels and horses) and therefore as a command against animal exploitation. Craig argues that the fast God "has chosen" is abstention from animal cruelty and that the "Daniel Diet" or "Daniel Fast" (no animal products or alcohol) is a practical form of this. He adds that avoiding animal products means avoiding slaughter, the taking of milk, and confinement and labor of animals.

### 5. Sodom, pride and overfeeding

Craig reads Ezekiel 16:49 as naming Sodom's sins as pride, "fullness of food," idleness and failure to help the poor and needy. He treats overfeeding and "indulging" as the link to the "woe to those who are full" and says the destruction of Sodom is a warning, not a prediction Jesus wants applied to the audience. In the chat he agreed with a commenter that Sodom's sin was "greed and meat eating," and tied this to Ezekiel's list.

### 6. Romans 14 and Paul's "I will never eat meat again"

Craig argues that the kingdom "is not food and drink" (Romans 14:17) means it is not about indulging the flesh but about righteousness, peace and joy, which he links to caring for animals (quoting Proverbs 12:10 on the righteous caring for the needs of their animals). He reads Romans 14:15 ("if your brother is grieved because of your food you are no longer walking in love") as a warning that eating meat in the presence of animal-welfare-minded believers causes them distress. He says Paul "will never eat meat again" and urges imitation of Paul as Paul imitates Christ, adding that Christ "also didn't eat meat by the way." He acknowledges that many readers use Romans 14 for the opposite conclusion and says that is a mistake. (No verse is given for Paul's statement in the talk.)

### 7. Mourning, weeping and groaning creation

The "woe to those who laugh now" is read against Romans 8:19-23, in which creation groans and awaits deliverance. Craig argues that those who mourn "over the state of the world" and "our hearts break for the suffering of animals" are the blessed ones, and that laughter at the status quo belongs to Sodom's attitude. He connects this to the kingdom prayer ("on Earth as it is in heaven").

### 8. Contrite heart versus the Pharisees' hypocrisy

Psalm 34:18 and the "contrite spirit" are presented as the repentance the woes call for, which Craig says includes both empathy for animal suffering and conviction to change. Matthew 23:23 (tithing mint and cumin while neglecting justice and mercy) and Luke 11:42-43 are read as condemnation of the powerful who seek the "best seats," and Craig uses them to ask whether we are "treating them justly or are we paying to have their throat slit." Matthew 23:25-28 is used to press the point that outward appearance is not enough. Craig says that being "spoken well of" is the warning sign, and that being hated or excluded for telling people to change is the blessing (Luke 6:22-23).

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Luke's woes are skipped because they are less pleasant.** Craig says many people quote Matthew's Beatitudes ("it's prettier") and forget that Luke has woes, so they skip Luke's warnings.
- **"Gluttonous eaters of meat" means eating too much meat.** Craig says the Hebrew word for flesh means flesh in general, so the verse is against eating animal flesh, and that church readings that say "just don't overeat" distort it.
- **Romans 14 permits eating meat.** Craig says many people use Romans 14 for that conclusion, but its real point is that eating meat is not the kingdom and that causing distress to a brother by eating it is not walking in love.
- **1 Timothy 4 shows that veganism is demonic.** Craig says opponents use 1 Timothy 4 this way, and argues that the reading is wrong because Genesis 1:29 presents the plant diet as God's good design.
- **Sodom's sin was only inhospitality or sexual sin.** Craig reads Ezekiel 16:49 as naming pride, overfeeding and neglect of the poor, and treats meat-eating and indulgence as part of that picture.
- **Daniel's diet is only a Lenten practice.** Craig says he encourages churches to use Lent as an entry point and then to show from Scripture that it is God's will to continue.
