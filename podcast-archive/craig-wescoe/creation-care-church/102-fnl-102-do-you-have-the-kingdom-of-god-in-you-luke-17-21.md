## Summary

Craig Wescoe opens with Luke 17:21 ("the kingdom of God is within you") and asks what it means to have the kingdom of God in you and how a person can test whether they have it. He answers that the kingdom is God's rule as king, received through the indwelling Holy Spirit, and that the Spirit-led life shows itself in fruit, including gentleness and mercy toward animals. He ties this to a vision of no killing and no death, which he finds at the end of Isaiah 11 and Revelation 21 and at the start in Genesis 1. On this reading, a Spirit-filled person does not eat animals. His main evidence is a chain of proof-texts across the Hebrew Bible, the Gospels, Paul, Hebrews, 1 John, and James, plus his reading of Genesis 1:28-30 as a plant-based diet at creation.

He tests the claim with 1 John 2:4-6, 2 Corinthians 13:5, and James 2, and presents the self-examination as a call to repentance. The second half is a Q&A with live commenters. Craig answers questions on dominion, the kingdom and nationalism, future animal sacrifice, and violent passages such as the Amalekite texts. He also rejects the idea that Jesus' kingdom comes by force. The talk ends with an altar-call style prayer for viewers to "receive" the Spirit, a closing prayer, and a preview of next week's session on Hebrews 11:1.

Craig is a devotional teacher in a Christian vegan framework. This is a sermon-style presentation, not a historical study. He does not address the historical Jesus, the Jerusalem community, or the formation of the Gospels.

## Key Teachings

### The kingdom is God's rule, received inwardly through the Spirit
Craig reads 1 Samuel 8:4-9 as the people rejecting God as their king, and John 18:36-37 and Revelation 17:14 as showing Jesus as the king who is "the word of God." From this he concludes that to have the kingdom within is to have God as one's only king. He supports the inward reception with Ezekiel 36:27 ("I will put my spirit within you"), John 3:5-6 (born of water and spirit), and Galatians 2:20 (crucified with Christ, the flesh crucified). He says the Spirit "causes us to walk in the ways of the king."

### The fruit of the Spirit includes mercy toward animals
Using Galatians 5:16-17 and 5:22-23, Craig sets the flesh against the Spirit. His concrete example is diet: the flesh's "bloodlust" and wish "to eat their secretions," against a Spirit that "desires mercy" and "self-control." He says the fruit should be shown "to animals and to the whole creation," not only to people. He presents veganism as the practical result of this, and in the talk's opening, he says that going vegan was "the second best decision" of his life after giving his life to God.

### The end and the beginning: no killing, no death
Craig argues that God "declares the end from the beginning" (Isaiah 46:10). The end is shown in Isaiah 11:6-9, where predators and prey live together and "they shall not hurt nor destroy," and in Revelation 21:1-5, where "there shall be no more death." He then reads Genesis 1:29-30 as God giving humans and animals only plants for food, with the animals having "nephesh chaya" ("a living soul"). His conclusion is that because there is no death in the end, there should be none now. He ties this to Genesis 2:16-17 (death from eating the forbidden tree) as showing death was never God's plan.

### Dominion is stewardship in God's image, not license to use or kill
Craig reads Genesis 1:26-28 and Genesis 2:15 as making humans stewards ("to tend and keep" the garden) and image-bearers who should rule as God would. He cites Luke 12:42-43 (the faithful steward) as the picture of the servant the master wants to find on his return. He says that people who claim dominion as a reason to eat animals are reading only the verse and not the next one (Genesis 1:29). In the Q&A, he says that people keep finding "the next excuse" when one is refuted, and calls this a refusal of God's will.

### Testing yourself: love, fruit, and works
Craig offers three tests. First, 1 John 2:4-6: keeping his word, so that "the love of God is perfected in him," and walking as Jesus walked. Second, 2 Corinthians 13:5 ("Examine yourselves... Jesus Christ is in you?"), with the good tree and bad fruit image from Matthew 7 (he paraphrases it without a citation). Third, Ephesians 2:8-10 and James 2:17-18, 26: faith is the starting point of grace, and works show that faith is alive. He tells viewers not to excuse "bad fruit" as normal, but to take it as a call to repent.

### The kingdom spreads by conversion, not violence
Using Matthew 11:12 ("the kingdom of heaven suffers violence") and Revelation 11:18, Craig argues that the kingdom must not be taken by force. In the Q&A, he rejects a link between the kingdom and any nation, citing Abraham as a witness to the nations. He says the kingdom spreads through "preaching, this multiplying, this converting," and that the new covenant is written "on their hearts" (he cites Jeremiah 31, hedging on the chapter).

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Dominion means the right to eat, use, and abuse animals.** Craig says this reads Genesis 1:28 without the next verse. He argues that dominion is stewardship "according to his will," and that the Genesis text gives plants as food.
- **God's kingdom or future world will include animal sacrifice or killing.** Craig says some people believe this, and he rejects it, citing Isaiah 11, Revelation 21, and Hebrews.
- **Texts such as the Amalekite genocide or Jephthah's vow justify violence.** He says these convoluted readings distort the big picture of God's character, which he identifies with love and peace.
- **The kingdom of God is a nation or political power.** In answer to a question, he says the kingdom is spread by preaching and conversion, not by nations or military conquest.
- **Faith alone is enough without works.** He cites James 2 and Ephesians 2 to say saving faith produces good works, and that dead faith is not saving faith.
- **Bad fruit is normal and God accepts it.** He says this is self-deception and that it should lead to repentance.
