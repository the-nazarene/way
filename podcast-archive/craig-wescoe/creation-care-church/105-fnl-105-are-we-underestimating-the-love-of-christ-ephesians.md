## Summary

Wescoe asks whether professing Christians are underestimating the love of Christ when they say God's love is for humans only and not for animals. He opens with Ephesians 3:17-19 and argues that this love is "immeasurable." He supports this with Romans 5:8 (God's love while "we were still sinners") and Romans 8:37-39 (nothing can separate us from that love), and takes from these that God's love is not conditional on merit.

His main case is a sequence of scriptural passages he reads in order. From Genesis 1 he argues that God creates and blesses the animals before humans, and that the "dominion" given to humans in Genesis 1:28 is not a license to kill, since Genesis 1:29-30 assigns plants as food for both people and animals. He then reads Genesis 9:16 and Hosea 2:18 as covenants that include animals, Jonah 4:11 and Exodus 17:3 / Numbers 20:8 as signs of God's concern for animals, Exodus 20:8-11 as including cattle in the Sabbath rest, and Joel 2:28 and Revelation 5:13 as including animals in the outpouring of the Spirit and in creaturely praise. He reads the Hebrew phrase rendered "all flesh" as covering humans and animals alike, and treats that reading as part of his argument rather than as a point he defends at length. He presents the outcome as a call for Christians to adopt a vegan diet and lifestyle.

The talk ends with a long live Q&A. Wescoe answers questions on naming animals and emotional bonds, on the treatment of dogs and cats in China and the consistency of that outrage with eating other animals, on children raised for 4-H-style livestock sales, and on Isaiah 11:6 and Matthew 18:3 ("become like a child"). He closes by announcing next week's talk on anthropocentrism and theocentrism in reading scripture.

### Key Teachings

### 1. God's love is unconditional and cosmic in scope
Wescoe reads Ephesians 3:17-19 and Romans 5:8 to argue that God's love is not earned and "passes all understanding." From Romans 8:37-39 he takes the point that nothing "created" can separate believers from it. He then says that to limit this love to humans underestimates it.

### 2. Animals are created first and blessed, and their habitat is part of the creation purpose
Using Genesis 1:20-23 and 1:27-28, Wescoe notes that God blesses the sea creatures and birds with "be fruitful and multiply" and that God creates the animals before humans. From Isaiah 45:18 ("who formed it to be inhabited") he argues that the earth was made to be inhabited by both humans and animals. He reads the one difference in Genesis 1:28, the human "dominion," as a call to stewardship and not a warrant for killing.

### 3. Dominion is not a license to kill; the original diet is vegan
Wescoe reads Genesis 1:29-31, in which God gives "every herb that yields seed" and "every green herb" as food, and argues this shows the intended diet is plant-based. He says the diet is "not just the diet it's the whole lifestyle," reasoning that if no one kills and eats another creature, violence has no basis. He presents this as the text's clarification of what "dominion" means.

### 4. Covenants include animals
From Genesis 9:16 Wescoe takes the phrase "every living creature of All Flesh" to show that the "Everlasting Covenant" is with animals as well as humans, and says the chapter mentions animals "five times." He argues that God does not make covenants with things he does not love. From Hosea 2:18 he reads a promise that the "bow and sword of battle" will be shattered so that the animals can "lie down safely," and presents this as God's restored design for a peaceable creation.

### 5. God's concern for animals in narratives and law
Wescoe cites Jonah 4:11 ("and also many animals") as God explicitly pitying the animals of Nineveh; Exodus 17:3 and Numbers 20:8, where the people and "their animals" are given water; and Exodus 20:8-11, where "your cattle" are included in the Sabbath rest and the Sabbath is called a sign of the covenant. He presents these as evidence that God names animals in his compassion and law.

### 6. The Spirit on "All Flesh," and animal praise
Wescoe reads Joel 2:28 ("I will pour out my spirit on All Flesh") and argues that the Hebrew phrase covers animals as well as humans, a reading he supports by pointing to the parallel categories in Genesis 1 and to Revelation 5:13, where "every creature" in heaven, on earth, under the earth, and in the sea praises God and the Lamb. He adds Psalm 148:7-10 and Psalm 150:6 ("let everything that has breath praise the Lord") and says animals have a capacity to praise God and cry out to him, including in slaughter.

### 7. Friends, not food, and the Kingdom
In the Q&A Wescoe presents Jesus' statement "I no longer call you servants I call you friends" (no reference given) as a model for how people should regard animals: as friends, not servants or food. He reads Isaiah 11:6-9 ("a little child shall lead them") as the image behind Jesus' words in Matthew 18:3 about entering the kingdom of God, and says the child-like leading of animals "in the way of peace" is the dominion God intends.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **God's love is only for humans.** Wescoe's correction: God's love is immeasurable and extends to his whole creation, based on Genesis, Hosea, Jonah, Joel, and Revelation.
- **"Dominion" means the right to kill and eat animals.** Wescoe's correction: Genesis 1:29-30 limits food to plants, so dominion is stewardship and "a license to kill" is the thing it excludes.
- **Animals are an afterthought in creation.** Wescoe's correction: God creates and blesses the animals before humans (Genesis 1:20-28).
- **Animals are servants or food, so we need not feel bad about killing them.** Wescoe's correction (in the Q&A): animals are to be treated as friends, and the emotional bond built with a named animal is not what makes God's view of them different, since God cares for every animal, named or not.
- **Outrage at dog and cat killing is inconsistent with eating farmed animals.** Wescoe argues the moral principle applies to all animals, and the outrage should extend to farmed animals too (he does not treat the two as unrelated). He presents this as a point of consistency rather than a rebuke of those who are outraged.
