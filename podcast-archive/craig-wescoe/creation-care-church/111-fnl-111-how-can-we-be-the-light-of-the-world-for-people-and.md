## Summary

Craig Wescoe, host of Creation Care Church, asks how a believer can be "the light of the world" for both people and animals, working from Matthew 5:14-16. He presents the talk as a Bible study for a Friday livestream, and he gives no reading of Jesus that departs from his own faith tradition. He begins with the Festival of Lights (Hanukkah), citing 1 and 2 Maccabees and Nehemiah for its origin, and then reads John 10:22-23 and John 1:1-14 to argue that Jesus is the divine Word and light that creates and enlightens all people. He ties this to Matthew 5:14-16: if Jesus is the light, and believers are reborn "of the spirit," then the light shining through believers is Christ's own, and the "good works" it produces are the fruit of God's Spirit.

Wescoe then builds a case through a long chain of proof-texts (Psalms 27, 36, and 119, Proverbs 6:23, 1 John 1, Ephesians 5, 2 Corinthians 4:6, James 1:17, Luke 11:34, Isaiah 42 and 58). His main claim is that God's instructions, "the Torah," are the lamp that guides walking in light, and that the Isaiah 58 images of burdens, yokes and chains describe the literal exploitation of animals as well as the oppression of people. He reads the Great Commission ("preach the gospel to every creature") as a call to show love, through care and advocacy, to animals as well as humans, and he closes with the Kingdom "on earth as in heaven" as the goal of this shining.

The talk ends with a live Q&A. Wescoe offers practical advice: answer critics on their own terms, show that caring for animals does not mean caring less for people, use visits to sanctuaries and witness at slaughter-truck events to help others see animals as individuals, and pray for people while patiently "chipping away." The talk is advocacy within a Christian frame, not a historical argument, and Wescoe's claims about the text are presented as his own interpretation.

## Key Teachings

### Jesus as the divine Word and light (John 1; John 8)
Wescoe reads John 1:1-14 as identifying Jesus with the Word that was God, through whom "all things were made," and whose light "shines in the darkness." He then links this to John 8 ("I am the light of the world") and argues that those who receive this light become "children of God," which he equates with rebirth through "water and the spirit" (citing John 3:3-5). He explicitly says the connection between Jesus and "the Lord" of Psalm 27:1 is made in John 1.

### Matthew 5:14-16: the believer's light is Christ's light
His central argument is that Jesus says "I am the light" and then, in Matthew 5:14-16, tells his followers to shine. Wescoe reasons that because the light is Christ's own, the good works seen by the world are the result of God's Spirit working through believers, and those works lead people to glorify God. He identifies the fruit of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22-23, cited only briefly) as the evidence of this light, and he cites Matthew 7:16-20 ("by their fruit you will know them") as a test.

### Walking in the light means keeping God's instructions (Torah)
Wescoe reads Psalm 119:105 ("your word is a lamp to my feet") and Proverbs 6:23 ("the commandment is a lamp and the law is light") to argue that God's Torah is the light that guides the walk. In his reading, "walking in the light" equals following Jesus's commandments (John 14:15), loving neighbors "both human and animal," and rejecting violence and cruelty. He describes this as the "ways of Peace."

### Isaiah 58 and Psalm 36 as literal concern for animals
This is his distinctive interpretive move. Wescoe reads Isaiah 58:6 ("loose the bonds of wickedness... undo the heavy burdens... break every yoke") as describing animal exploitation (burdened pack animals, chained or caged animals, plowing yokes) and insists that this is "not just a metaphor" but also literal. He also reads Psalm 36:6 ("you preserve man and Beast") as God's will to preserve animal life, contrasting it with the thief of John 10:10. He argues that his listeners must not "turn a blind eye" to the oppression described in Isaiah.

### Preaching the gospel to every creature, through love
From Mark 16:15 he derives an obligation to preach to animals. He says this is done "through the language of love," not by verbal evangelism to animals. Caring for animals' needs, keeping peace with them, and "speaking out" for them are, in his terms, the practical form of preaching. He appeals to the Sabbath saying about helping an animal in a ditch (no verse given) to argue that rescuing animals from slaughterhouses and factory farms is "lawful to do good."

### The Kingdom on earth and the rejection of violence
Wescoe connects the light to the Kingdom of God and the instruction "your kingdom come, your will be done on Earth as it is in heaven." He pictures the goal as swords beaten into plowshares, animals "lying down in safety," and people "growing food to feed to our neighbor" rather than weapons. He frames this ideal as the outcome of walking in the light, and he rejects war, weapons, and eating or exploiting animals as incompatible with it.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Isaiah 58's yoke and burden language is only a metaphor for human oppression.** Wescoe argues it also literally describes the exploitation of animals (pack animals, plowing yokes, caged and chained animals).
- **Caring about animals means caring less about people.** In the Q&A he answers that people can care about several things at once, and that his advocacy for humans and animals comes from the same faith.
- **Veganism is a New Age or secular, anti-human idea.** He tells the questioner to answer such labels directly, saying he worships "the creator of animals," not the animals themselves, and does not practice crystal-based spirituality.
- **"Preach the gospel to every creature" means evangelizing humans only.** He reads it as a call to show animals love and care, which he calls "the language of love."
- **Animals have no spiritual life, so the gospel is wasted on them.** He argues that animals have a "light that shines within their spirit," citing Balaam's donkey as an example.

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Wescoe draws most of his "light" teaching from John and the Psalms, but he relies heavily on Paul (Ephesians 5:8, 2 Corinthians 4:6) as authority for the children-of-light language. He does not discuss any difference between Jesus and Paul.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet and nonviolence:** The talk's Torah reading (Psalm 119:105, Proverbs 6:23) is central, and diet and nonviolence are explicit goals. Eating animals is treated as incompatible with walking in the light. Sacrifice is not discussed.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** He reads "your kingdom come, your will be done on Earth as it is in heaven" as the goal of the light, with peace and no weapons or animal exploitation.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Wescoe reads John 1 and John 8 as presenting Jesus as the divine Word and light, equal to God and the agent of creation. He treats this as the reading of the New Testament, not a historical claim.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** He notes that Catholic and Orthodox readers count 1 and 2 Maccabees as Scripture while many Protestants do not, but he does not analyze the formation of the canon.
