## Summary

Craig asks who Jesus is according to the Gospel of John, focusing on the "I am" sayings, and opens with John 8:58 ("before Abraham was I am"). He argues the phrase echoes God's self-declaration to Moses at the burning bush (Exodus 3:14). From there he reads a series of Johannine "I am" statements (bread of life, good shepherd, king, way/truth/life, light, resurrection) against Hebrew Scripture, presenting Jesus as the God of Israel, the word of God through whom creation was made, and a non-violent king whose kingdom is "on earth as it is in heaven."

His method is almost entirely intra-scriptural. He pairs each Johannine saying with an Old Testament or other biblical passage (Deuteronomy 8:3, Psalm-era kingship in 1 Samuel 8, Genesis 1, Proverbs 3) and draws moral conclusions. Those conclusions are mostly ethical: refusing violence, not eating animals, and caring for creation. He says the talk covers only about half of the "I am" sayings because it is a 30-minute overview.

The talk ends with an altar call, a prayer of conversion that Craig invites listeners to say, and a Q&A with chat participants. Topics include animals in the Kingdom, whether God changes his mind (Nineveh), and whether "dominion" permits killing animals. It closes with a preview of the next talk on Matthew 10:27.

### Key Teachings

### 1. "I am" as a claim to divine identity
Craig argues that John 8:58 is not "before Abraham was I was" but "before Abraham was I am," which he reads as a deliberate echo of God's name in Exodus 3:14. He presents this as the interpretive key for the other "I am" sayings.

### 2. Bread of life and the word of God
He links John 6:35 ("I am the bread of life") to Deuteronomy 8:3 and Matthew 4:4, where the person does not live by bread alone but by every word from God. His reading is that Jesus is "the word of God in the flesh," so living by God's word means living by Jesus.

### 3. Good Shepherd: protection versus the thief
Using John 10:10-11, Craig contrasts the thief who "steals, kills, and destroys" with the Good Shepherd who lays down his life. He applies this to human violence (bombing) and to animal slaughter, saying the Shepherd's followers should not be the destroyers of creation.

### 4. A non-violent king, illustrated by 1 Samuel 8 and Numbers 11
In John 18:36-37 Jesus says "my kingdom is not of this world," and Craig says the popular expectation of a military Messiah was wrong. He reads 1 Samuel 8:5-7 (Israel asks for a human king, rejecting God) and Numbers 11 (the craving for meat) as parallels: people who reject God's rule for a human ruler's kind of kingdom. Craig's conclusion is that Jesus is a king of a different sort, whose kingdom is established without violence.

### 5. The way, the truth, and the Garden of Eden
John 14:6 ("no one comes to the Father except through me") is read as an exclusive claim, not an "all paths lead to God" statement. He ties this to John 8:44, where the devil is called a liar and murderer "from the beginning," and interprets this through the Garden of Eden story (Genesis 2-3) and Proverbs 3:17-18 (wisdom as a tree of life).

### 6. Logos and Genesis 1
Craig reads John 1:1-5 as the "hymn of the logos" and compares it to Genesis 1:1-5, where God creates by speaking. For him, John's "all things were made through him" identifies Jesus as the creative Word of God, which he calls "the word of God incarnate."

### 7. Resurrection to life or to judgment
Using John 5:28-29, Craig says those who "have done good" rise to life and those who "have done evil" rise to condemnation. He links good works to the fruit of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22-23) and treats the Lake of Fire / second death from Revelation as the fate of those without the Spirit. He says that Jesus "is the resurrection," but he does not locate the saying (he suggests it is in a nearby passage, unchecked on screen).

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus as a military king who will defeat Israel's enemies.** Craig says this was the expected Messiah, that Jesus rejected it, and that his kingdom is not established by force.
- **"All paths lead to God."** Craig says John 14:6 says the only way to the Father is through Jesus.
- **"Dominion" means humans may kill and eat animals.** Craig argues Genesis 1 gives fruit and plants as food and that Christlike dominion lays down life rather than takes it.
- **Enemies are people.** Craig says people are deceived and "ensnared by sin," and the enemy is the spiritual adversary.
- **God changes his mind (Nineveh).** Craig says God's character and plan of salvation do not change; people's response changes how he deals with them.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Who Jesus thought he was:** The talk's central question. Craig reads the "I am" sayings as claims to the divine name and identity, interpreted through Exodus 3:14 and John 1.
- **Torah:** Craig draws heavily on Deuteronomy 8:3, Genesis 1-3, Exodus 3:14, Numbers 11, and 1 Samuel 8 as the interpretive frame for John, not as law to be observed. He does not discuss commandments such as the Sabbath as a binding practice beyond a brief mention in the animal section.
- **Diet and nonviolence:** A large share of the talk. Craig reads Genesis 1:29 as an original vegetarian diet and John 10 and 18 as a rejection of violence, extending both to animals.
- **Kingdom on earth:** Craig says Jesus is "establishing a kingdom on Earth as it is in heaven" with no killing, and that the animals will be in it (Isaiah 11).
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** John 5:28-29 is read as two resurrections: to life for those with the Spirit, to judgment for those without. Craig ties this to Revelation's second death and the Lake of Fire, and to John 14:2 as a "prepared place."
- **Paul:** Absent from the argument. The only Pauline reference, Galatians 5:22-23, appears as scripture on the fruit of the Spirit.
- **Gospels and canon:** Craig treats John as a unified text and does not discuss its composition, sources, or relation to the other Gospels. He says he is reading "what the Gospel of John is trying to tell us."
