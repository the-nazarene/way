## Summary

The host asks whether cuddling was part of God's "very good" design in Eden, whether Adam and Eve cuddled with animals, and whether there will be cuddling with animals in the coming kingdom. He answers yes to the first two and treats the third as following from the first. His case rests on Genesis, with Ecclesiastes 4:11, 1 John, Isaiah 11 and Habakkuk 2 as supporting texts. He reasons that Genesis 2:25 presents the first humans as naked and unashamed, that Genesis 3:8 mentions a "cool part of the day," and that the only remedy for cold without clothing would be to huddle with animals, as animals do. He reads the Hebrew word *azer* ("helper") as meaning a necessary helper, and says God named the animals to create close relationships with them. He argues that the fall brought fear and violence between humans and animals (Genesis 9:2, Habakkuk 2:17), and that the restoration in Isaiah 11:6 will bring back peaceable, cuddling relations and a vegan diet (Genesis 1:29-30, Isaiah 65:25).

The talk is mostly the host reading and commenting on passages. Its evidence is largely inference from silence in the text ("it doesn't say" he was ashamed or that God killed an animal), plus a claim from unnamed studies about infant touch. He presents these as his reading, not as settled. The second half is a live Q&A in which he answers chat questions about naked worship, animal skins, bunnies, and cats. He closes with prayer, a plug for a Zoom fellowship and a volunteer page, and a preview of next week's talk on Isaiah 58:6 ("undo the heavy burdens").

## Key Teachings

### Nakedness, the cool part of the day, and why cuddling would be needed
The host reads Genesis 2:25 ("they were both naked... and were not ashamed") as showing that nakedness was part of God's design. He says "it doesn't say and God saw that they were naked and said it's not good for man to be naked" (Genesis 1 uses "very good" for each day). From Genesis 3:8 ("the cool of the day") and Genesis 1:5 (evening and morning as part of the good day), he infers that a cool part of the day existed before the fall. His reasoning: if the first humans had no clothing and it got cool each evening, the warmth available would come from close contact with animals. He calls this "one way" to stay warm, not a documented fact.

### *Azer* as "necessary helper," not sidekick
The host says the Hebrew *azer* in Genesis 2:18 and 2:20 is misread as "assistant," and cites Psalm 33:20 ("he is our help and our Shield") to argue that it means a helper without whom one fails. He applies this to Eve (Adam cannot fulfill "be fruitful and multiply" alone) and then to animals: animals are necessary helpers to humans, which he links to mutual warmth. He notes Genesis 2:18 ("it is not good that man should be alone") names the one thing God calls "not good," and says the text does not call nakedness "not good."

### Naming the animals as building kinship
He argues that God had already done the classifying (God "created the cattle, created the fish"), so Adam's naming of animals in Genesis 2:20 must mean giving them individual names and forming bonds with them. He illustrates with a cattle rancher who tells children not to name the animals, because naming creates an emotional bond that makes slaughter devastating. He draws a contrast between animals treated as "a number" and animals treated as family. He does not discuss Hebrew scholarship on Genesis 2:20 beyond his own reading.

### Scientific and scriptural case for touch and community
He cites Ecclesiastes 4:11 ("if two lie down together they will keep warm") and observes squirrels, chicks, and Jesus's "mother hen" image (which he attributes to the Psalms and Jesus without giving a verse) as examples of animals keeping warm. He then says "scientifically speaking" that human infant contact is "absolutely necessary," recalling an orphanage study he describes only from memory ("I think there was a study done not on purpose") in which babies without holding died at higher rates. The study is not identified, and the host does not give figures or a source. He links this to 1 John 4:8, 4:16 and Galatians 5:22 (love as God's primary attribute) to argue that relationships of mutual benefit, not exploitation, are God's design.

### The fall as rupture: fear, violence, and parasitic relationship
The host says the change comes in Genesis 9:2 ("the fear of you and the dread of you will be on every beast"), which he reads as the start of animals fleeing humans. He links this to Habakkuk 2:17 ("the violence done to Lebanon... the plunder of beasts which made them afraid"), arguing that human violence toward animals produces their fear. He reads Genesis 3:7 (fig leaves from fear) against 1 John 4:18 ("perfect love casts out fear"). Human clothing from animal skins (Genesis 3:21) is, for him, a sign of the broken relationship. He says the text does not say God killed an animal for the skins, and that the skins could be "spoken into existence" or even shed snakeskin. He calls the human-animal relationship after the fall "parasitic" and "one-sided," and reads Genesis 3:16-19 as warnings about the consequences of disobedience.

### Diet as part of the design, and restoration
He reads Genesis 1:29 as the plant diet God gave humans, and Genesis 1:30 as giving every green herb to beasts and birds, which he takes to mean all animals were vegan at creation. He says Isaiah 11:6-9 and Isaiah 65:25 ("the lion will eat straw like the ox") show the same diet returning in the restored kingdom. He adds a claim that fruit seeds are protected through digestion and that pruning herbs keeps them growing, so that plant-based eating is "symbiotic" and not parasitic. These are his observations and are not sourced.

### The restored kingdom as one family
He reads Isaiah 11:6 ("the wolf also shall dwell with the lamb... the leopard shall lie down with the young goat") as the restoration of God's design: predators and prey living at peace and "lying down" together, which he equates with sharing kinship. He ties this to Matthew 18:3 (becoming like a child) and to the Lord's prayer phrase "your kingdom come on Earth as it is in heaven." He presents the kingdom as a community in which humans act as caretakers, not exploiters, of animals.
