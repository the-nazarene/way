## Key Teachings

### Hebrew terms for violence: *paritz* and *hamas*

The host says *paritz* (Strong's 6530) is usually translated "robber," "destroyer," "ravenous," or "vicious," but almost always means someone who sheds blood or tears apart. The host warns that translators may obscure meaning, so one should compare many verses. *Hamas* ("violence, cruelty, wrong") is treated as the same concept, and is linked to God's righteousness, since violence is the opposite of it. Examples cited include Genesis 6:11, Judges 9:24, and Ezekiel 7:23.

### The temple as "a den of violence" (Jeremiah 7:11, Matthew 21:12-13)

The host reads Jeremiah 7:11 as God's charge that the house named for him had become a "den of violence," meaning shedding blood and tearing apart. Matthew 21:12-13 (which the host notes appears in all four Gospels) is cited as Jesus quoting this verse alongside Isaiah 56:7 ("a House of Prayer"). The host argues the temple priests, who were meant to lead people in God's ways, were instead leading them into violence, and that Jesus's action was a direct protest against animal slaughter as merchandise. The host extends "house of God" to the community of believers (ekklesia), so the warning applies to churches too.

### Jesus's nonviolent confrontation

The host argues Jesus was "actively disrupting the temple" but did not take up the sword, because doing so would make him "the violent one." Jesus's refusal of violence is presented as consistent with Isaiah 53:9 ("he had done no violence nor was any deceit in his mouth"). The host says Jesus accepted the cost of this stand, including the crucifixion, which the host calls a stand "for all of creation, both humans and animals." John 10:10-11 (the thief who "comes to steal and to kill and destroy," versus the Good Shepherd who lays down his life) is used as the contrast.

### Animal sacrifice, slaughter for food, and mercy

Animal sacrifice is described as something the first-century followers of Jesus helped end, and the host argues the reason was that it was "contrary to his will." From this, the host argues that slaughter for food is also contrary to God's will. Amos 3:10 is read as "I desire Mercy not sacrifice" (no verse given), and Proverbs 12:10 ("a righteous man regards the life of his animal") is used to say that even kind acts by the wicked are "cruel." The host says the rejection of animal sacrifice is one thing Christians often accept, but they then "have the other thing backwards."

### Normalized violence and the peaceable kingdom

Isaiah 11:6-9 and Isaiah 35:9 are read as a picture of the Kingdom with no violent beast, led by a child in peace. Isaiah 60:18 ("violence shall no longer be heard in your land") is read as a call for communities and individuals to stop violence. The host argues that violence is normalized by systems out of sight (animal agriculture, drive-thrus, distant wars) and that believers should "stand apart" from these normal practices.

### Noah, Enoch, and the origin of flesh-eating

Genesis 6 is read as the reason for the flood: a world "filled with violence," where people ate flesh and drank blood. The host adds that the Book of Enoch describes the Nephilim as the first to eat flesh and drink blood. The host says this is a fuller account of the violence in Genesis 6, and points to a past live talk on Enoch (see Books & Resources). The host does not quote Enoch directly in this talk.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That *paritz* means "robber" or "thief."** The host says it means one who sheds blood or tears apart, and that translations can obscure this.
- **That Christians can reject animal sacrifice and still eat meat.** The host argues the reason sacrifice ended (it was contrary to God's will) also rules out slaughter for food.
- **That Jesus's temple action was itself violent.** The host argues he disrupted the temple nonviolently and refused the sword.
- **That the people in Amos 3:10 simply "don't know what is right."** In a chat reply, the host clarifies that Amos is describing people who ignore what they know, shaped by what they were taught.
