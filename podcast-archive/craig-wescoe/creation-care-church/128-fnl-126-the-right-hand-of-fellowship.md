## Summary

Wescoe asks what the "right hand of fellowship" in Galatians 2:9 meant, and why James, Peter and John would extend it to Paul, whom he describes (drawing on Galatians 1:13 and the book of Acts) as a former persecutor of the church. His answer is that the gesture is an act of returning evil with good, which he ties to Proverbs 25:21-22, Romans 12:18-21 and Jesus' command to love enemies in Matthew 5:44-45. He then argues that "right hand" language across Scripture (Psalm 110:1, Matthew 26:64, Ephesians 1:20-23) signals Christ's enthronement and the church as his people, and that the Matthew 25 sheep-and-goats judgment separates those who give practical charity to "the least of these" from those who do not.

Wescoe's main extension is to animals. Working from the Noah covenant in Genesis 9:16 and the Abraham promise in Genesis 15:6 and Galatians 3:29, he argues that God's covenant of fellowship includes all living creatures, so humans who confine, kill or exploit animals are "standing in the way" of that covenant. He draws on James 3, Matthew 7 and James 4 to frame diet and animal treatment as a test of whether one's words and works are truly blessing God's creation, and he presents a plant-based diet as the good will of God (Romans 12:2). The talk ends with a live Q&A (chat questions on Bible translations, animal organ transplants, the meaning of "bless," and related matters), a closing prayer, and an announcement that next week's talk will be on the resurrection, from 1 Corinthians 15:20.

Note that the talk is one speaker's devotional reading. Its arguments rest on his selection and paraphrase of verses, and several of its claims (for example, the reading of Galatians 2:9 as Paul's repentance being recognized) are his interpretation, not settled fact.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The right hand of fellowship as returning evil with good
Wescoe reads Galatians 2:9 as the Jerusalem leaders accepting Paul after his persecution, which he attributes to "the grace of God" received "through faith" and to Paul's repentance. He reads Proverbs 25:21-22 ("if your enemy is hungry give him bread") as the source of the idea of "heaping coals of fire," and links it to Romans 12:18-21 and Matthew 5:44-45. His stated goal is that God's kingdom be "inside us and all around us," and that extending fellowship to an adversary is one way to bring it about. He says the gesture does not mean enemies may keep persecuting while being called friends.

### 2. The right hand as Christ's enthronement and the church
He reads Psalm 110:1 ("sit at my right hand till I make your enemies your footstool") as Christ's dominion, with enemies under his feet meaning they follow rather than harm him. He cites Jesus' words at Matthew 26:64 as an assertion of messianic authority, and Ephesians 1:20-23 as Paul's teaching that Christ "gave all things" to the church, "which is his body." For Wescoe, the church is "the people," not a building, and its members are Christ's representatives who receive the repentant on his behalf.

### 3. The sheep and goats: charity to "the least of these"
Using Matthew 25:31-46, he argues the right hand (sheep) is those who feed, clothe, visit and shelter those in need, while the left (goats) is those who do not, and that the passage contrasts "everlasting life" with "everlasting punishment." He says the imagery is an analogy about shepherding, not literal sheep. He then connects this to John 10:10-11 and 27, where the thief "comes only to steal and to kill," the Good Shepherd gives his life, and his sheep "hear my voice and I know them and they follow me." He reads the thief as "probably a violent one," referring to a previous talk not included in this transcript.

### 4. The covenant of fellowship extends to all creatures
He traces a sequence of covenant invitations: Noah (Genesis 9:16, a covenant "between God... and every living creature of All Flesh"), Abraham (Genesis 15:6, "friend of God," with the phrase also tied to Isaiah 41:8 and Chronicles), and then Galatians 3:29 ("if you are Christ's then you are Abraham's seed"). He concludes that the covenant is "not just with humans" but "with all creatures," and that animals are therefore invited into fellowship with God. He cites Revelation 5:13, where every creature in heaven, earth, under the earth and in the sea praises God, as a picture of this.

### 5. Blessing, cursing, fruit and diet
Drawing on James 3:10-13 (fresh and salt water from one spring), Matthew 7:12-24 (good trees bear good fruit), and James 4:1-4 (wars and lusts, "asking amiss"), Wescoe argues that how one treats animals is part of whether one's praise of God is real. He reads Genesis 1 (God blessed the animals before the humans) and says God's diet for humans is "the fruit of the tree and the plants of the ground" (he gives no verse for this). He asserts that eating animal products "that come from animal cruelty" is asking amiss, and that Romans 12:2 calls believers away from "conform[ing] to this world." He also applies Proverbs 12:10 ("the righteous care for the needs of their animals") to zoos, aquariums, hunting and factory farming.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That animals may be used for human benefit, such as organ transplants, because humans are more valuable.** Wescoe rejects this, saying it rests on seeing animals as commodities. He argues that the search for solutions should look "in other places" that do not involve animal cruelty.
- **That accepting Jesus as Lord is enough, whatever one's treatment of animals.** He says calling Jesus "Lord, Lord" is not enough, and that one must take on Jesus' Spirit and bear its fruit (Matthew 7:21-23 in his reading).
- **That humans were created before animals (a commenter's claim, which Wescoe affirms).** Sharon's comment that the Genesis order placed the heavens, seas, earth and animals before Adam and Eve is accepted by Wescoe as correct.
