## Summary

This is a live Friday Night Live session from Creation Care Church, recorded before Resurrection Sunday. Host Craig Wescoe carries nearly all of it. He asks why the resurrection of Jesus matters, using the title verse, 1 Corinthians 15:20 ("Christ is risen from the dead and has become the first fruits"), as the thread.

He walks through John 19 and 20 in sequence: the crucifixion, the death, the burial, the empty tomb and Mary Magdalene's encounter with the risen Jesus. He calls the resurrection "the climax of The Story" and argues it does two things. It shows that death can be overcome by God's power, and it gives believers hope that death is not the end for them either. He then argues that Paul's statement in 1 Corinthians 15:3-4 that Christ rose "according to the scriptures" points to Daniel 12:2, Isaiah 26:19, Isaiah 25:9 and Isaiah 52, and to Psalm 16 when a chat viewer asks for a more direct Old Testament prophecy. Wescoe reads the "first fruits" image against the spring harvest calendar, with Jesus raised first and believers in the later harvest. He links the "word" that spoke creation (Genesis 1, John 1) with the voice that will raise the dead (1 Thessalonians 4:16), and he rejects reincarnation as a rival view.

The second half is a question-and-answer session with viewers. Wescoe answers questions on the thief's promise in Luke 23:43, whether "Easter" has pagan origins (presented as a matter he considers questionable, and "Up For Debate"), animal inclusion in the resurrection hope, and how to join the discipleship school. He closes by announcing that next week's talk will address the forbidden fruit of Genesis 2:17. The talk ends with a closing prayer. Wescoe presents his positions as devotional teaching and says the forbidden-fruit question is his personal research, not settled doctrine.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The passion sequence in John 19-20 as the climax of the story
Wescoe reads John 19:16-19, 19:28-30, 19:39-42 and John 20:1-8 and 20:11-18 in order, summarizing each stage as crucifixion, death, burial, empty tomb and resurrection. He points out that the beloved disciple, whom he identifies as "likely John," believes on seeing the empty tomb. Mary Magdalene's encounter ("Jesus said to her Mary") is read as the first appearance. He compares her weeping to the Lazarus scene in John 11, a parallel he presents as close in mood.

### 2. The resurrection proves power over death and gives hope
Wescoe says the resurrection "proves a couple of things": that "the power of death can be overcome by the power of God," and that believers have a hope beyond their own death. He ties this to John 11:25 ("I am the resurrection and the life") and Acts 3:15 ("the Prince of Life whom God raised from the dead"). He adds that some translations render this "author of Life" and that the phrase echoes John 1 and Genesis 1, where God creates by speaking.

### 3. "According to the scriptures": the Old Testament background
Wescoe reads 1 Corinthians 15:3-4 as Paul's summary of the gospel he received, and says both the death and resurrection were "according to the scriptures." For the death, he points to Isaiah 52 (spoken as "Isaiah 52," with the details of the garments and the piercing of the hands). For the resurrection, he cites:
- Daniel 12:2, where "many of those who sleep in the dust of the Earth shall awake," with the caveat that he is using a text tradition he names as "sepagan or the mastic text" (in context, the Septuagint or the Masoretic text, a fix to the caption garble)
- Isaiah 26:19, "your dead shall live"
- Isaiah 25:9, which he reads as the salvation the awakened rejoice in

He says sleep is "a euphemism" for death, used across the scriptures.

### 4. Firstfruits and the harvest
Wescoe connects 1 Corinthians 15:20 to the Feast of Firstfruits, which he says falls on the day Jesus rose, and to the week of unleavened bread that is beginning that week. Jesus is "the first to be raised to eternal life" and believers are "the latter Harvest" who "will likewise be raised." He supports this with Romans 8:11 (the Spirit of him who raised Jesus "will also give life to your mortal bodies") and 1 Peter 1:3 (a "living hope through the resurrection").

### 5. The creative word and the coming voice
Wescoe reads 1 Thessalonians 4:14-16 about the Lord descending with a shout and the dead in Christ rising first. He argues that the same voice that spoke creation into being (Genesis 1, John 1:14) will raise the dead, and that the resurrection of Jesus is the proof of that voice's power. He stresses that the faithful should be prepared "no matter what," since "nobody knows that day or hour."

### 6. Belief that produces works; rejection of reincarnation; the hope for animals
On John 6:40 and 1 Corinthians 6:14, Wescoe distinguishes a "dead faith" from living belief that produces fruit, while noting that "even the demons believe." He rejects two rival views: that death is simply the end, and that souls are "reincarnated into an animal body or another human body." He closes with Revelation 21:4-5 and argues, citing Isaiah 11, Hosea 2:18 and Revelation 5:13, that animals are included in the new creation.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Death is the end, or souls cycle through reincarnation.** Wescoe says the scriptures teach a resurrection to eternal life after this life, not cycling into another body.
- **The resurrection is a late or merely poetic idea, not in the Old Testament.** He argues the resurrection is "also according to the scriptures," citing Daniel 12:2, Isaiah 26:19 and Psalm 16 as direct or near-direct texts.
- **Easter's imagery (bunnies, eggs, baskets) is a neutral part of the resurrection celebration.** He argues these have "pagan fertility origins," and he wants to avoid merging them with the resurrection.
- **The thief on the cross proves there is no resurrection, since he goes to Paradise immediately.** Wescoe says some use Luke 23:43 this way, but he says the passage is debated and he is "not sure" of the correct reading. This is a claim about how others argue, not an argument he settles.
- **Blame for the crucifixion rests on one group.** A chat viewer raises the Romans versus the priests. Wescoe says "we're all to blame" and that the important point is forgiveness, not who is more responsible.
