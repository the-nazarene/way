## Summary

Craig Wescoe's video is a review of the film *Christspiracy*, which he describes as a film about Jesus's opposition to animal sacrifice and the veganism of his earliest followers. He frames the review as 18 points from the film and says he agrees with 17 of them. The question the review implicitly asks is whether the film's central claims hold up: that Jesus disrupted the Temple to oppose animal slaughter, that the Temple was a profit-driven slaughterhouse, and that Jesus and his first followers were vegan.

Wescoe answers largely in the affirmative. His evidence is mostly textual and comes from the Hebrew Bible and a few early Christian sources. He cites Daniel 1 and Genesis 1 for a vegetarian diet and for the reading of dominion as stewardship. He reads Jeremiah 7 as the basis for the word *parim*, which he renders "violent ones," and he treats Jesus's quotation of that passage as opposition to sacrifice. For the Jerusalem community, he relies on a quotation from Epiphanius's *Panarion* 30.16, which he says reaches him through Keith Akers's book *The Lost Religion of Jesus*. He says Akers's sources are verifiable and that the quotation is "exactly the kind of thing Jesus would say."

His one disagreement is with the film's claim that Paul was anti-vegetarian. He argues that Paul's own statements in 1 Corinthians (8:13 and 11:1) sound like a testimony in favor of abstaining from meat. He says he wishes the film had left the point out. The review ends with a Resurrection Sunday call to "resurrect the message of animal liberation," to pressure religious institutions toward veganism, and to stop mocking people of faith in animal-rights circles. He notes that his fuller arguments are in other videos and articles, which he links.

## Key Teachings

### Temple as slaughterhouse and marketplace

Wescoe says the film does well in showing the Temple as an animal-selling and slaughter operation. He describes priests as "slaughterhouse workers" and the drainage channels under the Temple as carrying the blood of the animals. He says the film shows the sacrificial system as a profit-making enterprise. He ties this to the Sadducees, whom he says Herod appointed to control the Temple, and to modern kosher and halal certification, which he calls "a trillion dollar industry." These are his characterizations; he gives no sources for the Herodian appointment or the economic figures in this video.

### Jesus's quotation of Jeremiah and the meaning of *parim*

He argues that the Temple action was about animals, not only money changers. He notes that the money changers are not mentioned in the Lukan account. He says that the verses Jesus quotes come from Jeremiah and the prophets, which in his reading criticize animal sacrifice, not from a Proverbs-style passage about dishonest scales. He builds his case around the word *parim*. Wescoe says the film is "exactly correct" that the word usually rendered "thieves or robbers" is better rendered "violent ones," which makes the Temple a "den of violence." He cites a Creation Care Church live talk (FNL 125, "God hates violence") for a fuller treatment. He cites Jeremiah 7:1 as the reference for the passage, and he does not give the verse number in the Jeremiah quotation.

### Diet and creation in Genesis and Daniel

Wescoe presents the diet of Daniel 1 as evidence that vegetable food produced better health and wisdom. He reads Genesis 1:26-31 as showing a creation without killing, in which God gives plants as food, and he calls the "dominion" of Genesis 1 a matter of "responsibility and stewardship," not a "license to kill." He says that the verse after dominion, Genesis 1:29, is God's own statement that plant food was intended, and that this is "the exact opposite" of permission to kill animals.

### Early Jerusalem community as vegan: the Ebionites and Epiphanius

This is the point where Wescoe leans most on a single source. He reports the film's claim, drawn from Akers, that Epiphanius's *Panarion* 30.16 preserves an early saying: "I came to destroy the sacrifices and if you cease not from sacrificing the Wrath will not cease from you." He notes that it is unclear whether this came from an original Gospel of Matthew or from another text. He identifies Epiphanius's Ebionites with the Jerusalem church under James, which he describes as among the earliest forms of Christianity. He also says that the quotation fits the Temple disruption, the Epistle to the Hebrews, and "pretty much every first century text" in opposing the sacrificial system. He mentions an objection a source critic might raise, that the evidence for early veganism is second-century material. He answers it by pointing to the extra-biblical sources the film uses. He also points to a longer video on "evidence for veganism in the early church," which he says goes through "dozens of early church passages."

### The Nazarene: who Jesus was, not where he was from

He presents the film's claim, based on an interview with Robert Eisenman, that no early source names a town called Nazareth. He says Josephus mentions roughly "200" Galilean places, none of them Nazareth, and that the Hebrew Bible does not mention it. He says the name "Nazarene" probably means something like "the righteous one" or "the devoted one," and he links this to John 14 ("the way of the truth and the life"). He concludes that "Nazarene" refers to who Jesus was, not his hometown. He does not walk through the textual basis for this beyond the single verse he cites.

### Paul and diet: the one point he rejects

The film says Paul was anti-vegetarian, and Andrew Lindsay appears on that point. Wescoe says he disagrees. He notes the three passages usually cited against Paul (1 Timothy 4, Romans 14, and 1 Corinthians 10) and points to his own live talks on them. His main argument is that 1 Corinthians 8:13 ("I will never again eat meat") and 11:1 ("follow my example as I follow the example of Christ") read as a testimony for abstaining. He worries that the film's Paul claim will lead Christians to dismiss the message, since Paul wrote much of the New Testament.

### Hypocrisy, "cognitive dissonance," and the critique of Christian practice

Wescoe agrees with the film's examples of Christians who pray for hunting success or who support animal cruelty. He notes that the film shows the same hypocrisy across world religions, so it is not unique to Christianity. He uses the term "cognitive dissonance" (Melanie Joy's field), and he identifies it with what Jesus called "hypocrisy." He praises the film's treatment of a former roadkill-eater whom Christians prayed to deliver, who later went vegan. He says the film's contrast with people in drive-thru lines was effective. He also rejects "free range" and "regenerative" meat as fixes, calling them largely "false advertising."
