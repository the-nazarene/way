## Summary

Craig asks what the forbidden fruit of Genesis 2:17 was, treating it as one of Scripture's most debated puzzles. He states up front that the talk is his personal research, not the official view of Creation Care Church, and that it is "not intended to be the once forall answer" (00:02). His answer is that the forbidden fruit is symbolic: the "flesh and blood" of grapes stands for the flesh and blood of animals, and the prohibition is against eating animal flesh with its blood, the one restriction he says God imposed before and after the flood.

His evidence is a chain of textual parallels. He lists four trees in Genesis 3 (tree of life, fig, tree of knowledge, thorn bush) and matches them to four trees in Judges 9 (olive, fig, vine, bramble), assigning the olive to the tree of life and the vine to the tree of knowledge. He argues that the Nazarite vow in Numbers 6 (no grape products, no cutting hair, no touching a dead body) reflects the same prohibition. He also points to Genesis 1:29-30, where only plants are given for food, and Genesis 9:4, which forbids eating flesh with its blood. He connects the death warning in Genesis 2:17 to Galatians 6:7 and Hosea 10:12-13, and reads Proverbs 3:13-18 as identifying wisdom with the tree of life. The wine-press image (grape juice looking like blood, trampled up to the knees) and the image of a priest standing in blood are offered as the link between grapes and slaughter.

The talk ends with a long live question-and-answer period. Craig repeatedly invites alternative biblical evidence, grants that the text is ambiguous about whether Eve's added rule about touching was her own invention or an unrecorded command, and gives his personal position on drinking wine (he abstains). He argues that the serpent was Satan, citing Revelation, and suggests that Jesus and John the Baptist may have been Nazarites. He closes with prayer and a preview of the next live talk on Mark 11:23-26.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The forbidden fruit as a symbol for flesh and blood
Craig's central claim is that the grape is a symbol. He says the forbidden fruit "is symbolized by the Flesh and Blood of grapes from which wine is obtained" (about 31:22-32:59). His reasoning is that God's only dietary restriction in Genesis 1 is the one against eating animals, since Genesis 1:29-30 gives "every herb that yields seed" and "every tree whose fruit yield seed" as food. He reads Genesis 9:4 ("but you shall not eat flesh with its life that is its blood") as the explicit form of that restriction. He does not argue from Greek or Hebrew lexical detail for the "tree" itself; the evidence he gives is the structure of the passages.

### 2. The four-tree parallel between Genesis 3 and Judges 9
He lists four trees in Genesis 3 (tree of life, fig tree, tree of knowledge of good and evil, and the thorn bush, Hebrew *coats*, which he says is Strong's 6975) and four in Judges 9:7-15 (olive, fig, vine, bramble, also *coats*). He matches fig to fig and bramble to bramble, then assigns olive to tree of life and vine to tree of knowledge. The argument depends on the pattern being intentional; he says "I don't see why there wouldn't be" one. He does not address whether the matching is coincidental, and he gives no source for the claim that the bramble and thorn bush are the same Hebrew word beyond the Strong's reference.

### 3. Olive oil, bread and the anointing
The olive is linked to anointing (1 Samuel 16:1) and to oil used in bread (1 Kings 17:7-16). He pairs bread and wine as a recurring couple, citing the Last Supper and Melchizedek in Genesis 14. This is how he justifies the olive as the tree of life, since the olive supplies oil that symbolizes God's wisdom.

### 4. The Nazarite vow as the key to the prohibition
The Nazarite vow of Numbers 6:1-8 forbids grape products "from the seed to the skin," forbids cutting the hair, and forbids going near or touching a dead body. Craig argues these prohibitions form "a unifying purpose" and that the grape-vine prohibition is the same one that the Genesis prohibition expresses. He also uses Genesis 3:3, where Eve says they may not "touch it," as an echo of the Nazarite rule against touching the dead.

### 5. Death, reaping and wisdom
He reads "you shall surely die" (Genesis 2:17) as a sowing-and-reaping consequence. Using Galatians 6:7 ("whatever a man sows that he will also reap") and Hosea 10:12-13, he argues that killing animals to eat them means reaping death. He contrasts this with Proverbs 3:13-18, where wisdom is "a tree of life" with "ways of pleasantness" and "all her ways are ways of Peace." The tree of life is thus the wisdom of God, and the tree of knowledge is a deceptive "wisdom" gained by violence.

### 6. Eve's extra rule and Matthew 5
Craig addresses Eve's addition of "nor shall you touch it" (Genesis 3:3). He first says the text is ambiguous (about 39:13), then proposes that Eve, like the Sermon on the Mount's teaching on anger (Matthew 5:21-22), may have chosen a safeguard by not even approaching the forbidden thing.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The forbidden fruit is an apple (or fig, pomegranate, etc.).** Craig says those who hold this view should show biblical evidence, asking "where are apples even mentioned in the bible," and says his own reading is evidence-based.
- **The serpent was just a snake.** Craig argues from Revelation that the serpent was Satan in serpent form, and reads Genesis 3:14 ("more cunning than any beast") as indicating that the snake was not a mere animal.
- **Grape juice and wine are clearly different in the Bible.** He says the same Hebrew word is often used for both ("new wine" and "old wine"), so the distinction is hard to draw.
- **Nazarene and Nazarite are the same.** He says the *nazarin* of the heresiologists may not be the same thing as Nazarites, and that the film's claim is distinct from a Nazarite claim.
- **God forbade touching the fruit.** He says the text does not state this and that Eve may have added it on her own, or God may have said it without it being recorded.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Torah:** The argument relies on Genesis 1 and 9 and on the Nazarite vow of Numbers 6, reading the dietary and purity rules as a single thread from creation to Numbers.
- **Diet and nonviolence:** This is the talk's core. Craig argues that God's original and post-flood dietary limit was against eating animal flesh with blood, and that the vow and Proverbs point the same way.
- **Jesus' teaching:** He cites Hosea 6:6 ("mercy not sacrifice") and the Sermon on the Mount's anger teaching (Matthew 5:21-22) as parallels to his safeguard reading of Eve.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** He suggests Jesus and John the Baptist may have been Nazarites (Matthew 2:23, Luke 1:15), and that the wine on the cross could complete a Nazarite vow. He says this is not the claim the film makes.
- **Paul and the Jerusalem community:** He cites Paul's comparison of drinking and eating flesh, a table-fellowship dispute he attributes to Paul and Peter, and the Jerusalem Council of Acts 15 as evidence that the early church debated these rules. He does not discuss James by name.
- **Nazarenes/Ebionites:** His mention of the *nazarin* in heresiology books (Irenaeus, Epiphanius) is the one direct point of contact with the repo's Nazarene question, though he does not develop it.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** Death in Genesis 2:17 is read as sowing and reaping (Galatians 6:7), not as an afterlife claim.
