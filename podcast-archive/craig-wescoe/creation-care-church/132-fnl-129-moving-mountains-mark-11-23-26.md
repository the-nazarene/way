## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks what Jesus meant by "moving mountains" in Mark 11:23-26, and why the saying sits next to a call to forgive. He argues the "mountain" is a barrier to reconciliation between two believers, not a literal or psychic power. His main evidence is a set of parallels: Luke 17:3-6 (the mulberry tree), Matthew 18:15-22 (the two or three agreeing, and the seventy-times-seven exchange), and Gospel of Thomas 48, which he calls "a fourth witness." He reads repentance as a condition of forgiveness and treats Matthew 18:19-20 as the key to the "agreement" promise.

Wescoe then extends the point in two directions. First, he links peacemaking to Ephesians, Matthew 5:9, James 3:17-18, Romans 12:18 and Colossians 3, and argues that "all" in Romans 12:18 includes animals. Second, he argues from 1 Corinthians 5:11, 2 Thessalonians 3:6, Romans 14:15, and Proverbs 23:20 that the community should withdraw from unrepentant people, including those who abuse animals or eat animal flesh. He presents this as the reverse of enabling abuse.

The talk ends with about thirty minutes of live Q&A, mostly about animal rights and vegan outreach inside churches. Wescoe responds to questions on Luke 23:34, Proverbs 24:11, anger at animal abuse, and church potlucks. He closes by announcing the next talk on the "sons of God" (Job 38:4-7).

## Key Teachings

### Moving mountains as reconciliation, not psychic power
Wescoe reads Mark 11:23-26 as joined to the forgiveness saying that follows it, so the mountain stands for an obstacle between people. He explicitly rejects a reading of the passage as "this psychic power to you know bend spoons and move mountains." In his words, we move mountains "in the sense that when we're being reconciled with each other there's no obstacle too large to stand in the way of us making peace."

### Luke 17 and Matthew 18 as parallels
He treats Luke 17:3-6 (the mulberry tree uprooted and planted in the sea, prompted by the apostles asking to "increase our faith") and Matthew 18:15-22 (the stepwise process: private rebuke, then two or three witnesses, then the church) as versions of the same saying. He argues that forgiveness is owed when the brother has actually repented, "has actually turned away from the thing." If the brother continues and refuses to listen, he says the conclusion differs.

On Matthew 18:21-22 he says Jesus' answer of "up to 70 times 7" means the limit is not a count of 490. He says "that's probably not what we should take away from this"; the lesson is to be "always ready to forgive."

### Matthew 18:19-20 and the two who agree
Wescoe takes the promise that "if two of you agree on Earth concerning anything that they ask it will be done" as the explanation for Mark's "believe" and "do not doubt." The agreement is the agreement to be reconciled, and the presence of Christ "in the midst of them" is the assurance.

### Gospel of Thomas 48 as a fourth witness
He describes the Gospel of Thomas as an extra-biblical text "that dates back to likely the 2 century," with about half its sayings having canonical parallels. He calls Thomas 48 "a bit more succinct" than the other three accounts and reads it as the same message. He admits the relationship between Thomas and the canonical versions is hard to make out: "it's hard to kind of make out exactly what it is."

### Peacemaking, including toward animals
Drawing on Matthew 5:9 ("blessed are the peacemakers"), James 3:17-18, Colossians 3:14-15, and Romans 12:18, he argues that peace is "in his house" and the house is "the whole world." He reads Romans 12:18's "panton" ("all") as not restricted to humans. He says the point is "it is about people and animals."

### Church discipline and withdrawal
He draws on 1 Corinthians 5:11 ("not even to eat with such a person"), 2 Thessalonians 3:5-6 (withdraw from the disorderly brother), and probably Deuteronomy 13:5 ("cast out the evil from among you"). He argues that forgiveness does not mean enabling abuse. The procedure is to confront, bring witnesses, bring the congregation, and then withdraw. He says repentance is not mere confession: "repentance means quite literally to turn around."

### Diet and Romans 14 / Proverbs 23
He applies Romans 14:15 to those who eat animal flesh, calling it failing "to walk in love." He reads Proverbs 23:20 as warning against associating with "gluttonous Eaters of Flesh," arguing that drunkards and meat eaters are both predisposed to violence. He says the Hebrew word there, "baser," means flesh, while the general word for food would be "broma." He also cites Proverbs 24:11 as a "right to rescue" animals destined for slaughter.

### The parable of the unforgiving servant
Wescoe reads Matthew 18:21-35 as showing that the forgiven debtor who refuses to forgive is "delivered to the torturers." He calls the debt Christ pays "zero," and offers a car-payment analogy. He credits a line from Matthew West's song "Forgiveness": "when you forgive... the prisoner that it really freeze is you."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mountain-moving as psychic or supernatural power:** Wescoe says the passage is not about "psychic power to you know bend spoons and move mountains," but about reconciliation.
- **Literal seventy-times-seven limit:** He says the 490 count is not the takeaway; the lesson is to be always ready to forgive.
- **Forgiveness without repentance:** He argues forgiveness is owed when repentance is real, and that Luke 23:34 must be read against Matthew 4:17's opening call to repent.
- **Confession as repentance:** He says saying "I'm sorry" is only "the beginning of repentance"; repentance means turning around and changing conduct.
- **Forgiveness requires tolerating abuse:** He argues the opposite: withdraw from the unrepentant after the stepwise process (Matthew 18; 1 Corinthians 5; 2 Thessalonians 3).
- **Peace and forgiveness are only about humans:** He says Romans 12:18's "all" includes animals.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Jesus vs. Paul:** Wescoe presents Jesus' Gospel sayings (Mark, Luke, Matthew, Thomas) and Paul's letters as one message on peace and discipline. He treats Paul's letters as authoritative on withdrawal and diet, without distinguishing Paul's voice from Jesus'.
- **James:** He cites James 3:17-18 on peacemaking, briefly and in support of the reconciliation reading.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet and nonviolence:** He reads Romans 14 and Proverbs 23 as prohibiting animal-flesh eating and links it to violence, and he says God "doesn't want these animal sacrifices," citing the prophets in the Q&A. This is a central thread of the talk.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** He ties Matthew 4:17 ("the kingdom of heaven is at hand") to repentance and mentions the Lord's Prayer's "your kingdom can come on Earth" in closing prayer.
- **Gospels and canon:** He uses the Gospel of Thomas as an independent parallel while acknowledging its textual relationship is "hard to kind of make out." He does not discuss how the canon was formed.
- **Resurrection, afterlife, hell:** Not a substantive topic, though he says Matthew 18's binding and loosing and the Matthew 18:35 "torturers" passage concern consequences; he says not to wish for eternal punishment on others.
