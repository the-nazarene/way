## Summary

The talk asks how to understand Paul, given that people disagree about whether he was a faithful apostle or a heretic, whether he endorsed or opposed veganism, and whether his letters have been accurately preserved and translated. Craig Wescoe organizes the answer around six "challenges": the Koine Greek language and Paul's dialectic style, authorship and textual redaction, interpretation, the authority of his letters, and their intended purpose. He treats apparent contradictions between Paul and Jesus or James as signs of misreading, not as proof that Paul was a heretic. He argues that the Pastoral Epistles, Ephesians and Colossians, and 2 Thessalonians are contested, and that the text may have been altered.

The main case for veganism comes from two passages. He argues that Romans 14:21 uses a Greek verb meaning "strike against," so that the danger Paul names is violence, which he links to eating meat and drinking alcohol. He argues that 1 Corinthians 10:25 means "marketplace" rather than "meat market," and that 1 Timothy 4:4 means "created things," which he reads as pointing back to the plant-based diet of Genesis 1:29. He says the Jerusalem Council's abstention rules and the Paul-James tension in Galatians and Acts frame these questions. He presents this as a charitable reading and says that anyone who reaches a different conclusion should reject Paul's contradictory-seeming statements in favour of Jesus. The second half is a Q&A in which he answers chat questions on the Paul-James rivalry, the Ebionites and Nazarenes, Torah observance, and the "bear joke." ## Key Teachings

### 1. Koine Greek and the dialectic style make Paul hard to read
Wescoe says Koine Greek was written in unbroken capitals with no spaces or punctuation (he attributes this point to "Theologian Don Bartlett"), so translators must guess whether a sentence is a question or a statement. He describes Paul's "dialectic" style: Paul sets out one position, then a second, then often his own third position. Wescoe says the marker "Lego" (the Greek word for "I say") indicates it, but Paul does not always use it, and sometimes he implies an opponent without naming one. His example is Romans 10:9-21, where he reads verses 18-19 as Paul posing questions ("have they not heard?") that a translator may render as statements.

### 2. Authorship and redaction
Wescoe says the scholarly consensus, as he states it, is that seven letters are authentic, three questionable (2 Thessalonians, Ephesians, Colossians), three doubtful (the Pastoral Epistles: 1 and 2 Timothy and Titus), and Hebrews sits outside the group because it does not claim to be by Paul. He says 1 Timothy shows signs of second-century composition, "over 50 years after Paul's death." He notes that Paul's letters name co-senders (Sylvanus, Timothy), which could explain differences in style. He cites the Marcionite "apostolic" collection, which lacks Romans 15 and the Pastorals, and says Romans 13 may show "internal literary signs of possible redaction." He adds that Paul was "one of the most forged authors in the ancient world," pointing to the Seneca correspondence and the Acts of Paul and Thecla (captioned "acts of Thea" and "Paul Anda").

### 3. Authority, intention, and the Jerusalem tension
Wescoe asks whether Paul's letters were meant as Scripture or as ordinary correspondence, citing 2 Timothy 4:13 (the request for a coat and papers). He notes that 1 Corinthians 7:10-12 separates "not I but the Lord" from "I, not the Lord," which he says is hard to explain if Paul was simply inspired. He argues that the Epistle of James reads like a response to Galatians, and that Paul's account of confronting Peter (Galatians 2:11) and the Acts 15 decree (abstain from idol meat, strangled meat, blood, and porneia) together show that Paul and Jerusalem disagreed on table fellowship and food. He says the Jerusalem Council's rules "may be a correction" of Romans 14 and 1 Corinthians 8-10. He also says Paul's own statements of submission (1 Corinthians 1:12-13; Galatians 2:1-2) show that Paul acknowledged Jerusalem's authority.

### 4. Romans 14:21: "stumble" should be "strike against," and the concern is violence
Wescoe argues that the verb rendered "stumble" in Romans 14:21 (the transliterated form "proscope toe" or proskoptō) literally means "to strike or beat against." He supports this with Matthew 4:6 and Luke 4:11 ("strike your foot against a stone") and Matthew 7:27 (the winds "beat against" the house). He reads Romans 14:21 and 1 Corinthians 8:13 together as Paul saying he will "never again eat meat," because eating meat and drinking wine make a person "more prone to violence and less self-controlled." He adds that before his conversion Paul "had been quite literally striking" the believers (citing the stoning of Stephen and the pushing of James from the temple pinnacle), and he asserts that the early Christians were vegan, non-drinking, and nonviolent, referring listeners to a separate FNL talk for that claim.

### 5. 1 Corinthians 10:25: "marketplace," not "meat market"
Wescoe says the Greek word *makellon* (rendered "meat market" or "shambles") simply means marketplace, and that the word *kreas* (meat) does not appear in 1 Corinthians 10:25. He also reads the translated phrase "without raising questions of conscience" as "without questions of moral consciousness." His reading: Paul is telling believers to eat whatever is sold in the marketplace that is not morally questionable, and meat and alcohol, which Paul elsewhere treats as making people prone to violence, fall into the questionable category. He cites 1 Corinthians 11:1 ("follow my example as I follow the example of Christ") as Paul urging others to adopt the same restraint.

### 6. 1 Timothy 4:1-4: "every creature" means created things, and the reference is to diet and marriage
Wescoe rejects the rendering "every creature" for the Greek *pas ktisis*. He argues that if "creature" meant any animal, the verse would permit cannibalism, since "creature" is used at Mark 16:15 for human hearers of the gospel. He cites the other uses of the phrase, such as Romans 8:22 ("the whole creation"), and reads 1 Timothy 4:4 as saying "everything that was created by God and declared good should not be forbidden," which he takes to include marriage and "plant-based diet." He ties this to Genesis 1:29, which he calls "the very good diet instituted by God." He also says God "did not create animals to be eaten."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Paul wrote all thirteen letters.** The apologetic position assumes single authorship. Wescoe says scholars divide the letters into seven authentic, three questionable, three doubtful, plus Hebrews, and that Paul's letters name co-senders.
- **Paul's letters are uniformly inerrant and inspired in every sentence.** Wescoe asks whether 2 Timothy 4:13 was meant as Scripture and says there may have been developments in Paul's understanding and possible redaction.
- **Paul contradicts Jesus or James.** He says apparent contradictions usually come from a dialectic style, a question read as a statement, or a translation error, and that the correct reading may show agreement.
- **Romans 14:21 is about offending a brother's sensitivities.** He says the verb means "strike against," so the concern is violence.
- **1 Corinthians 10:25 refers to a "meat market."** He says the Greek means marketplace and that "meat" is not in the text.
- **1 Timothy 4:4 permits eating animals if one gives thanks.** He says "every creature" means created things, which in context includes marriage and a plant-based diet.
