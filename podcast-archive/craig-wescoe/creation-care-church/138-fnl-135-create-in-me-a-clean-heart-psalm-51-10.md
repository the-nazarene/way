## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks what Psalm 51:10 ("create in me a clean heart") means and how the heart is connected to sin, death and forgiveness. He works from the Hebrew of the psalm, where he glosses "clean" as *tahor* (Strong's 2889) and "steadfast" as *naken* (Strong's 3559), and then moves through Ezekiel 36, Matthew 5:8, James 4 and 1, Proverbs, Jeremiah 17 and 29, Matthew 5, 1 John 3, Genesis 4, Matthew 18 and 6, and John 13. He presents a single process: an evil desire arising in the heart, which if nurtured becomes sin, which in turn brings death (he cites James 1:14-15 and the Matthew 5 teachings on anger and lust). His remedy is to guard the heart, submit it to God, and root out desire at its first stage, with love for God and neighbour as the umbrella that keeps the heart pure so that one can "see God" (Matthew 5:8).

Wescoe's argument is largely homiletic and rests on close reading of the chosen verses rather than on outside historical evidence. He draws a sharp line between inward forgiveness, which he says should uproot any wish for another's harm, and the communal process of Matthew 18, which he says protects the community from unrepentant members. Much of the talk's final third is Q&A on his church's veganism. He argues that Cain's offering was rejected because of Cain's jealous heart rather than because it was plant-based, citing Genesis as God's stated diet of fruit and plants; he also argues that eating flesh is tied to idolatry, while veganism is the opposite of idolatry.

The talk ends with a note that the audio was garbled for much of the stream, a prayer, and an announcement that the next talk will be on the Wedding Banquet of Luke 14, with an argument that Christians declining God's call to vegan stewardship may leave it to unbelievers to carry out.

## Key Teachings

### The clean heart as a Hebrew-grounded purification
Wescoe reads Psalm 51 (he says "51:1" but quotes the text of 51:10) and claims that *tahor* ("clean," sometimes "pure") and *naken* ("steadfast," sometimes "established," "prepared" or "ready") describe a heart purified and a spirit made ready for God's kingdom. He presents this as the psalm's meaning, and does not discuss textual alternatives beyond the glosses listed. The talk then sets the clean heart against the "Heart of Stone" that Ezekiel 36:25-27 says God will replace with a heart of flesh.

### Heart, idolatry and flesh-eating
Reading Ezekiel 36, Wescoe ties the "filthiness" and "Idols" that God cleanses away to idolatry, which he calls "primarily" making a god with human hands and then sacrificing to it. He says flesh-eating is "often coupled" with this practice and later calls flesh-eating "usually the number one or number two" thing associated with idolatry. Note that the claim about idolatry is asserted by the speaker and not argued from ancient sources in this transcript.

### The heart as deceitful, and not to be followed
Proverbs 4:23 ("guard your heart") and Jeremiah 17:9-10 ("the heart is deceitful above all things") are used to argue that the heart is not a reliable guide. Wescoe contrasts this with the popular advice to "just follow your heart," and with Proverbs 3:5-6, which he uses to say one should lean on God's understanding rather than one's own. He cites Jeremiah 29:13 as summarising the whole Bible: seeking God "with all your heart."

### The process: desire, sin, death
Wescoe's central structural claim is James 1:14-15: desire is conceived, gives birth to sin, and sin, full-grown, brings forth death. He treats this as a horticultural maturation process (roots, sprouting, bearing fruit) and says Jesus uses similar imagery. The practical advice he draws is to root out the evil desire at its first stage, and he applies this to anger in Matthew 5:20-22 (including "raka" and "you fool," and the note that "some manuscripts" lack "without a cause") and to lust in Matthew 5:27-28. He reads 1 John 3:11-12 and Genesis 4:6-8 as a case study: Cain, warned that sin "lies at the door," did not rule over the desire and killed Abel.

### Forgiveness from the heart versus community discipline
Wescoe separates two things: forgiving someone in the heart, which he says uproots the desire for another's harm, and the communal procedure of Matthew 18:15-17, which he describes as a sequence of private, two-or-three-witness, and church-level confrontation ending with the unrepentant being treated "like a heathen and a tax collector." He says the latter protects the community and does not license hatred. He reads Matthew 18:35 and Matthew 6:14-15 as requiring inward forgiveness, and says God's wish is for repentance rather than harm.

### Love as the umbrella and the mark of discipleship
John 13:34-35 and the "greatest commandment" are presented as the umbrella that prevents the sins of adultery, murder and theft. Wescoe extends this to "spiritual adultery" against God, including "celebrating their worship days" and flesh-eating. He ties the whole teaching to the Lord's Prayer ("your kingdom come") and to a vision of the kingdom in which everyone follows God's will.

### The Cain and Abel offering (Q&A)
In response to viewer questions, Wescoe argues against the common view that God accepted Abel's animal offering and rejected Cain's plant offering. He says the text does not state an animal sacrifice, that God's stated diet in Genesis is fruit and plants, and that the text's qualifier (Abel gave "the first" and "the fat") implies Abel offered his best while Cain did not. He concludes that the rejection was tied to Cain's heart, specifically his jealousy and desire to murder. He says a different interpretive picture exists in another source, which he does not name in the transcript.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Just follow your heart."** The popular view that the heart is a trustworthy guide. Wescoe argues from Jeremiah 17 and Proverbs 3 that the heart is deceitful and must be guarded and submitted to God.
- **Cain's offering was rejected because it was plant-based and God wanted animal sacrifice.** Wescoe disputes this common argument (raised by viewers in Q&A), citing Genesis's diet passage and arguing the text points to Cain's jealous heart and the absence of Abel's "first" qualifier.
- **Veganism is a form of idolatry, "worshiping animals."** A critique his viewers report hearing. He answers that eating flesh, not veganism, is the idolatrous practice, since animal sacrifices are idolatry.
- **Forgiving someone means accepting the wrong or letting them corrupt the community.** Wescoe distinguishes inward forgiveness, which he says removes the wish for harm, from the communal discipline of Matthew 18, which protects the church.
