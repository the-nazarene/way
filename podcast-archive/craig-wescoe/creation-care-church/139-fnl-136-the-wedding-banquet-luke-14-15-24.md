## Summary

Wescoe, host of the Creation Care Church Friday Night Live series, takes Jesus' parable of the great supper (Luke 14:15-24) as a question about present obedience: are Christians accepting the invitation to the wedding banquet, or making excuses and deferring God's will to "the Kingdom" later? He argues that the Kingdom's will includes the stewardship and compassionate treatment of animals, and that a vegan diet is God's ideal from the beginning (Genesis 1:29). He also argues that when Christians decline this work, non-believers, including atheists and vegans, end up leading it, which should shame the church.

His evidence is a chained series of short scripture readings rather than a historical or exegetical argument. He moves through humility sayings (Luke 14:8-11, Proverbs 29:23, Matthew 18:4), reciprocity and treasure in heaven (Luke 14:12-14, Matthew 6:19-21), the parable itself alongside Luke 9:59-62 and Matthew 21:28-32, the wedding garment (Matthew 22:11-14, Revelation 19:7-9), and the new creation (Revelation 21:1-6, Isaiah 11:6-9). He reads Genesis 1 as the "Alpha" and Revelation and Isaiah as the "Omega," with humans and animals restored as neighbors. He uses Hosea 6:6 ("mercy, not sacrifice") to support mercy toward animals. He does not discuss the parable's Lukan setting, its relation to Matthew's version beyond noting the wedding garment, or the original audience. His reading of the text is presented as his own understanding.

The talk ends with a live Q&A. Questions about marriage and children get a response that the Genesis command was given to Adam and Eve and to humanity collectively, not to every individual, and that Jesus and Paul treat celibate devotion to the Kingdom as acceptable. The host closes with prayer and announces next week's session on Clement and the "Clementine homes" as an early church text. The Q&A also includes comments read aloud from viewers, and the host's replies to them are part of the talk.

## Key Teachings

### Humility versus "dominion" (Luke 14:8-11; Proverbs 29:23; Matthew 18:4) [00:02, 04:45]
Wescoe reads the wedding-seat saying in Luke 14 as a lesson in humility: exalting oneself leads to being humbled. He applies this to animals, arguing that claiming a right to do whatever one wants with them is self-exaltation, and that the proper stance is the stewardship "according to God's image and likeness" (he cites Genesis 1:26-29). He ties this to Matthew 18:4 and to the example of Jesus, "I came to serve not to be served." He then asks whether Christians are postponing this stewardship to "the Kingdom" or doing it now.

### Giving to those who cannot repay (Luke 14:12-14; Matthew 6:19-21) [07:51, 09:25]
He reads the Luke instruction to invite the poor, maimed, lame and blind, who "cannot repay you," as a model for care that expects no return. He concedes that animals "by and large they can't" repay, and says help given to them is done out of obedience and love. Matthew 6:19-21 ("lay up for yourselves Treasures in Heaven") supports the point that the reward is deferred to God, "at the resurrection of the just."

### The great supper and the excuses (Luke 14:15-27; Luke 9:59-62; Matthew 21:28-32) [12:33, 14:06, 15:44, 17:19]
The central argument. The invited guests refuse with excuses (a field, five yoke of oxen, a marriage), and the master sends to the poor and to the highways. Wescoe reads this as God's response to believers who refuse his call, and says God then calls "people who don't even want to be my followers." He compares the excuses to Christians who look forward to heaven but decline present stewardship. He adds Luke 9:59-62 (the plow) and the Matthew 21:28-32 parable of the two sons, stressing that the one who actually does the Father's will is the one who counts, even after first refusing. The application is that Christians who have said no should repent and act now.

### The wedding garment as the righteousness of Christ (Matthew 22:11-14; Revelation 19:7-9) [18:53, 20:26, 22:00]
He notes that Matthew adds the wedding garment detail that Luke lacks, and asks what the garment is. Citing Revelation 19:7-9 ("fine linen clean and bright... the righteous acts of the Saints"), he argues it is not self-earned merit but the righteousness that "comes from that lamb," received by following Christ and doing his will. He calls Jesus "the gate that leads to that Kingdom."

### The new creation: Genesis, Isaiah and Revelation (Revelation 21:1-6; Genesis 1:26-31; Isaiah 11:6-9) [23:37, 25:11, 26:44]
Wescoe makes this the core of his reading of animal ethics. He links Revelation 21's "no more death" to a call to stop killing animals, and reads Genesis 1 (dominion, and "every herb that yields seed" for food, "very good") as the "Alpha" (the beginning). He reads Isaiah 11:6-9 (wolf with lamb, a little child leading them) as the "Omega" (the end) and as a Kingdom in which humans and animals are "neighbors." He concludes that the Alpha and the Omega "look nearly identical," so God's will is the same at beginning and end. The diet argument rests on Genesis 1:29 with Isaiah and Revelation as the restored state.

### Mercy over sacrifice and God's covenant with animals (Hosea 6:6; Hosea 2:18; Genesis 9; 2 John; Mark 16:15) [29:51, 31:26, 36:09, 37:46, 40:54]
In answer to viewer comments, he argues that God covenants with animals (Hosea 2:18, Genesis 9), which he takes as a sign of a distinctive relationship, and that animals have "that same breath" (Ecclesiastes 3:19). He reads Hosea 6:6 ("I desire Mercy not sacrifice") as a command for mercy toward animals, not as a critique of ritual sacrifice alone, and connects it to "blessed are the merciful." He quotes 2 John's "love one another" as addressed to animals as well as people, and Mark 16:15 ("preach the gospel to Every Creature") as extending the mission to animals. He says that praying before killing an animal is not the answer, and that the respectful response is not killing it at all.

### Children and marriage (Matthew; Luke; Paul; Revelation 21) [42:30, 44:04, 51:52]
Responding to viewer questions, he says that Jesus' remarks on making oneself a eunuch for the Kingdom and Paul's preference for remaining unmarried show that devoting oneself to Kingdom work is an acceptable ideal and that childbearing is not required of everyone. He reads the Genesis command to be fruitful as a command "for Humanity as a whole" and not for each person, and says the resurrection sayings ("neither marry nor are given in marriage") suggest the command is fulfilled collectively. This section is partly a side discussion, which he says was not the talk's intent.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Veganism is an atheist or new-age philosophy and therefore anti-Christian.** Wescoe says the opposite is true, that veganism is God's will "from the beginning," and that churches should stop perpetuating the misunderstanding [55:01, 56:35].
- **God gave humans permission to eat animals (Genesis 9; Noah).** He lists this as one of the excuses believers make, not a reason [39:20]. He says the Genesis 9 covenant shows a special relationship with animals [40:54].
- **Animals are merely part of the environment, so sustainable slaughter or hunting is acceptable.** He endorses a viewer's view that animals are not "part of the environment" any more than people are, and rejects "sustainable slaughter" as the answer [40:54].
- **Stewardship is something to do in the Kingdom or after Jesus returns.** He calls this an excuse; he argues the Lord's Prayer calls for God's will "on Earth as it is in heaven" now [06:18, 15:44].
- **Genesis 1:28's "be fruitful and multiply" obliges every individual to have children.** He says the command was for Adam and Eve and for humanity collectively, not for each person [44:04, 51:52].
- **Having no children is wrong for a believer.** He says he does not think it is wrong, citing Jesus' and Paul's remarks on celibate devotion [42:30].

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** The talk draws on Paul only lightly and does not separate Jesus' teaching from Paul's. Wescoe treats Romans 12:2 and Paul's marriage and food remarks as consistent with Jesus, without discussing differences.
- **Kingdom on earth:** This is the talk's central theme. Wescoe reads "God's will... on Earth as it is in heaven" and Isaiah 11 as an earthly, present-tense Kingdom in which animals and humans live in harmony, and he argues Christians should begin it now.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** The "resurrection of the just" appears briefly (Luke 14:14) as the time of reward, and "no more death" (Revelation 21) as the new creation. No discussion of hell.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet, nonviolence:** The diet argument rests on Genesis 1:29; Hosea 6:6 is used against sacrifice as a call to mercy. The talk does not engage with Torah's food laws or with sacrificial law in any detail.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Wescoe identifies Jesus with the "Alpha and the Omega," the "Emmanuel" of Revelation 21, and "the gate" to the Kingdom. He does not discuss historical questions about Jesus' self-understanding.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Only indirectly, through the harmonization of Luke and Matthew and the use of Revelation as a single prophetic book. Clement (next week) is flagged as an early church source but not discussed.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Only a passing citation of James's "true religion" line, read from a viewer comment.
- **Nazarenes, Ebionites, Essenes:** Not addressed.
