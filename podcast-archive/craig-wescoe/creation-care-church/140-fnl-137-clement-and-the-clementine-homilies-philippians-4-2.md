## Summary

Wescoe opens the first of four talks on the Clementine Homilies by asking who Clement of Rome was and what the Homilies are. He takes Philippians 4:2-3 as his anchor, where Paul names a "Clement" among his fellow workers. He argues that every source he cites (Irenaeus, Tertullian, Eusebius, Jerome, Photius) agrees this is the same Clement. The real dispute is whether Clement was ordained directly by Peter or came two generations later, after Linus and Anacletus. Irenaeus gives the two-generation list, but Irenaeus also says Clement conversed with the apostles. Tertullian, Jerome, and the Clementine Homilies themselves describe a direct Peter-to-Clement succession, and Wescoe concludes that "the preponderance of the evidence" favors the direct line.

He then turns to the Clementine literature: the Homilies, the two prefatory letters, the Recognitions (in Rufinus's Latin), and the Syriac manuscript. He compares their relationship to the Synoptic problem and says he is "personally convinced" that material unique to the Homilies goes back to Peter, Clement, and Jesus. He gives a date range of about 50 to 325 CE, with Eusebius as the latest attestation. He places the teaching in the circle of the Ebionites and Nazarenes, and offers two hypotheses. Either the material is authentic, or it was preserved and reworked by later Ebionite-Nazarene groups who rejected proto-orthodoxy. On either hypothesis, he says, it still points back to Jesus and the apostles.

The talk ends by previewing the next three sessions: the "prophet like Moses," the "key of knowledge/David/the kingdom," and the "table of demons." It also includes a long Q&A. In it Wescoe admits that the authenticity of Peter's words cannot be known with certainty, and he links the table-of-demons material to his advocacy of vegan diet and his view that early proto-orthodoxy suppressed Jesus-and-apostles teaching on animals.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Clement was a worker named by Paul and known to all the sources
Wescoe reads Philippians 4:2-3 and says Irenaeus, Tertullian, Eusebius (3.15-16), and Jerome (*On Famous Men* 15) all identify the Clement of that passage with the later bishop of Rome. He calls this the one point of agreement. Clement is also named in the *Shepherd of Hermas* as the one who corresponds with foreign cities.

### 2. The succession dispute: Peter directly, or Linus and Anacletus in between
- Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.3.2-3, c. 180 per Wescoe) lists Linus, then Anacletus, then Clement, which places "two generations" between Clement and the apostles. Yet the same Irenaeus says Clement "had seen and conversed with the blessed apostles."
- Tertullian (*Prescription against Heretics* 32.2, c. 200) says Rome's church records show Clement "was ordained by Peter," using a Smyrna/Polycarp-John parallel.
- Jerome (c. 400) says that "most of the Latins" hold Clement as second after Peter, while he notes a minority view placing Linus and Anacletus first.
- Photius (9th century, *Bibliotheca* 112-113) reports the debate still continuing.
- Wescoe's argument: the records and the Roman tradition favor direct succession, and Irenaeus's own admission that Clement knew the apostles undercuts his two-generation claim. He grants that Linus and Anacletus could be a later harmonization (he suggests a merger of a Pauline and a Petrine church in Rome) but argues the Homilies' narrative would be "corroborated" in that case.

### 3. The Clementine Homilies' own account of Clement's life
Wescoe quotes Homilies 5.28 (as Clement speaking): educated in Greek philosophy, found the Greek schools unsatisfying, and was converted by a Jewish linen merchant who taught him the unity of God. He then met Barnabas, who introduced him to Peter. In Peter's letter to James (prefixed to the Homilies), Peter names Clement as his successor as bishop and says Clement has heard "all of my homilies."

### 4. The literary family: Homilies, Recognitions, and the Syriac manuscript
- Homilies: about 20 books; the fuller form includes dialogues with Appion, which Wescoe links to Eusebius's remark about "recently" circulating writings.
- Recognitions: Rufinus's 4th-century Latin translation. Wescoe says its first book, which he calls "the Essence of James" (the caption may garble this title), is a source for the Book of Acts and predates 170 CE.
- Syriac manuscript: dated by Wescoe to the 4th or 5th century, with the number 411 on it.
- The three share large verbatim or near-verbatim blocks, and Wescoe compares their relationship to the Synoptic problem.
- He calls the Clementine letters and First and Second Clement related but unresolved.

### 5. Dating and authenticity: two hypotheses
- Earliest possible date: the mid-1st century, if Clement wrote it directly.
- Latest possible date: about 325 CE, using Eusebius's mention of dialogues of Peter and Appion.
- Wescoe's "most skeptical" option: composition in the late 3rd or early 4th century by Ebionite-Nazarene groups who rejected proto-orthodoxy.
- His "authentic" option: material going back to Jesus, Peter, and Clement.
- Either way, he argues, the teaching preserves a line back to Jesus and the apostles, and it can be tested against the Gospels.

### 6. Three topics previewed: prophet like Moses, keys, table of demons
- Prophet like Moses: Acts 3:22 and Deuteronomy 18:18.
- Keys of the kingdom: Isaiah 22:22, Revelation 3:7-8, Luke 11:52 (the Pharisees "took away the keys of knowledge"), and Matthew 16:19 (Peter receives the keys). Wescoe says the Homilies pass these keys from Jesus to Peter to Clement.
- Table of demons: 1 Corinthians 10:21 and Clementine Homilies 7.8 (abstain from food offered to idols, from carcasses, from strangled animals, and from blood). He links this to the Jerusalem Council in Acts 15 and to a diet without "the product of violence."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Clement was a minor figure only mentioned once.** Wescoe says Clement appears in Philippians, Hermas, Irenaeus, Eusebius, and the Homilies, and that the sources treat him as a major early bishop. A viewer's remark that Clement is mentioned only once in the Bible is answered by pointing to sources outside the Bible.
- **The Clementine Homilies are an invented fiction to be ignored.** Wescoe rejects a "made-up" framing. He offers two live options (authentic early material, or later Ebionite-Nazarene preservation), and says both would still trace a line back to Jesus and the apostles.
- **Only what is in the Bible counts as apostolic.** Wescoe says the canon criterion of apostolic authorship excluded First Clement and the Homilies, but that canon formation is a historical process and not a warrant that texts outside it are false. In the Q&A he calls the question of canon "not necessarily a thing."
- **Jesus and the apostles did not care about animals; the vegan view is a later invention.** This is the talk's closing claim, made in the Q&A. Wescoe says the mainstream view of the 4th century came to power in the 2nd century, and he argues that the Homilies' table-of-demons material ("abstain... from blood") shows an earlier, animal-respecting teaching. This is his interpretation, not a settled historical result.
