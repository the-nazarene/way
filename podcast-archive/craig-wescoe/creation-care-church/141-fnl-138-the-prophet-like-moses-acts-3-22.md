## Summary

The talk asks why the apostles called Jesus "the prophet like Moses" (Acts 3:22, citing Deuteronomy 18:18), and what that title meant. Craig Wescoe answers mainly by reading passages from the Clementine Homilies, in which Peter speaks to Clement, and matching them to New Testament verses. In his reading, the "true prophet" is the one person who knows the truth fully, so knowledge of God depends on him. The Homilies describe him as sinless, faultless, foreknowing, inwardly filled with the Holy Spirit from conception, and speaking in brief declarations that anyone can understand. Wescoe says the Homilies' wording and their New Testament parallels suggest the material goes back to Jesus and the apostles. He presents this as a historical reading, not as the official view of his church.

Most of the evidence is textual comparison. Wescoe treats the Homilies as a source to be tested against scripture, not as an authority in itself. He also gives a historical sidebar on Peter: he questions the Galilean-fisherman account in Mark, favors John's account of Peter's calling, doubts that Nazareth existed, and discusses the Gospel of Peter and Justin Martyr's description of Mark as Peter's "memoir." In the Q&A he takes questions on donkeys, animal use, Paul's letters (Romans 14 and Marcion's shorter text), and whether his historical doubts threaten faith.

The talk ends with the Q&A, a closing prayer, and an announcement of the next talks on the Key of Knowledge and the Table of Demons. Wescoe does not reach a firm historical conclusion on most of the disputed points. He says that what matters most is knowing God and receiving his Spirit, not settling every detail of the historical record.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The true prophet must be tested, then trusted
Wescoe quotes Homily 1.19: people need a true prophet to tell them "things that are as they are," and must first "test the prophet by every prophetic sign," then believe him "in everything." He links this to John 6:14, where the crowd calls Jesus "the prophet who is to come into the world" after the loaves, and to 1 John 4:1 ("test the spirits") and Matthew 7:15-20 (know prophets "by their fruits"). He presents the apostles as having tested Jesus before following him without question.

### 2. Only the true prophet gives certain knowledge
Citing Homilies 2.5, 2.6 and 2.8, Wescoe argues that the good things of life (eternal life, health, understanding, immortality) cannot be had "without first knowing things as they are," and that this knowledge comes only through the true prophet. He ties this to John 17:3, John 1:18, John 14:6, and Colossians 2:8, which warns against "hollow and deceptive philosophy." He notes that Homily 2.6 compares this to Meno's paradox from Greek philosophy: you cannot seek what you do not know. Homily 2.6 allows one exception, moral knowledge, which reason can reach. Wescoe links this to the Golden Rule in Matthew 7:12. Knowledge of creation or of the end times, by contrast, cannot be reached by reason alone, according to the Homilies.

### 3. The truth is accessible to all, not only the learned
Homily 2.9 says the true prophet can be recognized even by people who are "wholly unlearned," so that "neither the barbarians might be powerless nor the Greeks unable to find him." Wescoe reads this as universal in scope and connects it to Matthew 11:25 ("you have hidden these things from the wise and learned and revealed them to little children").

### 4. Foreknowledge, origin in the beginning, and an inborn Spirit
Homily 2.10 says the true prophet knows the past and the future. Wescoe links this to John 1:1-3 and 1:14: the Word made all things, so the one who was with God "in the beginning" knows its beginning, and "God declares the end from the beginning." Homily 3.14 says the prophet was filled with the Spirit "by an inborn and ever-flowing spirit." Wescoe ties this to Matthew 1:18 and explicitly rejects "adoptionism," the view that Jesus received the Spirit at his baptism. He argues instead that Jesus had the Spirit from conception and never lost it. Revelation 22:12-13 ("the Alpha and the Omega") supports the claim that Jesus sees the beginning and the end.

### 5. Faultlessness, brief speech, and a sacrifice that ends sacrifice
Homily 3.13 says that anyone who thinks the true prophet was "sometimes" inspired and "sometimes" ignorant is deceived and is "drunken beside the altars and gorged with fat." Wescoe reads this as a polemic against animal sacrifice and against eating its fat and blood, which he says he will take up in the Table of Demons talk. He ties the faultless prophet to Hebrews 7:26-27, which contrasts Jesus, who "does not need to offer daily sacrifices," with human high priests who first atoned for their own sins. In Homily 17.6, Jesus makes "concise declarations" partly because he had limited time, and Wescoe connects this to Ecclesiastes 5:2 ("let your words be few"). He also observes that Jesus' sayings in the Gospels are brief.

### 6. Prophecy of the temple's destruction
Homily 3.14 has the prophet predict the destruction of the temple and the siege of Jerusalem. Wescoe compares this to Matthew 24:2 ("Not one stone here will be left on another"). He says the Homilies preserve this saying, and that its accuracy shows the prophet's authority.

### 7. Peter as apostle, and the question of sources
Homily 20.19 calls Peter "the true apostle of the true prophet." Wescoe stresses that Peter is an apostle of Jesus, not the true prophet himself, and he corrects a questioner on this point. He links the "sent for the salvation of the world" language to John 3:16-17. He then turns to source questions. He says the Gospel of Peter was attested early but survived only in fragments, discovered in recent decades, and focused on the tomb guards. He cites Justin Martyr, writing in the 150s, who calls the Gospel of Mark "the memoir of Peter," and he asks which Peter the sources portray. He says that Mark, as the later and non-eyewitness account, is less reliable than John on Peter's calling, and that the Nazareth question is open. He calls the Clementine Homilies an "alternate history" from what the late second-century proto-orthodox came to accept.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus received the Holy Spirit at his baptism (adoptionism).** Wescoe rejects this, arguing from Matthew 1:18 that Jesus was filled with the Spirit from conception and never lost it.
- **Jesus could sometimes be wrong or inspired only at times.** Homily 3.13 is read as condemning this view; Wescoe holds that Jesus' teaching is always true.
- **Reason and philosophy can reach full truth on their own.** Wescoe, following Homilies 2.6-2.8 and Colossians 2:8, argues that reason reaches only moral knowledge, and that the full truth depends on Christ.
- **Jesus ate fish, so animal eating is acceptable.** Wescoe says this reading of Luke 24:42-43 ignores the many passages on mercy and a plant-based diet, and that the same logic could justify animal exploitation (the donkey example).
- **Peter was a Galilean fisherman.** Wescoe questions this, favoring John's account over Mark's. He also corrects a questioner who called Peter "the true prophet"; Peter is an apostle of the true prophet, Jesus.
