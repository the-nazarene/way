## Summary

Wescoe asks what Paul means in 1 Corinthians 10:21 when he says one cannot share "the table of the Lord" and "the table of demons," and what "the table of demons" concretely refers to. He answers mainly from the Clementine Homilies (Homilies 4, 7, 8, 9, 11, 12, 13 as numbered in newadvent.org/fathers/0808.htm), which he treats as early Christian teaching that the church has lost. In his reading, the table of demons is eating food offered to idols, carrion, animals that were suffocated or killed by beasts, and blood. He links this to the golden rule and to the Jerusalem Council decree of Acts 15, which he reads (via Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 3.12.14) as a "burden" of abstaining from idol meat, blood, and "porneias." He traces the origin of flesh-eating to the Nephilim of Genesis 6 as described in 1 Enoch 7, and argues that the homilies make eating flesh and blood a route to demonic possession (Homilies 8.19-20, 9.9-10).

The evidence is almost entirely the Homilies, with Genesis, Numbers, Isaiah, Job, and several New Testament verses (including Philippians 3:19, Ephesians 4:27, James 4:7, Matthew 12:45 and 17:21, and Acts 8 on Simon) read alongside them. Wescoe presents this as "what Jesus taught" without showing a direct saying of Jesus on the table of demons; the chain runs through Peter, Clement, and James. He does not cite Gospel passages on eating or table fellowship as the basis of the teaching.

The talk closes with the live chat. Wescoe answers viewers on fireworks, animal welfare, whether one may sit at a table where animal products are served (he says he "would not fault someone" who sits there without eating), and Clement's dating (he places Clement in the mid-first century, as a disciple of Peter). He ends by announcing next week's three-year retrospective and the new Zoom fellowship link, then closes in prayer.

## Key Teachings

### The table of demons defined (Clementine Homilies 7.4; Acts 15)
Wescoe reads Homilies 7.4 as defining the table of demons: abstaining "from food offered to idols," "from dead carcasses," "from animals which have been suffocated or caught by wild beasts," and "from blood." He says this includes slaughterhouse animals (killed by gas) and fish (suffocated out of water), and that milk containing blood is not exempt merely because it meets a USDA threshold: "God's standard is abstain completely from these things." He connects this to the golden rule, quoting the homily's "do to his neighbor those good things he wishes for himself," and then to the Acts 15 decree, which he reads through Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.12.14) as the "earliest version" of Acts 15:28. He calls this decree "James's letter" and says it defines "walking by the Holy Spirit" as abstaining from the table of demons and doing to others as one would have them do to you.

### Flesh-eating begins with the Nephilim (Genesis 6; 1 Enoch 7; Homilies 8.16-17)
Wescoe reads Genesis 6:1-4 as the story of the sons of God and the Nephilim, then cites Homilies 8.16 and 1 Enoch 7 for the claim that the giants, unsatisfied by manna, "tasted flesh" and then human flesh. He cites Homilies 8.17 on pollution: the blood-shedding defiled the air, bred diseases and "poison darting" creatures, and shortened human life. He ties this to Genesis 6:11-13 ("the earth was filled with violence") and argues that eating flesh is itself the violence the flood story describes. He notes he has previously done a live talk on 1 Enoch. He also draws a modern analogy to radioactive contamination of fish, which is his own illustration and not a source claim.

### Demons enter through the table (Homilies 8.19-20, 9.9-10, 9.15, 11.15)
This is the core of the talk. Wescoe reads Homilies 8.19-20 as saying demons "have no power over you unless you sit at their table," so that eating flesh, blood, or sacrifice is worship and invites possession. He cites Homilies 9.9 (food "admitted into your bodies by your own hands," mixing demons with souls) and 9.10 (demons seek bodies to satisfy appetites for meat, drink and sex; fasting and abstinence help drive them out). He reads Job as a parallel: Satan has power over Job only within a limit. He supports the "no power without a foothold" point with James 4:7 and Ephesians 4:27. He describes spirits as the disembodied remains of the giants, a point he attributes to the Enoch material.

### Simon Magus (Homilies 4.4; Acts 8:9-11, 18-21)
Wescoe reads Homilies 4.4, in which Simon "under pretense of a banquet, having slain an ox and given them to eat of it, he infected them with various diseases and subjected them to demons." He says this matches the Simon of Acts 8, and compares the sickness to Numbers 11. He uses this as a second example that feasting on slaughtered flesh brings demonic affliction.

### Separation at the table and the Lord's table (Homilies 13.4; Acts 2:42)
Homilies 13.4 is read as forbidding shared meals with Gentiles until they are baptized, with the baptismal formula of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Wescoe says that only after one abstains from the table of demons and is baptized can one "sit at the table of the Lord." He explicitly rejects a symbolic reading in which one eats a cracker and grape juice while still eating animal products. He reads Acts 2:42 (shared breaking of bread and meeting common needs) as a model of table fellowship.

### Peter's diet (Homilies 12.6; Acts 10:14)
Wescoe cites Homilies 12.6, where Peter describes living on "bread and olives and rarely pot herbs," and says Peter and Andrew were raised on this diet. He cites Acts 10:14 ("I have never eaten anything defiled or polluted") as confirmation, and refers to an earlier talk on veganism among the apostles.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That "sitting at the table of demons" is a normal part of church life.** Wescoe argues the church has "covered up" the early teaching and "gotten it completely backwards," and that eating animal products at church functions contradicts the apostolic teaching.
- **That the USDA's permitted level of blood in milk makes it acceptable.** He says God's standard is complete abstention, not a regulatory threshold.
- **That the Lord's table is satisfied by a cracker and grape juice after a meal of meat.** He says the Lord's table requires full abstention and baptism, not a token ritual.
- **That the table of demons is only idol worship in a narrow sense.** He reads it broadly to include carrion, strangled animals, blood, and sacrificial meat generally.
