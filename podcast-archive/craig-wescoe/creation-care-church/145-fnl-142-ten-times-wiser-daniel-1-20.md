## Summary

The talk asks why Daniel and three companions (Meshach, Shadrach and Abednego) refused the king of Babylon's food and wine in Daniel 1, and why the text says they were "ten times better" than the court magicians (the speaker renders this as "wiser"). Craig Wescoe argues that the "king's Delicacies" were animal flesh, that eating flesh is "deceptive food" contrary to God's wisdom, and that the wisdom to refuse it comes from God's plant-based diet in Genesis 1:29-31. He supports this with Proverbs 23:1-3 and 23:20, which he reads as warning against the king's flesh and wine, and with Genesis 9:4 on blood as life. He then links "the wisdom of God" to Jesus: through James 3:17 and 1:5, Proverbs 3:13-18, Matthew 7 and Matthew 12:42, he argues that Jesus is the wisdom of God incarnate, and that following him means living out the same peaceable, non-violent diet.

The talk is a sermon-style lecture with a live Q&A. Wescoe asks listeners to see the connection between Daniel and a plant-based diet as plain in the text, while also admitting that some links (such as the Nazirite explanation for the wine) are his own best guess. The Q&A covers whether the king's wine was animal-derived, the documentary *Game Changers*, *Forks Over Knives*, masculinity and protein, and whether "Lord, Lord" is enough. It ends with an announcement that the next live talk will address Psalm 147:9 (whether God answers animals' prayers) and a closing prayer for vegans who do not yet know Jesus.

## Key Teachings

### Daniel 1: the king's food was flesh, and refusing it was a stand for God's wisdom
Wescoe reads Daniel 1:1-21 closely. Nebuchadnezzar besieges Jerusalem and takes young men, including Daniel, to train them in Chaldean learning. Daniel "purposed in his heart" not to defile himself with the king's food and wine, and asks for vegetables and water for ten days. At the end, the four are healthier-looking, and the king finds them "10 times better" than his magicians and astrologers. Wescoe's main claim is that the food was animal flesh and the wine was alcohol, and that the story shows God's wisdom in diet. He treats the ten-day test as evidence that the plant diet was healthier, and he notes that Daniel continued this diet for years (a point made in a chat comment he endorses, not from the biblical text itself).

### Proverbs 23: "Delicacies" means flesh
Wescoe reads Proverbs 23:1-3 ("do not desire his Delicacies for they are deceptive food") against the Daniel story, and says the warning is not about gluttony or cravings in general. He then reads Proverbs 23:20 ("gluttonous Eaters of meat") and claims the Hebrew word translated "meat" (*basar*, which he spells "baser") "always means flesh in the Old Testament." This is a word-level claim the speaker gives without a lexicon; the group should check it. He concludes that the king's Delicacies are animal flesh and the wine is the accompanying drink.

### Genesis 9:4 and 1:29-31: blood is life, and God's diet is plants
Genesis 9:4 ("you shall not eat flesh with its life that is its blood") is taken as showing that blood is life, so eating flesh means eating the life that God reserves. Wescoe then turns to Genesis 1:29-31, where God gives "every herb that yields seed" and "every tree whose fruit yields seed" as food. He says this is the only place in the Bible where God calls a diet "very good," and that it involves no killing. He also says the serpent's deception in Genesis 3 was an invitation to eat what God forbade, and that this forbidden food was animal flesh (referring the audience to an earlier live talk on the forbidden fruit for the argument).

### Wisdom from above and the fruit of the Spirit: James, Proverbs, Ephesians
Wescoe reads James 3:17-18, the list of qualities of wisdom from above ("first pure, then peaceable, gentle..."), and applies each to diet. Purity means not defiling oneself, peaceableness means not committing violence, gentleness is impossible in killing an animal, and mercy is what Jesus asks for. He reads James 1:5 to argue that wisdom must be asked of God and then lived out. He reads Proverbs 3:13-18, where wisdom is "a tree of life" and "her paths are Paths of Peace," and contrasts it with the forbidden tree and with violence. He reads Ephesians 3:10 (the church is to make God's "manifold wisdom" known to the principalities and powers) as meaning that diet and the treatment of animals are part of that witness.

### Matthew 7 and 12: fruit, the narrow way, and Jesus as the wisdom of God
Wescoe reads the end of the Sermon on the Mount. He takes Matthew 7:12 (the golden rule) as the summary of "the law and the prophets," and Matthew 7:13-14 (narrow gate) as the difficult way. Matthew 7:15-20 ("by their fruits you will know them") is applied to diet: bearing "bad fruit" means eating animals. Matthew 7:21-23 ("not everyone who says to me Lord Lord") is used against the idea that confessing Jesus as Lord is enough. He reads Matthew 7:24-27 (wise and foolish builders) and Matthew 7:28-29 (Jesus teaches "as one having Authority"), then Matthew 12:42 ("one greater than Solomon is here"). His conclusion is that Solomon prayed for and taught the wisdom of God, and that Jesus is that wisdom embodied, so his words are the rule for diet and conduct.

### The Nazirite explanation for the wine (speculative)
When asked whether the king's wine might have been mixed with animal products, Wescoe says the text does not say the four were Nazirites, though it "does suggest that they were set apart." He offers two possibilities for abstaining from wine: that Nazirites avoid grapes, which he calls a symbol of blood, or that the wine is alcohol and he advises abstinence on the grounds that a first drink weakens judgment. He calls these "front runner" explanations of his own, not a conclusion from the text. He also says the Nazirite example includes Yeshua.
