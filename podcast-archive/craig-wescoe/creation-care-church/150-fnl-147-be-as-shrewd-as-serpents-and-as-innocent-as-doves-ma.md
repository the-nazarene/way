## Summary

Wescoe asks what Jesus meant by "be as shrewd as serpents and as innocent as doves" (Matthew 10:16). He argues that the two animals are symbols that recur across scripture. The serpent stands for deception, which he ties to the serpent in Genesis 3 and to the "Father of Lies" in John 8:44. The dove stands for the Holy Spirit, which he links to the dove at Jesus's baptism in John 1:29-34 and to worship "in spirit and truth" in John 4:24. On his reading, the disciple should know the deceptions without using them, and should reveal them "through the light of Truth" while bearing the fruit of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22-23; Luke 6:43-45).

Most of the talk is a chain of proof-texts. Wescoe reads Genesis 1 as God's original plan, in which humans are made in God's image, given dominion, and assigned a plant-based diet (Genesis 1:29-30). He reads the serpent's promise in Genesis 3 ("you will be like God") as the original deception, and he reads Ezekiel 28 as a corruption of wisdom through violence and pride. He also uses James 4:6-7, the temptation of Jesus in Matthew 4:8-11, Luke 12:42-44, and Hebrews 1:1-3 to argue that the proper human response is to submit to God and to be a faithful steward of creation. His argument turns explicitly toward animal ethics: dominion is not license to kill animals, and the speaker says "the Animals" should receive the same compassion.

The second half is a live Q&A. Wescoe answers comments on whether God created evil (he says God created good, and evil entered through disobedience), on whether Genesis 1 permits eating animals, and on whether any single passage forbids eating animal flesh. He admits there is no single explicit prohibition and points to a cumulative biblical case, including Genesis 9:3-4 and Jeremiah 7-8. The talk closes with a prayer for the kingdom "as it is in Heaven" and an announcement of the next session in two weeks.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The serpent is the deceiver, and the dove is the Spirit
Wescoe's central move is a symbol key built from scripture. The serpent of Genesis 3 is "cunning" and promises that eating the forbidden fruit will make humans "like God." He then identifies this figure with the devil through John 8:44 ("a murderer from the beginning," "a liar and the father of it"). The dove is linked to the Spirit at Jesus's baptism (John 1:29-34) and to worship "in spirit and truth" (John 4:24). From this he concludes that "shrewd" in Matthew 10:16 means knowing the deception without practicing it. He also says the word here is better translated "shrewd" than "wise," and that this is a deceptive kind of wisdom, not the wisdom of God.

### 2. Genesis 1 as God's original plan: image, dominion and plant-based food
He reads Genesis 1:26-28 as God making humans "in our image and likeness" with dominion over fish, birds, cattle and "every creeping thing." He then reads Genesis 1:29-30 as assigning plants, "every green herb," as food for humans and animals alike. He calls this "an entirely plant-based diet" and presents dominion as stewardship rather than a right to kill. The serpent's offer, on this reading, is an invitation to reject God's design and become "your own God."

### 3. Ezekiel 28 and the corruption of wisdom
Wescoe reads Ezekiel 28:11-17, addressed to the king of Tyre, as describing a figure who was "perfect in your ways" until "iniquity was found in you," who became "filled with violence within," and who "corrupted your wisdom for the sake of splendor." He links this to Isaiah 11:6 and 11:9 (the holy mountain where "there is no harming nor destroying") and argues that the deception "you will become wise" is the opposite of the truth, because real wisdom is the image of God already given to humans. Note that the speaker moves between passages quickly and does not discuss the identity of the king of Tyre or the historical setting of Ezekiel.

### 4. Obedience, resisting the devil and the faithful steward
Using James 4:6-7 ("submit to God, resist the devil"), Matthew 4:8-11 (Jesus's reply, "Away with you, Satan"), and Luke 12:42-44 (the faithful and wise steward), he argues that the believer resists temptation by submitting to God's instructions, including those on animals and food. He treats the story of Cain (Genesis 4:6-7) as a parallel: Cain was told that if he did well he would be accepted, and sin "lies at the door," but he chose his own way. Ephesians 4:24-27 ("do not give place to the devil") is used in the same way.

### 5. Evil as corruption, and the question of God's creation
In the Q&A, Wescoe answers a question about whether God created evil. He says "everything God created was very good" (Genesis 1:31), that evil is potential in the freedom to choose, and that it became actual through disobedience (Genesis 3) and through the sons of God in Genesis 6, which he calls the second entry of sin, death and cursing. He also says that Jesus, as "the Lamb of God," represents the innocence that humans were meant to share. He is explicit that this is his own view, and he refers listeners to a separate talk on the problem of evil.

### 6. The biblical case for veganism is cumulative
Asked why God never explicitly forbade eating animals, he concedes "there's not sort of any one passage that says it is a sin to eat animal flesh." He argues instead that many passages hint at it: Genesis 9:3-4 (life is in the blood), the statement that God "doesn't desire" it, the call to mercy and compassion, the plant-based design of Genesis 1, and a claim that those who eat flesh "die in a grave of craving" (Wescoe does not give a reference for this last point in the talk). He points listeners to a website with "over a hundred passages" he says support veganism and to other talks. In answer to a comment on Noah, he says either that God "meets us where we're at" in allowing sacrifice, or that Jeremiah 7-8 shows the lying scribes inserting sacrifice; either view is acceptable if the conclusion is not harming creation.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Genesis 1:28 gives humans license to use and eat animals.** Wescoe says that critics cite this verse, but that the very next verse assigns plants, not animals, as food, so dominion is stewardship under God's design.
- **God created evil.** Wescoe says God created everything "very good." Evil entered through the freedom to choose disobedience (Genesis 3), and it became actual evil when humans brought it into the world.
- **The serpent's offer is just harmless self-improvement.** He reads the offer ("you will be like God") as the original lie, because humans already bear God's image and the offer asks them to reject it.
