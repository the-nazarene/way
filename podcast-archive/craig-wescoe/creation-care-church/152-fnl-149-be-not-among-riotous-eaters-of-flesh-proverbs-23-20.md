## Summary

Craig, presenting a live Friday talk for Creation Care Church, asks what Proverbs 23:20 means when it warns against being "among riotous eaters of flesh," how that principle applies today, and what to do about gatherings built around flesh-eating. He opens by arguing that *basar* means flesh specifically, not food in general, and that the Hebrew adjective (*zalal*, Strong's 2151) describes a rebellious, prodigal character more than overeating. He supports this by comparing other uses of the word in Proverbs 28:7, Jeremiah 15:18-19 and Deuteronomy 21:20.

The main argument is that flesh-eating is a form of disobedience and, in the early church, a form of demonic worship. Craig reads 1 Corinthians 10:21 ("the table of demons") alongside Clementine Homilies 7.4, 8.19-20 and 13.4, the Jerusalem Council decree as quoted by Irenaeus (attributed to James), and the origin story of flesh-eating in 1 Enoch 7 and Genesis 6. He concludes that the earliest believers abstained from flesh and avoided shared tables with eaters of flesh. He presents this as his own reading of the evidence, leaves the level of separation to each listener (sitting at the table without eating, or refusing to sit at all, is for each person to decide), and stresses that the one point he regards as clear is not to partake.

The Q&A covers practical cases such as workplace meals, caring for family members who eat meat, and whether to take a "liberation pledge" (wrapping a fork around the wrist to refuse shared tables). Craig says these are case-by-case decisions. He ends with prayer, a reminder of a replay of an earlier talk, and an invitation to a Zoom fellowship.

## Key Teachings

### 1. "Basar" means flesh, and the adjective describes character, not quantity
Craig argues that *basar* "always means flesh in particular," so the verse forbids mixing with people who eat flesh, not with people who eat too much food. He says the adjective translated "gluttonous" (King James: "riotous") is better rendered as rebellious or prodigal, based on Strong's Hebrew 2151 (*zalal*). He points to Proverbs 28:7, where the companion of such eaters "shames his father," and to the prodigal-son framing, in which returning to the Father means repenting of flesh-eating.

### 2. The "table of demons" and the Lord's table
Starting from 1 Corinthians 10:21 ("You cannot drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons, too"), Craig says the verse is not explained in 1 Corinthians, so its meaning has to be recovered from other early sources. He leans on Clementine Homilies 7.4, which lists abstaining "from the table of devils, that is, from food offered to idols, from dead carcasses... and from blood." He refers listeners to a separate talk (FNL #140, "The Table of Demons") for more detail.

### 3. The Jerusalem decree and the golden rule (attributed to James)
Craig quotes Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 3.12.4, as preserving an early form of the Jerusalem Council decree, which he says James spoke ("This is James speaking, James the brother of Jesus who Jesus put over the church in Jerusalem"). He reads the decree as requiring abstention from meats offered to idols, from blood, and from *pornea*, paired with the golden rule. He then argues that the golden rule must extend to animals, since no one wants to be killed and eaten.

### 4. Flesh-eating as the origin of demonic possession (Clementine Homilies and 1 Enoch)
Craig reads Clementine Homilies 8.5 and 8.19-20, which link flesh-eating and blood-drinking to the giants born of fallen angels in 1 Enoch 7 and Genesis 6. He says these texts teach that flesh-eating "invites demonic possession" and that sitting at the table of flesh-eaters means partaking in demon worship. He also argues that God gave manna as food that was not meant for killing, and that the people who craved flesh were buried at Kibroth Hattaavah. He cites this manna and craving story as "Numbers chapter 4" (see the Scripture Citations table for a note).

### 5. Early separate tables and the "pure table fellowship"
Craig reads Clementine Homilies 13.4, in which Peter tells Clement's mother that they cannot eat with her until she is baptized and shares their way of life. He presents this as evidence that the earliest church did not share meals with outsiders and that abstaining from flesh was part of discipleship. He ties this to Acts 2:42 ("the breaking of bread"), which he reads as pure table fellowship. He also says that some people reject this material because it is controversial.

### 6. Application and personal choice
Craig presents the evidence as a basis for individual decision. He describes three options: abstain from eating flesh and sit at the table; refuse the table altogether (the "liberation pledge" with a fork on the wrist); or sit at the table as a form of outreach, as one participant described. He repeatedly says he is not telling everyone what to do and that the decision should be made prayerfully and case by case. For someone changing to plant-based eating, he advises putting safeguards in place against temptation before anything else.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Basar" in Proverbs 23:20 just means food, so the verse is about food in general.** Craig says *basar* always means flesh.
- **"Gluttonous" means overeating.** Craig says the adjective describes a rebellious or prodigal character, not the quantity eaten.
- **"1 Corinthians 10:21's 'table of demons' is a vague phrase about not worshipping God."** Craig says it refers to flesh-eating and that the early sources (Clementine Homilies) explain it.
- **"Jesus made all food clean," or "Jesus ate fish," settles the diet question.** In the Q&A Craig says people use these passages to avoid the question and that the early church texts should be read alongside them, without fully rebutting the verses.
- **The Jerusalem Council was only about purity and ritual.** Craig reads the decree as including a moral duty toward animals through the golden rule.
