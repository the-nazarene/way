## Summary

The talk asks what the Gospel of James (also called the Protoevangelium of James, or the Infancy Gospel of James) tells us about the birth of Mary and of Jesus, and whether it is an early source for the birth stories in Matthew and Luke. The presenter reads the text chapter by chapter in the Roberts-Donaldson translation, pairing each passage with Hebrew Bible and New Testament parallels. He reads it as the story of Anna and Joachim's barren-then-blessed marriage, Mary's upbringing in the temple and her fed-by-an-angel diet, Joseph's reluctant guardianship, the annunciation, the trial by bitter water, the census journey, the cave birth, the star and the Magi, Herod's killing of the children, and the murder of Zacharias.

Wescoe argues that the text is early and possibly authentic. He says it was likely written in Hebrew for a Hebrew-literate audience, that it predates the 130s, and that Justin Martyr used it in the 150s, citing its cave birth, which Matthew and Luke do not mention. He also says that a writer in the 170s quotes it while claiming to rely on inherited sources (the captions garble that writer's name). He leans toward attributing the main narrative to James, Jesus's half-brother, and dates Jesus's birth to roughly 6 to 2 BCE on the basis of Herod's death. He presents these as his own reading of the evidence, not settled results.

The talk ends with an open chat Q&A. Questions cover whether James is Jesus's brother, whether the Quran draws on this literature, the dragon and woman in Revelation 12 (which Wescoe reads as referring to Mary), and whether the text mentions Egypt. Wescoe notes that Egypt appears in Luke and in the Acts of Pilate but not in James. The session closes with a preview of next week's talk on Genesis 2 and the seventh day, and a prayer.

## Key Teachings

### Date, authorship and early attestation
Wescoe says the text was probably first composed in a Semitic language, most likely Hebrew, for an audience steeped in the Hebrew Bible, and that its Hebraisms support this. He dates it before the 130s, when Greek had not yet become the main language of Christian texts. He takes Justin Martyr's use in the 150s as the key external witness, because Justin says Jesus was born in a cave, a detail found only in James. He says the 170s writer also quotes the text but claims to rely on sources handed down to him. He argues that if James wrote it, it would be among the earliest Christian texts, and possibly one of only two attested texts that could have been composed during Jesus's lifetime, since the canonical gospels all narrate his death. He says the Oracles of Matthew could be a similar early case. He adds that the two chapters attributed to Joseph's voice (chapters 18-19) are a separate section, and that the narrator otherwise identifies himself as James.

### Mary's birth, dedication and diet
The opening chapters are modelled on the barren-wife stories. Wescoe links Joachim and Anna to Abraham and Sarah (Genesis 18:14; Genesis 21:1-3) and Anna's vow to Hannah's dedication of Samuel (1 Samuel 1:27-28). Anna keeps her daughter's room free of "anything common or unclean." Wescoe connects that wording to Acts 10:14 and to Peter's diet in the Clementine Homilies, which he describes as seeds and herbs. He says that when Mary is "fed by the hand of an Angel," the reference is to manna, which he ties to Psalm 78:24-25. He reads this as a picture of an undefiled diet with no animal products, and he returns to this point in the closing prayer, citing Genesis 1:29. Wescoe presents the diet reading as his own interpretation.

### Annunciation, Joseph's dilemma and the ordeal
Joseph is chosen by lot as Mary's guardian, and he is a widower who already has children. After Mary is found pregnant, Joseph's dilemma and the angelic dream closely follow Matthew 1:18-21. The annunciation to Mary repeats the wording of Luke 1:35 and Matthew 1:21 with small differences. Wescoe notes that the text calls Joseph "husband" in the Matthean sense but treats the marriage more like a guardianship. When the scribe Annas accuses the couple, the priest makes them drink the bitter water. Wescoe ties this to the ordeal of Numbers 5:19-24, which the couple passes, and he reads the episode as a public divine vindication of Mary's innocence.

### The census, the cave, the star and the Magi
Wescoe connects the census to Luke 2:1-3 and the birth in Bethlehem to Luke 2:7. The cave is three miles outside Bethlehem, which he says makes a cave-stable plausible. He describes a cosmic stillness in which the animals, workers and shepherd stop and look up, and he treats it as a sign in the sky. He reads this alongside Luke 2:8-9 and Matthew 2:2, and says that the text never identifies the sign as a star, though a star is a reasonable inference. The Magi visit, with Herod's inquiry and the killing of the boys (Matthew 2:1-16), is followed by the flight of Mary and Elizabeth with their children. Wescoe reads Herod's massacre as a parallel to Pharaoh's order in Exodus 1:22, presenting Jesus as a prophet like Moses.

### Elizabeth, John and the murder of Zacharias
Elizabeth is the mother of John the Baptist, and the two pregnancies run in parallel. Wescoe links the leaping child to Luke 1:41. He says that Mary and Elizabeth were descendants of David who sewed the temple's scarlet curtain, which may be the veil torn at Jesus's death. Herod seeks John, and Zacharias refuses to reveal where the child is hidden. Zacharias is then murdered at the temple. Simeon, warned by the Holy Spirit, is chosen to succeed him, and Wescoe connects this to Simeon's words in Luke 2:25-32.

### Chronology: the birth between 6 and 2 BCE
Wescoe reasons that Herod died about 2 BCE, according to what he calls most historical sources, and that the events of the text, including the massacre and the flight, must end before that. He concludes that Jesus was born roughly between 6 and 2 BCE. He admits the dating is approximate for events about twenty years older than the year counted from the Common Era. In the Q&A he says a proposed Revelation-based date of 9 BC would be plausible within this range, but he has not studied that claim closely.

### Revelation 12 and the chat questions
Responding to a chat question, Wescoe reads Revelation 12:1-5 and agrees with a participant that the woman and the dragon refer to Mary and the child. He presents this as a point of agreement in the chat, not a demonstration.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Popular belief:** the birth stories of Jesus are known only from Matthew and Luke, and the extra-canonical material is later embellishment. **Correction:** Wescoe argues that James is an early text, attested by Justin Martyr in the 150s, and that Matthew and Luke draw on a shared source close to it.
- **Popular belief:** the cave birth is part of the gospel tradition. **Correction:** he notes that the cave is found in James, not in Matthew or Luke, which is how he traces James's use by Justin.
- **Popular belief (implied in the Q&A):** Joseph was simply Jesus's father. **Correction:** Wescoe reads James's Joseph as a widowed guardian with children from an earlier marriage, which also makes James the half-brother of Jesus.
