## Summary

Wescoe opens with five questions about Genesis 2:2-3: what the Creator's rest on the seventh day signifies, why that day was set apart as holy, why the Sabbath is called a sign of the covenant, how one enters that rest, and whether animals can enter it too. He reads Genesis 1 as a design in which every creature receives blessing and a diet of plants, with the humans given "dominion." He then reads the Sabbath commandment in Exodus 20 and 31:13 and the Noahic covenant in Genesis 9:12-17 as covenants that include "every living creature," so that the rest extends to animals.

His main evidence for reading the covenant as nonviolent comes from the prophets and the Gospels. He takes Jesus's temple action in John 2 as a protest against animal sacrifice and reads Matthew 9:13 ("I desire mercy, not sacrifice") as a quotation of Hosea 6:6. He pairs this with Hosea 2:18 and Isaiah 11, which he takes as promises that weapons of violence will be removed. He argues that "entering the rest" means ceasing from works contrary to God's will (Hebrews 4:9-11), being born again or from above (John 3:3), and having the law written on the heart (Jeremiah 31:31-34), with love and the fruit of the Spirit (John 13:34-35; Galatians 5:22-25) as the visible result. He presents the Ark of the Covenant's mercy seat as the place where God speaks to a people reconciled to him.

The talk ends with a live Q&A in which Wescoe answers chat questions about the Ark, dominion, the "opposite of love," and vegan practice. He reads a fragment attributed to Papias, which he says reads the six days as referring to Christ in the whole church. He closes with prayer and a reminder of the group's fellowship, prayer meeting and book club. He states that the views are his own research and that Sabbath observance is "between you and God." The talk is one speaker's reading and is not presented as a consensus claim.

## Key Teachings

### The Seventh Day as a Covenant Sign That Includes Animals
Wescoe reads Genesis 2:2-3 alongside Exodus 20:8-11, which lists animals among those who must rest on the Sabbath. He argues that because God blessed the animals in Genesis 1:22 and 1:28, their inclusion in the rest command shows they are "very much part of this created world." Exodus 31:13 gives the Sabbath as "a sign between me and you throughout your generations," which he reads as a covenant relationship that the weekly Shabbat renews. In Genesis 9:12-17 he says the covenant is "between me and you and every living creature," and so "it's humans and the animals," lasting "forever." He concludes that the Sabbath is a weekly reminder of a covenant God designed for all flesh.

### A Plant-Based Original Design and the Blood Prohibition
Wescoe reads Genesis 1:29-30 as God's assignment of food: "every herb that yields seed" and "every tree whose fruit yields seed" for humans, and "every green herb" for the animals. He says this makes the original diet "entirely plant-based for every creature." He reads Genesis 9:4 ("you shall not eat flesh with its life, that is its blood") as a later restriction, and he links Exodus 20's "you shall not murder" to not killing for food. He treats the Genesis 2:17 prohibition on the tree of knowledge as the one command whose breaking "introduc[ed] death into this world." These are his readings of the text; he does not cite other scholars for them.

### Temple Sacrifice Rejected: Mercy, Not Sacrifice
Wescoe reads John 2:13-16 as Jesus driving out animal sellers and "liberat[ing]" the animals, and he calls the scene a protest against sacrifice as commerce. He argues against the view that Jesus was only angry about overcharging. His evidence is that Luke does not mention the money changers, and that the cleansing appears in all four Gospels. He then reads Matthew 9:13 as Jesus telling people to "go and learn what this means" and quotes Hosea 6:6, noting that Hosea 6:7 mentions Adam's transgression of the covenant. He concludes that God "desires mercy" and that sacrifice is not what he wants.

### Rest as Ceasing From Contrary Works, and Being Born Again
Using Hebrews 4:9-11, Wescoe argues that the rest God entered is one believers "must enter" by ceasing "from our works," which he defines as the things "contrary to the will of Elohim." He equates this with repentance, "to turn around and to stop doing something," and with allowing God's spirit to work through the believer. He links it to John 3:3, which he reads through the alternative rendering "born from above," as the new birth that makes this rest possible. Only in this section does he speak of the believer's rest as present reality, not solely future hope.

### The Ark, the Mercy Seat, and Nonviolence
Wescoe reads Exodus 25:17-22 as describing the mercy seat between the cherubim, where God speaks to Israel and where the Ten Commandments (including the Sabbath) are kept. He calls the mercy seat "symbolic" of the covenant relationship restored in the believer. He contrasts it with Noah's ark, which he says people often confuse with the Ark of the Covenant. He reads Hosea 2:18 and Isaiah 11:9 (with Isaiah 11:6's "little child") as promises that weapons of violence will be removed and that "they shall not hurt nor destroy." He describes this future as a place of "nonviolence, no killing, no bloodshed."

### Law on the Heart, Love, and the Fruit of the Spirit
Wescoe reads Jeremiah 31:31-34 as a shift from laws written on stone to laws written on the heart, so that obedience comes from desire and not from fear of consequences. He pairs this with John 13:34-35 ("love one another") and Galatians 5:22-25, which he uses to define what love and the fruit of the Spirit look like in practice. He reads the fruit of the Spirit as the positive opposite of the forbidden fruit: love instead of hatred, kindness instead of cruelty, and self-control instead of craving "cheese or... bacon." He cites Proverbs 12:10 as contrast between righteous care for animals and the "cruel" acts of the wicked. He frames dominion as "exercising dominion in his image and likeness, which is that of love."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus cleansed the temple only because of overcharging.** Wescoe argues the protest was against sacrifice itself, citing Matthew 9:13 and noting that Luke omits the money changers.
- **Noah's ark and the Ark of the Covenant are the same thing.** Wescoe says people often conflate them, and that the Ark of the Covenant is the one that holds the commandments and the mercy seat.
- **"Dominion" licenses people to do what they want.** He says dominion is the opposite of this and means ruling in God's image and likeness, which is love.
- **God's commands are a burden.** He argues they are "a blessing," and that people who think otherwise "don't realize what you're missing out on."
- **Veganism is too hard.** He says it is hard only "if you're thinking, 'Oh, I really want those things,'" and that most people adjust after a while.
- **Love and hate are opposites.** He qualifies this in the chat, saying "hate might not be the perfect word" and that indifference to suffering is one way of not showing love.
