## Summary

Host Craig Wescoe asks whether humanity is "reaping the violence we sow toward animals," meaning the harm done to animals in farming, egg and dairy production, laboratories, and the fur, leather, and fashion trades. He answers that "when we look around in the world the answer is clearly yes," and argues that the violence has become so normalized that the same methods later get used on people, with the health effects of animal products offered as one form of that reaping.

Wescoe's method is an extended proof-text chain rather than historical or textual analysis. He starts with the sowing-and-reaping sayings in Galatians 6:7-9, 2 Corinthians 9:6-8, and Luke 6:38, then moves through Proverbs, Job, Matthew 6 and 7, Hosea 10, Galatians 5, and Jeremiah 17. He reads "good works" as whatever God himself has declared good, pointing to Genesis 1:29-31 (the herb-and-fruit diet and the instruction to tend the animals) and to Hosea 6:6 ("I desire mercy not sacrifice"). He reads Daniel 1 as evidence that abstaining from animal food yields better health.

The talk ends with a live Q&A in which Wescoe responds to chat comments on Isaiah 58:6, Habakkuk 2:17, and the "gas chamber" parallel. He says the timing of reaping is left to God, and closes with a prayer and a vision of the kingdom in which no one harms or destroys across God's holy mountain.

## Key Teachings

### Sowing and reaping as a moral law, with God's good works as the content

Wescoe grounds the talk in the agricultural image of sowing and reaping. He says "a large part of the audience is gardeners," so the image fits how people in the Bible would have understood it, and he treats it as a general principle that "is used over and over again throughout the Bible." He cites Galatians 6:7-9, 2 Corinthians 9:6-8 (which he says ties reaping to "good works"), and Luke 6:38, where the measure you use is the measure returned. He notes that Luke 6:36 ("be merciful just as your father also is merciful") is the seed he links to that measure. He stresses that we should not "determine for ourselves what those good works are," and that God must define them.

### Genesis 1 as God's definition of good works, including diet

Wescoe reads Genesis 1:29-31 as God declaring the original diet and the original care for animals "very good," and so as the baseline for good works. He argues that humans were placed in the garden to "tend and keep it" and to oversee the animals "in His image and likeness," so mercy toward animals is part of the original commission. He pairs this with John 15:16-17 ("I chose you... that you should go and bear fruit") and says Jesus, "the word of God made flesh," was pointing back to these original instructions.

### Mercy over sacrifice and the "fruit" test

Wescoe presents Hosea 6:6 ("I desire mercy not sacrifice") as something Jesus quoted, and uses it to argue that God does not want animals slaughtered and offered to him. He develops this through Proverbs 12:10 ("a righteous man regards the life of his animal"), Proverbs 11:17-18, and Matthew 7:16-18 (know them by their fruits). He treats the good fruit of mercy as the test of a true follower, and connects it to Galatians 5:22-23 (love, joy, peace, kindness, gentleness, self-control).

### Reward comes from God, not from recognition

Drawing on Matthew 6:1-4, Genesis 26:12 (Isaac sowed and reaped a hundredfold), and Colossians 3:23-24, Wescoe says good works should be done in secret for God, not for public recognition, and that God is the one who rewards. He uses the story of Joseph and Potiphar's wife (Genesis 39; he says "Piper's wife") as an example of prioritizing God's view over one's reputation. He says the timing of harvest is left to God, and the answer to "compassion fatigue" is to keep sowing "in tears" until the reaping in joy (Psalm 126:5).

### Language and normalization: "harvest," "meat packing," and calling animals "someone"

In the Q&A, Wescoe argues that euphemisms sustain animal abuse. He objects to calling animal slaughter a "harvest" ("you harvest plants you don't Harvest animals"), to "meat packing plant" for slaughterhouse, and to saying "something" instead of "someone" for a shot animal. He says children are taught these words early, and that changing the words helps restore the right perspective. He also links the slaughter of pigs in gas chambers to the later use of gas chambers on people, which he presents as "an unfortunate consequence" of normalized violence.

### Health, wisdom, and Daniel 1

In reply to a chat question about physical disease, Wescoe says Daniel abstained from certain foods and was "seen as healthier and 10 times wiser," and treats that as evidence that living by God's wisdom leads to better outcomes. He then draws on Proverbs 3:13-18 ("her ways are ways of pleasantness") and Philippians 3:19 (whose God is their belly). The health claim rests on the Daniel narrative as he reads it; he offers no medical or scientific sources in the transcript.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Animal use is ordinary and ethically neutral.** Wescoe challenges the view that "animals are food items" and that cruelty to them is "just the way of the world," arguing that the normalized violence has consequences for humans.
- **Sowing and reaping is a vague cosmic law.** He argues it is specifically tied to God's definition of good works, with reward from God and not from human approval.
- **Euphemisms like "harvest" and "meat packing plant" are neutral.** He says they hide killing and should be replaced with plainer words.
