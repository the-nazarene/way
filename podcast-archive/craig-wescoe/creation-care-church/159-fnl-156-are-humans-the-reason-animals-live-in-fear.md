## Summary

Craig opens from Habakkuk 2:17, which in the NKJV reads that the "plunder of beasts which made them afraid" comes "because of men's blood and the violence of the land." His question is whether human violence toward animals is the cause of their fear, and whether that violence is God's design or contrary to it. He says the talk reflects "my own research" and does not necessarily represent the views of his group.

Craig's answer is yes, and he argues it from the Hebrew Bible's opening chapters and their restoration in Isaiah. He reads Genesis 9:2 ("given into your hand") as conferring power without endorsement, using Job 2:6 as a parallel. He reads Genesis 1:26-31 as establishing human dominion as stewardship, with plants rather than animals as food for both people and beasts, and Isaiah 11:6-9 as the restored version of that peace. He places the origin of fear at the Fall (Genesis 3:8-10), so that fear is a consequence of disobedience rather than part of creation. He then argues that perfect love casts out fear (1 John 4:18) and that humans must repent and act as faithful stewards so the animals can "lie down in safety."

The talk ends with a Q&A in which viewers ask about the Genesis 9 and burnt-offering passages, the status of diet within salvation, and how to memorize verses. Craig recommends flashcards, points to earlier talks on the flood and "pleasing aroma," and closes with a prayer. Most of the argument rests on quoted verses rather than on historical, textual or manuscript evidence, and the Hebrew and Greek readings are not examined.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Habakkuk 2:17 names human violence as the cause of animal fear
Craig takes the verse as stating the cause directly: the animals' fear comes "because of our violence because we're plundering them." He frames the question as whether this violence is "by Design" or "the opposite" of God's will, and the rest of the talk is a series of passages used to answer that.

### 2. "Given into your hand" (Genesis 9:2) and Job 2:6 mean power, not endorsement
Craig says many readers take Genesis 9:2 as permission to do as they like with animals. He answers that Genesis 8:20-21 and the earlier note about the imagination of the human heart being evil (he points to Genesis 6) show God is not approving human conduct. He then offers Job 2:6, where God tells Satan Job is "in your hand but spare his life," as an example of a phrase that grants power without approving its use. For Craig, the phrase "just means you have power over them."

### 3. Dominion as stewardship: Genesis 1:26-31, Genesis 2:15, Luke 12:42-43 and Isaiah 11:6-9
Craig reads Genesis 1 as giving humans dominion and then specifying the food: "every herb that yields seed" and "every green herb for food" for people and beasts alike (Genesis 1:29-30). Genesis 2:15 (tend and keep the garden) is his second anchor. Luke 12:42-43 (the faithful and wise steward who gives food "in due season") is his New Testament parallel. In Isaiah 11:6-9, the child who leads the wolf, lion and lamb is, in his reading, the restoration of the Genesis design, which he calls "the end is just like the beginning." He concludes that violence toward animals is "the opposite of God's will" at both ends of the biblical story.

### 4. The burnt offerings of Noah (Genesis 8:20-21) and the "pleasing aroma"
Craig treats Noah's altar as an example of the violence he is describing: Noah "took of every clean animal... and burning their bodies as an offering." He says God's approving smell is not about burnt flesh, and cites an idol passage in Ezekiel (no verse given) where idols cannot smell, so the aroma must be pleasing to the one offering it. He says he reads the aroma as pleasing for "the obedience," and that an earlier live talk covers this in more detail. He treats the sacrificial system as a sign of the human heart's evil imagination rather than as a legitimate arrangement.

### 5. Fear comes from the Fall, and perfect love casts it out
Craig says Genesis 3:8-10 contains "the first mention of fear in the Bible," which he links to Adam and Eve hiding after disobedience. He concludes that fear "isn't part of God's design." He then gathers verses that present love or God's presence as the remedy: 1 John 4:16-18 ("perfect love casts out fear"), 2 Timothy 1:7, Psalm 34:4, Isaiah 41:10 and Joshua 1:9. He asks the animals' perspective on these verses, and uses Psalm 23 and John 10:10-11 (the Good Shepherd versus the thief who "steals and kills and destroys") to contrast the protector with the plunderer.

### 6. Repentance, fruit, and the restoration of the land
Craig argues that the current state of animal fear is continuing and will end only when people "repent" and "stop doing those things." He cites Jeremiah 2:7 (the land defiled) and 2 Chronicles 7:14 (healing after repentance). He also says that one's diet or treatment of animals is a fruit test, quoting "a good tree bears good fruit" without a verse, and rejects the view that diet "has nothing to do with our Salvation" as a deception. He cites Joel 2:22, 28 and Hosea 2:18 to argue that the restored order includes the animals.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Given into your hand" means God permits people to do whatever they want with animals.** Craig says the phrase grants power, not approval, and cites Genesis 8:20-21, the heart's evil imagination, and Job 2:6 in support.
- **Fear of humans is part of God's design for animals.** Craig argues fear enters through the Fall and disobedience (Genesis 3:8-10), not creation, and that the restored peace of Isaiah 11 reverses it.
- **God enjoys the smell of burnt animal flesh (the "pleasing aroma").** Craig says the aroma is pleasing to the one offering it, and that God's pleasure is in "the obedience" (a reading he attributes to his own understanding in the Q&A).
- **Diet and treatment of animals have nothing to do with salvation.** Craig argues that bearing "bad fruit" is evidence about whether salvation has taken place, and that a Christian who dismisses this is being deceived.
- **Verses such as Psalm 23 and Isaiah 41:10 are only about personal comfort.** Craig says they also describe how humans should treat animals, since humans are the image-bearers protecting them.
