## Summary

Presenter Craig Wescoe, speaking at a live "Friday Night Live" session of Creation Care Church, asks whether animal cruelty is something to repent from. He answers yes. His method is a chain of scripture references: repentance (metanoia-style "turning around," as he glosses it) leads to forgiveness and the gift of the Holy Spirit, the Spirit produces fruit, and cruelty is a "fruit" of the flesh that must be turned from. He reads Proverbs 12:10 and 28:13, James 2:13, Luke 6:36, Luke 15:7, and Romans 2:4 as showing that God's mercy requires human mercy, and he takes Matthew 9:13 (quoting Hosea 6:6, "I desire mercy and not sacrifice") to show that Jesus rejected animal sacrifice and that slaughter for food falls under the same condemnation. He treats Genesis 1:29-31 as the "first works" that Revelation 2:5 calls the church to return to, and he reads Isaiah 11 and Revelation 21:4 as a picture of a vegan creation and kingdom.

Most of the talk is a sermon with no independent evidence beyond the scriptures themselves. The live chat (roughly the last third of the talk) mostly consists of greetings and testimonies about veganism, including a personal account of visiting a farm sanctuary and a street-outreach conversation. ## Key Teachings

### 1. Repentance as turning, with fruit as its proof

The speaker opens with Matthew 4:17 and argues that because "Repent" is the first word of Jesus's ministry it should be central for believers. He reads Acts 3:19 and Acts 2:38 (repentance tied to forgiveness and receiving the Holy Spirit), 2 Peter 3:9 (God is "long-suffering" so that all may come to repentance), and 2 Chronicles 7:14 (humbling, prayer, and turning from wicked ways). He then uses Matthew 3:8 ("bear fruits worthy of repentance") and Matthew 7:16-20 ("by their fruits you will know them") to argue that a person cannot bear both the fruit of the Spirit and the "bad fruit" at once. Repentance, in this reading, is the precondition for the Spirit's fruit. The speaker's gloss that repentance means "turning away from evil" is his own paraphrase; he does not work through the Greek or Hebrew terms in the talk.

### 2. Galatians 5 and the flesh: cruelty as the "fruit" of consumption

Galatians 5:16-25 is the centre of the argument. The speaker lists the fruit of the Spirit (love, joy, peace, long-suffering, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control) and asks, for each, whether our treatment of animals displays it. His central claim is that most people causing animal cruelty "aren't desiring to cause animal cruelty. They're just desiring the fruit of that cruelty," meaning the meat, milk and eggs. He argues that repentance must therefore include turning from the products, not only from the act. This is the talk's main bridge from the Pauline text to diet. Galatians 5:17 ("the flesh lusts against the spirit") frames the contrast.

### 3. Proverbs and the confession that must be followed by forsaking

Proverbs 12:10 ("a righteous man regards the life of his animal, but the tender mercies of the wicked are cruel") is read as a moral test: which side of animal cruelty are you on? Proverbs 28:13 ("he who covers his sins will not prosper, but whoever confesses and forsakes them will have mercy") is read as ruling out excuses. The speaker says the drive-through customer who "orders a number three" is covering sin in the manner of Adam and Eve blaming each other. Confession must be joined by forsaking, which the speaker presents as the core of repentance.

### 4. Matthew 9:13, Hosea 6:6, and the sacrificial system

This is the most historically consequential claim. The speaker quotes Matthew 9:13 ("I desire mercy and not sacrifice ... I did not come to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance") and links it to Hosea 6:6. He says Jesus is "disrupting the temple" and that the prophets Amos, Jeremiah, David, Ezekiel and Joel "were all saying" that God does not want animal sacrifices and "were opposed to the sacrificial system." He applies this to any killing of animals, whether ritual or for food: "whether we're sacrificing the animals as some ritual ... or whether we're paying a slaughterhouse worker." The claim that these prophets opposed the sacrificial system, as opposed to particular practices, is asserted with no textual discussion in the talk. Group members should check it against the passages themselves before accepting the generalization.

### 5. Mercy and judgment: James, Luke, Romans

James 2:12-13 ("judgment is without mercy to the one who has shown no mercy. Mercy triumphs over judgment") is the speaker's principal text for a reciprocal principle: we must show mercy to receive it. He pairs this with Luke 6:36 ("be merciful, just as your Father also is merciful") and a Psalm he cannot place (he thinks Psalm 145 or 150) saying God's mercy is over all his works, which he extends to non-human creatures. Romans 2:4 (the "goodness of God leads you to repentance") is used to argue that God forgives without demanding payment, and Luke 15:7 ("more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents") is used to picture heaven's rejoicing. In this section the speaker most clearly presents repentance as a free offer, and he says that past cruelty is forgiven "in an instant" when it is confessed and abandoned.

### 6. Genesis 1:29-31 as the "first works" and the vegan original

Revelation 2:5 ("remember, therefore, from where you have fallen. Repent and do the first works") becomes, for the speaker, a call back to creation. He quotes Genesis 1:29-31 (the herb-and-fruit diet given to humans and to "every beast of the earth") and adds Isaiah 11:6 and 11:9 ("they will neither hurt nor destroy") and Revelation 21:4 in the Q&A ("there shall be no more pain," which he takes to exclude killing animals). The argument is that the original diet of Genesis is the "very good" state from which humanity "fell," so repentance means returning to plant-based eating. This is a theological reading of Genesis, not a historical claim about early practice, and the talk gives no evidence beyond the text itself.

### 7. Q&A extensions: feeling sorry is not repentance; veganism as liberation

In answering the live chat the speaker makes several further points. He says repentance is commonly reduced to "feel badly or be sorry," and that "are you even really sorry if you have no intention of changing?" He says he will send "resources for the definition" of repentance but gives no Hebrew word in the talk. He says going vegan opened his diet, rather than restricting it, and that on meeting animals at a sanctuary he came to believe they have "a soul" and "personalities." He argues that "anthropocentric" thinking (what is in it for me?) is the wrong attitude and that drawing near to God is the real reward. These statements are personal testimony and do not form historical claims.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Animal cruelty is not a sin, and God does not require people to stop.** The speaker says some Christians defend cruelty by saying "it's not a sin," and he argues that Scripture makes it a matter of repentance.
- **Repentance is just feeling sorry.** He says confession must be followed by forsaking. A chat participant (April) raised the point, and he agrees with the correction.
- **Killing an animal does not harm the animal.** He reports a street-outreach conversation in which a couple said this, and argues that dismembering an animal is clearly harmful.
- **Consumers are not responsible for cruelty they do not directly commit.** He argues that paying for meat, milk and eggs is participation, and that "I'm just ordering a number three" is a form of covering sin.
- **Jesus made all food clean and does not care about animals.** He says churches use a "false view" of Jesus, and that the "Jesus disrupting the temple" picture changes people's minds.
- **Dominion means domination over animals.** He says the churches are "swayed by the false definition of dominion," and offers "Christ-like dominion" as the true sense.
- **Going vegan is restrictive.** He says that after going vegan his diet opened up and that he now finds it more varied.
