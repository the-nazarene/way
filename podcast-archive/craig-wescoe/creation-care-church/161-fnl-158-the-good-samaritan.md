## Summary

The host asks what point Jesus was making in the Good Samaritan parable (Luke 10:25-37) and what it shows about Jesus's opposition to animal sacrifice. He argues that Jesus does not answer the lawyer's "who is my neighbor?" with a category. Instead he tells a story and asks who acted as neighbor, so the answer is whoever shows mercy. The host says the usual reading, that the Samaritan is an outcast contrasted with the priest and Levite, is "partially true" but misses the main element: the priest and Levite represent the Jerusalem temple sacrificial system, which the host says Jesus was opposing. He lists the Samaritan-Judean hostility from the "no dealings" line in John 4:9 as the standard explanation, and says the historical evidence does not clearly support it.

His evidence runs through a chain of texts. He traces the Levites' and Aaron's priesthood to the golden calf episode in Exodus 32, where the Levites kill about 3,000 men. He cites Hosea 6:6 ("I desire mercy and not sacrifice") as quoted by Jesus in Matthew 9:13, then the temple cleansing in Matthew 21:12-13, John 10:10 ("the thief"), and Hebrews 7 on a priesthood "according to the order of Melchizedek" that ends the need for Levitical sacrifice. He also argues from Jeremiah 8:8 that "the lying pen of the scribe" added animal sacrifices to the Torah, and he offers this as one of two readings he considers acceptable.

The talk ends with a live Q&A from the chat (questions about the bodies of sacrificed animals, Jeremiah 8:8, and Revelation 21:4), a preview of upcoming episodes, and a closing prayer for an end to killing for both people and animals. The host states at the start that the views are his own and "don't necessarily represent everyone in creation care church."

## Key Teachings

### 1. Jesus redefines "neighbor" by answering with a story
Jesus does not answer "who is my neighbor?" directly. He asks which of three passersby acted as neighbor, and the lawyer answers "He who showed mercy on him" (Luke 10:37). The host reads this as a rejection of any narrow boundary (fellow Israelites, humans only, or those nearby). It also parallels the lawyer's earlier answer on loving God and neighbor (Deuteronomy 6:5, Leviticus 19:18), which Jesus affirms.

### 2. The Samaritan context: Gerizim, Jerusalem, and John 4
The host gives background on the northern kingdom (Israel, later Samaria) and the southern kingdom (Judah, later Judea). He says Samaritans worshiped on Mount Gerizim and have a Pentateuch with an "11th commandment" to worship there. He says the Gerizim temple was destroyed "about a hundred or so years before Christ" and the Jerusalem temple in the 1st century CE. He then reads John 4:5-39, where many Samaritans of Sychar believe in Jesus, and treats the Samaritan of the parable as a model of someone who hears and follows Jesus. He notes that the Samaritans are "children of Jacob," so they are also Israelites, and he calls the usual "Jews have no dealings with Samaritans" reading only part of the story.

### 3. The priests and Levites and the origin of the sacrificial system
The host describes the Levites as temple workers and the priests as Aaronic descendants who perform the killing, blood-draining, and sprinkling. To explain their rise, he reads Exodus 27:21-28:1 (Aaron and his sons as priests), then Exodus 32:1-6 (the golden calf, with Aaron's altar and "feast to Yahweh"), and Exodus 32:25-35 (the Levites killing their brothers, companions, and neighbors at Moses' command). His reading is that the sacrificial system's leadership traces to this episode, and that the parable's priest and Levite represent it. He says that in Jesus's day the priests and Levites of the temple were the Sadducees, installed by Herod, and that the parable does not target the Pharisees.

### 4. The prophets and Jesus's temple action
The host argues that Jesus repeats the prophetic critique of sacrifice: "I desire mercy and not sacrifice" (Matthew 9:13, quoting Hosea 6:6). He lists David (Psalms), Jeremiah, Amos, Isaiah, and Joel as making the same point. He then links the temple cleansing (Matthew 21:12-13, citing Isaiah 56:7 and Jeremiah 7) to this critique. He reads the Greek word translated "thieves" as "violent ones," and argues that Jesus is disrupting a violent, profit-driven sacrificial trade. He says that in John 10:10-11 Jesus contrasts the "thief" (the violent one) with the Good Shepherd who "gives his life."

### 5. Hebrews 7 and the Melchizedek priesthood
The host reads Hebrews 7:15-28, arguing that Jesus is a priest "forever according to the order of Melchizedek" (Psalm 110:4). He says this annuls the former commandment about Levitical sacrifice, that the Levitical law "made nothing perfect," and that Jesus, unlike the mortal priests, "does not need daily... to offer up sacrifices." He links this to Genesis 14:18-20, where Melchizedek brings out bread and wine, and calls that priesthood older than the Aaronic one. He concludes that Jesus's single offering "on the cross" ended the sacrificial system, or was meant to. He also cites John 14:6 for the line "I'm the way the truth and the life," though the reference is not given in the captions.

### 6. Jeremiah 8:8 and the "lying pen of the scribe"
The host reads Jeremiah 8:8 ("the lying pen of the scribe has produced a falsehood") as a claim that scribes added animal sacrifices to the Torah. He supports this with Jeremiah 7:21-24, where God says he did not command burnt offerings when he brought Israel out of Egypt, and he links this to the scroll found in the temple in Kings. He admits other readers hold that God did command sacrifice but only as a "permissive will" to meet people in "the hardness of our heart," which Jesus then set aside. He says either reading leads to the same practical conclusion: do not sacrifice animals, and show mercy.

### 7. Mercy extends to animals
Building on Luke 6:36 ("be merciful just as your father also is merciful") and the parallel in Matthew ("perfect"), the host argues that love for enemies implies love without exclusion, and that this extends to animals. He cites Psalm 145:9 ("God's Compassion... are over his entire creation") and Revelation 21:4 and Isaiah 11:6-10 as prophecies of a world without killing. He also argues that the origin of animal sacrifice is demon worship, saying this is treated in an earlier talk on the Book of Enoch.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Good Samaritan is just about the Samaritan being a social outcast.** The host says that is "partially true" but that the story's "even more important element" is the critique of the animal sacrifice system.
- **"Jews have no dealings with Samaritans" (John 4:9) is the full picture of the Judean-Samaritan relationship.** The host says this is the standard reading he was taught, but that the historical evidence does not clearly support it, and that the Samaritans were also children of Jacob and Israelites.
- **Animal sacrifice was always commanded by God.** The host argues from Jeremiah 7:21-24 and 8:8 that sacrifices were added by scribes (or, on the alternate reading, were a permissive concession). He does not think Exodus and Leviticus should be read at face value on this point.
- **Jesus was opposing the Pharisees or Judaism as a whole.** The host says the parable's priest and Levite are the temple sacrificial establishment (in his view, the Sadducees), and that the Pharisees are not targeted.
- **Jesus's teaching on sacrifice is only about the cross.** The host argues Jesus repeated the prophetic critique, cleansed the temple, and established the Melchizedek priesthood, so sacrifice is opposed throughout his ministry.
