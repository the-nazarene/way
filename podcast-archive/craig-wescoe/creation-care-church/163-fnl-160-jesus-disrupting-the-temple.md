## Summary

Wescoe asks why Jesus disrupted the Temple, as John 2:13-16 describes. He challenges the common church explanation that the point was dishonest money changers and sellers. He argues that the event was about animal sacrifice itself. He says Luke's account never mentions money changers, that Jesus quotes Jeremiah 7:11 ("den of robbers") rather than texts about dishonest trade, and that the prophets (Jeremiah, David, Hosea, Samuel, Isaiah, Micah) reject sacrifice as God's desire. He ties this to Hebrews 10 and John 14:6, which he reads as ending the sacrificial system, and to the Passover setting with its lamb, the poor's substitute offerings, and barley bread.

His strongest-sounding evidence for an animal-focused reading is a passage he attributes to Heracleon's commentary on John (as quoted by Origen), which says the whip was not made of dead leather. He presents this as an early witness, a claim the group should check against the primary text. He also says the whip was used to drive animals out, so the event was an act of animal liberation. The gospels do not say the animals were released, and Wescoe concedes that point is speculative.

The talk ends with a Q&A from the live chat, in which Wescoe concedes that some listeners may hold a second view: God first permitted sacrifice to draw Israel away from sacrificing to other gods, and Jesus later ended sacrifice. He says he favors the "lying scribes" view but thinks there is room for both. The stream closes with a prayer, announcements, and an aside that he observes Passover and resurrection timing by his Enochian calendar.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Passover and the animal trade
Wescoe reads Exodus 12:1-3 and 12:6-7 (the lamb taken on the 10th, killed at twilight, blood on the doorposts) and Leviticus 5:7 and 5:11 (turtle doves, pigeons, or a tenth of an ephah of flour for those who cannot afford a lamb). He argues that the animals sold in the Temple courts were these substitutes, which made the sellers serve the poor. He links this to Luke 6:20 and James 2:5, where the poor are called blessed and "chosen," and to Exodus 23:15 ("no one may appear before me empty-handed"). He then describes the Passover week as the barley-harvest unleavened-bread week, and he says he baked barley bread for it. Note: the link between poor-person offerings and the Temple sellers is his inference, not a point stated in the Gospels.

### 2. The money changers are not the main point
Wescoe notes that Luke 19:45-46, which describes the same action, does not mention money changers at all. He argues that the focus is on those selling animals for slaughter. He also says that if the issue were dishonest sellers, Jesus would have quoted texts about fraud, such as Hosea 12:7 or Proverbs 11:1. Instead he quotes Jeremiah 7:11. He also notes that Luke 19:41-44 (Jesus weeping over Jerusalem) is linked by "some" to the Roman conquest of the 130s and the founding of Aelia Capitolina, a dating he attributes to others and does not defend.

### 3. "Den of robbers" as "den of violence"
Wescoe argues that the Hebrew word in Jeremiah 7:11 (rendered "robbers" or "thieves") means violent or tyrannical, not merely dishonest. He cites a Strong's entry (the number is garbled in the captions). On this reading, Jesus calls the Temple a den of violence because of the blood being shed there. He reads Jeremiah 7:4-8 as condemning shedding "innocent blood in this place" and oppressing the foreigner, the fatherless and the widow.

### 4. The prophets against sacrifice, and "the lying scribes"
Wescoe reads Jeremiah 7:21-26 ("I did not speak to your fathers or command them ... concerning burnt offerings or sacrifices") and Jeremiah 8:8 ("the lying pen of the scribes") as saying God never commanded sacrifice and that scribes inserted it into the Torah. He then lines up Psalm 40:6, Hosea 6:6, 1 Samuel 15:22 and Isaiah 1:11 as agreeing that God does not desire sacrifice. He notes that Jeremiah 7:21-26 says God sent prophets "daily," and treats the prophets as one consistent message. He treats the authorship claim as the stronger view but admits it is a contested reading of the Torah.

### 5. Hebrews 10 and John 14:6: ending the system
He reads Hebrews 10:3-4 (sacrifices as "a reminder of sins") and Hebrews 10:11-12 (the one sacrifice that ends the daily offerings) as declaring the animal system finished, with Jesus's death as the end of it. He reads John 14:6 ("I am the way, the truth, and the life") as Jesus rejecting the priestly path to God and as "the truth" against the scribes' lies. He also connects John 10:10 (the thief who comes to steal, kill and destroy) to the sellers. Wescoe's reading of Hebrews and John is his own interpretation.

### 6. The whip, and the "vegan whip"
Wescoe says a critic once asked whether the whip (John 2:15) was made of leather. He quotes what he calls Heracleon's fragment 13 (a Johannine commentary, quoted by Origen in the early 3rd century, per Wescoe). The passage says the whip was made of cords and wood, and that Jesus "did not make it of dead leather." It reads the wood as a type of the cross. Wescoe calls Heracleon "our earliest witness" to a commentary on John; the group should check that claim. He argues that the cords were used to drive animals out, which he takes as liberation rather than violence. In the chat, a viewer offered a commentary saying the cords were made of straw or grass. Wescoe did not verify that claim and says he would look into it.

### 7. Two views, held with room for both
In the Q&A, Wescoe describes a view he once held: God permitted sacrifice to stop Israel from sacrificing to other gods, and Jesus later ended sacrifice altogether, so the change came in two stages. He says he favors the "lying scribes" view but thinks listeners who follow the Holy Spirit and choose peace may hold either. He says the second view has "its own evidence" without giving that evidence.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The money changers were ripping people off, so that is why Jesus acted.** Wescoe says the money changers are absent from Luke, and that the sellers of animals were the focus; the Jeremiah quotation points to violence against animals, not fraud.
- **Jesus only opposed dishonest trade, and sacrifice was God's will.** Wescoe argues the Torah's sacrifice laws were not God's command (per Jeremiah 7 and 8), and that the prophets and Hebrews agree sacrifice was not what God wanted.
- **The whip was leather, like any ordinary whip.** Wescoe cites Heracleon's commentary to say it was made of cords and wood, and that Jesus did not use dead leather.
- **The gospels show the animals were freed.** Wescoe concedes the gospels do not say the animals were released, only driven out; he argues the use of the whip to crack against the ground implies liberation, which he admits is speculative.
- **Noah's covenant was only with humans.** Wescoe says Genesis 9 names the animals five times as party to the covenant. This is his reading of the chapter.
