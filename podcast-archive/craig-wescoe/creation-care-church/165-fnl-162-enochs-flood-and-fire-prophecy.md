## Key Teachings

### 1. The first 19 chapters of 1 Enoch as a flood-and-fire narrative
Wescoe calls 1 Enoch 1-19 the "core story" of the book. He says early Christian writers quote and reference it more often than the rest of the book combined. He argues that its parallels to Genesis and Job, which he describes as among the oldest biblical narratives, show that this core story is also very old. The chain he walks through runs: the righteous man (Enoch, Noah), the adversary (hashatan, Satan), the angelic transgression (Genesis 6:1-4 expanded in 1 Enoch 6-7), the flood announcement (1 Enoch 10:2-3 with Genesis 6:17-22), and the final fiery judgment and healing of the earth.

### 2. Flesh-eating, blood-drinking and sacrifice traced to the giants and demons
Wescoe reads 1 Enoch 7:1-6 as the source of human flesh-eating and blood-drinking. The giants, he says, consumed the produce of the earth and then "began to injure beasts, birds, beasts, reptiles, and fishes to eat their flesh... and to drink their blood." He takes Genesis 6:11-12 and Matthew 24:38-39 ("eating and drinking, marrying and giving in marriage") to mean this, and says the phrase "marrying and giving in marriage" refers to the Watchers' unions with human women, not ordinary marriage. He then reads 1 Enoch 15:8-12 and 19:1-2 (as cited in the talk) to say the spirits of the dead giants, unable to ascend and lacking bodies, possess humans and animals. He links this to demonic sacrifice, quoting the passage that the Watchers "shall lead them astray into sacrificing to demons as gods." He treats Revelation 9:20-21 and 1 Corinthians 10:21 as continuing that pattern of demon worship and food. His argument is that the Bible's food laws (Genesis 9:4, Acts 15:28-29, Romans 14:20-21) carry the same concern as 1 Enoch.

### 3. Jude, Peter and Jesus as witnesses that 1 Enoch is authoritative
Wescoe's central claim about authority is that Jude 14-15 quotes 1 Enoch 1:9 and presents it as the prophecy of Enoch "the seventh from Adam." He says that the Enoch in Genesis 5 is therefore the same figure, and that Jude is "testifying that the book of Enoch is authentically from Enoch." He addresses the objection that Paul quotes a pagan poet (Titus 1:12 is not cited, but Paul's use of a pagan poet is raised) by arguing that Enoch, unlike a pagan poet, is described as walking with God. He also argues that 2 Peter 2:4 and Jude 6 depend on the Enoch account of bound angels. His strongest claim is about Jesus: in Matthew 22:29-30 Jesus says angels neither marry nor are given in marriage, and Wescoe says no Old Testament text says this, so Jesus must be pointing to 1 Enoch 15:3-7. On his reading, Jesus therefore "is calling the book of Enoch scripture."

### 4. The adversary, the Watchers and the fiery judgment
Wescoe connects Satan's accusation in Job 1:6-12 with the adversary Azazel of 1 Enoch 8 and 10. He reads Luke 10:18 ("I saw Satan fall like lightning") and Revelation 12:4 as references to the Watchers' fall, and Leviticus 16:8 as a biblical mention of Azazel in the Day of Atonement goat ritual. For judgment he cites 1 Enoch 10:4-6 (the binding of the angels), 1 Enoch 18:14-16 (the stars that transgressed "at the appointed times"), and 1 Enoch 10:12-15 (the chains and abyss). He then sets these beside Matthew 25:41, 2 Peter 2:4 and Revelation 20:10-15, which he says "basically" say the same thing. He notes that the demons in Matthew 8:28-29 ask whether Jesus has come to torture them "before our appointed time."

### 5. Restoration: a vegan world and the healing of creation
The talk ends on promise rather than destruction. Wescoe reads 1 Enoch 10:16-19 and 10:7 ("heal the earth which the angels have corrupted") as the start of a restored earth of abundance. He pairs that with Genesis 1:28-31 (the plant diet given to humans and animals alike) and Genesis 9:7-11 (the covenant including the animals), and with Romans 8:20-22, Acts 3:21, Matthew 5:5 and Revelation 21:4. His conclusion is that the animals are "as much a part" of the covenant as humans, that slaughter and sacrifice will end, and that believers are called to be "pioneers" of that restoration. He presents this as God's purpose, not merely as his own preference.

### 6. Authority, dating and the history of the book (as the speaker presents it)
Wescoe says that before the Dead Sea Scrolls the scholarly "general consensus" was that 1 Enoch was a pseudepigraphal second-century Christian work. He says the discovery of Enoch fragments at Qumran means the book predates the New Testament, so New Testament writers treated it as authentic. He also says the Ethiopian (Tewahedo) church holds the book as canonical. He explicitly frames these as his claims for the audience to evaluate, and presents his study edition as a way to check them.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"'Marrying and eating and drinking' in the days of Noah was ordinary human life."** Wescoe says the phrase refers to angels marrying human women to produce giants, and the eating and drinking refers to the giants' consumption of flesh and blood.
- **"1 Enoch is not scripture, or is a dangerous book."** He argues that Jude quotes it as Enoch's prophecy, that Peter alludes to it, and that Jesus implicitly calls it Scripture, so pastors who dismiss it are "calling Jesus, Jude, and Peter false prophets."
- **"Jude quoting Enoch is like Paul quoting a pagan poet, which doesn't make the poem Scripture."** He answers that the pagan poet is not described as walking with God, while Enoch is.
- **"The Nephilim/giants were the sons of Seth marrying the daughters of Cain."** In the Q&A he says this reading arose in later Judaism and was adopted by Augustine; before that, the predominant view was fallen angels.
- **"1 Enoch is a second-century Christian forgery."** He says this was the scholarly consensus before the Dead Sea Scrolls, and that the discovery of Enoch fragments there shows it predates the New Testament.
