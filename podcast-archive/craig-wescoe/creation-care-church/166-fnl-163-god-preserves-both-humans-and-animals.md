## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks whether God's preservation of "both people and animals" (Psalm 36:6) implies that humans, as God's image-bearers, should actively rescue and preserve animals, and whether current human treatment of animals (especially in farming and slaughter) runs against that calling. He presents this as a solo talk, though the second half is a live question-and-answer with the online audience.

His answer is an unambiguous yes, built almost entirely by stringing together proof texts. He reads Genesis 6 (Noah taking animals into the ark) and Jonah 4:11 (God's compassion for the animals of Nineveh) as evidence that God preserves animals. He reads Jesus' cleansing of the temple (John 2:14-16), Matthew 12:11-12, and Hosea 6:6 as Jesus opposing animal bloodshed. He argues from Genesis 1:26-29 that "dominion" was never permission to kill and eat animals, since God assigned plant food, and from 1 John 4:16 and John 10:10-11 that God's nature is love and that Jesus is the "good shepherd." He ties this to a call to diet change (veganism), to avoiding animal products, and to speaking out and "interceding" for animals.

Wescoe frequently signals that he is interpreting rather than reporting: he says of Noah, "we're sort of reading between the lines here," and of the Noah scene, "it doesn't elaborate." He presents his reading as "what the text plainly says," without engaging historical or critical scholarship. The talk ends with a closing prayer for those "destined for slaughter" and a reminder of upcoming events. The live chat questions, on topics such as the image of God in animals, how to discern scripture, and whether animal sacrifice was commanded, are answered only briefly.

## Key Teachings

### God preserves humans and animals; Noah as the model
Wescoe reads Psalm 36:6 as showing that God "cares not only about people, but also about animals," and argues that if God preserves animals, humans should too. He uses Genesis 6:19-20 (two of every kind brought into the ark "to keep them alive") and draws an analogy to animals coming to him at a park for food: animals come to Noah because he is "righteous" and preserves them, and he supports this with Proverbs 12:10 ("the righteous care for the needs of the animals"). He then cites Jonah 4:11, where God's compassion extends to the "many animals" of Nineveh, and says the hope for the world is that people will "turn away" from harm and do "the righteous things," which he says include "Genesis 1:29, eating a plant-based diet."

### Jesus' temple action as opposition to animal bloodshed
Wescoe reads John 2:14-16 as Jesus driving out the sellers of oxen, sheep and doves, and says "the animals are the innocent blood that's being shed in the temple." He notes that in "one of the other Gospels" Jesus quotes Jeremiah 7:11 ("a den of violence"), and claims the "lying scribes" of Jeremiah 8 had made animal sacrifice seem God's will. He concludes that Jesus wanted mercy rather than sacrifice, citing Hosea 6:6 ("I desire mercy, not sacrifice") and Micah 6:8. He also says that "according to Jeremiah, he didn't" command animal sacrifices, but this is stated without a reference, and he treats the question of sacrifice's origins as "complicated" and requiring historical understanding that he does not supply.

### Matthew 12:11-12 and the value of animals
Wescoe reads Jesus' saying about lifting a sheep out of a pit on the Sabbath as showing that doing good to animals is lawful on the Sabbath. He argues that people who use "Of how much more value then is a man than a sheep?" to dismiss animal welfare have "the whole thing backwards," because the point presupposes that animals are valuable to God. He says humans have greater responsibility as stewards, and that a human who destroys God's creatures would be "the least valuable," with "the most negative value of any creature."

### Dominion as stewardship, not license (Genesis 1:26-29)
This is the talk's central exegetical claim. Wescoe reads "Have dominion over the fish of the sea..." and then "See, I have given you every herb that yields seed..." as a deliberate limit: "the one thing he specifically says dominion is not is killing and eating the animals." He calls humans God's "viceroy" and "steward," and says God "does give us the freedom that we can do whatever we want, but he doesn't authorize us to do whatever we want." In the chat he expands on this with the idea that the "image of God" for humans means responsibility, while angels hold dominion over the heavenly realm.

### Love, the good shepherd, and rescue
Wescoe argues that God's character is love (1 John 4:16) and that the fruit of the Spirit is love (Galatians 5:22). He contrasts Jesus' "thief" who "steals and kills and destroys" with Jesus as "the good shepherd" (John 10:10-11), and asks whether humans are the thieves or the protectors of animals. He reads Proverbs 24:11 ("rescue those who are drawn toward death") and Psalm 82:3-4 ("Defend the poor and the fatherless") as applying to animals, which he calls "the most vulnerable" because billions are killed by humans each year. He cites Romans 6:23 (wages of sin / free gift of life) and Hebrews 7:25 (Jesus' intercession) as models for human intercession on behalf of animals.

### Covetousness and animal products
Wescoe defines covetousness as "desiring something that's not intended for you" and applies it to milk, cheese, honey and flesh, arguing that cow's milk "is for baby cows," honey "belongs to the bees," and animal flesh "belongs to the animal." He presents this as a Ten Commandments issue ("Do not covet") without giving a verse. He also explains that "God's everlasting covenant is with the animals" (Genesis 9, which he says mentions this five times) and that people overlook this.

### Practical application: diet, lifestyle and multiplication
Wescoe urges listeners to go vegan, "not just our diet, but our whole lifestyle," avoiding leather, fur, eyelash products, and animal-tested items. He describes the diet change as manageable, mentioning trying new foods, tofu, and cuisines. He offers a multiplication argument: if one person saves about 200 animals per year over 60 years, that is a large number, and each person converted "saves that many animals again." He ties this to 1 Corinthians-style "temple" language by asking whether listeners are "making our temples a den of violence" through what they consume.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Misconception:** Jesus' statement that humans are "more valuable" than sheep means humans need not care about animals. **Correction:** Wescoe says this reverses Jesus' point; the example itself shows that animals are valuable to God and that humans, as stewards, carry greater responsibility.
- **Misconception:** "Dominion" gives humans permission to kill and eat animals. **Correction:** Wescoe reads Genesis 1:26-29 as granting dominion immediately followed by a plant-based food provision, so dominion means stewardship.
- **Misconception:** Covetousness means only desiring something. **Correction:** Wescoe defines it as "desiring something that's not intended for you," which he applies to animal products.
- **Misconception:** Animals are inferior and therefore not worth protection. **Correction:** Wescoe argues that salvation is a free gift not earned or merited, so the question of whether animals "merit" protection should not arise. He also says humans can communicate with animals through gesture and attention.
