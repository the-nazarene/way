## Summary

This is a live "Friday Night Live" talk from the Creation Care Church channel, presented by Craig Wescoe, with a Q&A from the chat in the second half. The question is what Pentecost means: how it relates to the Old Testament harvest feasts, to Joel's prophecy of the Spirit poured out on "all flesh," and to the "peaceable kingdom" of Isaiah 11, and what it means for how people treat animals.

Wescoe's answer is that Pentecost is one of three harvest festivals (Exodus 34:22-23), tied to Genesis 1:29-31, where God gives plants and fruit as food and calls creation "very good." He reads Joel's "all flesh" (which he gives as the Hebrew *basar*) as including animals, and he links the outpouring of the Spirit to the fruit of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22-23), to peace, and to the renewal of creation (Romans 8:19-22). He presents the Lord's Prayer's "your kingdom come, your will be done on earth" as a prayer for this peaceable order.

The talk ends with a closing prayer and a note on upcoming events. Wescoe frames the speech as "my own views, not representative of everyone in creation care." Most of the Q&A is greetings and encouragement, though he does answer a chat question about whether eating animals is sin, pointing to James 4:17 read against Genesis 1:29-31.

### Key Teachings

### 1. Pentecost as one of three harvest feasts, and God's "very good" diet
Wescoe reads Exodus 34:22-23 as naming three harvest festivals (barley in early spring, wheat at Pentecost, and fruit at the year's end). He says Pentecost is "the shabowat, which is the middle one," the wheat harvest. He uses Genesis 1:29 ("every herb that yields seed... every tree whose fruit yields seed... for food") and Genesis 1:31 ("very good") to argue that these feasts are meant to remind people of God's original plant-based design. He describes the feasts as family-style reunions where people eat the fruit of the land together.

### 2. Joel 2:28's "all flesh" includes animals
Wescoe takes Acts 2:17, which quotes Joel, and goes to Joel 2:28 ("I will pour out my spirit on all flesh"). He says the Hebrew term "doesn't just mean all people. It also means all animals." He then reads Genesis 1:2 (the Spirit hovering over the waters) as the beginning of the same Spirit, and Psalm 104:30 as Spirit renewing the earth. In his reading, Romans 8:19-22 has creation waiting for "the children of God," so that the Spirit's outpouring on people is meant to reach the animals too.

### 3. The Spirit's fruit is peace, gentleness and love, and this shapes how one treats animals
He cites Galatians 5:22-23 ("love, joy, peace, longsuffering, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control") as the character of the Spirit. He then asks whether people "are being violent toward them" or "slaughtering them," and whether appetite is controlling them (he cites "serving... our belly" from Philippians). He concludes that living in the Spirit means "acting from the spirit of peace."

### 4. The peaceable kingdom and the "little child" who leads
Isaiah 11:6-9 is presented as the "peaceable kingdom." Wescoe notes that a little child leads the animals, and he links this to Jesus's saying that one "cannot enter the kingdom unless you change and become like these little children." He treats the children as Spirit-filled people who lead creation in peace. He also cites Jeremiah 31:33 (Torah written on the heart, not stone) and Romans 12:2 (transformed, not conformed) as the inner change through which this happens.

### 5. Dove, lamb and the reversal of Babel
Wescoe reads Matthew 3:16 (the Spirit descending "like a dove") as peace. He reads John 1:29 ("Behold the lamb of God") as innocence and gentleness, and he says a lamb is "a vegan animal" that is "not some animal that's tearing apart other animals." He cites Matthew for "shrewd as serpents but as innocent as doves." He then reads Genesis 11:1-9 (Babel, a scattering of languages) against Acts 2:1-11, where each hears in his own language, and he calls the common understanding "the language of love," which he says even animals understand. He reads Galatians 3:28 ("one in Christ") as including animals in God's kingdom, and Revelation 5:13-14 as "all the animals" praising the Lamb.

### 6. Romans 14 and James 4:17 as arguments against eating animals
This is the strongest use of Paul in the talk. Wescoe reads Romans 14:21 ("It is good neither to eat meat...") and says the Greek term rendered "stumble" should be read as "beat on," so Paul is telling the reader to stop being violent. He reads Romans 14:15 ("Do not destroy with your food the one for whom Christ died") as a verse for a lone vegan in a non-vegan church, a grief that can be voiced to fellow believers. He also cites Romans 14:14, and in the Q&A he answers "where in the Bible does it say that eating animals is a sin?" with James 4:17 ("to him who knows to do good and does not do it, to him it is sin"), read alongside Genesis 1:29-31.

### 7. Renewal and the kingdom's prayer
He ends with the Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:9-10) as the prayer for "your spirit... to flood the earth," so that "all will live according to your ways of peace." He cites Revelation 21:4 for the promise that God will wipe away tears, and he says that "the victory has been won." He mentions that God "has the power to resurrect humans and animals," although this is a brief remark rather than a developed argument.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Pentecost is only a uniquely Christian event, not tied to Jewish festivals.** Wescoe says Christians often treat the festivals Jesus observed as "more of a Jewish thing," but that Pentecost "maps on to the same time as shabowat," and the two "are supposed to be the same thing."
- **Joel's "all flesh" means all people.** He argues the Hebrew term also means all animals.
- **Romans 14:21 only means not offending a brother with food.** He argues "stumble" should be read as "beat on," so the passage is about avoiding violence, not only offence.
- **Nothing in the Bible calls eating animals a sin.** He answers with James 4:17, read alongside Genesis 1:29-31.
- **Lambs in John 1:29 signify sacrifice.** He reads the lamb as innocence and gentleness, and says a lamb is "a vegan animal" that does not tear other animals apart. (He does not discuss sacrificial readings.)
