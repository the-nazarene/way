## Summary

Craig Wescoe opens with John 14:23 ("If anyone loves me... my Father will love him, and we will come to him and make our home with him") and asks how a believer can experience God's presence now rather than only later. His answer is that God is available everywhere and at all times to anyone who loves him and keeps his word. He supports this mainly from the Hebrew Bible and Psalms (1 Kings 19:11-13 and Psalm 139:7-10 for God's presence in every place, Psalm 16:11 and Psalm 100 for joy and song) and from Jesus's sayings in Matthew, Luke and Revelation. He reads the "kingdom of God" as something that is already present within believers and can be brought about "through us" now, while full restoration (Acts 3:20-21, Revelation 21:1-4) is still in God's hands and "in due time."

Wescoe presents practical remedies: prayer, singing, time in nature ("ask the animals," Job 12:7-10), music, and scripture written on walls and doorposts. He stresses that these practices do not draw God closer; they help us notice that he is already near. The second half is a live Q&A in which he answers chat questions about whether full presence is withheld until the kingdom comes (he says it is not: he draws on the Eden serpent story and Genesis 1), about the book of Job (he says most scholars think it was written in stages), and about the "no more sea" of Revelation 21 (he reads it as no more division between peoples, citing his own earlier blog article).

The talk ends with the Matthew 28:18-20 commission, a closing prayer, and an invitation to a Zoom fellowship and a book club on a vegan-themed book. The argument is devotional and does not engage historical questions about the first followers, but it is relevant to the repo's interest in what Jesus taught about the kingdom and the restoration of creation.

## Key Teachings

### God's presence is everywhere, not confined to a place or a priest

Using 1 Kings 19:11-13, Wescoe argues that God was not in the wind, earthquake or fire but in "a still small voice," so believers must "calm down and listen." He pairs this with Psalm 139:7-10 ("If I ascend into heaven, you are there... if I make my bed in Sheol, behold, you are there") to argue that God is reachable wherever a person is. He contrasts this with a picture of God "far removed," reachable only through "some priest sacrificing animals" or "someone climb a mountain." That contrast is his main theological point in this section, though he does not engage the Temple or sacrificial system historically.

### Joy, song and prayer as ways of remembering God's nearness

Psalm 16:11 ("In your presence is fullness of joy") and Psalm 100:1-2 are used to argue that joy is a fruit of experiencing God's presence and can persist in hardship. Wescoe treats singing (including "if we don't have a good singing voice") and continual prayer ("pray always," cited simply as "Thessalonians") as ongoing practices of communion. He adds that "It's not about doing these things to draw God closer. It's about doing these things to remember that he's already close."

### Nature and animals as reminders of God

Citing Job 12:7-10 ("ask the beasts and they will teach you"), Wescoe recommends going into nature and reports that a squirrel asking for a peanut reminds him of "the innocence and the beauty of God's plan." He links this to Jesus going off alone and Moses's forty days on the mountain. He also mentions Mark 1:13 (through a chat reader's comment about wild animals in the wilderness) and Isaiah 11 as a "glimmer" of a kingdom where animals are not afraid of people.

### The kingdom is already within believers and is brought about through them

Matthew 19:26 ("With God all things are possible") and Matthew 4:17 (repentance as "turning away" from what blocks God's presence) anchor his claim that God's kingdom can come "in this life." Luke 17:20-21 ("the kingdom of God is within you") and the light metaphor (John 8:12 is alluded to through "I am the light of the world," without reference) support the idea that believers shine God's light into a dark world. Wescoe distinguishes the future full restoration (Acts 3:20-21; Revelation 21:1-4, no more death, sorrow or pain) from present assignment: "we don't want to fast forward through this life now to try to get there."

### Obedience, Spirit and the Matthew 28 commission

Wescoe ties John 14:23 to "keep my word" and to obedience to Jesus's teaching. He reads Matthew 28:18-20 (the last three verses of Matthew, he notes) as Jesus sending disciples "to observe all the things I have commanded you," and he asks a chat questioner which commandments are meant. He answers that Exodus 20 is one place, but that Jesus's commands, "including... the sermon on the mount," are the main reference, and he describes this as "putting on the yoke of the Lord." He also argues that God's Spirit is the gift of Luke 11-style reasoning (a "how much more" from a human father to the heavenly Father, which he attributes to Jesus without a verse) and that love is God's nature.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **God is distant and must be approached through priests, sacrifice or mountain-climbing.** Wescoe says God is "ready and willing to come into our lives even now" and is found wherever one is (1 Kings 19; Psalm 139).
- **God's full presence is withheld until the kingdom comes.** Answering a chat question, he says God "has not withheld anything from us" and that the Eden serpent's claim was a lie.
- **The kingdom of God on earth is impossible.** He answers with Matthew 19:26: "if man were trying to bring it about, maybe that's not possible. But this is God who's going to be bringing it about."
- **You must be worthy or qualified to experience God.** He says "if you're ready to follow him, then you're qualified," and cites "God doesn't call the equipped. He equips the called."
- **Repentance means only feeling sorry.** He says repentance is "the turning around," a turning away from what blocks God's presence (Matthew 4:17).
