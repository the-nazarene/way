## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks what the Bible says about goodness and what that should mean for how people live. He takes Psalm 34:8 ("taste and see that the Lord is good") as the starting point and argues that God's goodness is closely tied to mercy and compassion extended to all creation (Psalm 145:8-9). Working through passages in Genesis, the prophets, the Gospels, Paul and James, he argues that God alone is good (Mark 10:17-22), that humans are made in God's image to reflect that goodness, and that the standard for "good" is the state of creation "in the beginning" (Genesis 1, Matthew 19:8). On this reading, the plant-based diet of Genesis 1:29-30 is the only diet the Bible calls "very good," and the prophets' and Jesus' criticism of sacrifice and violence follows from the same standard.

His main methods are close reading of cited verses, appeal to the Clementine Homilies' test that what accords with creation is of God (which he attributes to his own earlier talk), and harmonizing apparent conflicts. He reads Genesis 9 and Leviticus as human concessions or failures rather than God's ideal, and he reads Romans 8:28 as meaning that God can repurpose evil but does not plan it. He says these are his own interpretations and admits some points (such as the meaning of "heaping coals of fire") are uncertain.

The talk is a live session, delivered late because of technical problems, and it ends with a long chat Q&A in which Craig answers commenters on veganism, enemy-love, suffering, and the phrase "everything happens for a reason." The closing prayer asks for the kingdom to come "on earth as it is in heaven," which Craig presents as the goal of Jesus' teaching.

## Key Teachings

### God alone is good, and his goodness is mercy toward all creation
Craig begins with Psalm 145:8-9, where God is "good to all" and his "tender mercies are over all his works," and equates that goodness with compassion. He then reads Mark 10:17-22 as Jesus saying "God alone is good." He gives two readings of Jesus' question "why do you call me good?" depending on one's view of Jesus' divinity. His own reading is that Jesus is hinting he is God, citing the Gospel of John's "word of God that became flesh." Either way, he takes the central point to be that human goodness must reflect the one good God. He adds Micah 6:8 ("do justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God") as the summary of what God requires.

### Image-bearing, dominion and the "very good" diet
From Genesis 1:28-31, Craig argues that humans' dominion over animals should look like God's compassion, not exploitation. He says the only food God gives humans in Genesis 1 is seed-bearing plants and fruit, and that God calls this arrangement "very good." He proposes this as the diet that "reflects God's image and likeness" and says it causes no harm to God's other creatures. He links this to Isaiah 11:6-9, where a "little child" leads the wolf and lamb and "they shall not hurt nor destroy," which he reads as the kingdom's eventual state.

### Testing, and the rule of "in the beginning"
Craig uses Isaiah 5:20 ("Woe to those who call evil good and good evil") and 1 Thessalonians 5:21 ("Test all things. Hold fast to what is good") to argue that people must not accept claims about what is good at face value. He says the test is the Clementine Homilies' principle, "whatever is in accordance with the creation is of God," and that which opposes creation's original form is false. He pairs this with Matthew 19:8, where Jesus says Moses permitted divorce "because of the hardness of your hearts, but from the beginning it was not so." Craig takes this as a model: a Torah provision is not automatically good simply because it is written, and the test is Genesis 1. He applies this to sacrifice and to diet.

### Overcoming evil with good, and exposing darkness
Using Romans 12:20-21, Craig argues that believers should feed enemies rather than starve or besiege them, and overcome evil with good. He offers a best-guess reading of "heaping coals of fire" as providing what an enemy needs, and says he is unsure that is correct. From Romans 12:9 he says believers should "abhor" evil in the sense of exposing it, as light dispels darkness, not by fighting it with evil. He applies this to animal agriculture, animal experimentation, the fur trade, and other forms of human violence and exploitation, saying believers should not participate in them and should call people out of them.

### God does not change his mind about meat
Responding to the usual reading of Genesis 9 as permission to eat animals, Craig argues from James 1:17 ("no variation or shadow of turning") that God does not change course. He says Genesis 1:30 gave the green herb to the animals, not to humans, and that the humans' later permission to eat the animals reflects a failure of stewardship rather than a new divine ideal. He adds that the covenant with the animals in Genesis 9 would make no sense if they were "menu items." This is one of the points where he most directly addresses a traditional reading, and he says he is not rejecting Paul but people who "twist" Paul's words.

### Everything does not happen for a reason
Craig reads Romans 8:28 ("all things work together for good to those who love God") as saying God can use harm done by the adversary (the "hashatan") for good, not that God plans the harm. He uses Genesis 50:20 (Joseph: "you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good") as his example. He returns to this in the Q&A, saying the common phrase "everything happens for a reason" is not in the Bible and is "directly opposed" to its teaching, because the reason is the adversary's, even if God redeems it.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Everything happens for a reason; God sends suffering for a purpose."** Craig says this phrase is not in the Bible and is opposed to Romans 8:28 and Genesis 50:20. The adversary intends the harm; God can use it for good.
- **"God changed his mind after the flood and allowed people to eat animals."** Craig argues from James 1:17 and Genesis 1:30 that God does not change course. The human permission reflects a failure of stewardship, and the Genesis 9 covenant with animals would make no sense if they were "menu items."
- **"If the Torah permits something, it is good."** Craig says that, per Matthew 19:8, Leviticus allowances such as divorce certificates are not the standard. The standard is "from the beginning," as in Genesis 1.
- **"Paul's letters allow what Genesis and Jesus forbid, so Paul overrides the rest."** Craig says he is not rejecting Paul, only people who twist Paul's words to mean the opposite of his teaching.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Jesus vs. Paul:** Craig does not set Jesus against Paul. He treats Paul's letters (Romans 12, Galatians 5, 1 Thessalonians 5) as supporting the same standard of goodness as Genesis and Jesus, and he uses Matthew 19:8 as the principle that the "beginning" trumps later provisions. He does not discuss James of Jerusalem or the Jerusalem community.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet, nonviolence:** This is the talk's core. Craig argues that the prophets and Jesus opposed the sacrificial system (citing Isaiah 5 and the temple incident), that Genesis 1 sets the only "very good" diet, and that Romans 12 calls for overcoming violence with good. He presents these as applications of his reading, not as the only possible reading.
- **Kingdom on earth:** He links the Lord's Prayer ("on earth as it is in heaven"), Matthew 18:3-4, and Isaiah 11:6-9 to a future state where animals and people live without harm, and says believers should live that goal now.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Craig's reading of Mark 10:18 is that Jesus implicitly claims to be God, based on John's Gospel. He presents this as his own view, noting that others read the same verse the opposite way.
- **Resurrection, afterlife, hell:** Not discussed beyond a brief reference to "eternal life" in the Mark 10 story.
- **Gospels and canon:** Not discussed beyond his use of John's Gospel and his reliance on Clementine Homilies for a test of truth.
