## Summary

Wescoe takes up a common objection: that vegans who value animals "worship the creature rather than the Creator," as Romans 1:25 says of those who "worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator." He argues the charge is backwards. In his reading, Romans 1 draws on Genesis 1, where God gives humans dominion, stewardship and plant-based food. The "creature" that people serve is their own appetite and flesh, not the animals, and eating animals is the act of exchanging God's truth for a lie.

His main evidence is a chain of texts. He reads Genesis 1:26-29 and 1:20-25 as showing that animals were blessed and called "good" before humans were made, which makes them "neighbors" under the command to love one's neighbor (Leviticus 19:18, quoted in Luke 10:27). He reads Jeremiah 7-8 and Jesus' temple action as condemning violent sacrifice, and cites the prophetic line "I desire mercy, not sacrifice." He reads Numbers 11 as a warning that craving for meat leads to death. He then tests meat-eating against the fruit of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22-25) and against the commands to love (John 14:15, John 15:12, 1 John 4:8-16).

The talk ends with a prayer and a long Q&A. Wescoe answers viewers on love versus niceness, judgment, humility, free will and the problem of animal suffering, and points to his own lessons for fuller treatment. He does not directly engage the New Testament texts on food that a viewer raises (Acts 10 and the post-flood permission), beyond calling them the same "excuses" as other objections. The talk is a sermon-style exposition rather than a historical study, and it contains no citations to ancient sources or scholarship.

### Key Teachings

### 1. Romans 1 is read through Genesis 1
Wescoe says Romans 1:20-23 refers back to "the beginning of creation," and therefore to Genesis 1. He reads Genesis 1:29 ("every herb that yields seed... To you it shall be for food") as God's original diet for humans. "Glorifying God" then means keeping what God declared from the beginning, and corrupting God's image means serving oneself. In his reading, the "creature" in Romans 1:25 is humanity serving its own flesh and "belly" (Philippians 3:19).

### 2. "Exchanging the truth for a lie" means following lying scribes
He reads Jeremiah 8:8 ("the false pen of the scribe certainly works falsehood") as a picture of teachers who claim to be wise while following lies. He ties this to Jesus in the temple, quoting Jeremiah 7:11 ("a den of thieves" / "den of violence"). In his account, the temple's animal sacrifice is the lie. Jesus is "the truth" (he cites John 14, "I'm the way, the life, and the truth") and so following Jesus means rejecting the lie.

### 3. Numbers 11: craving for meat is a judgment
He presents the quail story (Numbers 11:18-20, 33-34) as God "giving them over" to their cravings. The plague comes "while the meat was still between their teeth," and the place is named Kibroth-hattaavah ("graves of craving," which he glosses as "grave of craving"). Wescoe treats this as the outcome of living for the flesh, and he insists God is not "permitting" or "blessing" the eating in the story.

### 4. Love and the fruit of the Spirit as the test
Using Galatians 5:22-25, Wescoe asks whether humans show love, peace, kindness, gentleness and self-control toward animals, or whether they "make our bodies a grave of craving." He moves from there to John 14:15 and John 15:12 (love as the commandment), 1 John 4:8-16 ("God is love"), and James 2:8 (the "royal law" of Leviticus 19:18). In his reading, these verses make animal flesh-eating incompatible with the fruit of the Spirit.

### 5. The Good Samaritan and animals as neighbors
He draws on Luke 10:25-37 but argues that Jesus turns the question "who is my neighbor?" back to "what it means to be neighborly." Because the animals were created and blessed before humans (Genesis 1:20-25 before 1:26-29), Wescoe concludes that animals are neighbors "from the beginning," and the command to love them predates humanity. He reinforces this with Luke 6:36 ("be merciful") and Luke 12:42-44 (the faithful steward who is found doing the master's will).

### 6. Mercy, not sacrifice
He quotes "I desire mercy, not sacrifice" and attributes it to "all the prophets," citing Hosea (spoken in the talk as "Hosea 3:6"; the line appears at Hosea 6:6). He adds a David line about sacrifice (no reference given), Jeremiah's statement that God did not command animal sacrifice when bringing Israel out of Egypt (no reference given), and Micah 6:8 (paraphrased, in Q&A). For Wescoe, this makes the sacrificial system a human corruption that God rejects.

### Misconceptions Addressed

- **That vegans who care for animals are worshiping the creature instead of God.** Wescoe argues that the "creature" in Romans 1:25 is humans serving their own flesh, and that eating animals is what the passage condemns.
- **That God permits or blesses meat-eating (e.g., Jesus ate fish, God changed his mind after the flood, Acts 10 "rise, kill, and eat").** He calls these "excuses" that reflect the same attitude as worldly rationalizations. He does not engage the texts themselves in detail.
- **That God commanded animal sacrifice.** He cites Hosea, David and Jeremiah ("I did not command animal sacrifices") to argue that God's desire is mercy.
- **That loving animals means being "nice," or that moral criticism is judgmental.** He says love is not always niceness, and that encouraging people toward what is best is not condemning them to death.

### Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Jesus vs. Paul:** The talk does not set Paul against Jesus. It reads Romans 1 as continuous with Genesis, the prophets, and Jesus' teachings, with no discussion of Paul's own position on food or the law. Paul is treated as an authority.
- **Torah and sacrifice:** The talk's strongest historical claim is that the prophets and Jesus prefer mercy over sacrifice (Hosea, Jeremiah 7, Micah 6:8). It treats animal sacrifice as a corruption, not as God's command, and presents this as a reading of the texts rather than a historical reconstruction.
- **Diet:** Genesis 1:29 is the basis for a plant-based original diet, and Wescoe reads it as God's intention for humans. The Numbers 11 quail story is used as a warning against meat-craving.
- **Nonviolence:** The temple scene is read as Jesus condemning "violence" in sacrifice, and mercy is set as the standard for how humans treat animals.
- **Kingdom on earth:** He closes by linking the Lord's Prayer ("your will be done on earth as it is in heaven") to living stewardship now.
- **Gospels and canon:** The talk treats Luke, John, James and the Torah as one harmonized witness, without addressing textual or compositional questions.
