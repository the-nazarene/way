## Summary

Craig Wescoe, host of Creation Care Church's Friday night live talk, takes Hebrews 10:24-25 ("let us not neglect meeting together... but let us encourage one another") as the text for a talk on community. He argues that God made humans for community from the start, and that this community includes animals. His main evidence is a chain of Bible passages read in order: Genesis 2:18 ("it is not good that man should be alone"), the Ten Commandments in Exodus 20 (including the Sabbath, which he says applies to "your cattle"), the feasts in Deuteronomy 16, and Paul's body metaphor in Romans 12 and 1 Corinthians 12. He ties each passage to a vegan reading of Genesis 1:29-31 and presents the "Shabbat made for man" saying of Jesus as an example of compassion overriding rest.

Wescoe also presents community as a practical obligation: confessing sins and praying for one another (James 5:16), bearing one another's burdens (Galatians 6:2), sharing food and resources as in Acts, and being a visible, separate community that "proves" God's will to others (Romans 12:2). He says online fellowship is a necessary substitute for in-person gathering, given that members live across the world, and he walks through the church's ministries (live talks, Zoom fellowship, a monthly book club, a monthly prayer meeting, and a discipleship school).

Most of the second half is a live question-and-answer session with chat participants. Wescoe answers comments about loneliness as a vegan Christian, animal exploitation, the image of God, and translations (he calls the New Living Translation a paraphrase that he finds right about 10% of the time). The talk ends with a closing prayer, a plug for upcoming events, and a reminder to join the Zoom fellowship.

## Key Teachings

### Community as the created purpose
Wescoe reads Genesis 1 as showing that everything God made is "very good," until Genesis 2:18 says it is "not good that man should be alone." He concludes that God designed humans for community, and that the community is meant to include animals. He reads Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 16 as showing that the Sabbath and feasts ("moadim," appointed times) are gatherings that the animals also join. He calls the Sabbath "the sign of his covenant," and says that "not forsaking the assembling together once a week" is part of that sign.

### God's presence in the gathered community ("Emmanuel")
He interprets Matthew 18:20 ("where two or three are gathered in my name, I am there") as the New Testament form of God dwelling with his people, the same idea as the tabernacle in the wilderness and the cloud over the camp. He ties this to his habit of closing prayers with "Emmanuel, God with us," and to the live chat count ("according to Streamyard, it says there's 24 members present").

### The body of Christ and spiritual gifts (Paul)
Wescoe relies on Romans 12:4-5 and 1 Corinthians 12:27 for the image of many members in one body, with Christ as the head giving instructions. He uses the analogy to argue against comparing one's usefulness with others' (the eye and the heart do different jobs). He says that members can discover their gifts by participating in the community over time, and that spiritual-gift tests are available through Sarah Scott. He describes the scattered church as similar to the second-century diaspora, with internet communication making unity possible.

### Ten Commandments extended to animals
Wescoe reads the Sabbath command in Exodus 20:8-11 as including "your cattle" and argues that the commandments apply to animals as well as humans. He reads Matthew 19:17-19 as Jesus telling the questioner to love "your neighbor," and says that Jesus did not limit neighbors to humans. He reads the coveting commandment as covering animal products: milk meant for a calf, flesh meant for an animal, and eggs meant for hatching. He presents murder, adultery, theft and false witness as acts that destroy community.

### Diet as part of community: Genesis 1:29-31 and the harvest festivals
He reads Genesis 1:29 and 1:30 as God's original diet (plants and fruit) for both humans and animals. He reads Genesis 2:15 ("to tend and keep it") as humans being gardeners who grow food for themselves and for the animals. He then reads Deuteronomy 16:13-17 as requiring three yearly gatherings where people bring the produce of their hands, and he calls this "literally a fruit festival." He says the older members should be cared for "according to their abilities, according to their need, as it says in Acts" (no verse given). He mentions that the current week is Sukkot and that he is camping in his van for it.

### Mutual care: confession, prayer and bearing burdens
Wescoe reads James 5:16 ("confess your sins to one another") as requiring open confession inside the community, followed by prayer. He reads Galatians 6:2 ("bear one another's burdens and so fulfill the law of Christ") and glosses the "law of Christ" as "the Torah of Christ," the teachings of Christ. He lists practical examples of burden-bearing: financial help, food, and welcoming isolated people. He also cites 1 John 1:7, 1 John 4:11, Acts 2:42 and John 13:35 ("by this all will know that you are my disciples") to support the point that love among believers is the visible mark of discipleship.

### Image of God, dominion, and the vegan reading of Genesis 1
In answer to a chat comment that animals are not made in God's image, Wescoe reads Genesis 1:26 and 1:28 and argues that the image of God is given to humans alone. He then argues that the first thing God says about "dominion" is the food instruction of Genesis 1:28-29, so true dominion in God's image means being vegan. He says that exploiting animals is "the exact opposite" and that it reflects "the image of the adversary."

### The Sabbath and rescue: "Shabbat is made for man"
Answering a question about Luke 13:15 and Matthew 12:11 (rescuing an animal on the Sabbath), Wescoe argues that Jesus's saying that "man is not made for the Shabbat, but Shabbat is made for man" means that the Sabbath is a gift, not a reason to neglect compassion. He uses this to argue that Christians should rescue animals in distress, and he links it to campaigns against animal agriculture, zoos and laboratory testing.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Animals are not made in God's image, so they may be used.** Wescoe answers that Genesis 1:26 gives the image to humans, but that Genesis 1:28-29 defines dominion as a plant-based diet, so exploitation contradicts the image instead of justifying it.
- **The Sabbath forbids all work, including rescue.** Wescoe reads Jesus's "Shabbat is made for man" saying as making compassion (rescuing an animal in a pit, for example) more important than rigid rest.
- **Being a vegan Christian means being isolated.** He acknowledges that many vegans feel lonely in church and that Christian vegans feel isolated among vegans, and presents online fellowship and in-person groups (such as a revived Southern California group) as ways to find community.
- **Paraphrase translations are reliable.** He says the New Living Translation and The Message are not word-for-word and are "hit or miss," and warns against treating them as accurate renderings.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Wescoe uses Paul's body metaphor (Romans 12:4-5; 1 Corinthians 12:27) and Romans 12:2 as authorities, and frames Jesus's commands (John 13:35; Matthew 19:17-19; "Shabbat is made for man") in the same frame. The talk does not separate Jesus's teaching from Paul's.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet and nonviolence:** The talk is almost entirely about Torah (the Ten Commandments, Sabbath, feasts) and diet (Genesis 1:29-30; Deuteronomy 16). Sacrifice is not discussed. Animal welfare is the main ethical focus, not human nonviolence.
- **Gospels and canon:** Only a brief passage on Marcion's canon ordering of Galatians, plus the mention of his research on the Gospel of Marcion. No argument is made about the canon's formation.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** The talk reads Jesus as "Christ as our head" and "Emmanuel," with God dwelling among the gathered community (Matthew 18:20). Wescoe does not discuss the question in historical terms.
- **Kingdom on earth:** Only the closing prayer ("your kingdom to come, your will to be done on earth as it is in heaven") and the Isaiah 11:9 reference to a future peaceable kingdom.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Only Acts 2:42 and James 5:16 are used, as descriptions of early community practice, not as historical claims about the Jerusalem church.
