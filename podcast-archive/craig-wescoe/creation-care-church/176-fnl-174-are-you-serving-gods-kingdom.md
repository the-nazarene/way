## Summary

Wescoe asks what it means to serve God's kingdom, and answers that serving it means doing God's will "on earth as it is in heaven" (Matthew 6:10). He argues that this will includes nonviolence toward animals and a plant-based diet for humans and animals, which he finds in Genesis 1:29-30. His main evidence is a chain of texts: Jesus's example of service (Matthew 20:28), his statement that his kingdom is "not of this world" (John 18:36), Isaiah 11:6-9 and 46:9-10 (read as God declaring "the end from the beginning"), and Genesis 1 on human "dominion." He then reads Matthew 11:12 ("the violent take it by force") as a condemnation of animal sacrifice and identifies the violent force with the adversary, using Ezekiel 28 as his reference to Satan. He ends with James 2:8 (the "royal law"), Luke 12:42-44 (the master's return), Mark 9:35 and Matthew 23:11-12 (servanthood), and the Rebecca story in Genesis 24 as a model of compassion toward animals. He presents James the brother of Jesus as a vegan who opposed animal sacrifice, a claim he says rests on "all of our indications."

The second half is mostly Creation Care Church business: a recap of the annual meeting in which Craig Wescoe was re-elected president, Tracy Ford was vice president, Tanya Comfort secretary, Luke Daniels treasurer, and Ross Smith trustee, followed by a live Q&A on whether animals sin, whether Jesus died for animals, and how to handle non-vegan churches. The talk ends with a closing prayer and an invitation to the Zoom fellowship. Many of the central claims (that the prophets and apostles were vegan, that James was vegan, that animal flesh-eating is "demonic idolatry") are stated by Wescoe as his own reading or belief and are not supported in the talk by the ancient sources themselves.

## Key Teachings

### Kingdom as God's will, served through the king's example
Wescoe reads Matthew 20:28 as showing that Jesus, the king, serves rather than demanding service, so members of his kingdom should also serve. He contrasts Jesus's kingdom with worldly kingdoms (barricades, armies, conquest, treasure) using John 18:36. He reads the Lord's Prayer ("Your kingdom come. Your will be done on earth as it is in heaven," Matthew 6:10) as a prayer for earth to be restored to the state of heaven, where God's will is done. On this reading, the kingdom is about "those doing the will of God," and he says the idea "has been established from the beginning," not newly invented by Jesus.

### Isaiah 11 and 46 as "the end declared from the beginning"
Wescoe uses Isaiah 11:6-9 (the wolf with the lamb, the lion eating straw, "They shall not hurt nor destroy in all my holy mountain") as a prophecy of a peaceable, plant-eating kingdom. He reads Isaiah 46:9-10 ("declaring the end from the beginning") as meaning that this end was already declared in Genesis. He links the Hebrew principle of not hurting or destroying to the Hebrew word "lora," and offers a Sanskrit gloss on the same idea (captioned as "aims," presumably ahimsa). These glosses are his own comparative remarks; he does not cite a source for them in the talk.

### Genesis 1: dominion as stewardship, plant-based food for all
Wescoe reads Genesis 1:26-31 as giving humans "dominion" in God's image, but insists dominion means the opposite of domination: "no hurting or destroying." He treats Genesis 1:29-30 (green herbs and fruit as food for humans and "every beast of the earth") as the original, "entirely vegan" design, and calls it "God's very good will." He says the final restoration in Isaiah returns to this original state. In the Q&A he identifies the "us" of Genesis 1:26 with either God and the holy angels (citing Jubilees and an unnamed Psalms or Job passage on the "morning stars") or the Trinity (citing John 1). He says he finds these two readings most likely.

### Violence, sacrifice, and the adversary
Matthew 11:12-13 ("from the days of John the Baptist until now, the kingdom of heaven suffers violence") is read by Wescoe as the prophets, from Isaiah, Amos, Samuel, David, and Hosea, condemning animal sacrifice. He ties this to Jesus's temple cleansing, quoting Jeremiah 7:11 (as he renders it, "You've made my father's house a den of violence"). Ezekiel 28:16-17, which he says is "pretty universally acknowledged" as a description of Satan even though it is addressed to the king of Tyre, is used to identify the violent "taker" with the hashatan (the adversary), whose pride and violence got him cast out of the holy mountain. In the Q&A he answers a question on whether violence can come from God: "where there's violence, that can't be from God. It's from the adversary." He also argues that meat-eating requires violence, so it belongs to the adversary.

### James as the "servant of all" and a vegan
Wescoe says Jesus established his brother James as head of the Jerusalem church, citing "Gospel of Thomas chapter 12" (captioned uncertainly; he says "I think") and Acts 15, where James gives the declaration at the Jerusalem Council. He then says "all of our indications of James is that he was a vegan" and that he opposed animal sacrifice like Jesus did. No specific ancient text on James's diet is quoted in the talk, so this claim is his inference from the sources he names.

### Jesus's death and animal sacrifice
Wescoe argues (in answer to a question) that Jesus's temple disruption was aimed at ending the animal sacrificial system, which he says caused the Sadducees to seek his death. He reads Hebrews as teaching that Jesus's offering of himself ended the need for animal sacrifice. On whether Jesus died for animals, he says "I believe yes," citing Hosea 2:18 (captioned as "21:18") and Genesis 9's covenant "with the animals," and the Ecclesiastes passage that animals share humanity's "same hope" (the reference is not given in the captions). He argues that God "doesn't forget" animals, pointing to the Ten Commandments' Sabbath and covetousness commands.

### Human responsibility for animals
In the Q&A on whether animals sin, Wescoe says violence would be sin, but that animals are not fully responsible because they lack awareness of right and wrong (agreeing with a commenter). He places the main blame on humans, whom God gave dominion, and lists selective breeding (cats for hunting, cows for milk, turkeys and pigs for meat, sheep for wool) as human sin against animals. He says he will teach more on this later.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- The popular belief that "dominion" over animals (Genesis 1:26-28) licenses killing or exploiting them. Wescoe says dominion is stewardship, and that the original design was plant-based (Genesis 1:29-30).
- The popular belief that Jesus's death was only for humans. Wescoe says "I believe yes," that Jesus died for animals as well, citing Genesis 9, Hosea, and Ecclesiastes.
- The popular reading that only meat "offered to idols" is a problem (a phrase often read as a narrow prohibition). Wescoe says that all animal-flesh eating is demonic idolatry.
- The popular view that animal sacrifice was simply part of God's will. Wescoe reads the prophets (Matthew 11:12-13 and Jeremiah 7:11) as condemning it.
