## Summary

Wescoe, speaking as the leader of Creation Care Church in a live Friday talk, asks how a believer can cast out demons and keep them out permanently. His answer is that demons are the disembodied spirits of the Nephilim giants of Genesis 6, who need human or animal bodies to act and whose appetite is violence and bloodshed. The main evidence is a chain of texts: Luke 10:17 and Matthew 17:21 for the authority and method of exorcism; Matthew 8 and Mark 5 for the Gerasene demoniac and the drowned herd; 1 Enoch 7:3-5 for the origin of the giants; 1 Corinthians 10:21 with the Clementine Homilies (7.4, 8.20, 9.9) for the "table of demons"; and Acts 15:28 with Irenaeus, Against Heresies 3.12.14, for the early abstention from blood and flesh. From this he argues that eating flesh and animal sacrifice (which he equates with the slaughterhouse and the priestly system in the Torah of Yahweh) are the doorway by which demons enter, and that the cure is to stop "sitting at the table of demons," to fast, to put on the armor of Ephesians 6, and to fill the body with Christ's light.

The talk is delivered as a running sermon with live chat, and much of its second half is responses to viewers' questions (on whether a verse is in Matthew 17:21, whether "ghosts" are demons, whether Jewish animal offerings were demonic, and whether one can cast out a demon from a meat-eater). Wescoe presents these answers as his own view and says he has personally seen people "make a decision" to change diet after being shown their situation, which he calls God doing the casting out. The talk ends with a closing prayer and announcements about fellowship, a book club, and another channel's service. No independent evidence is offered for the claims that demons are Nephilim spirits or that the Clementine Homilies preserve teaching going back to Jesus; these are the speaker's readings, which he himself qualifies as "a little bit different" from ghosts and as "a theory" in other places.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Demons are the spirits of the Nephilim, and their nature is violence
Wescoe reads Genesis 6:11-13 ("the earth was filled with violence") as describing the sons of God (ben elohim) and their giant offspring. He quotes 1 Enoch 7:3-5 as saying the giants consumed the produce of men, then turned to eating humans, animals, and drinking their blood, and he treats this as the origin of flesh-eating and blood-drinking. He then says the giants' disembodied spirits "lived on the earth as demons" after death, and that those demons keep the same appetite for violence. The Gerasene demoniac (Matthew 8:28-32; Mark 5:1-15) is his main illustration: the man was violent, the legion of demons moved into the swine, and the herd ran into the sea. He presents the drowning as the demons' violence working through the pigs, not as Jesus's act. (The transcript garbles "Gergesenes" as "Gurgosines" and Mark 5:1-15 as "5:E1 15.")

### 2. The "table of demons" and the Jerusalem decree
The argument turns on 1 Corinthians 10:21 ("You cannot drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons too"). Wescoe says this is the only explicit New Testament mention of the "table of demons," so he turns to the Clementine Homilies, which he dates "at least to the middle of the second century" while saying they "contain a lot of material that would go back to Jesus and the apostles." In Homily 7.4 (speaking as Peter), he reads a list of things to abstain from: food offered to idols, dead carcasses, animals suffocated or taken by beasts, and blood. He says the golden rule is applied to animals in the same passage. He connects this to the Jerusalem Council decree in Acts 15:28 and to Irenaeus, Against Heresies 3.12.14, which he calls "the earliest witness" of the same four prohibitions. The point he draws is that abstaining from flesh and blood keeps the believer from the demons' table.

### 3. Animal sacrifice and slaughter as worship of demons
Wescoe reads idolatry and animal sacrifice throughout Scripture as demon worship. He cites Paul's statement that worshiping statues is worshiping demons, and he reads Homily 8.20 as saying that whoever "sacrifices to them or partakes with them of their table" becomes subject to them. He challenges the idea that animal sacrifice is only ancient history, arguing that slaughterhouses and meat on the grill are today's "altar." In the Q&A he says the animal offerings by Jewish priests were in effect demonic. To support this he cites Jeremiah 8:8 ("the lying pen of the scribe") and Jeremiah 7:22-24 (God never commanded burnt offerings) against the Torah of Yahweh, especially Leviticus, which he says commands sacrifice. He suggests this was a "second Torah," a priestly law that "likely came about significantly later," which replaced the prophetic Torah of Elohim. He explicitly says this was a sub-branch of Judaism and not Jews as a whole, since "all the prophets and all the the Essenes were against this," and that the whole pagan world did the same.

### 4. The body as temple and the "den of violence"
From 1 Corinthians 6:19-20 Wescoe argues that the body belongs to God and is a temple for the Holy Spirit, so it must not be defiled by demonic influence. He reads Matthew 21:12-13, where Jesus overturns the tables in the temple and quotes Jeremiah 7:11 ("you have made it a den of violence"). He says the Greek word for "robbers" in Jeremiah refers to "violent ones, shedders of flesh," and that the temple had become a place of animal sales and slaughter. His application is that a believer's body and home can become a "den of violence" in the same way, and that Jesus's act is a model for overturning one's own "table of demons."

### 5. Keeping demons out: prayer, fasting, armor, light, and community
Wescoe reads Matthew 17:21 ("this kind does not go out except by prayer and fasting") and Matthew 12:45 (the returning spirits are "more wicked than itself") as warnings that an exorcised demon can return with others if the condition that attracted it is not changed. He recommends fasting from flesh as a practical way to break an addiction, and he reads Isaiah's fast passage ("break every yoke") as applying. He then uses Ephesians 6:10-12 as the armor against "principalities" and "powers," James 4:7 and Ephesians 4:27 ("do not give the devil a foothold"), and Philippians 1:9-11 ("filled with the fruits of righteousness") as the layers of defense: knowledge, armor, light in the house, and a godly community that will not "corrupt good character" (he says "bad company corrupts good character"). He describes these as "three layers of protection."

### 6. Atonement: the blood of Jesus as the end of animal sacrifice
In the Q&A, answering a viewer on Genesis 4:4 and Hebrews 9:22, Wescoe says Hebrews was written to people following "the Torah of Yahweh" to show that Jesus fulfills it. He reads Hebrews 10:4 ("the blood of animals cannot take away sin") and argues that Jesus's sacrifice, offered once for all by a high priest who remains forever, ends the sacrificial system and "no more bloodshed" is needed. He presents this as consistent with his own dietary conclusions, though the talk does not clearly reconcile the two in the same argument.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Demons are just ghosts.** Wescoe says "kind of," but distinguishes them: demons "can't be seen in any way and they can't affect the world," so they can't knock over objects, and they "need a body to be able to do anything in the world."
- **Animal sacrifice is a thing of the past.** He argues it continues through slaughterhouses and meat-eating, which he says are the "temples today."
- **The Jews were offering sacrifices to Yahweh, so Yahweh accepted them.** He argues the offerings were made by priests who had turned the prophetic Torah into a priestly law, and that the prophets and Essenes opposed them. He says the whole pagan world did the same.
- **The NIV removes verses from the Bible.** Wescoe says the difference is manuscript evidence: some manuscripts lack Matthew 17:21 and others have it, so translators choose differently. He favors the manuscripts that include it.
- **"Thou shalt not kill" refers only to humans.** He argues from the Ten Commandments that since other commandments mention animals, the commandment also covers them.
- **Watching a violent film is itself demonic.** He says the film is not itself demonic, but it "can sort of persuade us" and normalize violence, so avoidance is wise.
