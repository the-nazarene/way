## Summary

Wescoe asks what Genesis 15:6 means when it says Abram "believed the LORD, and it was credited to him as righteousness," and how a person can tell whether they are "believing righteously." He reads Genesis 15 through 22 in sequence and argues that righteous belief means accepting God's word and acting on it without demanding proof or taking matters into one's own hands. In his reading, Abram's first response, belief with no demand for evidence, is the model. Each later episode is a failure of that belief: Abram asks for a sign and gets a blood covenant (Genesis 15:7-21), takes Hagar at Sarai's suggestion (Genesis 16), laughs at the promise and accepts circumcision as a bargain (Genesis 17), and is then tested with the near-sacrifice of Isaac (Genesis 22). He reads the ram "caught in the thicket" as a sign that God did not want the sacrifice, and he connects the "lamb" that God "will provide" to Jesus, citing John 1 and John 3:16.

Wescoe then extends the argument to animals. He reads Genesis 1, where humans are given dominion and a plant-based diet, as God's original instruction, which the righteous "believe" by caring for animals and not shedding their blood. He cites Proverbs 12:10, Galatians 3:6, Hebrews 11, James 2, and John 8 (where Jesus contrasts Abraham's children with a "murderer from the beginning") in support. He is explicit that this is one of two readings: the traditional view treats Genesis as a factual account of Abraham's errors, while a "historical critical" or midrashic reading treats Abraham as righteous throughout and the failures as a story about the reader's own unbelief. He mentions a second-century tradition, drawn from the Clementine Homilies, that early followers of Jesus rejected Torah stories portraying God negatively, but he does not develop the point.

The talk ends with a Q&A from the live chat (on blind obedience, Ishmael, whether the ram was God's provision, and whether the Genesis 22 command came from God or from an adversary) and a closing prayer for a world that "believes righteously" and stops shedding blood. Several answers are tentative ("I'm not sure," "it's complicated"), and the speaker acknowledges that the traditional and alternate readings do not fully reconcile.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Righteous belief is accepting God's word and acting on it without requiring proof
Wescoe reads Genesis 15:1-6 as the core of "believing righteously": God promises a son and descendants "like the stars," and Abram simply accepts it. He says "Your word is good enough for me. I believe" is the whole of righteous belief. He uses Galatians 3:6 and Hebrews 11:8-10 to support this: faith is a readiness to act on a promise (Abraham "went out not knowing where he was going"), and James 2 is cited, though skipped for time, as saying faith shows in works.

### 2. Each later episode is a failure of belief
- **Genesis 15:8-21:** Abram asks "how shall I know" and God answers with the cutting of animals and a passing fire-pot. Wescoe reads this as a Chaldean covenant ritual (he says Jeremiah describes the same practice, without a verse) and says God "did not have to prove himself" in the first place.
- **Genesis 16:** Sarai proposes Hagar as a substitute. Wescoe calls this "trying to concoct our own plan."
- **Genesis 17:** Abraham laughs at the promise and bargains for Ishmael. Circumcision is described as another blood covenant.
- **Genesis 21:** Isaac is born, and Wescoe says the conflict between Ishmael's and Isaac's descendants (not read aloud) would have been avoided had Abraham simply believed at the start.

### 3. Genesis 22 as a test, and the "lamb" as Jesus
Wescoe says God's command to sacrifice Isaac was "a test," and that the angel's intervention shows God did not want the killing. He argues that Isaac's question "where is the lamb" and Abraham's reply that "God will provide for himself the lamb" point forward to the gift of God's own "only begotten son." He cites John 3:16 and the Baptist's "Behold the lamb of God who takes away the sins of the world" (John, no chapter given). He states that "Jesus is that lamb" and that the ram in the thicket was not the provision God intended. A question from the chat is answered with the claim that the adversary's voice, not God's, may have commanded the killing, though the speaker calls that "a reasonable midrash," not a settled reading.

### 4. Blood sacrifice is not what God desires
Wescoe argues that the sacrifices in Genesis (Noah in Genesis 8:20-21, Abraham at the thicket) were acts of human initiative, not divine commands. He cites Proverbs 12:10 ("The righteous care for the needs of the animals") and paraphrases a Davidic psalm on mercy rather than sacrifice (no verse given). He also says that God "doesn't need" to shed blood to make a covenant, so "when there's righteous belief, there's no shedding of animal blood."

### 5. Genesis 1 as God's original diet and a model for "dominion"
This is the talk's most developed application. Wescoe reads Genesis 1:26-31 as humans being made in God's image with dominion, and Genesis 1:29 as God's plant-based food instruction for both humans and animals. He calls this God's "original" instruction that God "sees" as "very good," and says righteous belief means accepting it: caring for animals and eating no animal flesh. He links this to the golden rule and to the "royal law" of neighbor-love (James, and Paul's letters, unspecified), noting the speaker's view that "neighbor" includes animals, since God created them first.

### 6. Two readings of the text: traditional and historical-critical
Wescoe distinguishes the traditional view, that Abraham really did these things and the lesson is to avoid repeating them, from a midrashic or historical-critical reading, in which Abraham is the righteous believer and the failures are a mirror for the reader. He says the second reading is "one way of sort of uh this is a sort of midrash on the Torah." He presents the second as more consistent with the story's outcome, since God fulfills the promise anyway. He does not claim certainty for either.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That God commanded Abraham to offer the ram.** Wescoe says the text never says God asked for a ram; the ram is Abraham's own choice, and God's provision was the lamb (Genesis 22:8, 13, as read by Wescoe).
- **That the Genesis 22 command reflects God's will.** He argues the command was a test and that God's stopping of the sacrifice shows he never wanted it. In the Q&A he allows that the voice may have been an adversary's.
- **That God requires blood sacrifice or blood covenants.** He reads Genesis 15, 17, and Proverbs 12:10 as showing that God does not need animals killed to make promises, and that sacrifice is human initiative (Noah, Abraham).
- **That the Bible's diet and animal instructions are neutral to Christian faith.** He argues that Genesis 1:29 and the royal law of neighbor-love include animals, so that eating animals is a failure of righteous belief.
