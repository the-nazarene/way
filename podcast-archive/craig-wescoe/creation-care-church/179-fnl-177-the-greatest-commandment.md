## Summary

Craig Wescoe, host of the Creation Care Church Friday live talk, asks what Jesus meant by "the first and greatest commandment" (Matthew 22:37-38), how it relates to the golden rule (Matthew 22:39, Leviticus 19:18), and what follows for veganism. He argues that the first commandment is wholehearted faithfulness to God, with no other gods and no idolatry, and that the second, loving one's neighbor, flows from it. For Wescoe, "neighbor" includes animals, and loving them means not killing or eating them.

His evidence is mostly a sequence of scriptural passages read in order. He starts with Genesis (the animals as neighbors, the food given in Genesis 1:29-30, and the seventh-day rest as a sign of a "vegan world"). He then reads the Decalogue as a unity in which each command, including the Sabbath, is an expression of faithfulness or of love for neighbor. He takes the golden calf episode in Exodus 32 as the paradigm of unfaithfulness, and he treats the recurring language of sacrificing animals and eating flesh and blood in those narratives as its defining mark. Several New Testament passages are then read as confirming this: Acts 15:29 (the Jerusalem decree, which he attributes to James), Revelation 2:14 and 2:20, and 1 Corinthians 10:14-22. A large part of the case for why animal sacrifice counts as demonic idolatry comes from the Book of Enoch, which Wescoe presents as the source of the origin of flesh-eating and of the demonic spirits behind sacrifice. He admits some of this rests on his own reading of Enoch.

The talk closes with a chat question-and-answer segment (Sabbath observance at work, why one is vegan, whether doctrine matters more than love, the Clementine Homilies' "key of knowledge," and Daniel 1), then a prayer and a fellowship call. Wescoe presents his positions as his own reading ("That's what I believe"), and several claims are asserted without citation.

## Key Teachings

### The first commandment is faithfulness, and the golden rule follows from it
Wescoe reads Matthew 22:37-38 as pointing back to Deuteronomy 6:5 and the second command as Leviticus 19:18. He argues the order matters: Luke 4:8 (Jesus refusing Satan with "You shall worship the Lord your God, and him only you shall serve") and the first three commands of Exodus 20 show that devotion to God comes before moral goodness. "It's not all about just being a good person." Good works should be the "outpouring" of that faithfulness, and he cites Hebrews 11:6 ("without faith it is impossible to please God") to say a person can be good and still be an unfaithful adulterer before God.

### Animals are included in "neighbor," and the Decalogue is read through that lens
Wescoe says Leviticus 19:18 is phrased "your neighbor" rather than "humans" because "that includes all flesh," and that animals were the first neighbors before humans existed. He reads the commands of Exodus 20:12-17 concretely: killing animals as murder, dairy as theft of the calf's milk, and coveting the milk or flesh that is "intended for" another creature. Dairy, he says, "breaks all of God's commandments." He describes the Sabbath as the command that "ties the two together," and he reads Genesis as showing the seventh-day rest following a world in which humans were already vegan.

### Idolatry is marked by sacrifice and the eating of flesh and blood
Wescoe's central claim is that "in nearly every instance" of unfaithfulness in the Bible, sacrificing animals and eating their flesh and blood is part of the idolatry. He points to Exodus 32:1-9 (the golden calf, burnt offerings, peace offerings, eating and drinking) and to Numbers 11, where the people crave meat and say they were better off in Egypt, as evidence that flesh-eating is tied to the idolatry of the wilderness. He connects this to the Noah narrative ("when they were eating and drinking") via the Book of Enoch, which he says identifies flesh- and blood-eating as the practice of the giants. He also says "If you eat flesh, then you consume blood. Period."

### The New Testament, in his reading, prohibits eating flesh and blood
Wescoe reads Acts 15:29 as the decree of the Jerusalem council, with James speaking, prohibiting things offered to idols, blood, and things strangled, and reads porneia as defilement broadly. He cites Irenaeus as an early witness to the decree with the golden rule added. He cites Revelation 2:14 and 2:20 as Jesus rebuking churches for eating things sacrificed to idols. He then reads 1 Corinthians 10:14-22, where Paul says the things the nations sacrifice are sacrificed "to demons and not to God," and concludes "You cannot drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons." For Wescoe, this makes the choice binary: "Either you're a murderer who ignores his commandments or you're faithful to God." He says in this passage Paul is "reiterating" the testimony of Enoch about demons.

### Demons, the Nephilim and the Book of Enoch
Wescoe explains that in the Book of Enoch the giants (the Nephilim, offspring of the "Ben Elohim" and human women) died as bodies, but their disembodied spirits stayed on earth as the demons of the New Testament. Those spirits, he says, must possess bodies to satisfy their "blood lust," and so they drive humans and animals to violence and sacrifice. This is the basis of his claim that "whenever people are building altars and sacrificing animals... they're doing it to demons." He is careful to distinguish fallen angels from demons when asked. He acknowledges that his reading draws on "my book, Book of Enoch study edition."

### Vegan practice, faith motivation, and the Sabbath in a chat Q&A
In the chat segment Wescoe says "It's not enough to be vegetarian. We got to be vegan." He grants that smoothies are not idolatry because God called fruit "very good," and that idolatry concerns the object of desire, not the intensity of it. On Sabbath work he says it is "between you and God," citing Jesus on lawful Sabbath good (saving an animal from a pit), and suggests talking to an employer about religious accommodation. Asked whether motive matters, he says he is vegan "first and foremost because of my faith," and that health, ethics, and environment are all subsumed under this faith. He also cites Ellen G. White on animal compassion, and Daniel 1 as an example of refusing defilement.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The greatest commandment is just the golden rule / being a good person.** Wescoe argues the first commandment is faithfulness to God, and the golden rule is its consequence, not its substitute.
- **Being vegetarian is enough.** Wescoe says "It's not enough to be vegetarian. We got to be vegan," because dairy and eggs-type practices he says break several commandments.
- **Sunday is the Sabbath.** He says the Sabbath runs Friday sunset to Saturday sunset and that the change to Sunday came from a later Catholic decision, which he describes without citing a source.
- **Fallen angels and demons are the same.** He says "as someone who's an Enoch scholar, I know the difference," and separates them.
- **Eating fruit or smoothies is idolatry because one enjoys them.** He says idolatry concerns the object of desire, not the intensity of pleasure, and that God called the fruit good.
