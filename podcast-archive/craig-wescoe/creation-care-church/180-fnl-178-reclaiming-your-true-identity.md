## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks who God created humans to be and how a person can "reclaim" that identity. His answer is that humans are image bearers (Genesis 1:27) called to reflect God's compassion and mercy over all creation. He argues that the food God gives in Genesis 1:29 is a plant-based diet, and that "dominion" in Genesis 1 is therefore not a license to kill animals. He presents this as the first step in living out the image, and says that refusing it means not "bearing his image, period" (28:37). He ties the argument to grace, faith, repentance and forgiveness, to a passage on the body as God's temple, and to Jesus calling his followers friends rather than servants.

The evidence is almost entirely a chain of scripture readings, mostly from Genesis, Psalms, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Proverbs and the Johannine and Pauline letters. Wescoe reads these devotionally and does not engage textual history, original languages (beyond a brief note on the name Yeshua and the Hebrew for "hide"), or the scholarly debate over these texts. He does not argue from the Synoptic Gospels' teachings about Torah or from any reconstruction of the first followers. His main historical aside is that the earliest followers in Jerusalem were called "Nazarenes," which he takes from Acts.

The talk is a live Friday devotional for the Creation Care Church community. After the main message (ending around 39:44), the second half is a chat-based Q&A with commenters, covering clothing for Adam and Eve, whether animals will be in the kingdom, the pre-tribulation rapture, and the timing of "kingdom now" versus "kingdom later." It ends with announcements (a Sukkot retreat idea, website and newsletter plans, a book in progress) and a closing prayer for God's kingdom to come "on earth as it is in heaven" (1:10:09).

## Key Teachings

### 1. Image bearing as reflecting God's compassion toward all creation
Wescoe starts from Psalm 145:8-9 ("The Lord is good to all, and his tender mercies are over all his works") and Genesis 1:27. From these he argues that God's character is compassion over every creature, and that humans, as image bearers, are to reflect that compassion "toward his whole creation" (07:49). He treats this as the core of what it means to be a child of God and to have a "true identity." The argument rests on the text's own wording and on his reading of God's character; he does not discuss the meaning of the Hebrew for "image" (*tselem*) or competing interpretations.

### 2. Genesis 1:29 as the content of dominion
Wescoe reads Genesis 1:28 ("have dominion") together with 1:29 ("every herb... to you it shall be for food"). He argues that the plant diet is the first concrete expression of how to rule "in his image and likeness," and that because the text "doesn't elaborate much," the diet is what God emphasizes (26:15). He says many people use "dominion" to justify killing animals and that this misreads the passage. He also says that God's "very good" verdict (1:31) should not be set aside. He argues that Jesus did not change this, citing Hebrews (no verse given) for Jesus being "the same yesterday, today, and forever" (27:09). He rejects the objection that "Jesus made all food clean" as an excuse (27:09).

### 3. "Go back to the beginning" over Levitical law
In the Q&A portion and the main message he says Jesus, on divorce, pointed people back to creation rather than to the Levitical laws, and that Jesus similarly directs his followers to "return to those first works" (22:19, 27:53). He frames Genesis's account of creation as God's "ideal," which Christians should use as the standard for diet and treatment of animals. He does not cite the Gospel passage on divorce by verse, and he does not discuss how Torah provisions on animals or food were handled in the Second Temple period.

### 4. Grace, faith, repentance and forgiveness
Wescoe uses Ephesians 2:8-10 (salvation by grace through faith, "created in Christ Jesus for good works"), 1 John 1:9 (confession and cleansing), Romans 6:23 (wages of sin is death), and Proverbs 12:10 (the righteous "regards the life of the animal") to argue that repentance includes changing one's treatment of animals and diet. He says forgiveness is a complete cancellation of debt, not a repayment plan (35:45). He equates the "wages of sin" with living against one's created nature and treats death as "not part of his plan," citing Revelation 21:4 (no verse given in the talk; he refers to "Revelation 21:4").

### 5. Friends, not servants; the body as temple
Using John 15:15 ("no longer do I call you servants... I have called you friends"), Wescoe argues that humans should treat animals as "friends, not food" (20:35). He ties this to Abraham as a "friend of God" and to 1 Corinthians 6:19-20 (body as temple of the Holy Spirit), so that human bodies, and the animals humans rule, are to be treated as God's creations. He also cites 1 Corinthians 12:27 for the corporate "body of Christ" and John 15:5 (the vine and branches) for bearing fruit.

### 6. Judgment by works, and "don't aim for the D- minus"
Wescoe rejects the idea that the gospel is "just saying a prayer to go to heaven," and argues that diet and animal treatment are part of what Jesus expects (59:03). He quotes the Sermon on the Mount's emphasis on doing God's will (no verse given; "Matthew 5-7" is his material) and the "Lord, Lord" saying (no verse given), and argues that judgment is "according to our works" (1:00:35). He concludes that a Christian should aim for the highest standard rather than the minimum needed for salvation, using a grading metaphor.

### 7. The early Nazarene identity (brief aside)
In answer to a commenter's question about Christians and veganism, Wescoe says the first followers in Jerusalem were called "Nazarenes," drawing on Acts, and that this term "is a term that a lot of people are adopting nowadays" (1:03:10). He suggests that Acts records "Christians" first appearing in Antioch. He does not cite a verse or discuss Acts' chronology. (The transcript reads "the first account of them being Christians is Antioch in the mid-2nd century," which conflicts with his reference to Acts; treat this as a possible caption error and check the audio before quoting.)

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Dominion" gives humans permission to kill and eat animals.** Wescoe argues that Genesis 1:28 must be read with 1:29, and that the plant diet is what God emphasizes as ruling "in his image and likeness."
- **Jesus made all food clean, so Genesis 1:29 no longer applies.** He says this is an excuse; God "never said, 'This isn't very good anymore'" and Jesus did not change God's design.
- **Diet is not a salvation issue.** He says even if one grants this, aiming only for minimal salvation is the wrong attitude, and "what if you're wrong?"
- **Christianity is just saying a prayer to go to heaven.** He argues the gospel is about God's "heaven" coming into people now, and about doing God's will.
- **God killed an animal to clothe Adam and Eve.** In the Q&A he says the text does not say this and that nothing was created from death before that point.
- **Early followers were called "Christians" from the beginning.** He says the Jerusalem community was called "Nazarenes," citing Acts.
- **Christians who do not care for animals are false followers of Jesus.** He says he would not go that far, and that most people are ignorant and need education.

- **Nazarenes/Ebionites/Essenes:** The Nazarene aside is the only touch point. The speaker connects the term to his current movement without discussing the later Nazarene or Ebionite groups.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet and nonviolence:** The talk's central claims are diet (Genesis 1:29) and nonviolence toward animals. It sets Genesis as the ideal against "Levitical laws." Sacrifice is not discussed.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** The closing prayer and Q&A frame the kingdom "on earth as it is in heaven" as the goal of reclaiming identity and of creation care.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** Eternal life and judgment by works are discussed; Revelation 21:4 is used to say death is not part of God's plan. Heaven and animal presence in the kingdom are covered in the Q&A. No resurrection or hell analysis.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Jesus is "the word of God become flesh" (John 1), "express image of God" (2 Corinthians 5 context), and Yeshua meaning "salvation" (Matthew 1:21). This is devotional christology, not historical analysis.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Not addressed, beyond reading the Gospels and letters as one unified scripture.
