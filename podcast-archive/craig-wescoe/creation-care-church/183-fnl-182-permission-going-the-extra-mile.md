## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks whether humans actually have God's permission to kill and eat animals, a view he says many Christians hold and use to justify ignoring what God wants. He adapts a blog post he wrote about nine years earlier, "Permission & Going the Extra Mile." He answers no. His method is to take the verses most often cited for permission and read each one with its following verses and wider context. He starts with the "dominion" of Genesis 1:26-28 and the post-Flood permission of Genesis 9:3, arguing that Genesis 1:29 gives a plant-based diet and that Genesis 9:4 forbids eating flesh with its blood (9:3-4, [09:08]-[12:44]). He then turns to Mark 7:19, which he says has a translator's gloss, "thus Jesus declared all foods clean," that is not in the Greek text as he reads it, and argues that the chapter concerns handwashing traditions, not food (Mark 7:1-23, [14:00]-[19:52]). He reads 1 Timothy 4:4 as no permission at all, and then cites Acts 15:29 (the Jerusalem Council, which he attributes to James), Revelation 2, Romans 14, 1 Corinthians 8 and 10 to show that the apostles opposed eating animal flesh and that Paul himself gave up meat out of love ([23:26]-[33:19]).

The talk closes by saying that Jesus summed up the law as love for God and neighbor (Matthew 22:34-40), and Craig argues that the moral question is love, not permission (1 John 4; Luke 10:29-37; [34:06]-[44:38]). The final section is a live Q&A with chat comments, in which Craig touches on the book of Enoch, slaughterhouse footage, and the growth of veganism, and ends with a prayer. The talk is one presenter's argument. Craig makes his case by appeal to the text and the apostles, and he does not engage with the scholarly literature on Mark 7:19, Genesis 9, or the Jerusalem Council.

## Key Teachings

### 1. "Dominion" in Genesis 1 is not permission to kill animals
Craig says the usual argument runs: humans alone were made in God's image and given "dominion" over the animals, so killing them is allowed ([06:40]-[07:26]). He answers by reading the next verse, Genesis 1:29, where God gives humans "every herb that yields seed" and "every tree whose fruit yields seed" as food, and gives the animals "every green herb" ([07:26]-[08:16]). For him, the qualification of dominion is a plant diet. He also notes that God calls the result "very good" ([08:16]-[09:08]).

### 2. Genesis 9:3-4 forbids flesh with its blood
People cite Genesis 9:3 ("every moving thing that lives shall be food for you") as God's later permission. Craig says the next verse, Genesis 9:4, says "you shall not eat the flesh with its life, that is its blood," and he argues that the post-Flood speech is not a permission to eat animals as such. He adds that Noah's burnt offerings in Genesis 8:20 are followed by God's statement about the human heart's evil imagination (cf. Genesis 6:5), which he takes as God's disapproval, not approval, of the sacrifices ([10:07]-[12:44]).

### 3. Mark 7:19 is about handwashing, not food
Craig argues that "thus Jesus declared all foods clean" is a marginal gloss added by translators, not part of the text itself ([14:47]). He reads Mark 7:1-23 as a dispute over the "tradition of the elders" about washing hands before eating, and he says Jesus answers by quoting Isaiah against "the commandments of men" ([16:21]-[18:50]). He then argues that Mark 7:20-23 lists evil things that defile a person from within, and he counts seven items (evil thoughts, murders, theft, covetousness, lewdness, pride, foolishness) that he says apply to killing animals for food ([19:52]-[22:45]). His own reading of Mark 7:20-23 is interpretive, and the chapter's connection to killing is his extension.

### 4. 1 Timothy 4:4 and Acts 15:29: the apostles' necessary requirements
Craig says that 1 Timothy 4:4 ("every creature of God is good") is often used to say that anything received with thanksgiving may be eaten. He reads "good" as the things God called good in Genesis 1, including the plant diet, and then turns to Acts 15:29, the Jerusalem Council, which he says James announced. He reads the four requirements (things offered to idols, blood, things strangled, and porneia) as abstentions tied to animal flesh and says slaughter involves strangling. He adds that Irenaeus's earliest account of the council also includes the golden rule ([24:14]-[27:05]). Craig's reading of "strangled" and of porneia as "defilement" is his own, and his claim that Irenaeus's account adds the golden rule is stated only in the talk.

### 5. Revelation 2 and Paul's own food teaching
Craig cites Revelation 2:14 and 2:20 to show that eating things sacrificed to idols is condemned as "defilement" in those churches ([28:24]-[30:25]). He then argues that Romans 14:15 and 14:21 and 1 Corinthians 8:13 show Paul choosing to abstain from meat out of love for those who keep the apostolic instructions. He also quotes 1 Corinthians 10:23-24 ("all things are lawful for me, but all things are not helpful") to show Paul rejecting the appeal to permission even where it exists ([30:25]-[33:19]).

### 6. Love, not permission, is the standard
Craig sets this against Matthew 22:34-40 (love God, love your neighbor) and 1 John 4:8, 4:16-19 ("God is love"), and reads the Good Samaritan parable (Luke 10:29-37) as defining neighbor broadly. He argues that Genesis 1 and Isaiah 11:9 (no one "hurting or destroying") describe the same peaceable community that all creatures share ([34:06]-[41:47]). He ends by saying Christians who make the case from permission drive vegans away from the church, and he urges love in action ([41:47]-[44:38]).

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Genesis 1 gives humans permission to kill and eat animals through "dominion."** Craig says the next verse (Genesis 1:29) gives humans and animals a plant diet, so dominion is not permission to kill.
- **Genesis 9:3 lets humans eat every moving thing.** Craig says Genesis 9:4 immediately forbids eating flesh with its blood, so the verse does not grant permission to eat animal flesh.
- **Jesus declared all foods clean in Mark 7:19.** Craig says the phrase is a translator's gloss, and the chapter is about handwashing traditions, not food.
- **1 Timothy 4:4 lets Christians eat anything if they give thanks.** Craig reads "every creature of God is good" as God's own good creation, which in Genesis 1 is a plant diet for humans.
- **Paul allowed eating meat.** Craig cites 1 Corinthians 8:13 and Romans 14:21 as Paul's own abstention from meat, and he argues that Paul rejected appeal to permission (1 Corinthians 10:23-24).
- **The apostles did not forbid meat.** Craig reads Acts 15:29 and Revelation 2 as forbidding eating animal flesh offered to idols, and he equates that with eating animal flesh generally.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Jesus vs. Paul:** Craig reads Paul as teaching the same thing as Jesus and the other apostles, and he treats Paul's own abstention as evidence of that. He does not discuss any tension between them.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Craig attributes the Acts 15 decree to James ("James, the head of the church") and reads its four requirements as binding on Gentile believers, including abstention from animal flesh and blood. - **Torah, sacrifice, diet:** The talk is about diet and sacrifice. Craig argues that Noah's altar offerings in Genesis 8:20 are not God's will, and that the Acts 15 abstentions and Genesis 9:4 prohibit blood and animal flesh.

- **Kingdom on earth:** Craig ties the Lord's Prayer ("your kingdom come") to a world without killing animals, and to the restoration of all things.
