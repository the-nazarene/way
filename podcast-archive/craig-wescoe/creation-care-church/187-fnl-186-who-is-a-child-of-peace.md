## Summary

Wescoe asks what Jesus meant by "child of peace" in Luke 10:6, a phrase he says is rarely studied. He argues that Luke 10:5-7 is about the disciples' mission: seek out a receptive household, say "Peace to this house," stay there, and shake off the dust if the peace does not return. He rejects the common objection that Luke 10:7 ("eat such things as are set before you") forces a vegan to eat animal products. In his reading, the instruction to eat applies only once a child of peace has been found, and a child of peace would not serve violence. He also says the Dead Sea Scrolls used "child of peace" or "child of light" as the most common self-description of the Essenes, though he gives no reference for this.

To build the case, he reads a chain of texts: the Beatitudes (Matthew 5), Isaiah 11's peaceable kingdom, Isaiah 9:6's "Prince of Peace," John 14 and 16 on Jesus's peace, Psalm 11:5 and Proverbs 3:31 on God's hatred of violence, and Revelation 21 on the renewal of all things. He treats these as a single line of teaching that Jesus is the Prince of Peace and his followers should embody peace, including a vegan diet. He also applies the argument to churches: when he visits one, he looks for vegans, and a church with none is, in his view, failing to host "children of peace."

The talk ends as a 40-minute live Q&A. Wescoe answers viewer questions on the asteroid theory of Sodom's destruction, the sword of Matthew 10:34, end-times schemes, and the Lord's Prayer. He presents eschatological disagreement as unimportant compared with the shared hope of eternal life in God's peaceful kingdom. He closes with donation requests and a prayer.

## Key Teachings

### The mission discourse and the dietary objection (Luke 10:1-12)
Wescoe argues that the phrase "child of peace" needs its whole context. He reads Luke 10:1-12 aloud and says the disciples are sent "two by two" to find a household that welcomes them. The verse that critics use against veganism, Luke 10:7, is in his view conditional: seek out the child of peace first, then eat what that household serves. He concludes that Jesus is not telling disciples to eat "the products of violence." He describes the rejection side of the passage, shaking off dust and the comparison with Sodom, as the consequence for households that refuse the message.

### The Beatitudes and the light of the world (Matthew 5)
He reads Matthew 5:1-16 and links "peacemakers" (5:9) to the "children of God" and to the "light of the world" (5:14). In his reading, those who do these things are "blessed" even while they face revilement and persecution, and the blessing is not a promise of an easy life. He ties his own experience of being attacked online to Matthew 5:11. He also reads the church as the place where God "plants one vegan" and then lets that person multiply, comparing it to a seed producing a tree.

### Isaiah 11, Proverbs 12:10 and the Prince of Peace
Wescoe reads Isaiah 11:1-9 as a messianic passage about "the root of Jesse," which he identifies with Jesus. The child who leads the animals and judges "the meek of the earth" is, in his reading, the same figure. He cites Proverbs 12:10 ("The righteous care for the needs of the animals") to explain the kind of judgment the figure executes. He then reads Isaiah 9:6 ("Prince of Peace") and argues that followers, as "children of peace," should embody that peace. He contrasts this with "the prince of the air" (the adversary).

### Peace as the Spirit's gift (John 14:27, John 16:33)
He reads John 14:26-27 and identifies Jesus's peace with the Holy Spirit, whom he calls the Paraclete (transliterated "Paracletos"). He describes baptism as initiation into this Spirit and into the role of children of peace. He says Jesus's peace is "not as the world gives," meaning it is free and asks only that the recipient live in it. He also connects peace to the "fruit of the Spirit" and to John 16:33 ("In the world you will have tribulation"), which he uses to predict opposition from other believers who reject the peace message.

### God's hatred of violence and the wall of salvation (Psalm 11:5, Proverbs 3:31, Isaiah 60:18)
Wescoe reads Psalm 11:5 ("the one who loves violence, his soul hates") as a direct warning to Christians who use scripture to defend violence, and says it should "convict" them. He reads Proverbs 3:31 as a command not to envy or imitate the violent. He reads Isaiah 60:18 and says "Yeshua" (Jesus's Hebrew name) is the "salvation" of the walls, so that a house built on Jesus should have no violence in it. He urges self-examination and repentance.

### The end, the restoration, and the minimal role of eschatology (Revelation 21:1-5, Matthew 10:34, the Lord's Prayer)
He reads Revelation 21:1-5 and says that "in the beginning, we're all vegan," and that Isaiah 11's peaceable kingdom will be restored in the end. On end-times debates (pre-, post-, a-millennial and so on), he says they "don't really matter" next to the shared hope of eternal life in God's kingdom. On Matthew 10:34 ("I did not come to bring peace but a sword"), he argues that Jesus is drawing a firm boundary between those who do God's will and those who oppose it, not endorsing violence. On the Lord's Prayer ("your will be done on earth as in heaven"), he says that to pray for God's kingdom of peace is to commit to living by it.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Luke 10:7 requires eating whatever a host serves, including meat.** Wescoe says the verse is conditional: first seek out a child of peace, and a child of peace would not serve violence. He reads the verse as not about animal products.
- **Matthew 10:34 means Jesus came to bring violence.** He says Jesus is "drawing a firm boundary" between those who obey and those who oppose God, not endorsing violence.
- **End-times details must be settled before one can follow Jesus.** He says the eschatological debates "don't really matter," and that the shared hope is eternal life in God's kingdom.
- **Reciting the Lord's Prayer is enough.** He argues that praying "your will be done on earth" commits the one praying to living by God's peaceful kingdom.
