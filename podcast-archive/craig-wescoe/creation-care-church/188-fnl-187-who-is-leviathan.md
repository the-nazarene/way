## Summary

Craig opens by asking who Leviathan is: a creature, a human, or a spiritual being. He builds the answer from a close reading of Isaiah 27:1, Job 40-41, Psalms 74 and 104, Revelation 12 and 20, Genesis 3, Ezekiel 28 and John 8:44, reading the passages side by side and drawing links between them. Each link is presented as a connection the speaker is "trying to make," not as a fixed result.

His answer is that Leviathan is "another name for the devil and Satan" (36:12). The main evidence is Isaiah 27:1's pairing of Leviathan with "the dragon of the sea," Revelation 20:2's identification of "that serpent of old" with "the devil and Satan," and Genesis 3's serpent, who contradicts God and is later cursed. He reads Ezekiel 28's lament for the king of Tyre as describing a cherub in Eden who was cast out of God's holy mountain for violence, and he treats Leviathan's kingship "over all the children of pride" as the opposite of the "children of peace." He also argues that the serpent's "food" of dust, and the flesh-eating that follows, point toward a moral and possibly literal link between Satan and the consumption of animals, which ties the identification to his veganism.

The talk ends with a long audience Q&A. Craig says that after a viewer's challenge and his own further research he is "convinced" Leviathan is Satan. He stays hedged on Behemoth, saying a hippopotamus is his "best guess" while allowing that it may be symbolic. The talk closes with a prayer and a note about the next talk in two weeks.

## Key Teachings

### Leviathan as a serpent and dragon, and as a spiritual being

Craig starts with Isaiah 27:1 (Berean Standard Bible), where the LORD judges "Leviathan the fleeing serpent" and "Leviathan the coiling serpent" and slays "the dragon of the sea." He reads the descriptions in Job 41 (scales, fire from the mouth, "king over all the children of pride") and Psalm 74:13 and 104:26 as physical details of a sea creature, then argues the details outgrow a plain animal. A fire-breathing sea snake that is "king over all the children of pride" would need "some special powers at the minimum" (17:12). He concludes that the passages point to a "spiritual being" that "might have the body of an animal" or be "a sort of symbolic description" (19:40).

### The Revelation identifications

Craig's strongest textual link is Revelation 20:2, where the dragon and "that serpent of old" are explicitly "the devil and Satan." He then reads Revelation 12:7-9, where Michael and his angels fight "the dragon and his angels" in heaven and the dragon is cast down to earth, as a war between good and evil in which the dragon is a powerful spiritual being (21:28-22:12). He does not discuss the Greek text, dating, or the genre of Revelation.

### Genesis 3 and Ezekiel 28: the serpent, dust, and the cherub

Craig reads Genesis 3 as the serpent who is "more cunning" than the beasts, contradicts God ("You will not surely die"), and is cursed to "eat dust all the days of your life" (23:49-25:21). He ties the dust to Genesis 2's creation of man from dust and to Genesis 3:19's "for dust you are." He reads Ezekiel 28:11-19 as a lament for the king of Tyre that describes a figure "in Eden, the garden of God," an "anointed cherub who covers," perfect until "iniquity was found in you," who was cast out of "the holy mountain of God" for violence. He links this to Isaiah 11:9's "They will neither hurt nor destroy in all my holy mountain" and the "lifted up" heart, arguing that the cherub is the same spiritual being he identified in Revelation. He is explicit that he does not think the passage is about a human ruler (36:12).

### Pride, humility and the children of peace

Craig draws a contrast between "the king over all the children of pride" (Job 41:34) and the "Prince of Peace" and "king over all the children of peace" (14:08, 14:55). He says Satan's followers are "those that are full of pride, the opposite of humility," and that Christ is "the embodiment of the humility" (34:28). The prayer that closes the talk applies the same contrast: humility rather than a "puffed up prideful view" of animals as existing to serve people (1:04:12).

### Diet and flesh-eating

Craig reads the "dust" and "devourer" language as possibly referring to Satan as one who "devours" people, citing 1 Peter-style "roaring lion" imagery without giving a verse (he mentions "a roaring lion looking for who he can devour"). He then suggests a more literal component: "it might be referring to someone who's desiring the to eat flesh." He says that in his earlier Book of Enoch talks he linked flesh-eating and animal sacrifice to "evil spiritual beings" (38:27). He concludes that those who oppose eating animals "since that's contrary to God's ideal" have "pretty reasonable evidence" here, and that vegans should not participate in "the thing that is from Leviathan." Note that the text he cites for the diet connection is the Genesis 3 curse on the serpent and the Ezekiel 28 "violence"; the link from these to animal-eating is his inference.

### Translation, animal identification, and hedging

Several passages in the Q&A address the identity of animals in general. Craig says translators "are taking shots in the dark" on animal lists, that the King James "unicorn" may be a rhinoceros, and that Behemoth "most closely fits with hippopotamus" (40:24; 52:18-53:02). He says he has not studied Behemoth as much and does not claim it is a spiritual being. He also notes that Leviathan could be a whale, crocodile or giant squid as physical guesses, and in the chat acknowledges the Loch Ness Monster as a visual match (1:01:11). He presents these as ways of showing why the physical reading is less convincing than the spiritual one.
