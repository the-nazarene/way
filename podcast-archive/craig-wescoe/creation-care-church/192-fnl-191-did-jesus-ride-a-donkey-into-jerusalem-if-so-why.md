## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks whether Jesus really rode a donkey into Jerusalem, as the Gospels report, and if so why. The question matters to him because a common objection to veganism holds that Jesus' ride shows animal use is acceptable. He first argues that the ride does not license eating animals, since no such conclusion follows from it. He then argues from Zechariah 9:9-10 that the donkey was part of a prophetic sign: the king who comes "lowly and riding on a donkey" is the one who will bring peace to the nations. For Wescoe, Jesus' first act as this king is the temple disruption (Matthew 21:12-13), which he reads as a liberation of animals from sacrificial slaughter. He supports this with Isaiah 58:6, Isaiah 61:1, Luke 4:18-21, and a set of "animal covenant" texts (Hosea 2:18, Isaiah 11:9, Genesis 9:16). He also presents Balaam's donkey (Numbers 22) as a model of selfless, Christ-like love.

Wescoe's answer to the donkey question is qualified. He says Jesus rode only once, for a short distance, and never made a practice of riding, so the ride should not be imitated. He says the donkey was probably "a willing participant." But he also repeatedly concedes that the four Gospel accounts disagree on whether one or two animals were involved, and that "it's not guaranteed that he did" ride. His main evidence is the cumulative prophetic and scriptural reading he offers, not any external historical source. He does not treat the historicity question as settled, and he does not work through the Greek text or any scholarly literature on the triumphal entry.

The talk ends with a live Q&A from the chat. Wescoe takes questions on whether riding harms animals (he says equine backs are not designed for riders and that breaking them is harmful), on whether a mule or a "leading" translation could explain the text, and on whether animals may have jobs at all (he distinguishes a working sheep-herding dog from exploitation). He closes with prayer and announcements about the next FNL talk.

## Key Teachings

### The triumphal entry as a one-time fulfilment of Zechariah 9:9-10
Wescoe opens with Zechariah 9:9-10, which describes a king who is "lowly and riding on a donkey, a colt, the foal of a donkey," and whose dominion runs "from sea to sea." He reads Matthew 21:1-11 as the fulfilment, noting that the same episode appears in Mark 11:1-11, Luke 19:28-44 and John 12:12-19. He points out that Matthew alone describes two animals ("a donkey and a colt") and that Jesus is said to be seated on both. He says the other accounts mention only one animal and that he does not know "what the physics of that looks like." His interpretive claim is that the ride is a sign of messianic identity, not a model for practice: Jesus "doesn't make a practice of riding donkeys everywhere," and in the temple "he's clearly not riding it anymore."

### The temple disruption as liberation of animals
Wescoe argues that the entry leads straight into Matthew 21:12-13, where Jesus overturns the tables and drives out the sellers. He reads the episode as a condemnation of animal sacrifice, not chiefly a protest about money changers. He cites Luke's account, which he says omits the money changers, and a Jeremiah passage he describes as saying that God "never commanded animal sacrifice" (he attributes the claim that "lying scribes invented" it to that passage). He says Jesus is "liberating the animals," and calls the temple act "his first act as a king." Note that Wescoe gives no verse for the Jeremiah passage or the Proverbs reference he mentions. He reads the Isaiah yokes and burdens language as applying to animals first: "you don't put a yoke on a human. You put a yoke on an animal."

### Isaiah 58, Isaiah 61 and Luke 4 as the program Jesus enacts
He connects Isaiah 58:6 ("loose the bonds of wickedness... let the oppressed go free... break every yoke") and Isaiah 61:1 ("proclaim liberty to the captives... the opening of the prison") to the temple act. He also reads Luke 4:18-21, where Jesus quotes Isaiah 61 in the synagogue and says "Today this scripture is fulfilled in your hearing." Wescoe takes this as Jesus identifying himself with the animal-liberating program. He acknowledges the text can apply to enslaved humans "by extension," but argues the literal referent is animals.

### Balaam's donkey as a model of Christ-like love
In a long section on Numbers 22:22-34, Wescoe describes Balaam beating his donkey three times as she swerves to avoid the angel of the Lord, and the donkey's speech: "Am I not your donkey on which you have ridden ever since I became yours." He reads the donkey as acting selflessly, since the angel says he would have killed Balaam but "let her live." He compares this to Jesus' words on the cross ("Forgive them"), and calls the donkey "one of my favorite hero characters in the Bible." He describes Balaam's repentance as a model for non-vegans. Wescoe uses this to argue the animal's willingness is the key ethical signal, which he later applies to the Jesus ride.

### An everlasting covenant including the animals
Wescoe argues that Scripture repeatedly names animals as covenant recipients. He cites Genesis 9:16 ("everlasting covenant... between God and every living creature"), Hosea 2:18 (a covenant "with the beasts of the field" to end bow and sword, so that they "lie down safely"), Isaiah 11:9 ("They shall not hurt nor destroy in all my holy mountain"), and Exodus 20:17 (donkeys named in the commandment against coveting). He also reads Ecclesiastes 3:19 as showing no advantage of humans over beasts, arguing that the Hebrew term *nephesh chaya* is used for both (he attributes this reading to his own understanding of the Hebrew). He ends with Revelation 5:13, where "every creature" praises God, and Romans 8 on creation groaning. The Romans reference is his only mention of Paul.

### Doubts about the historicity and the accounts
Wescoe repeatedly flags that the Gospels disagree. He says that when accounts conflict "that generally means that something is going on." He suggests the ride might be a later embellishment: "it might have been written in afterwards," or Jesus "was walking alongside the donkey." He also notes that in Matthew, Mark and Luke, Simon is said to carry the cross, which he attributes to a later addition. He says he looked at Nag Hammadi literature on the figure (he suspects "Simon Magus," though this is his own tentative identification). He calls John's account "the eyewitness account that I think is the earliest version," an assessment he gives without argument. He admits the talk was designed to show the ride is "not a defeater" to veganism, and that "that doesn't mean he actually did do it."

### Riding as exploitation, with a qualification (Q&A)
In response to chat questions, Wescoe says riding animals is exploitation because equines "are not designed" to carry riders and must be "broken," which harms their spines. He allows that animals may have roles in community, offering the rescued dog and the herding dog as examples, and says "tail wags per minute" is a rough measure of happiness. He also suggests the Greek or translation might mean "carried him in" or "led" rather than "rode." He concludes the donkey was probably willing, and that the practice of riding is not to be copied.
