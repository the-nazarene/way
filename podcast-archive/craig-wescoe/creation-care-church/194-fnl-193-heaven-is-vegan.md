## Summary

Craig Wescoe, presenting a "Friday Night Live" talk for Creation Care Church, asks whether heaven will be vegan. Many meat-eaters concede that it will but say they will eat meat now, and some cite passages to argue that killing and eating animals continues in heaven. Wescoe's answer is yes. He builds it from Isaiah 11:9, Revelation 21:4 and Genesis 1:29, which he reads as a world without killing, pain or death. He then takes up the texts he says opponents use for the opposite: Ezekiel 43:19 (Levitical sacrifice), Psalm 51:18-19 (sacrifices to be pleased with after Jerusalem's walls are rebuilt), and the Luke 24 account of Jesus eating fish. For Ezekiel he argues from Hebrews that Jesus is the permanent high priest and that his single offering ended sacrifice. For Psalm 51 he argues the closing verses were added by Ezra, not David. For Luke 24 he argues the "fish" is a later addition and that the source Luke used said only "food."

The second half is a chat-driven Q&A. Wescoe answers questions on Psalm 51, the fish passage (where he argues that the Hebrew/Semitic word translated "honey" means date paste), and his broader claim that the historical evidence shows Jesus and his apostles were vegan. The talk ends with a prayer and an announcement of the next live talk. Wescoe frames his method as a choice between "traditional" and "historical" readings, and says the historical approach is the one that works for him.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Heaven is vegan: the promises of peace and no death
Wescoe reads Isaiah 11:9 ("They shall not hurt nor destroy in all My holy mountain") as excluding the killing of animals for food, since eating an animal involves "hurting or destroying." He cites Revelation 21:4 (no more death, sorrow, crying or pain) and argues that slaughter would contradict it. He reads Genesis 1:29 as God giving plants and seed-bearing fruit as food, with no animals, and presents this as a "vegan world" from beginning to end. He adds that if this is God's will in the end, Christians should be practicing it now (Romans 12:2 is quoted for "prove what is that good and acceptable and perfect will of God").

### 2. Ezekiel 43 and Hebrews: no return of animal sacrifice
Opponents, he says, read Ezekiel 43:19 (sacrifices by "the Levites... of the seed of Zadok") as a future temple with animal sacrifice in heaven. Wescoe's answers are: (a) Ezekiel speaks of a past time, not heaven; (b) Hebrews 7 presents Jesus as priest "forever according to the order of Melchizedek," so a return of Levitical priests would displace Jesus, which he calls "blasphemous"; (c) Hebrews 7:27 and 10:14 describe Christ's single offering as final. He argues that the Levitical system is "the shadow, the inferior priesthood." He does not discuss who wrote Hebrews, and treats it as an authoritative text without addressing authorship.

### 3. Mercy over sacrifice: Hosea 6:6, Mark 11, and Psalm 51
Wescoe cites Hosea 6:6 ("I desire mercy, not sacrifice") as God's stated preference, and says Jesus quoted it. He reads Jesus' temple action in Mark 11:15-18 as opposition to the sacrificial trade. He then reads Psalm 51:14-17 as David rejecting sacrifice and wanting a "broken spirit," and he treats Psalm 51:18-19 (rebuilding Jerusalem's walls, then pleasure in burnt offerings) as a contradiction. Using Nehemiah 2:17, the rebuilding of Jerusalem's walls is dated to after the Babylonian exile, and Wescoe argues that Ezra, a scribe, wrote those closing verses. He refers viewers to his own video on whether Ezra rewrote the Torah, in which he says Ezra combined the "Eloist and the Yahwist" scrolls. He presents this as a historical-critical claim and does not give the primary-source evidence for it in this talk.

### 4. God's covenant with the animals
Wescoe argues that God makes promises to animals: Hosea 2:18 (weapons abolished so creatures "lie down safely") and Genesis 9:16 (an "everlasting covenant" with "every living creature"). He says killing animals in heaven would make these promises meaningless, and would make God a liar. He also reads Psalm 145:8-9 ("his tender mercies are over all his works") as showing that God's compassion includes animals, and that human image-bearers should show the same.

### 5. Discipleship: the Great Commandment, the Lord's Prayer and repentance
He summarizes his ethic from Matthew 22:36-40 (love God and neighbor), taking animals as neighbors. He reads "your will be done on earth as it is in heaven" (Matthew 6:9-10) as a call to practice vegan living now, and calls eating animals while praying this "hypocrisy." He reads Matthew 4:17 ("Repent") as "stop doing the thing that's contrary to the will of God," with the kingdom at stake. He then describes Creation Care Church as a "mustard seed" community.

### 6. The Luke 24 fish passage: a later addition
This is the longest argument and was prompted by a chat question. Wescoe reads Luke 24:42-43 ("a piece of broiled fish and honeycomb") and argues:
- It is ambiguous whether the subject ate the fish or the honeycomb.
- The Hebrew/Semitic word typically translated "honey" means date paste (he says Jewish Tu BiShvat practice supports this).
- Manuscripts disagree: he says "half the manuscripts say fish and honeycomb and half of them just say fish," so the text has been modified.
- Luke's preface (Luke 1) says the author used sources from eyewitnesses and was not himself an eyewitness.
- The only second-century witness to the story he names is Marcion's Gospel, via Tertullian's *Against Marcion*, and in Tertullian's account the food is simply "food."
- Other post-resurrection accounts say bread, and Luke 24 itself has the bread-breaking at Emmaus.

He concludes that the fish reference is "about as flimsy of a piece of evidence as you could possibly give." Several links in this chain (the Marcion reading, the date-paste lexical claim, the manuscript history) are asserted in the talk and need checking against primary sources.
