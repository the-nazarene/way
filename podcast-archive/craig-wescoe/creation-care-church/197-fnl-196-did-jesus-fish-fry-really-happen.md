## Summary

Craig Wescoe opens with the common objection to veganism that John 21:9-10 shows Jesus cooking and serving fish on the beach, and asks whether that event historically happened. He answers that, on the historical evidence, it did not. His main argument is that no writer before the late second century shows knowledge of John 21. He contrasts this with Justin Martyr (mid-second century), who cites 18 of John's first 20 chapters, and Irenaeus (the 180s), who cites all 20. He then turns to Tertullian in the early third century, who quotes John 20:31 as the Gospel's ending. Craig takes this as evidence that John 21 was a later appendix, and says scholars widely agree on that point (citing Helmut Koester).

Craig argues that the chapter was added around the end of the second century to resolve three problems: the conflict between John and Mark about where and how the disciples were recruited (Galilean fishermen versus disciples of John the Baptist at Bethany beyond the Jordan), the question of whether Peter kept his authority after his threefold denial, and the rumor that some disciple would survive until Jesus returned (drawn from Mark 9:1). He treats the fish-fry and miraculous-catch material as borrowed from Mark and Luke and reshaped to fit this agenda, and he links the chapter's purpose to a rejection of the "kingdom on earth" ethic he associates with the vegan Nazarenes.

The talk ends with a prayer and a long live Q&A. Craig says his method is to start from historical evidence rather than traditional harmonization, and he points viewers to his other channels and earlier talks for those who want a traditional approach. Many of his claims about dating and motives are presented as his own reading of the evidence, and he says some points remain open for further research.

## Key Teachings

### The silence of early witnesses
Craig says no one in the first century (he names Paul, James, Peter, Jude and Clement) mentions the contents of John 21. He calls it less surprising than the silence of the second century. Justin Martyr cites material from 18 of John's first 20 chapters, but nothing from chapter 21. Irenaeus, in *Against Heresies*, cites every one of the first 20 chapters several times but says nothing about chapter 21. Craig acknowledges that the argument from silence has limits on its own, but says the pattern across both centuries is significant.

### Tertullian and the "terminus" of John
In *Against Praxeas* 25, Tertullian quotes what he treats as the ending of John's gospel, "that ye might believe that Jesus Christ is the son of God." Craig identifies this as John 20:30-31, not the end of chapter 21. He reads this as a witness that a version of John ended at chapter 20. He also presents Tertullian as one of the earliest witnesses to chapter 21, since Tertullian quotes from it, which Craig takes to show that both versions circulated. He notes that the chapter-20 ending reads as stylistically elegant while the chapter-21 ending (John 21:25) is "tacked on."

### Mark versus John on the disciples' calling
Craig argues that John has no sons of Zebedee and no Galilean fishermen. In John 1:35-43, Andrew and the other disciples are followers of John the Baptist at Bethany beyond the Jordan, and Jesus only sets out for Galilee the next day. He gives the distance as roughly 125 miles, about a week's walk, between the two places. He reads the first three verses of John 21 as an insertion that imports Mark's calling story (Mark 1:16-20), which John lacked. Craig treats the miraculous catch (John 21:4-6) as borrowed from Luke, which he dates around 170 and addresses to Theophilus, archbishop of Antioch (168-182). He also argues that the "same with the fish" phrasing in John 21:13 echoes John 6:11, and that the post-resurrection breakfast in John 21 conflicts with Luke 24:35, where recognition happens in "the breaking of the bread."

### Restoring Peter's authority
Craig says Mark ends with Peter's threefold denial (Mark 14:70-71) and never mentions him again. That raised a second-century question about whether Peter still held the authority given in Matthew 16:18-19, especially in a Roman context where James had led the Jerusalem church (Acts 15). He reads John 21:15-17, where Jesus asks Peter three times whether he loves him and then charges him to feed the lambs and sheep, as a reversal of the denials. He reads John 21:18-19 as a prediction of Peter's martyrdom, and links it to the late second-century Acts of Peter (chapters 37-38), which describes crucifixion head-downward.

### The rumor about the beloved disciple
John 21:22-23 says some believed the beloved disciple would not die before Jesus returned. Craig argues that this rumor presupposes that all the original disciples had already died, which would place the chapter around the early-to-mid second century. He traces the expectation to Mark 9:1 ("some standing here will not taste death before they see the kingdom of God arrive with power"). In his reading, John 21:23 reinterprets Jesus's words conditionally to defuse the problem, and it does not match what Mark 9:1 says.

### Who is the "real" Peter: the vegan Nazarenes
Craig contrasts the Peter of Mark, whom he presents as a bumbling Galilean fisherman, with the Peter of the Pseudo-Clementine *Homilies*, whom he describes as a vegan Nazarene who ate "only pot herbs and olives and salted bread." He argues that the Homilies preserve Peter's own account, and that Mark's account conflicts with it. He says the fishing and fish-eating motifs in John 21 were used to "turn the disciples into fish murderers" and that the chapter's purpose was to undercut a Nazarene vegan community that he associates with early Jerusalem. He ties this to Matthew 6:10 ("Your kingdom come, your will be done on earth as it is in heaven"), which he presents as the ethic John 21 was written to oppose.

### Q&A: interpretation and method
In the Q&A, Craig separates his two approaches. For traditional Christians he points to his first roughly 120 talks, which he describes as answering objections from a traditional perspective. For historical research he points to the Bible Origin channel. On the 153 fish, he says he cannot confirm the numerological claims a viewer raised and treats them as outside his scope. He also responds to a viewer who suggested the fish were symbolic: he says a symbolic reading consistent with God's character is preferable to assuming God endorses animal abuse. He does not claim certainty on every point.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The popular belief:** John 21 shows the risen Jesus cooking and eating fish, so Jesus approved of killing animals for food. **Craig's correction:** the scene is a late second-century addition, so it is not reliable evidence for what Jesus did or taught.
- **The popular belief:** Because the Bible is inerrant, John 21 must be a historical account that can be harmonized with the other Gospels. **Craig's correction:** he says harmonizing does not answer the contradictions (Galilee versus Bethany beyond the Jordan; Luke 24:35 versus the beach breakfast), and that he is working from historical evidence instead.
- **The popular belief:** Mark's account of the disciples and Peter is the eyewitness-based story, and John 21 aligns with it. **Craig's correction:** he argues that the Johannine account (and the Homilies' Peter) should be given more weight than Mark, which the tradition itself describes as second-hand.
