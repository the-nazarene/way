### Key Teachings

### 1. Historical versus traditional talks, and what the metrics show
Wescoe separates the talks into two kinds. "Traditional" talks accept the biblical canon as given and do not bring in outside historical evidence. "Historical" talks bring such evidence in. He reports that 15 of 24 talks were traditional, that the bottom 10 were all traditional, and that all nine historical talks ranked in the top 14. He says he has noticed this across all 197 talks and plans to produce more historical content. He also says Christians often "outright reject" historical evidence that conflicts with the biblical text, which is why he kept early talks within the traditional frame. Facebook reached about 470,000 views this year, and YouTube subscribers went from 513 to 785.

### 2. Reading Luke 10:6 as an Essene household rule
In the "Who is a child of peace?" talk (FNL 186), Wescoe says Luke 10:6 is often read through Mark's "all food clean" line or the instruction to "eat whatever they serve." He argues that Luke's "seek out the child of peace" identifies a household that does not eat animal flesh. He says the Essenes were such a group, and that "eat whatever they serve" presupposes that the hosts abstain from flesh. He gives no Essene source in the talk.

### 3. Vegan readings of creation, prophets and the kingdom
Several talks run the same argument from Genesis and the prophets. Genesis 1:29 is read as God's original diet. Isaiah 11:6-9 and Revelation 21:4 are read as a restored creation in which there is no killing ("heaven is vegan"). The prophets (Hosea, Jeremiah, Micah, Ezekiel 34:10 and 9:4) are cited as condemning animal slaughter. Wescoe argues that Jesus' statement "I came to serve, not to be served" (Matthew 20:28) should shape how humans treat animals, and that the "greatest commandment" frames this as love of God and neighbor. He treats these as points for his audience to accept, not as disputed points of exegesis.

### 4. The Book of Enoch, demons and the origin of animal sacrifice
In "Did demons invent animal sacrifice?" (ranked second), Wescoe argues from the Book of Enoch that demons, the disembodied spirits of the Genesis 6 giants, promoted eating flesh and blood and animal sacrifice. He says Leviticus 16:8 names Azazel, whom he identifies with the fallen angel in Enoch, as the recipient of one of the two goats. He also says Jesus calls Enoch "scripture" and that Peter and Jude quote it. He sets this against the 4th-century canon, saying he will "listen to Jesus rather than Augustine." The talk's exegesis is his own. The passages he discusses are not reproduced in this transcript.

### 5. John as the priority Gospel and the late addition of John 21
Wescoe calls himself a "Johannine prioritist," meaning he treats the Gospel of John as the most reliable account of Jesus. Where Mark conflicts with John, he says, John is the "true account," and he does not harmonize them. In "Did Jesus fish fry really happen?" (ranked fourth), he argues that John 21 was added in the late 2nd or early 3rd century, so the beach breakfast "likely never happened." He also says the John account of Jesus carrying his own cross is "the authentic eyewitness testimony," against Mark's account of Simon bearing it.

### 6. Responding to 1 Timothy 4 and the pastor-versus-priest debate
The most popular talk of the year (ranked first) answers a letter a friend received from a church leader. Wescoe goes through it "line by line," using 1 Peter 3:15 as the framework for a gentle reply. He says that in the most popular talk, the 1 Timothy 4 objection that vegans are "worshiping demons" can be turned back, since it would then call God's Spirit demonic. He treats Matthew 12:31-32 (blasphemy against the Spirit) as the key counter-text. The argument is his own rebuttal, not an historical claim.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"The Bible is the word of God."** Wescoe says this is wrong, since John 1:14 says the Word became flesh and that Word is Jesus. He adds that the Bible is a canon set in the 4th century and that its canons differ (Ethiopian Bible includes Enoch).
- **"1 Timothy 4 condemns vegans."** Wescoe argues the verse is misread. If it is taken to call God's Spirit demonic, it blasphemes the Holy Spirit (Matthew 12:31-32).
- **"Jesus rode a donkey, so using animals is justified."** Wescoe says that even on a traditional reading, a single donkey ride would not justify exploiting animals today. He also says there is "definitely reasons to believe that he didn't even ride the donkey this one time."
- **"The fish-fry breakfast in John 21 is historical."** Wescoe argues John 21 is a later addition and that there is no historical evidence for the event.
- **"Christians" was the name of the first followers.** Wescoe says the earliest followers called themselves "the Way" or were called Nazarenes, and the name "Christian" arose later in Antioch.
