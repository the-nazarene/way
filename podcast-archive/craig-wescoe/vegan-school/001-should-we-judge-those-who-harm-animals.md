## Summary

Wescoe asks whether the saying "Do not judge, so that you will not be judged" means Christians should refrain from judging people who harm animals. He argues that the saying is misread when used as a general rule against telling others what to do. In his reading, Jesus is speaking of a particular attitude: condemnation, a wish to see someone punished or harmed, usually over something they have done. He says Jesus clearly gave instructions and did not object to them, so "don't tell me what to do" cannot be the meaning. He separates judging from admonishing, which seeks change without a desire for vengeance, and he defines repentance as turning around rather than an emotional display.

He then applies this to animals. He cites the Temple scene in Mark 11:15 as Jesus confronting the treatment of animals sold for Passover sacrifice, argues that the money changers are not the whole point, and relates Jesus's "I desire mercy, not sacrifice" to the end of animal slaughter. He draws on Genesis 1:28, the Jonah account of Nineveh's repentance, the Revelation passages about repentance and animals in worship, and the "love your neighbor" command to argue that animals are included in the neighbor category. He concludes that Christians should not wish harm on people who harm animals, should not celebrate their misfortune, and should pray and work for their repentance and for mercy toward animals. He presents this as "my reading," and says he is "fairly confident" in it. The talk ends with a call to visit s2p.org for the FAQ, and no counterarguments are discussed.

## Key Teachings

### 1. "Judge not" targets condemnation, not instruction
Wescoe reads Matthew 7:1-2 (which he attributes to Mark and Matthew in the transcript) in its immediate context: loving enemies, being merciful "just as your father in heaven is merciful," and then "judge not... condemn not." He argues that if judging meant any instruction, Jesus would be guilty too, since he gives commands such as "turn the other cheek." The judgment in view is a desire for someone to be punished or harmed. He says the most frequent form he hears in church is "I hope to see this person burn in hell." His instruction is to pray for the offender and seek their repentance.

### 2. Admonishment versus judging
He defines judging as wishing for vengeance and admonishment as aiming "to get the person to change without the associated desire for vengeance." He says the Sermon on the Mount admonishes without judging, and he claims "most vegans are admonishing rather than judging." No source is cited for the claim about the Sermon on the Mount, which he presents as his own assessment.

### 3. Repentance is turning around
Wescoe says the word "repent quite literally means to turn around," and he illustrates this with a walker reversing course from destruction. He separates the emotional display of crying and apology from the "turning point" and the actual change of conduct, which he says "defines repentance." He ties this to Jesus's opening message ("repent, for the kingdom of heaven..."), to the Revelation pattern of unrepentant people facing plagues, and to Jonah 3, where the Ninevites repent and God relents. He also cites an unnamed verse saying God does not wish anyone destroyed but wants all to come to repentance. He notes that the rejoicing of angels over one repentant sinner is the right model, not celebration of an enemy's death.

### 4. Mark 11 concerns the animals as well as the money changers
He reads the Temple cleansing (Mark 11:15) as Jesus confronting the sale of animals for Passover sacrifice, with animals treated as property "to be sold... killed having no regard for them." He argues that "it's not all about the money changers," and states that the account appears in all four Gospels while only three mention the money changers. (That is his claim about the Gospel texts; he invites the group to compare them.) He then reads Jesus's "I desire mercy, not sacrifice" as an end to animal sacrifice, and presents the Last Supper, with bread and "wine... which could also refer to vinegar or grape juice," as a replacement for the Passover lamb's blood. He describes Jesus as "that lamb" who walked to his own slaughter. He does not give a source for the "vinegar or grape juice" translation point, and he does not cite the verses for the Passover or Last Supper accounts.

### 5. Animals as neighbors within God's creation
Wescoe argues that the command to love one's neighbor as oneself, which he calls the second greatest commandment, applies to animals. He reads Genesis 1:28 ("be fruitful and multiply") and the giving of the earth to humans as a shared home ("they are quite literally our neighbors"). He cites Revelation's picture of animals at the throne praising God, and argues: "if they are also capable of praising and worshipping God... why are we killing them in eating them." He also argues against narrowing "neighbor" to the person next door, and calls for the fruit of the Spirit (gentleness, kindness, peace) to extend to animals.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Jesus said not to judge, so no one may tell others what to do."** Wescoe says the saying is about condemnation and a desire for harm; Jesus gave many instructions himself.
- **"The Temple cleansing was only about money changers."** He argues the animals were also central, citing the Passover sacrifice context.
- **"Repentance means emotional tears and apology."** He says the emotional display is not the core; the core is turning around and changing conduct.
- **"Neighbor means the person next door."** He argues that the neighbor command extends to everyone, including animals.
- **"Vegans celebrate when hunters are killed."** He says this is a fringe view and not the Christian vegan attitude.
