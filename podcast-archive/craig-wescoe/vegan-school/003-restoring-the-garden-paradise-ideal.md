## Summary

Wescoe opens with a computer analogy: a "restore point" is a moment before corruption to which a system can be rolled back. He applies this to religious restoration movements, citing Nehemiah's rebuilding of Jerusalem's walls around the time of David and the Protestant Reformation's return to an early-church ideal. He asks whether there is an "optimal restore point," a moment in biblical history when the system was wholly uncorrupted.

His answer is the Eden account in Genesis 1-2, where God calls creation "very good" and gives humanity dominion, a plant-based diet and the task of tending the garden. He treats the post-flood world as an inferior restore point because Noah offers animal sacrifice, God then permits animals to be eaten and the animals are said to fear humans, and God describes human inclinations as evil from youth (Genesis 8-9). He says he argues this more fully elsewhere on his site and adds that the conclusion holds "even if my reasoning is flawed" on Noah. The main evidence is the internal sequence of Genesis, read as a straightforward narrative, along with later texts (Psalm 104, Isaiah 11, Galatians 5) that he reads back onto Eden.

The second half turns to how humans can return to that state. Wescoe gives a "short answer" (put faith in God, citing Matthew 6:10) and a "longer answer" built on five practices he draws from Genesis 1-2: caring for animals rather than killing them, returning to the plant diet of Genesis 1:29, growing food to feed people and animals, knowing and leading animals, and walking humbly without shame or blame. He ends by calling for love toward all creation and for faith in God, and he promotes more videos and his website.

## Key Teachings

### The restore point is Eden, before the fall in Genesis 3
Wescoe argues that the only point in Scripture where the created system is wholly free of corruption is the state described in Genesis 1-2. He notes that apart from Adam's aloneness, which God fixes by creating woman, nothing in those chapters is called "not good." Corruption begins in Genesis 3 when humans disobey and "start doing things our own way." He presents this as a reading of the text rather than as a historical-critical claim about its origins.

### Why the post-flood era is a weaker restore point
He flags Noah's sacrifice on leaving the ark as a departure from the Eden pattern, since sacrifice is not mentioned before Genesis 3 in his reading. He links the permission to eat animals and the "animal fear" God announces in Genesis 9 to a fall from the original arrangement. Because God says the human heart is evil from youth in the same passage, he concludes that the post-flood world still contains corruption. He explicitly brackets this argument and says the choice of Eden holds even if his reading of Noah is mistaken.

### The "short answer" and "longer answer" on restoration
Wescoe answers the practical question of "how do we get there" by saying the short answer is faith in God, supported by Matthew 6:10 ("your kingdom come"). The longer answer, he says, requires human responsibility: "through faith we have to do our part." He draws on the image of God in Genesis 1:26-28, Christ's character in the Gospels, and the fruits of the Spirit in Galatians 5:22-23 to describe how dominion should be exercised, namely with love, peace and gentleness.

### Five practices drawn from Genesis 1-2
He lists five instructions he says the Eden text gives:
1. Dominion as stewardship that refrains from harming animals and actively cares for them.
2. Return to the diet of Genesis 1:29, which he ties to 1 Corinthians 6:19-20 ("keep our bodies our temples").
3. Growing food in abundance to feed animals and people, linked to Psalm 104:28 and Matthew 24:45.
4. Maintaining close relationship with animals as Adam did in naming them (Genesis 2:19-20), to keep them from turning on each other, with Isaiah 11 as support.
5. Walking humbly, naked and unashamed as in Genesis 2:25, avoiding fear, shame and blame.

He argues that fear is the first thing mentioned after the fall (Genesis 3) and that animal fear is the first thing mentioned after the flood, so a diet that does not introduce killing "introduces no fear into the system."

### Covenant with animals and the limits of blame
Wescoe says God makes covenant promises with the animals as well as with humanity in Genesis 9, a point he says "a lot of people skip over." He also argues that blaming Satan, ancestors, Adam and Eve, or God is a way of avoiding responsibility, and that the shortcoming lies with humans, not God, who is "ready to restore things."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Restoring to Noah's post-flood world is a sound goal.** Wescoe argues that Noah's sacrifice, the permission to eat animals and the "animal fear" of Genesis 9 mark it as a departure from Eden, which he treats as the only fully "very good" state.
- **Dominion over animals means freedom to kill and use them.** He says "that's not what Dominion meant" and reads it as stewardship modeled on God's care, with instructions in Genesis 1-2 and not a license to kill.
- **Blame for the corruption lies with Satan, ancestors, Adam and Eve, or God.** He argues the shortcoming is human, since God is ready to forgive and restore if people turn back.
