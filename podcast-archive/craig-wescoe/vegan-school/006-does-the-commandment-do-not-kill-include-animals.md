## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks whether the sixth commandment, "Do not kill" (Exodus 20:13), covers only people or also animals. Because the commandment gives no recipients, he argues that the reader has to look at the context, and he finds the answer in the Sabbath commandment, the one he says names who the Ten Commandments are addressed to. That commandment includes "your cattle" alongside sons, daughters, servants and the stranger within your gates (Exodus 20:8-11).

His reasoning has three steps. He first frames the Ten Commandments as two halves, the first about worshiping God and the second about loving one's neighbor, with the Sabbath in the middle joining them. He then argues that the commandment lists are non-exhaustive, so the specific mention of cattle shows that animals fall within the scope of the commandments. Finally he concludes that "do not kill" applies to the same recipients as the Sabbath commandment, which includes animals. Wescoe concedes that the text "doesn't specify" and that "we don't know for certain," so he presents this as his reading rather than a settled result. ## Key Teachings

### The Sabbath as the key to the commandments' recipients
Wescoe says the commandments fall into two halves: the first concerns worship of God, and the second concerns love of neighbor. He places the Sabbath commandment in the middle as the link between them, since it both keeps God in view and gives a shared day of rest to everyone. He then uses this commandment to identify who the commandments are "for." In his reading, the Sabbath passage is the only one that names its recipients, so it is the reference point for the rest.

### The Sabbath text names cattle
He reads the Sabbath passage (Exodus 20:8-11) and points out that it lists "your son nor your daughter nor your manservant nor your maidservant nor your cattle nor your stranger who is within your gates." Wescoe argues that the explicit reference to cattle shows the commandments reach beyond humans to animals "within your power." He calls this "interestingly enough" and treats it as the central textual fact.

### The lists are non-exhaustive
He notes that the tenth commandment (coveting) names a neighbor's house, wife, servants, ox and donkey, and says this is "a non exhaustive list it can mean other things as well." He uses the same logic for "do not kill": since the text does not spell out its objects, it can cover more than the examples given. The commandment against coveting is the only one in the talk whose specific items he quotes.

### Applying the Sabbath recipients to "do not kill"
From the first two points he concludes that "do not kill" is directed at "any of the recipients... the commandments are talking about," and so includes the animals named in the Sabbath text. He acknowledges that this is an inference: "since it doesn't specify I guess we don't know for certain but it leads me to believe within the context."

### Jesus's summary of the commandments
Wescoe links the two halves to a saying of Jesus, that the greatest commandment is to love God with one's whole being and the second is to love one's neighbor as oneself. He treats this as a summary of the first and second halves of the Decalogue. The talk does not cite a Gospel passage for this, and it does not discuss how the saying relates to animals.
