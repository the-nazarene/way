## Summary

Wescoe asks what the story of Balaam's donkey in Numbers says about animals, and what follows for how we treat them. He reads the episode as one where the donkey is the faithful character: it sees an angel with a drawn sword that Balaam cannot see, turns aside three times, and is beaten each time. When the donkey speaks, Balaam does not react with surprise, and Wescoe takes that to mean the communication may not have been verbal. He says the exact mechanism does not matter, since the point is that the donkey protests being beaten for saving Balaam's life.

Wescoe then applies this to modern eating of animals. He says people justify killing animals because animals "can't talk" and are less intelligent, and he argues that animals communicate through body language, cries and howls, and that slaughtered animals would say what the donkey said: "why are you doing this to me." He describes slaughterhouse footage and concludes that viewers should listen to animals and consider what they are saying.

The talk ends with a call to listen to the animals humans harm. Wescoe offers no outside sources, and his claims about health and animal communication are stated as his own observations.

## Key Teachings

### The donkey as the faithful hero of Numbers
Wescoe presents the donkey as the moral center of the episode. The donkey stops three times because it sees the angel, Balaam beats it each time, and the donkey's question "why are you doing this to me" is answered by the angel's comment that the donkey saved Balaam's life. He calls this "one of the few times in the Bible where an animal communicates with a person," and mentions the serpent in Eden (Genesis 3) as another case. He says the donkey's words came from Hebrew text but are his paraphrase.

### Whether the speech was literal does not change the point
Wescoe says that Balaam's lack of surprise suggests the communication "may not have been a verbal communication," and then says that "regardless of whether this is like an actual conversation," the meaning is that the donkey protests unjust treatment. He treats the textual question as "kind of inconsequential" for his argument.

### Animals communicate, and humans do not need to dismiss them
He argues that people who kill and eat animals claim animals "don't matter" because they are dumb and cannot speak human languages. His reply is that animals "communicate perfectly well as long as you understand their language," citing hunger, needing to go outside, and excitement as examples, and pointing to anyone who has lived with a dog or cat.

### Application to eating animals
He says "we don't need to slaughter animals to be healthy," and that "in most cases it's unhealthy to eat animal dead bodies." These are his assertions; he gives no studies or sources for them. He concludes that the animals humans harm for food "would say exactly what Balaam's donkey said."

### Slaughterhouse footage as evidence of animal protest
He says the sounds and body language he sees in slaughterhouse videos, animals being abused, communicate "what are you doing to me, what have I done to deserve this," and treats this as the same message as the donkey's.
