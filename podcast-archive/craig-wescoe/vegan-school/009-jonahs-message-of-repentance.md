## Key Teachings

### Jonah's refusal and the storm
Wescoe explains that God commissioned Jonah to preach repentance to Nineveh, and Jonah refused because he judged the Ninevites beyond change. The storm that followed, in his telling, was a consequence of Jonah's disobedience, and Jonah offered himself to be thrown overboard to save the crew. The fish and the "three days and three nights" follow (Jonah 1-2). Wescoe treats the fish as a foreshadowing of Jesus' three days in the tomb, a parallel he states without argument.

### Nineveh's repentance and God's relenting
Wescoe describes the Ninevites as violent and wicked and reads their turning as a real change: they put on sackcloth, humbled themselves, fasted, prayed, and "turned from their evil way." He quotes Jonah 3:10 for God's response, that God "relented from the disaster." He defines repentance simply as turning around and doing the opposite of what one has been doing.

### God's mercy: 2 Peter 3:9 and the end of Jonah
Wescoe cites 2 Peter 3:9, that God does not want anyone to perish, to argue that God's warnings about the consequences of violent lifestyles are meant to produce repentance. He then reads the final passage of Jonah (Jonah 4) as God's answer to Jonah's anger: God asks whether he should not pity Nineveh, with its many people and its livestock. In Wescoe's reading, the point of judgment is repentance and forgiveness, and God is "patient with all of us."

### The sign of Jonah and the message of the kingdom
Wescoe says Jesus described his own sign as "the miracle of Jonah," and that Jesus' message was the same as Jonah's: repent, because the kingdom of God is at hand. He reasons that since Nineveh repented at Jonah's preaching, the people should repent at Jesus' preaching too, and he calls this Jesus' "greatest miracle." He gives no verse for this saying, and his reasoning from Nineveh to Jesus' hearers is his own inference.

### Nonviolence, mercy and the application
Wescoe's practical conclusion is that people should not be destroyed for wrongdoing but should be called to repent, adopting "a heart of love" and "good stewardship of the earth and all of its inhabitants including animals." He reads the Ninevites' ignorance ("who doesn't know the right hand from the left") as a reason for mercy, then ties this to Jesus on the cross praying that his executioners be forgiven (Luke 23).

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That God's judgment is mainly about punishing wrongdoers.** Wescoe argues that in the Jonah story God's threatened disaster was meant to bring repentance, and that God relents when people turn.
- **That the Ninevites (and by extension hard-hearted people) are beyond change.** Wescoe presents Jonah's expectation that nothing would change as wrong, since the city did repent.
