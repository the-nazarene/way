## Summary

Wescoe frames the talk as a Bible trivia question: after the flood, Noah builds an altar and offers burnt offerings of clean animals (Genesis 8:20), and the question is how God responded. His quiz answer is "all of the above," with three options he treats as God's response: God's resolve that he will not again curse the ground for man's sake (Genesis 8:21), animals' fear of humans (Genesis 9:2), and a covenant that includes animals. He argues that the covenant is often read as being only between God and humanity, but Genesis 9 names animals at least five times (Genesis 9:9-10, 9:12, 9:15, 9:16, and 9:17) as parties to it.

He then reads the context as a restart. Genesis 6:5 describes human corruption, and God's words after the sacrifice repeat that diagnosis. Wescoe argues that God's purpose in the restart was the one he gave at creation: animals and people to be fruitful and multiply (Genesis 8:16-17, and the blessing he gives Noah). He says Noah's altar was an unprompted act, and that the fear and dread of animals is a consequence of humanity acting on its own will rather than a new permission.

The talk closes with a takeaway. Genesis 1 through 3 should be read as "the blueprint to live by," God's ideal for humans and animals, and the passage about Noah leaving the ark should not be taken as permission to harm animals or as a change of divine mind. The talk is short (about 6:47) and makes no reference to scholarship; its argument depends on close reading of the Genesis text as it stands.

## Key Teachings

### The covenant explicitly includes animals
Wescoe's central point is that animals are parties to God's covenant with Noah. He reads Genesis 9:9-10 ("every living creature that is with you the birds the cattle and every beast of the earth"), Genesis 9:12, Genesis 9:15 ("every living creature of all flesh"), Genesis 9:16 ("every living creature of all flesh that is on the earth"), and Genesis 9:17 ("all flesh that is on the earth") as repeated, deliberate inclusion. He says this is overlooked and that "in case God didn't make that that clear" the text says it five times.

### Restart context: corruption, sacrifice, and the original purpose
He ties Genesis 6:5 (the world "became corrupt" and human inclination "was evil from youth") to God's words in Genesis 8:21, which he says repeat that diagnosis "in response to Noah sacrificing animals." He argues that the restart's aim was the original creation purpose: the animals and humans should be fruitful and multiply, with people as caretakers of the animals (Genesis 1, Genesis 8:16-17). He says the plan "was the same," and that God was restarting things "to be exactly the way they weren't Eden when he started it."

### Genesis 1 as the "blueprint," with animals made to praise God
Wescoe's practical conclusion is that Genesis 1 through 3 are "the blueprint to live by," which he calls "God's ideal." He says animals have a purpose ("to be fruitful multiply") and adds that they were created "to praise and give glory to God," citing Revelation without a verse. He presents this as what God "wants from us."

### The fear of humans and the meat-eating text are not divine permission
He addresses Genesis 9:2 (the fear and dread of humans on every beast). He rejects the reading that this is "God giving us permission to harm animals" or that "God changed his mind." Instead he calls the fear a consequence of "us doing our own will instead of God's will." The talk does not engage with the wording of the permission to eat animals beyond that; it treats the verse as a consequence, not as an instruction.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Noahic covenant is only between God and humanity.** Wescoe says it includes "every living creature" and that Genesis 9 names animals five times.
- **God gave permission to harm animals after the flood, or changed his mind about the original plan.** He argues that the flood-era text describes a consequence of human will, not a revised divine ideal, and that Genesis 1 remains the ideal.
