## Summary

Wescoe asks why God accepted Abel's offering in Genesis 4 but not Cain's, and what readers can take from the account for their own lives. He reads the two brothers as holding the two vocations God gave humanity, Cain as a tiller of the ground and Abel as a keeper of sheep. He argues that the difference lies in the quality of the gift. Cain brought "some of the fruit of the ground," while Abel brought "the firstlings of the fat of the flock," which he reads as Abel's best. He supports this with Hebrews 11:4, which says Abel's offering was accepted because it was offered in faith. Wescoe equates faith with offering God one's best.

The talk also argues against reading Genesis 4 as an animal sacrifice. He says the text "doesn't say anything here about the animal being killed," that the phrase is the only place in Scripture where this compound wording appears, and that altars and animal sacrifices appear only in Genesis 8. Readers who imagine a slaughtered sheep and gathered fat, he says, are reading a later practice back into an earlier story. The talk ends by drawing its applied lesson: "we should offer our very best to God." It gives no further outside argument, and the speaker does not name any scholars or translators.

## Key Teachings

### Two vocations, one contrast
Wescoe says God gave humanity two jobs, caretaking of animals and tilling the ground for food, and that Cain and Abel embody them. He reads the brothers' different occupations as the setting for the offerings. The talk does not give a verse for the divine charge; it describes it in general terms.

### "Some of the fruit" versus "the firstlings"
The central textual argument is the contrast in verses 3 and 4. Wescoe notes that the text says Cain brought "some of the fruit of the ground" and does not say "the first fruit." Abel brought "the firstlings of the fat of the flock." He takes the phrase "firstlings of the fat" to mean Abel's very best, and presents the lack of that qualifier for Cain as the reason for the rejection. He also says this compound phrase is the only time it is used in Scripture, though he does not show the Hebrew.

### Faith as offering one's best
Wescoe brings in Hebrews 11:4 to explain the acceptance. He says Abel's offering was accepted "because it was offered from faith," and he defines living by faith as trusting God's will over one's own and "offering God our best." He moves from Hebrews to Genesis without discussing how the two texts relate.

### Against the animal-sacrifice reading
Wescoe argues that some translators and readers assume Abel's gift must have been an animal sacrifice because it was an "offering," so they imagine Abel slaughtering a sheep and bringing back its fat. He rejects this, saying "it doesn't say anything here about the animal being killed." His reason is chronological: "altars don't even come about until Genesis 8 and animal sacrifices," so reading a later practice into Genesis 4 is an anachronism. He presents this as a common mistake.

### Sin at the door and the applied lesson
Wescoe paraphrases God's words to Cain, that sin "lies at the door" with its desire for Cain and that Cain should rule over it. He says Jesus "shows us how to overcome all sin later on," but he cites no text for this. He ends by saying that the key lesson is to offer God our very best, "that's what his brother came to not do."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Abel's offering was an animal sacrifice.** Wescoe says the text does not mention killing the animal. He argues that altars and animal sacrifices appear only in Genesis 8, so reading slaughter and burnt fat into Genesis 4 is anachronistic.
- **God's acceptance was arbitrary.** Wescoe frames the difference as one of quality and faith. Abel gave "the firstlings of the fat," his best, and Cain gave "some of the fruit," so the contrast is in the giving, not in the vocations.
