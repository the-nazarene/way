## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks what Jesus means by "faithful and wise steward" in Luke 12:42. He first explains a steward as someone an owner leaves in charge of an estate with instructions, and reads Jesus' question as "when he comes back who will he find doing the things that he instructed." Wescoe then turns to Genesis 1:27-31 and Genesis 2:15 to say what those instructions were from the start. In his reading, humanity is made in God's image, given dominion and told to tend the garden, and the food given to humans and animals in Genesis 1:29-30 is plant-based.

The talk ends with a summary: while waiting for the master's return, the faithful steward grows fruit and vegetation, feeds both themselves and the animals with it, and cares for the whole earth. Wescoe says that repentance means turning back to God and resuming these duties.

### Key Teachings

### 1. The steward who is found faithful (Luke 12:42)
Wescoe takes Luke 12:42 as Jesus' picture of a servant set over a household by a master who will return. Using a plain-language analogy (an estate manager following the owner's instructions while away), he says the test is whether the servant is found "doing the things that he instructed" when the master comes. The talk gives no discussion of the Greek, the parable's wider context in Luke 12, or other gospel versions.

### 2. Dominion as stewardship in Genesis 1-2
The core argument is that the stewardship Jesus invokes goes back to Genesis. Wescoe reads Genesis 1:28 ("be fruitful and multiply... subdue it, have dominion") and treats "dominion" as stewardship of the earth, not as license over it. He cites Genesis 1:29-30 (every seed-bearing herb and fruit tree given "for food," and the same herb given to beasts and birds) and says this shows God's original instruction was plant-based for people and animals alike. He adds Genesis 2:15 ("keepers to tend and keep the garden") to frame humans as "tillers of the ground," gardeners and food growers. He does not discuss the Hebrew terms or other translations of "dominion" or "subdue."

### 3. "First works" and the return to Eden (Revelation 2:4-7)
Wescoe reads the letter to Ephesus as praising the church's activity while faulting it on one point: it has left its "first works" and "first love." He reads "repent and do the first works" (2:5) as a call to return to the original instructions, and treats the lampstand warning and the "paradise of God" reference (2:7) as Eden imagery. He does not give dates, authorship, or the wider content of Revelation 2-3, and he does not say what the "first works" were in the text's own setting. His identification of them with Genesis is his own reading.

### 4. Synthesis: what faithful stewardship looks like
Wescoe combines the three passages into a practical definition. The faithful steward, while waiting for the master's return, grows fruit and vegetables, feeds humans and animals with them, and cares for the earth and its inhabitants. Failing this calls for repentance, which he defines as turning back to God and resuming those duties. The talk does not address Jesus' other teachings on Torah, sacrifice, or the kingdom, and does not discuss how its reading fits the wider New Testament.
