## Summary

Wescoe asks whether Romans 14 lets meat-eating believers ignore vegans who say they are weak in faith. The question comes from a common move: a meat-eater quotes Romans 14's "do not judge" and dismisses a vegan's objection to killing animals. The description frames the question as whether Paul's "weak" label means vegans can be brushed aside, and whether Paul gives advice on how to respond to a vegan troubled by animal harm.

Wescoe's answer is that the opening verses are only the start of the argument. He says Paul and the apostles were concerned with preventing division in the church, and that the disputes in view were about idolatry and sacrifices to false gods, not about whether veganism is right. He then reads on to Romans 14:15 ("do not destroy with your food the one for whom Christ died") and Romans 14:21 ("it is good neither to eat meat nor drink wine nor do anything by which your brother stumbles"). On his reading, Paul's conclusion is that causing a fellow believer distress over food is a failure to walk in love, so a believer who is troubled by animal harm should be heard, and the meat-eater should stop the practice.

## Key Teachings

### The "do not judge" opening is incomplete
Wescoe reads Romans 14:1-3 as the passage people usually cite: receive the one who is weak in faith, the one who "eats only vegetables," and do not let the eater despise the non-eater or the non-eater judge the eater. He says that stopping here leads people to conclude "we should not judge each other," and that this is only "the beginning of what he says."

### Paul is concerned with division, and the disputes are about idolatry, not veganism
Wescoe says Paul and the apostles were "trying to make peace whenever possible" and to prevent divisions in the church. He argues that they "are not even talking about whether you should be vegan" or whether harming animals is good, and that they were "oftentimes talking about idolatry," meaning meat sacrificed to false gods. He offers no textual argument for this reading inside the talk; it is his interpretive claim, and the reader should check it against Romans 14 and its context.

### Romans 14:15 and the call to walk in love
Wescoe cites Romans 14:15 ("if your brother is grieved because of your food you are no longer walking in love; do not destroy with your food the one for whom Christ died") and reads on to "the kingdom of God is not food and drink but righteousness and peace and joy in the holy spirit." He links this to a Pauline principle he paraphrases as "let everything you do be done with love," and applies it to a meat-eater whose brother or sister is "troubled because they have compassion for animals."

### Romans 14:21 as the conclusion, applied to animal harm
Wescoe presents Romans 14:21 as "the conclusion of the matter": abstaining from meat and wine is "good" if doing otherwise would make a brother stumble. He lists examples of what he calls harm to animals: "eating their dead bodies," "torturing cows to drink their milk and take their babies away." If a vegan believer is troubled by these practices, he argues, turning "a blind eye" is not acting in love, and the meat-eater should stop the practices. He concludes that quoting Romans 14 to justify harming animals is backwards, since the passage counsels giving up such practices.

- The belief that Romans 14 only says "do not judge," so meat-eaters can ignore vegans who call them harmful. Wescoe says that reading stops mid-argument and that Paul goes on to call for love and avoiding stumbling.
- The belief that Paul's food disputes were about eating meat at all; Wescoe says they concerned idolatry and sacrifices to false gods, and that Paul was not addressing veganism.
