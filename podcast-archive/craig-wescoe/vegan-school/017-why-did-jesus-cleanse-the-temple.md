## Summary

Wescoe asks why Jesus drove the animal sellers out of the temple, and whether the event was only about money changing. He argues it was an attack on animal sacrifice. He says the money changers are not mentioned in one of the accounts he cites, so the focus belongs on the animals. His main evidence is Jeremiah 7. He reads Jeremiah 7:11 ("a den of thieves") as a charge against people who serve God while doing what God did not command, and he reads Jeremiah 7:21-23 as God saying that burnt offerings and sacrifices were not part of what he commanded at the Exodus. He ties the "den of thieves" language to John 10:10, where a thief "comes to steal and to kill and destroy," and contrasts it with Jesus coming "that they may have life." He then links the cleansing to Isaiah 11:9, a picture of a peaceable holy mountain where nothing is hurt or destroyed. He closes by saying the cleansing "is to overturn animal sacrifices," which he says is "not what God wants." The talk is short (about five and a half minutes), and it is a single presenter's reading rather than a survey of scholarship.

## Key Teachings

### The "den of thieves" is the animal sellers, not the money changers
Wescoe opens by saying many people assume the event "was just all about the money changers." He points out that the money changers are "not even mentioned" in one account, and he reads John 2:14-15 (sheep, oxen, doves, the changers' money poured out, tables overturned) as a scene centered on those selling animals "to be slaughtered." He says Matthew 21 (quoting Matthew 21:13 with "my house shall be called a house of prayer, a den of thieves") is where Jesus calls them thieves. He treats the Matthew, Mark, Luke and John accounts as versions of the same event, though he does not compare their wording in detail.

### Jeremiah 7 as the interpretive key
This is the core argument. Wescoe reads Jeremiah 7:11 ("this house which is called by my name... become a den of thieves") and says the whole chapter criticizes people who represent God while "doing the reverse" and "following their own evil imaginations." He then reads Jeremiah 7:21-23, where God says to add burnt offerings to sacrifices and eat the meat, and also says that he "did not speak to your fathers or command them" about burnt offerings or sacrifices in the day of the Exodus, but commanded them to "obey my voice." Wescoe's reading is that God's core demand was obedience, not sacrifice, and that Jesus's act applies this same critique. Note that this is his interpretation of the Hebrew and of the chapter's structure; he does not discuss the wider debate over how Jeremiah 7 relates to Torah sacrificial law.

### The cleansing as an overturning of sacrifice practices done in God's name
Wescoe argues that the sacrifices were "done in the name of God," and that Jesus "overturns all that" by "overturning all these practices." In his reading, the cleansing is the action; the target is the sacrificial system's practices, not commerce as such.

### Nonviolence and the peaceable kingdom
He reads Isaiah 11:9 ("they will not hurt nor destroy in all my holy mountain") as the opposite of the "thief" who "steal[s] and kill[s] and destroy[s]" (John 10:10). He says that in the new order "nobody is killing and destroying" because people "lead the animals like a child." He connects this to a saying that you "can't enter the kingdom" unless you become like a child (he gives no verse for this). The sacrificers, in his framing, are the thieves who kill the animals.
