## Summary

Wescoe asks what "dominion" means in Genesis 1:28, since some people cite it to justify exploiting, mistreating and killing animals. He argues that the word describes a caring, stewardly role, and that the text points toward a plant-based diet for both humans and animals. His main evidence is the sequence of verses in Genesis 1:27-31: the blessing and dominion in verse 28, the herb and fruit diet in verse 29, the animals' food in verse 30, and God's approval in verse 31. He reads the fact that God gave humans instructions about both their own food and the animals' food as evidence that humans are to grow and feed the animals. He supports this with Genesis 2:15, Isaiah 11:6, Luke 12:42 and 1 John 4:8, 16.

The talk ends with Wescoe concluding that dominion "involves taking care of the animals and also being vegan," and that anyone using Genesis 1:28 against veganism should "take a closer look." The talk is a single speaker's close reading of a few verses. It does not engage with any other biblical passage, any ancient interpreter, or any historical context for these texts.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Dominion is tied to being created in God's image
Wescoe reads Genesis 1:27 ("God created man in His image") as the setting for the blessing in verse 28, so that "dominion" describes what it means to bear that image. He notes that Adam and Eve are both created in God's image. He treats the verse as a description of a role rather than a license, and he calls the common reading (that humans may do whatever they like to animals) a caricature he lists at the start of the talk.

### 2. Genesis 1:29-30 gives a plant-based diet
Wescoe quotes verse 29 ("every herb that yields seed... it shall be for food") and verse 30, which gives "every green herb for food" to every beast, bird and creeping thing. He argues that if dominion meant permission to kill and eat animals, verse 29 would not immediately follow with a plant diet. He concludes that God "essentially" prescribed "this plant-based vegan diet," and that the diet "was to not kill the animals and eat them but instead to eat fruit and vegetation."

### 3. The food instructions show that humans are to feed animals
Wescoe's central interpretive claim is structural. God blesses animals and humans with the same command to be fruitful and multiply, but only humans receive the added instruction "now for you have dominion" and then a statement of their food. God then tells humans what the animals eat. Wescoe reads this as meaning "part of our instruction of having Dominion is to feed the animals," so humans are to grow the food and feed it to the animals.

### 4. Supporting passages: Genesis 2:15, Isaiah 11:6, Luke 12:42, 1 John 4:8, 16
- Genesis 2:15 (Adam placed in the garden "to tend and keep it") is read as making humans gardeners and growers of food.
- Isaiah 11:6 ("a child leading the animals") is cited for the picture of humans leading and caring for animals.
- Luke 12:42 is cited for the "faithful and wise stewards" who feed others "their portion of food in due season," which Wescoe links to what "the master Jesus" expects on his return. He hedges on the reference ("I believe it's Luke 12 42").
- 1 John 4:8 and 4:16 ("God is love") are used to say that the image of God is one of love, so humans should rule in that love.

### 5. Conclusion: dominion and veganism are not opposed
Wescoe summarizes that dominion, read closely, means taking care of animals and feeding them, and that this is consistent with veganism. He says the anti-vegan use of the verse has the relationship "reverse[d]."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Dominion means permission to exploit, torture, mutilate or kill animals.** Wescoe argues that dominion is a charge to care for and feed animals, and that the diet God gives in Genesis 1:29-30 excludes killing them for food.
- **Genesis 1:28 supports eating meat, so it cannot support veganism.** Wescoe argues the opposite: the verses that follow the dominion command describe a plant-based diet, so the text supports veganism.
