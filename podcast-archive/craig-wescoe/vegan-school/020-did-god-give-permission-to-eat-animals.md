## Summary

Wescoe takes up a common reading of Genesis 8-9, in which God's words to Noah after the flood ("just as I've given you the green herb... now I give you everything") are taken to mean God changed his original vegan instruction and added animals to the menu. He asks whether that is what the text is doing, or whether it is granting a freedom with a warning attached.

His answer is that God grants freedom of choice, not approval. He reads Deuteronomy 30:15 and James 4:17 as setting out a choice between the path of life and the path that leads to death. He then argues that Genesis 8:20-21 presents Noah's burnt offering as something Noah did "on his own," not something God commanded, and that it comes from "evil inclinations." He contrasts this with Genesis 1, where creation is "very good," with plant food and no fear, and with the post-flood fear of humans among the animals (Genesis 9:2, Habakkuk 2:17). For him, fear entered with the fall (Genesis 3) and is linked to human violence against animals, which he says is "not part of God's plan." He supports this with Galatians 5:22-23 and a phrase about love casting out fear.

The talk ends with a practical conclusion. Wescoe says "we don't have permission," that God "gave us the freedom to kill animals," but that this brings consequences. Because the good is known (Genesis 1:29), failing to follow it is sin under James 4:17 and 1 Corinthians 10:23. He ends by urging listeners not to look for ways to justify acting against God's will, but to repent and trust God's knowledge of what is best.

### Key Teachings

### 1. Freedom to choose, not divine approval
Wescoe reads Deuteronomy 30:15 ("here's the path of life... and here's the path that is not good that leads to death") and James 4:17 as showing that God gives people a choice. He applies this to Genesis 9 and says God "wasn't saying I changed my mind." The reading is that God allows the choice without endorsing it.

### 2. Noah's altar as unauthorized
He points to Genesis 8:20-21, where Noah builds an altar and burns animals. He says this was "not what God instructed him to do," and that God's response calls human imagination and heart "evil." On this reading the sacrifice is evidence of human inclination, not of new divine instruction.

### 3. Genesis 1 contrasted with post-flood fear
Wescoe sets Genesis 1 (creation "very good," with humans given "dominion over the animals" and plant food, Genesis 1:29) against Genesis 9:2, where "the fear of you will be on the animals" and the world is "filled with violence." He adds Habakkuk 2:17, where animals are "terrified" because of human violence toward them. He argues that fear was absent in the beginning and appears after the fall.

### 4. Fear as the fruit of the fall, love as God's way
Linking Genesis 3 (Adam and Eve afraid and hiding after they sinned) to the animal passages, he argues that fear belongs to "the fallen world." He sets it against Galatians 5:22-23 (love, peace, gentleness, self-control) and a phrase he quotes as "perfect love casts out fear." His conclusion is that "God's way is love" and that fear and violence are the opposite of that way.

### 5. Knowing the good and not doing it
Using James 4:17 and 1 Corinthians 10:23, he argues that once God has shown the good (Genesis 1:29), not doing it is sin. He treats this as the basis for rejecting arguments that justify eating animals. The 1 Corinthians point is stated loosely in the transcript ("if we if we know to do good and we don't do it"), so the exact wording should be checked against the Greek or a translation before quoting.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Popular belief:** Genesis 9:3 shows God changed his mind about the original diet and added animals to the menu. **Correction (Wescoe):** Genesis 9 grants freedom of choice, not approval. Noah's altar was his own act, and the post-flood fear and violence are consequences of the fall, not part of God's plan.
