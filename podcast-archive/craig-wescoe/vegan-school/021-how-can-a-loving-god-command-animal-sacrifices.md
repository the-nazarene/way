## Summary

Craig Wescoe, presenting for Swords to Plowshares, asks how a loving God could command animal sacrifices. He frames the problem with Hebrews 10:4, which says the blood of bulls and goats cannot take away sin, and with Psalms and Isaiah passages that say God did not require sacrifice. If sacrifice could not atone and God did not want it, Wescoe asks, why was it commanded?

His answer has three parts. First, sacrifice was addressed to a rebellious people who ignored God's main instructions to care for creation and live by love and mercy. Sacrifice is "the first step toward obedience," not obedience itself, and it asked the people at least to acknowledge their sin and turn to God. Second, Wescoe points to Hosea 6:6, quoted in Matthew 9:13, as the stated divine preference for mercy over sacrifice. Third, he reads the command as a test that reveals what is in the heart, citing the binding of Isaac in Genesis 22 and 2 Chronicles 32:31.

The talk ends by saying that the topic is more complicated than a "bloodthirsty god" reading suggests, and it points viewers to a longer article on the Swords to Plowshares website. The argument is Wescoe's own harmonizing reading of the biblical texts. He cites no ancient or modern scholars and no historical or archaeological evidence.

### Key Teachings

### Sacrifice as a concession to a disobedient people
Wescoe's central claim is that God addressed sacrifice to Israelites who were "refusing to do God's will" and "refusing to live by mercy and love." He describes the original instructions as a charge to be "caretakers of the earth" and "leaders of the animals," which the people did not follow. In his reading, God then says, in effect, that if they will eat flesh and do as they please, they should at least offer the sacrifice to God as an acknowledgment of their sin.

### Hebrews and the Psalms: sacrifice cannot atone
He starts from Hebrews 10:4, which he presents as denying that animal blood can take away sin, and from Psalm 40:6, Psalm 51:16 and Isaiah 1:11, which he reads as saying God did not require animal sacrifice. The talk treats this tension as the question any "cursory reading" of the sacrificial laws would raise.

### Mercy over sacrifice as the stated divine desire
Wescoe quotes Hosea 6:6 ("I desire mercy not sacrifice") and links it to Matthew 9:13. He says that in these passages God names what he actually wants: obedience, knowledge of God and mercy. He argues that the laws of sacrifice have to be read against this stated preference.

### Sacrifice as a test that reveals the heart
To explain why God would command something he does not desire, Wescoe proposes that the command is a test. He cites the binding of Isaac in Genesis 22, presented as a test of whether Abraham put God above "the thing he cares most about," and 2 Chronicles 32:31, which he reads as another case of God testing to reveal what is in a person's heart. He asks viewers whether they are living by God's values of "love of joy of peace of patience mercy" or serving their own will.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That God is a bloodthirsty deity who simply wants animals killed.** Wescoe says this reading ignores the context. He argues that God repeatedly asks for love, mercy and obedience, and that sacrifice was a concession to people who were already ignoring those instructions.
- **That the sacrificial commands reflect God's ultimate will.** Wescoe argues that God's own words in Psalms, Isaiah and Hosea show he did not want sacrifice, and that the commandment must be read against those statements.
