## Key Teachings

### Animals as individuals, not commodities
Wescoe's central claim is that the movement should change how people view animals. He says he is "faith-based" while others are "strictly love based," and he rejects ownership language: "as a vegan I don't believe in owning another being I believe in taking care of another being." He treats rescue, guardianship and companionship as good stewardship. He criticizes buying animals on impulse (the Easter rabbit example) and the pet trade, saying that buying from breeders or puppy mills feeds shelter euthanasia.

### Biblical basis: Genesis and "loving your neighbor"
In answer to Pearlman's question about the Christian basis, Wescoe cites Genesis 1:29-31, which he reads as giving humans dominion while assigning a "completely plant-based diet of fruit and the herb of the ground" and charging them to care for animals. He cites Genesis 2:15 for the role of tending the earth. He then argues that the command to love one's neighbor "as yourself" extends to "our animal neighbors," because animals were created to share the earth. He gives no verse for the neighbor command.

### Swords into plowshares as a nonviolence principle
He explains his organization's name through Isaiah 2:4, which he reads as "instead of building weapons to use against our neighbor and against animals instead we'll be growing food to feed to our neighbor and the animals." This is the talk's clearest statement of the nonviolence theme.

### Specific industries: racing, circuses, factory farms
- **Horse racing:** He says deaths and injuries recur at racetracks across the country and that training and whipping are "inherent" to racing. He distinguishes horses running naturally from racing demands. He concedes he lacked statistics on the claim that horse deaths occur every year at almost every racetrack.
- **Circuses:** He calls for dropping wild animals, citing small cages and cruel training, while noting that the underlying problem is "exploiting animals for entertainment."
- **Factory farms:** He argues that the cruelty differs in degree among producers but that all are driven by profit. Pigs and dogs are compared as similarly intelligent. He cites the Yulin dog festival in China as a parallel he finds hypocritical in Western discourse.

### Protest, law and purchasing
Wescoe says protests can move elected officials, but he notes that lobbying and subsidies make legal change slow. His "primary purpose" is changing consumer demand: "if we change people's attitude to where they don't want to purchase these animal products... then everything else will follow." He also mentions veganic agriculture as an alternative to animal-manure fertilizer.

### Animal testing and fur
He calls animal testing "archaic" and says that its results often "don't even correlate with the safety of the product on humans in most cases." He offers no study or source for this in the interview. On fur, he says it "looks better on the animal that was born with it" and that wearing it is "violence." He answers Pearlman's question about an inherited mink stole with "I guess I would bury that coat."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Horses love to race, so racing is natural.** Wescoe grants that horses enjoy running but says racing requires overgrowing muscle mass in their legs and whipping, which differs from what horses do "naturally."
- **Pets and farm animals are a sharply divided issue within animal rights.** He says the divide "is not as much of a divide as it might seem," and blames the word "pet" for implying ownership.
- **Animal testing is needed for product safety.** He argues that animal testing is "archaic" and often does not predict human safety, though he gives no source for this.
- **Only the worst operations are cruel.** He says cruelty varies in degree but that corporate profit drives all of them toward "as inhumane as possible" treatment.
