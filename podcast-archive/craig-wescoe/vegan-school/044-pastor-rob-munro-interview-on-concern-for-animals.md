## Summary

Craig Wescoe interviews Pastor Rob Munro (transcribed as "robert monroe") at a 48-hour vigil for animal liberation. The question is whether Christians should show concern for animals, and whether being vegan is enough or whether believers should also speak out against animal abuse.

Munro says that veganism is only a starting point. He argues that Christians should become "vegan animal rights activists," and he grounds this in Jesus' sayings about "the least of these" (Matthew 25:40, 25:45), the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:30-37), and the account of Jesus driving animals and sellers out of the temple (John 2:15; Luke 19:45-46). He reads the temple episode as Jesus liberating animals from sale for profit. He also argues that Jesus was crucified for disrupting the animal trade and the sacrifices that supported the priests, not for overturning money-changers' tables. His evidence is mainly his reading of these texts, a claim about how many Gospels mention money-changers, and personal testimony that veganism deepened his connection with God. Munro also presents an eschatological vision drawn from Isaiah 11, Hosea 2, Revelation 21:4 and the Lord's Prayer, in which animals live in safety and the kingdom comes "on earth as it is in heaven."

The talk ends with a closing prayer that Munro and Wescoe say together, recited in part from the Lord's Prayer. Munro then gives where his Sunday services are broadcast. The exchange is short and is almost entirely Munro's argument; Wescoe mostly asks questions and affirms his points.

## Key Teachings

### Concern for "the least of these" extends to animals
Munro reads Matthew 25 as Jesus identifying himself with the vulnerable: "whatever you did to the least." He applies "the least of these" to animals, naming "a bunny rabbit a squirrel chicken" and "any sentient thing," and says that harming them harms Jesus. He pairs this with the corollary in Matthew 25:45 ("whoever doesn't do the least of these doesn't do for me") and the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:30-37), where the priest and Levite pass by and only the Samaritan helps. He applies this to animals in slaughterhouses and those killed for fur, and says failing to help them is "neglecting that person or that creature in need."

### The temple episode as Jesus liberating animals
Munro's central proof text is John 2:15, where Jesus makes a whip and drives out "sheep and cattle." He says the event is "mentioned in all four gospels and the money changers only mentioned in three of the four gospels," and he reads the John account as Jesus freeing the animals and driving out those "selling these animals to be slaughtered for profit." He quotes Luke 19:46 ("my house shall be a house of prayer... a den of thieves") and tells listeners to "look up the definition" of "den of thieves," suggesting it means something different from how it is usually heard. He claims that animals are treated as commodities, "sentient creatures created from the breath of life."

### Crucifixion as a response to the animal trade and sacrifice
Munro argues that Jesus "was not crucified for knocking over the money changes tables." He says Jesus was killed "for disrupting the culture which was the sale of animals which was supporting the priest's livelihood and the killing of animals." He describes the temple sacrifices as producing "rivers of blood" and calls Jesus "our first animal rights activist who was killed for the cause." He pictures the Passover as the setting for this disruption.

### Money versus God
Munro cites Matthew 6:24 ("You cannot serve both God and money") and argues that a God of "love," "mercy," "gentleness and compassion" cannot be served alongside treating animals "as a commodity to gain power and wealth." He connects this to everyday diet and consumption: "when we think of our own lifestyles and our own diets... who suffers as a result of that."

### Veganism as a beginning, activism as the requirement
Munro says that the movement needs more than diet. He notes "600 million vegans and vegetarians in the world today yet less than one percent are actually active," and calls for an "exponential" growth of activists. He frames Jesus' example as "not only disrupting" but "also teaching and living by example." Wescoe agrees that believers "need to be more than just vegan."

### Personal testimony: veganism and a closer connection with God
Munro says that giving up animals from his diet, wardrobe, and abuse "opens our soul to the lord." He says he was "a good man" and "a preacher" but "never had the connection that I had now," and reports that other clergy and Christians have told him the same. He concludes that if this is available, "you must you must go vegan," since "no one gets hurt and you get to have a direct connection to god." This is his personal and reported experience; the talk does not present it with independent evidence.

### Eschatological hope: the kingdom on earth
In the closing prayer, Munro asks God to "bring about your kingdom on earth as it is in heaven," invoking Isaiah 11 (animals "lying down in safety") and Hosea 2 ("bow and sword being banished from this earth"). He prays for an end to "tear" and "death" and "killing," tying this to the world God "designed it to be." He recites the Lord's Prayer as the close.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Being vegan is enough.** Munro says that believers "need to be more than just vegan" and must become animal rights activists who speak out against abuse.
- **Jesus was crucified for overturning the money-changers' tables.** Munro argues that the crucifixion was tied to disrupting the sale of animals and the sacrifices that supported the priests, not to the tables themselves.
- **"Den of thieves" means what it seems to mean.** Munro tells listeners to "look up the definition" and suggests the phrase has a different meaning from the usual one. He does not give the alternative reading in the transcript.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Torah, sacrifice, diet:** Munro treats the temple sacrifices as producing "rivers of blood" and argues that Jesus opposed the animal economy that supported them. He applies this to modern diet and consumption, asking "who suffers as a result of that" in our food and clothing choices. Torah itself is not discussed.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** The closing prayer asks for the kingdom to come "on earth as it is in heaven," and invokes Isaiah 11 and Hosea 2 for a world without killing or war. This is a central hope in the talk.
- **Nonviolence:** Munro draws on the prophetic vision of "bow and sword" being abolished (Hosea 2:18). The talk does not otherwise address Jesus' own use of force in the temple, though the whip is mentioned.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Munro presents Jesus as the "first animal rights activist" and as the example for Christian action, but he does not address Christological questions in depth.
- **How the Gospels were formed:** Munro's argument depends on counting which Gospels mention the money-changers. The claim is brief and not supported with sources, and the description's note flags it as inaccurate for Luke.
