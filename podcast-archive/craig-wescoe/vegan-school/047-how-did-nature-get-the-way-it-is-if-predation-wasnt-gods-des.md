## Summary

This is the second meeting of the Vegan Christian Discipleship School, a class session on the question "How did nature get the way it is if predation wasn't God's design?" The session follows a study guide Craig Wescoe wrote for the class (he says it runs about nine pages with roughly 28 footnotes, though the guide itself is not in the transcript). Participants take turns reading its paragraphs aloud, and Craig leads discussion after each one. Most of the substance is Craig's, with participants adding personal stories and occasional sources.

Craig's answer is that predation was never God's intention. He argues from Genesis 1:29-30, where animals and people are given plants for food, and from the human stewardship mandate in Genesis 1:28 and 2:15. He says humans failed that role by destroying habitats and breeding animals for profit (citing Genesis 30:41-43). He then argues that a deeper cause was spiritual: Genesis 6:4 and the Book of Enoch describe Giants whose spirits became demons who spread violence, which Craig treats as the background to the Flood. He reads Romans 8:19-22 as saying creation was subjected to futility by an adversary, not by God, and he presents Isaiah 11, Revelation 21:4 and 2 Chronicles 7:14 as the promise of restoration.

The session ends with a discussion of whether the vision is "believable," with participants comparing it to past technological change and noting the stakes for belief in the Fall and the origin of death. Craig suggests that questioning the biblical account of how sin and death entered the world also puts the hope of their overcoming at risk. The group closes in prayer and previews next month's session on Noah and the Flood, with a recording to be shared.

### Key Teachings

### Predation as corruption of a good original design

Craig's central claim is that God designed all creatures as herbivores. He cites Genesis 1:29-30 ("I have given every green plant for food") and reads the study guide's claim that many human philosophies reject this account, quoting Colossians 2:8 as a warning against "hollow and deceptive philosophy." He says the design argument is the starting point for everything else, and that teaching should begin at Genesis 1 rather than Genesis 3 or Genesis 9. When a participant mentions that some Christians start with Genesis 9 (which permits eating animals) instead of Genesis 1, Craig agrees this is a twisting of Scripture, and says that Genesis 9 will be taken up at the next meeting.

### Human stewardship failure and domestication

Craig argues that humans were charged with stewardship (Genesis 2:15, Genesis 1:28) and failed it by destroying the habitats designed for herbivores. He gives a story, told by a participant about an unnamed bird, of birds attacking sheep after their native plant food was uprooted and then reverting when the plants were replanted. He also argues that humans corrupted animals through selective breeding, citing Genesis 30:41-43 (Jacob's breeding), and extends this to modern cows bred for udder size and sheep bred for wool. On the science question, Craig distinguishes microevolution (which he calls testable, using dog breeding as an example) from macroevolution, which he calls "some sort of metascientific theory" that cannot be tested. He presents this as his own view, and a participant says that scientists would dispute the testability claim.

### Fallen angels, Giants and demons

This is the most detailed and most disputed part of the session. Craig reads Genesis 6:4, which speaks of "sons of God" and Giants, and the study guide's reading of 1 Enoch 7:4-5, which describes the Giants devouring people, animals and each other's flesh and drinking blood. He says the Book of Enoch was found among the Dead Sea Scrolls and predates the New Testament, that Jude quotes it, that early Christian sources treat it as Scripture, and that Jesus refers to it as Scripture in the Gospels. He says Craig's view of the "sons of God" is that they were angelic beings who went against their design, and that the Giants' spirits became the demons of the New Testament. He draws on Psalm 82 and Deuteronomy 32 to identify the sons of God as a divine council, and contrasts them with the "sons of God" in Romans 8, who are humans. A participant recommends Michael Heiser's video on the unseen realm, which Craig endorses as a resource.

### Romans 8: who subjected creation?

Craig reads Romans 8:19-22 and addresses the question of who "subjected" creation to frustration. He says some translators capitalize "one" to make it God, but he thinks the subject is the adversary. His reasoning is that Scripture elsewhere says there is no evil in God, and that Genesis 6 and Genesis 3 attribute corruption to dark spiritual forces. He reads the "pains of childbirth" language in Romans 8 as a temporary precursor to a new creation, and he describes Paul's Greek as very challenging to translate.

### Restoration and the end of predation

Craig presents the restoration side of the argument. He reads Isaiah 11:6-9 as a picture of peace among animals led by "a little child," and argues that humans must first do their part, leading animals "in the way of peace," before the animals can become what God created them to be. He links this to Revelation 21:4, 2 Chronicles 7:14 (which he reads as God's condition for healing the land), and 2 Peter 3:9 (God's patience). He also uses the parables of readiness and the fruit of the Spirit in Galatians 5:22-23 (which he connects to Torah as "teaching," with more to follow next meeting) to argue that humans should show self-control and gentleness toward animals.

### Is the end of predation believable?

Near the end, a participant reads an argument that human technological progress (telegrams to video calls, carriages to airplanes) shows that God's future can be believed. Craig and participants extend this with the example of Little Tike, a lioness reported to have refused meat, which a participant says is documented. A participant admits to the difficulty of setting evolutionary education aside, and Craig responds that the heart of the matter is the origin of sin and death: if the Fall did not happen as Genesis describes, the hope of Jesus's overcoming of sin and death is also called into question.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Predation is natural and was always part of God's creation.** Craig rejects this, citing Genesis 1:29-30 and reading predation as a product of the Fall and of corruption rather than design.
- **Genesis 9 gives permission to eat animals as the starting point.** Participants note this is common, and Craig says it twists Scripture by skipping Genesis 1.
- **Macroevolution is scientifically proven.** Craig says macroevolution is untestable and a metascientific theory, while microevolution is testable. A participant says scientists would dispute this.
- **Peace among animals is only a heavenly hope, not an earthly one.** A participant reports this common view, and Craig replies that Scripture begins with the original design in Genesis 1 and not with the Fall.
- **Romans 8:20 refers to God subjecting creation to frustration.** Craig argues the subject is the adversary, citing the lack of evil in God and the broader biblical picture.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Paul:** Romans 8:19-22 and Galatians 5:22-23 are central to the session's argument, and Craig reads them as apostolic authority. The talk does not contrast Paul with Jesus; it reads Paul as consistent with Genesis, Isaiah and Revelation.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Craig quotes James's teaching that faith without corresponding actions is dead, applying it to people who claim to care about animals but eat them.
- **Ebionites, Nazarenes and Essenes:** Craig mentions the Book of Enoch being found among the Dead Sea Scrolls and being quoted by Jude and treated as Scripture by early Christians. This is the only direct contact with the Qumran material, and it is used to establish the authority of Enoch rather than to discuss a community.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet and nonviolence:** Diet and nonviolence are central. Craig reads Genesis 1:29-30 as a herbivorous design and Matthew 5:9 and Isaiah 11 as nonviolent peace. He mentions Torah in connection with Galatians 5 and defers the discussion to next meeting.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Craig reads the Lord's Prayer and Isaiah 11 as a vision for peace on earth as in heaven, and ties it to the claim that God waits for human repentance (2 Chronicles 7:14, 2 Peter 3:9).
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** Revelation 21:4 is read as a promise of a new creation. Craig distinguishes this from a view that the peace is only in heaven, and he argues the origin of sin and death matters for the hope of their overcoming.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Jesus is referred to as the Word (John 1:1) and as the Prince of Peace (per the study guide paragraph). The talk does not develop a Christology.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Craig's claim that Jesus refers to Enoch as Scripture, and that Jude quotes Enoch, is offered as evidence for the status of Enoch in the first-century Jewish and early Christian mindset.
