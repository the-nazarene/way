## Summary

Craig Wescoe, presenting on his channel (the intro calls it "Swords to Plowshares"), and his guest Chris ask what Paul means by "the table of demons" in 1 Corinthians 10:21 and what the Clementine Homilies say about it. ## Key Teachings

### 1. The table of demons is the opposite of the Lord's table
Craig reads 1 Corinthians 10:21 ("you cannot drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons too") as meaning that participation in the Lord's table requires full abstention from the "table of demons." He describes the Lord's table as the community meal of bread and cup, and the table of demons as the eating of sacrificed food. He says Paul "doesn't elaborate very thoroughly" and that the Clementine literature goes "into much more detail." In his closing summary he says, "this material of eating at the table of demons uh is the exact opposite of eating at the table of the Lord," with flesh and blood on the demonic table.

### 2. The Clementine lists parallel the Jerusalem Council
Craig repeatedly sets the Homilies lists beside Acts 15. In Homily 7.8 he hears "abstain from the table of devils... from food offered to Idols from dead carcasses from animals which have been suffocated... and from blood." He matches this to the Acts 15 terms (idol-food, things strangled, blood, and porneia, which he reads as "faithfulness to God" rather than sexual immorality). He argues that James's conclusion at the Jerusalem Council made these abstentions the non-negotiable requirement, while circumcision was dropped for Gentiles. He suggests the Noahide laws as background for the minimum requirements.

### 3. Demonology drawn from 1 Enoch and Genesis
Craig and Chris tie the table of demons to the Enoch story of the Giants, who "longed only after the taste of blood" (1 Enoch 7:5, read in the discussion). Chris explains that, because demons are disembodied spirits without bodies of their own, they must possess humans or animals in order to eat. Demons are thus admitted "by your own hands" through the food given to them (Homily 9.9 as read). Craig links this to the post-flood prohibition on eating "flesh with its blood" (Genesis 9) and to the forbidden fruit of Genesis 2-3 as a first instance of "table fellowship with demons."

### 4. Afterlife consequences: demons, souls and purifying fire
Homily 9.9, read aloud, describes souls mixed with demons being "horribly punished" in the purifying fire at the end of all things, while the demon, made of fire, "is greatly pleased." Craig's reading is that the fire is painful to the soul because the soul is "made of light," and that the fire is the demon's element. He treats this as the fate of those who keep the demons in their bodies, and he does not present it as an eternal sentence imposed by God. He says the Homily frames the consequence as "the Judgment of evil Deeds," not "God's inflicting Vengeance."

### 5. The remedy: abstinence, fasting, prayer and baptism
Homilies 9.10 and 9.11 are read as hopeful: "abstinence and fasting and suffering of Affliction" are the most useful help against demons, while "the soul being turned by faith... quenches the demon as a spark of fire." Craig links the water to baptism and to Genesis 1:2 ("the spirit of God was hovering over the waters"). He cites Jesus's teaching that some demons go out only through prayer and fasting. He reads the "labor... to be solicitous about the putting to the flight of his own demon" as a personal duty, and he rejects the attitude that "I'm saved by grace anyway" so the matter can be ignored.

### 6. Paul's letters, Antioch, and the other side of the dialogue
Craig says that reading Paul alone gives "only Paul's side of the dialogue," and that the Clementine material helps reconstruct the disagreement behind passages on idol-meat, and on the "weak" who eat only vegetables (Romans 14; 1 Corinthians 8). He reads Paul's confrontation with Peter in Antioch (Galatians 2) as being about table fellowship and the same demonic-table concern. He thinks Paul was "kind of almost saying" the Jerusalem position was right "but let's still appease them." On the later Marcion debate, Craig cites the scholar Jason BeDuhn (name as garbled in the captions) for the view that Marcion's shorter Paul and shorter Luke came earlier than the versions the Proto-Orthodox kept. He suggests this could matter for whether Paul's letters were later edited, but he defers the question.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Acts 15 is only about circumcision and its food rules are obsolete.** Craig says the food requirements were the "absolute requirement" and that some claim they have been "nailed to the cross." He says that reading is wrong, since the Council came after the cross and James said the abstentions were the one thing that could not be compromised.
- **Paul's single mention of the table of demons means it is minor.** Craig says the list-pattern of related prohibitions recurs in more than a dozen New Testament passages and many Old Testament ones, so that a single explicit verse does not mean the topic was marginal.
- **Cannibalism is the only thing the "dead flesh" prohibition targets.** Craig rejects this: "you don't think that this is just referring to cannibalism... not at all."
- **Being "saved by grace" makes what you eat irrelevant.** Craig calls this an "wrong attitude" and says the Homilies make diligence in "putting to flight" demons a personal duty.
- **Paul was demon-possessed.** Craig says that, in his view, Paul was not possessed; a possessed person sent by Satan was harassing him. He defers the fuller treatment.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Craig reads 1 Corinthians 10:21 and Galatians 2 as evidence of a disagreement over table fellowship and idol-meat, with Paul "kind of almost saying... well we know we're the ones who are right but let's still appease them." - **James and the Jerusalem community:** The central thesis is that the Homilies preserve James-era teaching requiring abstention from demonic tables, and that Acts 15 set this as the non-negotiable minimum for Gentiles.
- **Nazarenes/Ebionites:** Craig calls the material part of "this whole Ebonite tradition" and says it was later "obscured by the various Proto Orthodox traditions." He also says it is "more Ebionite teaching" as usually classed.

- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Craig's Marcion discussion (citing BeDuhn) suggests that the shorter Marcionite Luke and Paul may predate the later canonical forms, and that the Homilies preserve earlier material that the Proto-Orthodox later reshaped.
