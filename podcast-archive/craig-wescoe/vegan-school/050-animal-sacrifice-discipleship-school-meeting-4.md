## Summary

This fourth meeting of the Vegan Christian Discipleship School asks whether God ever desired or required animal sacrifice, and if the Bible says he did, how a vegan Christian should answer. The study guide, read paragraph by paragraph by participants and narrated by Craig Wescoe, sets out two perspectives. The first is that God tolerated animal sacrifice as a temporary concession to a people already sacrificing to idols, and that Jesus ended the practice. The second, which Wescoe calls the historical-critical perspective, is that God never commanded sacrifice at all, and that "the lying pen of priest scribes" inserted those commands into the Torah later.

Wescoe's main evidence for the second view is internal to the Hebrew Bible: the prophets' statements that God did not require sacrifice (1 Samuel 15:22, Isaiah 1:11, Hosea 6:6, Psalm 40:6), Jeremiah 8:8 and 7:22-23, the "lost" law scroll found under Josiah in 2 Kings 22, Ezra's public reading in Nehemiah 8, and the apparent anachronism of Psalm 51:18-19 about rebuilt walls. He also relies on early Christian and Jewish authors (Clement, Justin, Maimonides) who describe sacrifice as pagan or accommodated. He argues that Jesus' temple action and his statement in Matthew 5:17-18 fit the second view better, since they imply the sacrificial law was never part of the true Torah.

The talk ends with Wescoe explicitly declining to settle which perspective is correct. He says either one concludes that animal sacrifice "falls short of God's ideal," and that Christians should not use sacrifice texts to justify harming animals. He personally says the first view "makes more sense to me," while acknowledging the second is a legitimate reading. The session closes with questions about the Passover, the size of the Temple, Jesus' last week, and non-canonical texts, plus a closing prayer.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The prophets reject sacrifice as God's desire
The talk opens with a block of prophetic texts that the group reads aloud: 1 Samuel 15:22 ("obedience is better than sacrifice"), Isaiah 1:11 ("I have no pleasure in the blood of bulls and lambs and goats"), Hosea 6:6 ("I desire Mercy not sacrifice"), and Psalm 40:6 ("burnt offerings and sin offerings you did not require"). Wescoe presents these as the common starting point for both perspectives, and as the basis for the claim that God "did not ever desire or require animal sacrifice."

### 2. The first perspective: a two-stage correction (accommodation, then Jesus)
Wescoe presents this as the view that God allowed sacrifice temporarily. Clement of Alexandria and Justin Martyr are read as describing sacrifice as either a human invention or a demonic practice that God accommodated. A reading from the Recognitions attributed to Clement and a Richard Schwarz quotation describe Moses permitting sacrifice "to God" only, to cut off "one half of the deeply ingrained evil." Jesus then completes the second stage. Hebrews 10:11-12 and the Melchizedek material (Hebrews, Genesis 14) support the claim that Jesus' single offering ended the levitical system. Wescoe says Hebrews is the best text to use with Christians for this view, but that Maimonides' *Guide for the Perplexed* is a better entry point for Jewish conversation partners.

### 3. The second perspective: a "lost" scroll and a "lying pen"
Wescoe argues the Torah scroll found by Hilkiah in 2 Kings 22 was a different law book from the one the priests were using. Jeremiah 8:8 ("the lying pen of the scribes has produced a deception") is read as evidence that a false Torah circulated. Jeremiah 7:22-23 is read as saying God did not command sacrifice in the wilderness. Wescoe admits that the 2 Kings text does not say the scroll lacked sacrifice; he describes this as a reconstruction ("it's like a puzzle"). He also acknowledges that, on the straightforward reading, Josiah would have stopped animal sacrifice, but that the record is "clouded."

### 4. Ezra as the editor who mixed the two Torahs
Nehemiah 8:9-10 is presented as Ezra reinstating a festival involving sacrifice and eating flesh, which the people first mourned. Wescoe proposes that Ezra combined the reformed scroll with the priestly one. He cites "Ezra 14:45" (a 40-day rewriting account) and a passage attributed to "arenus" (likely Irenaeus, per the caption) about the Scriptures being corrupted during the Babylonian exile. He notes that rabbinic tradition has a different account (translation into Aramaic) and that these accounts disagree about why Ezra wrote. He says the Genesis 1 (Elohim) and Genesis 2 (Yahweh) sources can be separated, and that he has made videos showing this.

### 5. Psalm 51 and the anachronism argument
Psalm 51:16-17 says God does not desire sacrifice, but Psalm 51:18-19 adds that God will delight in sacrifices once Jerusalem's walls are rebuilt. Wescoe argues that the walls were standing in David's time, and were down and rebuilt only in the time of Nehemiah 2:17, so the second half of Psalm 51 must come from the post-exilic period. He calls this "the more compelling piece of evidence." Wescoe also raises the Passover: Exodus 12 is read as commanding the slaughter of lambs, and Wescoe suggests the original command was the Feast of Unleavened Bread, which the "lying pen" altered.

### 6. Jesus, the temple, and Matthew 5:17-18
A reading of the temple incident (Jesus overturning tables and liberating animals, "a den of violence") is used to argue that Jesus knowingly opposed the sacrificial system. Matthew 5:17-18 ("not the smallest part of it") is then read as a problem for the claim that sacrifice was legitimately in the Torah. Wescoe says the tension is resolved if the sacrificial commands were never part of the original Torah. He also proposes a reconstruction of Jesus' last week from all four Gospels, in which the crowd that had acclaimed him enters Jerusalem and follows him into the Temple. He says this is his own view and that others might fit the evidence differently.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Misconception:** God desires or delights in animal sacrifice, so he must not care about animal suffering. **Correction:** The prophets (1 Samuel 15:22, Isaiah 1:11, Hosea 6:6, Psalm 40:6) say God does not desire or require it, and both perspectives agree on that.
- **Misconception:** Sacrifices were commanded because they prefigured Jesus, so they were required. **Correction:** Wescoe says this view is one perspective (the first), but that the second view holds that the sacrificial commands are not original; he also notes that people who read the Passover as prophecy may have taken it "literally."
- **Misconception:** The Torah we have is God's unchanged word, so every sacrifice command is authoritative. **Correction:** Wescoe argues that Ezra mixed a reformed scroll with a priestly version, and cites Jeremiah 8:8 and Nehemiah 8 as evidence.
- **Misconception:** Psalm 51 has David endorsing sacrifice. **Correction:** Wescoe argues that the second half of Psalm 51 is anachronistic because it assumes walls that were down until Nehemiah's time.
