## Summary

This is the fifth meeting of a Bible-study series run by Craig Wescoe, a vegan Christian teacher, whose channel posted the recording. The question is whether Jesus, his apostles and his family were vegan, and whether the earliest sources say they opposed killing animals. Wescoe frames the answer as a case built from "our earliest sources," read in class, not as settled fact. The group reads the Bible passages and quotations from later church writers aloud, and Wescoe explains each one.

The argument runs in two stages. First, Wescoe shows that the movement's names (Christians, Nazarenes, "followers of the Way," and Ebionites, or "the poor ones") are each linked in sources such as Theophilus of Antioch and Epiphanius to abstaining from meat and sacrifice. Second, he goes through individual figures: Matthew, Peter, Paul, Mary, John the Baptist, James, Judas Thomas, and a general statement by Eusebius that all the disciples abstained from flesh and wine. His main evidence is these quotations, along with Acts 15 and Genesis 1, which he uses as an interpretive key. He also says that Epiphanius, a fourth-century writer, is hostile to the Ebionites and so must be read with care.

The talk ends with a read-aloud conclusion that "it is hard to deny" that Jesus and his earliest followers were vegan. Group members affirm it, and Wescoe admits that the sources need weighing and that opponents will have counterarguments. He recommends the Greg Boyd material for readers who want an apologetic approach.

### Key Teachings

### 1. The movement's names point to a meat-avoiding group

Wescoe lists the titles used for Jesus's followers: Christians (Acts 11:26), Nazarenes (Acts 24:5, John 19:19), followers of the Way (Acts 24:14, John 14:6), and the poor ones (Ebionites). For the Nazarenes, Epiphanius is quoted as saying they "would not offer sacrifice or eat meat," and that "it was unlawful to eat meat or make sacrifices with it." Theophilus of Antioch, in a letter to Autolycus, is quoted saying that those who eat flesh and "injure those weaker than themselves" transgress God's law. Wescoe says Antioch was an important early center and that Theophilus was its bishop in the late second century.

### 2. The Nazarene name and the Nazarite vow

Wescoe connects "Nazarene" to the Nazarite vow in Numbers, which includes abstaining from grapes. He says a link between the terms is clear but that its exact nature is "unclear," and that he does not know whether all Nazarenes were Nazarites. He also treats the Nazarite vow as a symbol of avoiding flesh and blood.

### 3. The Way, the Didache and Acts 15

Wescoe reads the "two ways" catechism (which he calls the Didache) from Irenaeus's *Against Heresies* 3.24 (the talk's "324"). The way of life is to love God and one's neighbor, and to avoid food sacrificed to idols and "against that which is sacrificed to Idols." He compares this with the Jerusalem Council's decree in Acts 15:28-29 (abstain from things sacrificed to idols, blood, and fornication). He takes "fornication" (*porneia*) to mean defilement in a broad sense, including putting forbidden food into the body. He reads "bear what you are able" as "do the best you can" rather than as a demand for perfection.

### 4. The Ebionites, sacrifice and the Torah

Epiphanius (*Panarion* 30.18.7-9, read in class as "30187 to9") is quoted saying the Ebionites believed killing and eating animals was unlawful, rejected the Torah passages about Abraham, Noah and Moses eating meat or sacrificing, and claimed "Christ has revealed this to me." Another quotation (*Panarion* 30.16.5) has the Ebionites attributing to Jesus the words "I came to abolish the sacrifices." Wescoe argues that Epiphanius is trying to make the Ebionites heretics even though he admits they are the Jerusalem community, so his testimony is "trying to have it both ways." He says Epiphanius belongs to a "Proto-Orthodoxy" project and that his account needs to be separated from his agenda. Wescoe sums up: "if following Jesus makes someone a heretic so be it."

### 5. The Jerusalem community (the Ebionites) and James

Wescoe ties the Ebionites to the original Jerusalem community led by James, citing Luke 6:20 and Acts 2:44-45, which describe shared possessions. He argues that Paul's Galatians 2:1-10 confirms the authority of James, Peter and John as "pillars," and that Paul was asked to "remember the poor." He presents this as evidence that the Ebionites were the original Jerusalem church.

### 6. Individual figures

- **Matthew:** Clement of Alexandria (late second century) is quoted saying Matthew "partook of seeds and nuts and vegetables without flesh." Wescoe notes that the passage does not mention eggs or milk, and that he would not draw conclusions about dairy from it.
- **Peter:** Clement of Rome's *Homilies* (cited on screen as "homes 126 and 74") say Peter ate "only bread and olives and rarely pot herbs" and abstained "from the table of devils." Acts 10:14 is read as Peter refusing anything "common or unclean."
- **Mary and John the Baptist:** The *Protoevangelium of James* says Mary was fed by angels and that "nothing common or unclean" passed through her. Wescoe reports James Tabor's argument that the Greek word for locusts in Matthew 3:4 is a mistranslation of a word for honey cake, comparable to manna. Wescoe presents this as a possibility and says he is not sure it is correct.
- **James the Just and Judas Thomas:** Hegesippus, quoted by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 2.23.5-6), says James "never ate animal flesh nor did he wear wool." Wescoe argues the wool detail shows the abstention was about harming animals, not just diet. The *Acts of Thomas* says Thomas "eats only bread with salt." Wescoe notes that the name Thomas means "twin."
- **All the disciples:** Eusebius, *Demonstratio Evangelica* 3.5, is quoted saying the disciples abstained from "flesh eating" and "wine and meat." Wescoe says this is the clearest statement because it names the disciples as a group and not a sect.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Jesus and the apostles weren't vegan, so Christians needn't be."** Wescoe says the earliest sources describe the apostles and family as abstaining from flesh and opposing slaughter.
- **"Jesus's disciples ate fish and lamb, so they were not vegan."** Wescoe responds that the fishing stories and Passover lamb are not evidence against the sources, which describe the disciples as abstaining from flesh. He does not give a full answer to these specific claims.
- **"The fish symbol was about eating fish."** The group notes that the Ichthys acronym was for "Jesus Christ, Son of God, Savior" in Greek.
- **"Vegetarianism and veganism were always separate."** Wescoe says the terms split in the 1940s, and that earlier "vegetarianism" was effectively veganism.
- **"The later church has always preserved the original teaching."** Wescoe argues that later manuscripts changed Acts 15 to downplay the Golden Rule and the way of the Spirit, and that the fourth-century church recast the history.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Jesus vs. Paul:** Wescoe uses Paul's Galatians 2 as evidence that the Jerusalem leaders had authority and that Paul sought their approval. He also reads Romans 14:21 as Paul's advice on meat and wine.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Hegesippus is used to make James a strict abstainer who avoided wool. Wescoe ties the Jerusalem community to the Ebionites, and to the "poor ones" of Luke 6:20.
- **Nazarenes and Ebionites:** The talk's central claim is that the Nazarenes and Ebionites abstained from meat and sacrifice. The argument rests on Epiphanius and Theophilus, with the caveat that Epiphanius is hostile and writes in the fourth century.
- **Torah, sacrifice and diet:** Wescoe argues that the Ebionites rejected the Torah's sacrificial passages and that Jesus's "I came to abolish the sacrifices" saying (via Epiphanius) supports this. He reads Genesis 1:29 as God's ideal diet.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Wescoe argues that the canon and manuscripts were edited in the fourth century, and that a historical-critical method should weigh all the evidence rather than one manuscript.
