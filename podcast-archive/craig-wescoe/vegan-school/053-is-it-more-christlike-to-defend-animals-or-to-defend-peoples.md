## Summary

This is session seven of a "vegan Christian discipleship school" run by Craig Wescoe, part of the Swords to Plowshares series. The session takes a broader question than the usual objections about dominion, diet, or human value: if we set the two options side by side, which is more Christlike, defending animals or defending people's freedom to hurt them? The opening reading says the character of Christ is "humility love and mercy," and that he "cares for the vulnerable, speaks up for the voiceless and liberates the captives." The group then reads seven short sections, each built on a different theme: humility, the fruit of the Spirit, mercy and salvation, care for the vulnerable, a voice for the voiceless, the temple cleansing, and preaching the gospel and using freedom well. Each section pairs a scripture with a question asking the group to choose which side is more Christlike.

Wescoe's answer is that defending animals is more Christlike. His evidence is almost entirely scriptural and interpretive: passages from Psalms, Proverbs, Isaiah, Hosea, James, Luke, John and the Pauline letters are read as applying to animals, often by analogy. He gives special weight to the temple cleansing, which he reads as "an act of animal Liberation," drawing on the writers Keith Akers and Norm Phelps. He also argues that the animal-defending position is a useful route for evangelism, since many secular vegans see Christians as hypocrites. Wescoe repeatedly says that "most Christians would have to admit" the point, but he offers no survey or other evidence for that claim.

The session ends with a closing reading of Micah 6:8, a summary that the choice is "very clear," a discussion of how Christians can keep their message kind and avoid self-righteousness, and a prayer. Much of the discussion is devotional and testimonial. Wescoe does not engage historical questions about the gospels or the texts' origins; he says these belong to a separate study guide.

## Key Teachings

### The character of Christ as the test
The core argument is that the standard should be "what's more Christlike," not whether a given permission exists. Wescoe says this approach is meant to "take back" objections such as "Jesus ate fish" or "God gave us dominion" by setting them aside. Dominion is reframed as service: he says that since Jesus "came to serve," those given dominion over animals should serve them too. He reads the Good Shepherd image as meaning that the shepherd defends the sheep, and he argues that the metaphor only works if the literal care for animals is real. He also cites Genesis 1:29 (the plant diet given at creation) as part of the same picture of "very good" creation.

### The fruit of the Spirit
Galatians 5:22-23 is read as the test of Christlike conduct. Wescoe applies each fruit to animals: love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness and self-control. He argues that the Spirit cannot be "turned on" toward people and "turned off" toward animals. A participant cites Romans 2:4 (God's kindness leads to repentance), and the group treats kindness as the way to win others over.

### Mercy and salvation as a model
Psalm 68:20 and Psalm 91:3 are read as God rescuing people from a trap. Wescoe reads the trap as sin and the Fowler as the devil. Luke 6:36 ("be merciful just as your father is merciful") and 1 John 4:19 ("we love because he first loved us") are read as calling Christians to "save" animals from factory farms, hunting and slaughterhouses. He asks whether convincing someone to stop paying for animals' slaughter is more Christlike than defending someone's right to withhold mercy.

### Care for the vulnerable
James 1:27 (pure religion is caring for orphans and widows) and Proverbs 12:10 (the righteous care for their animals) are read as applying to farm animals. Wescoe argues that "it would be a far stretch" to say animals are not among the most vulnerable. He contrasts this with Jesus's warning against religious leaders who "devour widows' houses," which he reads as a picture of people who exploit animals for their own pleasure.

### Voice for the voiceless and liberation
Proverbs 31:8 ("speak up for those who cannot speak for themselves... appointed to die") and Isaiah 58:6 (loosing bonds and breaking yokes) are read as literal descriptions of animal agriculture. Wescoe describes the yellow ear tags with "kill dates" on young farm animals as evidence that animals are literally "appointed to die." He argues the Isaiah passage needs an animal reading to make sense, since the yoke and burden images come from oxen and donkeys. He contrasts the "voice for the voiceless" phrase, usually used for marginalized people who are not heard, with animals, which "have no power to really do anything."

### The temple cleansing as animal liberation
This is the longest argument. Wescoe reads John 2:15-16 (the whip, the driven-out sheep and cattle) and Luke 19:45-46 as Jesus acting against animal sacrifice. He quotes Akers that "the temple was more like a butcher shop" and Phelps's claim that the cleansing "was history's first direct action to liberate animals." He argues the money-changer reading fails because Jesus quotes prophetic passages against sacrifice, namely Hosea 6:6 (also Amos and Isaiah) and not Proverbs about dishonest scales. He also notes that Luke does not mention the money changers at this point. He presents Jesus's courage in this act as the "hill that he chose to die on," though he says the crucifixion had other causes too.

### Preaching the gospel and the use of freedom
Mark 16:15 (preach to every creature) and Romans 14:15 are used to argue that defending animal-harm hinders evangelism. Wescoe says that secular animal-rights activists see Christians who defend cruelty as hypocrites, and that a Christian standing with the activists can be heard. He describes his own outreach at a pier and in Montreal as examples. Galatians 5:13 ("do not use your freedom to indulge the flesh") and Romans 12:2 (transformed by the renewing of your mind) are read as favoring the choice that is "good" according to God. Wescoe presents the gospel argument as one of "about 10 different reasons," and says it should be sufficient even if the other reasons are rejected. He also cites Brittany Michaelson's "until that day I will continue to take action" as a model of acting now toward a future kingdom.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Jesus ate fish, so eating animals is allowed."** The session sets aside permission-based arguments and asks instead which option is more Christlike. (Wescoe presents this as a claim people make, and does not work through the fish episode in detail.)
- **"God gave us dominion, so we may hurt animals."** Dominion is reframed as Christlike service, following Jesus who "came to serve" and as the Good Shepherd, rather than domination.
- **"We are more valuable than animals, so harming them is fine."** Mentioned as an objection in the opening reading. The session says the broader question should be answered first, and does not argue the value comparison in depth.
- **"The temple cleansing was about money changers overcharging people."** Wescoe argues that Jesus quotes prophetic passages against sacrifice, not the Proverbs about dishonest scales, and that Luke does not mention the money changers in that account.
- **"Animal-defending Christians are superior to others."** A guest and Wescoe both say Christian veganism should be humble and should not look down on others.
- **"Thou shalt not kill is only in the King James version."** A member reports that this was said to them by a critic. The session does not give a direct response to that claim.

- **The Kingdom on earth:** The closing prayer and several participants link the Kingdom "on Earth as it is in heaven" to the restoration of creation, including animals, and cite Isaiah's vision of peace.
