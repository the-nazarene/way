## Summary

This is Class #8 of the Vegan Christian Discipleship School, a small group meeting run by Craig Wescoe and read through a written study guide. The session asks whether Christians should wait for Jesus to return before living by the kingdom's ideals, or prepare for the kingdom now. Wescoe states the opening frame: many Christians accept that the kingdom will have no killing and a plant-based diet, yet treat these as matters for after Christ returns, so they do not change their practice now. The group's answer, which the study guide sets out and the participants largely affirm, is that Jesus taught readiness in the present, and that readiness means embodying Christ's character (compassion, kindness, humility, gentleness, patience, self-control) in everything, including how people treat animals, what they eat, and how they steward creation.

The main evidence is drawn from Gospel parables (the wise and foolish virgins in Matthew 25, the faithful manager in Luke 12, the house built on the rock), Genesis 1:28-29 on human dominion and the plant diet, Colossians 3:12 and Revelation 19:6-8 on the "wedding garment," Romans 12:2 and 1 Peter 1:13 on readiness, and Isaiah 11:9 on the peaceable kingdom. The group also argues that animals are part of the promised kingdom, citing Isaiah, Ecclesiastes, Hosea and Genesis 9. The session ends with the group discussing outreach, the limits of what anyone can do, and the importance of faith expressed in action. Wescoe closes with feedback requests, announcements about the next semester, and a prayer. Much of the scriptural reading is taken verbatim from the study guide and read aloud by participants, so attribution of those claims belongs to the guide's authors rather than to the spoken discussion.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Seek the kingdom now, not only after Christ returns
The group reads from the guide that Jesus taught the kingdom should be prioritized above everything, citing Matthew 6:33 ("seek first the kingdom of God") and Jeremiah 29:13 ("you will seek me and find me when you search for me with all your heart"). Wescoe's argument is that Jeremiah's promise is conditional on present seeking, so "he doesn't say wait for me and then start seeking." The Lord's Prayer ("your kingdom come, your will be done on Earth as it is in heaven," Matthew 6:10) is read as a present petition: the speaker says it should be lived out now, not deferred.

### 2. Readiness as faithful stewardship (the parables)
The group reads Matthew 24:44 and the parable of the ten virgins (Matthew 25:1-4), with the oil in the lamps glossed, in a participant's words, as preparedness; the common reading of oil as the Holy Spirit is mentioned as a widespread interpretation the speaker holds loosely. The Luke 12:42-43 "faithful and wise manager" is tied to Genesis 1:28-29: humans were given dominion and charged to provide "their food at the proper time," so stewardship means caretaking, "not chop them up and kill them and eat them." Wescoe links the Genesis 1 food instructions directly to the manager image, arguing God gave the diet so humans could do their job. The Sermon on the Mount closing (the house on the rock) is read as "hears these things and puts them into practice" being the wise course.

### 3. The wedding garment is Christlike character, applied to animals too
Revelation 19:6-8 describes the bride's fine linen as "the righteous acts of the Saints." Galatians 3:26-27 is used to argue that those baptized into Christ are "clothed" with him, and Colossians 3:12 lists the virtues to be put on. Wescoe and a participant insist the virtues apply to animals as much as to people ("it's not like we take off the clothes when it comes to how we treat animals"). The group says the righteousness is Christ's, not the believer's own effort, and that the Holy Spirit within produces the fruit of Galatians 5:22-23, including self-control, which they say is needed to resist fleshly desires.

### 4. Animals are part of the future kingdom
Responding to the objection that animals are not in heaven, the group argues from Isaiah 11:9 (no harming or destroying on God's holy mountain) and the parallel description of the ox and lion, which Wescoe says cannot be merely symbolic. He also cites Ecclesiastes (humans and animals share the same breath and fate), Hosea 2:18, and the covenant with animals in Genesis 9, which he calls "the Everlasting Covenant." The inference drawn is that if God's promises include animals, the practical implication is present concern for them. A participant also states that the Bible's "ways of peace" (Proverbs) and God's hatred of violence should shape treatment of animals now.

### 5. Diet, violence and the flesh
A participant paraphrases Paul's statement that alcohol and animal flesh can make a person "beat on my brother," and links this to the Greek term for beating used of waves on a house (in the Matthew 7 parable). The argument is that eating flesh both kills animals violently and inclines the eater toward violence. The speaker adds that some people accept the first point less readily but may accept the second. The Galatians 5 "self-control" teaching is then offered as the means of resisting this. The Paul passage is not given a verse reference in the discussion.

### 6. Living faith requires action
Drawing on James 2:17 ("faith by itself if it is not accompanied by action is dead") and 1 Peter 1:13, the group argues that belief without changed practice is dead faith. A participant says Christians are "not told to have dead Faith we're told to have Living Faith." Romans 12:2 (renewed mind, "prove what is good and acceptable and perfect will of God") is taken as the test: the transformed community demonstrates God's will to the world. This is linked to Mark 16:15 (preach "to every creature") and Romans 8:22 (creation groaning), with a participant adding that believers can "speed his coming" by godly living, a claim attributed to 2 Peter in the discussion.

### 7. The peaceable table: Andrew Lindsay and John Chrysostom
The study guide quotes Andrew Lindsay: "human beings can now approximate the Peaceable Kingdom by living without killing the sentient for food." It also quotes a fourth-century homily by John Chrysostom, Archbishop of Constantinople, describing a table with no "streams of blood," no "unpleasing smells of meat," and "no fear" (the table being "an angel table free from all such turmoil"). Wescoe calls Chrysostom "very wise" and the group agrees the image fits. The Galapagos Islands are offered as an example of animals losing fear when people stop killing them, though no source is given for this claim.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Animals have no place in heaven or the afterlife, so what humans do to animals is irrelevant.** The group answers from Isaiah 11, Ecclesiastes, Hosea 2:18 and Genesis 9, arguing the Bible includes animals in the promised future.
- **Isaiah's peaceable animals are only symbolic.** Wescoe rejects the "analogy" reading and says it is "not talking about real animals" is not a warrant to set the passage aside (argued, not fully developed in the talk).
- **Believers must simply wait for Christ to fix everything, so present practice does not matter.** The session argues that Jesus taught present readiness, citing the parables and James 2:17.
- **Belief alone is enough; action is optional.** The group says faith without action is dead, citing James 2:17 and Romans 12:2.
- **Veganism is the answer to the world's problems.** A participant corrects this: veganism is part of the answer, but Christ and his kingdom are the answer.
