## Key Teachings

### 1. The same word, *nephesh*, describes humans and animals
Craig's first argument is lexical. Genesis 2:7 calls Adam a "living soul" (*nephesh*), and Genesis 1 uses the same word for living creatures, including the animals. He cites 1 Corinthians 15:45 as Paul's use of Genesis 2:7, which he says translates the Hebrew *nephesh* with the Greek *psyche*, and he links Deuteronomy 6:5 to Matthew 22:37 as further evidence that *nephesh* and *psyche* refer to the same thing. On this reading the soul is the living being, and both humans and animals are living souls. Craig says *ruach* (breath or spirit) is sometimes distinct from *nephesh*, but is usually translated "Spirit," and he treats the breath of God as what animates both humans and animals.

### 2. Translations have imposed a human/animal distinction
Craig cites Theodore Hiebert, who writes that translators have drawn a distinction between humans and animals "when none exists in the Hebrew text," and Matthew King, who makes a similar observation. Hiebert's example is that *nephesh* is rendered "living soul" or "person" for humans but "creature" for animals, and Craig notes that the New Living Translation does not use "person" for animals in Genesis even though it does for humans. Craig attributes this to human-centered thinking ("anthropocentrism," which he also calls speciesism) and says the translators "wanted to disregard animals." Participants raise Danish translations, and Craig adds that copyright constraints and translators' biases can both shape modern versions. He recommends comparing several translations side by side (NKJV, Berean, JPS, ESV, RSV, NASB) and names the Dead Sea Scrolls as evidence that has helped some translations.

### 3. Shared fate and the Kingdom
Ecclesiastes 3:19 says humans and animals share one breath and one end, so "humans have no advantage over animals." Craig reads this as implying a shared fate. He then reads Isaiah 11 (the lion eating straw, a child leading animals) as a picture of the Kingdom in which animals are included, and he argues that the Kingdom restores the plant-based peace of Eden. In a response to a participant, he cites Revelation 5:13 (animals praising God) and Colossians 1:20 ("all things" reconciled) as evidence that animals are in the Kingdom. He says the Bible's silence on whether animals go to heaven does not support excluding them, and that the earth's coming restoration includes them. Participants also reference Ecclesiastes 12:7 (the breath returns to God) to argue that the same breath and return apply to both.

### 4. Covenant and hope for all creation
Craig reads Genesis 9:9-10, in which God establishes a covenant with every living creature, and calls it "Everlasting." He reads Romans 8:19 (creation waits eagerly for the revealing of the children of God) and Mark 16:15 (preach the gospel "to every creature") as including animals in the hope of the gospel. He draws the practical conclusion that the gospel should be expressed through "acts of loving service and by doing no harm." He also cites Psalm 36:6 in the closing reading and Proverbs 12:10 for "a righteous man regards the life" of his animal, which he uses to argue against factory farming, laboratories and captivity for entertainment.

### 5. Animal sacrifice: two readings
Craig gives two readings he considers the best supported. The first is that sacrifice was a temporary, permissive "imperfect will" of God, set against the Eden diet of Genesis 1:29 and the craving episode in Numbers 11. The second, which he says is better supported "by the evidence both historical and biblical," is drawn from Jeremiah 7 and 8. Jeremiah says the "lying scribes" falsified the Torah, and Craig reads the example of Jeremiah 7 ("God never commanded animal sacrifices") as showing that the temple priesthood added sacrificial law. He ties this to the prophets' statements that God desires mercy and not sacrifice, and to Jesus overturning the temple tables. For Christians, a participant's question prompts Craig to repeat the reading that Hebrews (chapters 8 through 11, per Craig) presents Jesus as high priest and once-for-all sacrifice, so animal sacrifice is no longer needed. Craig says either reading leads to the conclusion that God does not want the sacrifices.

### 6. Diet and stewardship now
Craig argues that the plant-based diet in Genesis 1:29 is the original design. He presents Christians' inconsistency (opposing sacrifice yet eating animals) as a problem, and he uses the parable of the faithful and wise steward to argue that Christians should live now as Jesus commanded, "already doing the thing" when he returns. He cites Matthew 6-style "Kingdom now" language in the prayer "your will be done on earth as it is in heaven." Craig makes a related argument about plants: he says a diet of fallen fruit and trimmed herbs does not harm plants, and that the argument about plant suffering is an excuse. Participants also discuss Catholic Friday abstinence, and Craig says Lent and the "Daniel diet" (Daniel 1) can be an opening for conversation.

### 7. Why humans are "more valuable" than sparrows and sheep
Craig takes up Jesus's sayings about birds (the "do not worry" passage) and the sheep (rescuing one on the Sabbath). He argues that "more valuable" implies that animals have value to God, since nothing is compared to something worthless. He uses an analogy: on an airplane the pilot is the most valuable person because the flight depends on him, but on a ship the captain has that role. Humans have a stewardship role under God's dominion, and the role makes humans more important in that respect, though God cares for all creation.

### 8. Image of God as responsibility
Craig argues that the "image and likeness" of God in Genesis is about role, not superiority. The uniquely human responsibility is to care for animals, so humans fail the image when they do not. He describes dominion as a responsibility to do God's will, not license to act arbitrarily, and he rejects the idea that eating animals raises their soul. He says there is no evidence in the Old or New Testament for that idea, and that Jesus did not come to eat people in order to elevate them.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Animals have no soul.** Craig argues the Bible uses *nephesh* for both humans and animals, so animals are living souls (Genesis 1-2, Deuteronomy 6:5, 1 Corinthians 15:45).
- **Humans have a "rational" soul and animals a "vegetative" one (Aristotelian/Catholic framing).** Craig says no such distinction exists in the Hebrew or Greek and that it is imposed by philosophy and tradition.
- **Humans have a spirit and animals only a soul.** A participant reports this view; Craig answers that *ruach* (breath) is breathed into both humans and animals.
- **Animals are not in God's image, so they do not matter.** Craig reframes "image and likeness" as a role of stewardship, which he says makes human care for animals a responsibility.
- **Animals do not go to heaven.** A participant reports a pastor saying so; Craig cites Isaiah 11, Revelation 5:13 and Colossians 1:20 for their inclusion.
- **Jesus taught that animals have no value, since humans are "more valuable."** Craig argues the comparison implies animals have value to God, and that "more valuable" refers to role, as with the pilot analogy.
- **God commanded animal sacrifice.** Craig presents two readings, both ending with the view that God does not desire sacrifice (a permissive will, or priestly addition as in Jeremiah 7).
- **Animal skins in Genesis 3 show that God killed animals.** Craig says the text does not say this and questions the assumption.
- **Plants are harmed by a plant-based diet as much as animals are harmed by meat eating.** Craig argues this is an excuse, given the plant losses in feeding livestock. (A participant's claim, which Craig endorses.)

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Craig cites Paul (1 Corinthians 15:45, Romans 8:19, Romans 12:18) as an authority for the soul and creation arguments, and does not contrast Paul with Jesus. He does, however, say Christians should follow Jesus "every day" and not on a single day.
- **Torah and the scribes:** Craig's Jeremiah 7-8 argument that the lying scribes falsified the Torah with sacrificial law is directly relevant to the repo's Torah questions, and he ties it to Jesus overturning the temple tables.
- **Sacrifice:** Craig gives two readings (imperfect will vs. priestly addition) and uses Hebrews 8-11 to argue Jesus ended sacrifice. This is a central point of the session.
- **Diet:** Craig argues the Genesis 1:29 plant diet is God's perfect will and should be followed now, linking it to the Kingdom and to the parable of the faithful steward.
- **Nonviolence:** Craig's appeal to Romans 12:18, Proverbs 12:10 and the temple action (liberating animals) is an extended argument about violence toward animals.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Isaiah 11 and the Lord's Prayer ("your will be done on earth") are used to argue that Kingdom life, including care for animals, should be practiced now.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** Craig argues animals share the same fate as humans (Ecclesiastes 3:19), and that they are in the Kingdom (Isaiah 11, Revelation 5:13). He does not discuss hell.
- **Gospels and canon:** Craig says he focuses on "historically verifiable" texts and excludes the Essene Gospel of Peace and the Gospel of the Holy 12, which he judges not historical. He also notes that translation choices and copyright constraints shape modern Bibles.
- **Essenes:** Craig asserts that the Essenes "were all vegan" and that they were a real group, but gives no sources in this session.
