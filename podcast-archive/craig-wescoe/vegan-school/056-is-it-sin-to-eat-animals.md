## Summary

The talk asks whether eating animals is sin, and it is the second session of the second semester of the Vegan Christian Discipleship School. The presenter, Craig Wescoe, walks a small group through a nine-page study guide, which he says sets out a short answer and then elaborates on it. The guide's thesis is that the early church taught that eating animals was sin, that many biblical passages label it sin, and that other biblical principles (acting against known good, love and mercy, and "sinning against Christ") point the same way. Participants read the guide aloud section by section and ask questions along the way.

Wescoe's main evidence falls into three groups. The first is an early-church witness: a passage from Theophilus's letter *To Autolycus*, which the talk places in the 170s, where Theophilus contrasts those who "keep the law of God and eat of the seeds of the earth" with those who "eat flesh." The second is scriptural: Genesis 9:4 and Leviticus 3:17 (which the talk reads as a "lasting ordinance"), together with Genesis 1:29 and 1:31 (the "very good" fruit-and-vegetation diet), James 4:17, Proverbs 12:10, Luke 6:36, and 1 Corinthians 8:12-13. The third is an argument about Jesus. Wescoe grants the common premise that Luke 24:42-43 shows Jesus eating fish, but he argues that the passage's textual and source-critical problems make it weaker evidence than the broad case against eating animals, and that Jesus was sinless according to Hebrews, 1 John, 1 Peter and Paul.

The session closes with a summary of the reasons, a note that even someone unconvinced that it is sin should still abstain because it is "contrary to his design," a discussion of how to approach family and church members who eat meat, and a closing prayer. Several participants share personal experiences of family conflict, grief and burnout, and the group discusses the pastoral question of mercy toward people who are ignorant or addicted.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The early church taught that eating animals was sin (Theophilus)
The guide presents Theophilus, whom it describes as Archbishop of Antioch around 170 CE, as a witness that "the church in the 2nd century held this view." The reading quotes *To Autolycus*: those who "keep the law of God and eat of the seeds of the earth" are contrasted with those who "transgress the law of God God and eat flesh and injure those weaker than themselves." Wescoe reasons that since sin is transgression of the law (1 John 3:4), and eating flesh is presented as transgression, eating flesh counts as sin. He notes that Theophilus is mentioned twice in the Bible (Luke and Acts are dedicated to him), and that what is known of him comes from extra-biblical texts and records of bishops.

### 2. Scripture explicitly labels eating animals as sin
The study guide lists clean and unclean animals (unclean land, sea and air creatures, and carrion), along with animals that were strangled, cooked in their mother's milk, or offered to idols. It then cites Genesis 9:4 ("you shall not eat flesh with its life, that is its blood") and Leviticus 3:17 ("a lasting ordinance for the generations to come wherever you live") as blanket prohibitions on flesh, fat and blood. Wescoe argues that because the ordinance is described as lasting and universal, no clean-animal exception can fully satisfy it, and so the only fully compliant diet is the one of Genesis 1:29.

### 3. Acting against known good is sin (Genesis 1:29 with James 4:17)
Here the guide combines two verses. Genesis 1:29 gives the fruit-and-vegetation diet and Genesis 1:31 calls it "very good." James 4:17 says that whoever knows the good and does not do it commits sin. The argument is that someone who has read Genesis 1 and still eats animals is knowingly acting against what God calls good. Wescoe answers a participant who asks about believers who have not yet been convicted by saying that anyone reading the guide no longer has the excuse of ignorance, and he cites Jesus's teaching about servants who know the master's will (the talk does not give a reference). He adds that the aim should be "what is best," not merely what is permissible.

### 4. Acting contrary to love and mercy (1 Corinthians 16:14, Proverbs 12:10, Luke 6:36)
The guide argues that "do everything in love" (1 Corinthians 16:14) covers how animals are treated, not only people. It reads Proverbs 12:10 ("the kindest acts of the wicked are cruel") to mean that even a meal given to a hungry person is cruel if it is made from an animal. Wescoe offers the example of feeding a hungry person a vegan meal instead. He reads Luke 6:36 ("be merciful just as your father is merciful") together with the Good Samaritan parable and Psalm-based claims about God's compassion over all creation to argue that love of neighbor extends to animals. He allows one exception: a true life-and-death survival situation, such as a plane crash in the Andes, not a restaurant with no vegan option.

### 5. Sinning against Christ (1 Corinthians 8:12-13)
This section argues that eating animals wounds the consciences of those who are distressed by animal slaughter, and that doing so "sins against Christ" (1 Corinthians 8:12-13). It adds that eating animals hinders evangelism because it sends the message that "the God we preach" is cruel. Participants share examples of church potlucks and family hunting talk that cause them pain, and Wescoe responds that Paul's point is about the offense itself, not about whether one eats in private. He also notes that Nimrod and Esau are hunters portrayed negatively in Genesis (the talk gives no verse).

### 6. Jesus did not eat animals (Luke 24:42-43, sinlessness)
This is the longest section. The guide states the opposing argument: eating animals is sin, Jesus ate animals (Luke 24:42-43, "a piece of broiled fish and some honeycomb"), so Jesus was a sinner, or else eating animals is not sin. Wescoe rejects Jesus as a sinner, but on the opposite inference: since eating animals is sin, and Jesus was sinless (Hebrews 4:15, 1 John 3:5, 1 Peter 2:22, and Paul), Jesus did not eat animals. He then questions Luke 24. He argues that it is unclear whether Jesus ate the fish or the honeycomb; that manuscripts differ (he says the NKJV reads "fish and honeycomb" while other manuscript families read only "fish"); that the translated word for "fish" is uncertain; and that Luke, by the author's own account in Luke 1:1-4, was writing more than a century later from accounts handed down to him. A participant, Katie, reads the study guide's claim that the passage may be a "late second century addition" or misinterpreted, and Wescoe responds on manuscript variation and scribal glosses. He notes that the adulteress passage (John) is widely regarded by scholars, including conservative ones as a participant reports, as a later addition, which some participants treat as an opening for source-critical questions about the wider Bible.

### 7. Objections and pastoral application
The talk addresses several objections. (a) The post-flood permission in Genesis 9 is handled in earlier school sessions, which the speaker says are linked to the guide. (b) "It is not a salvation issue" is answered by saying that God's will matters apart from salvation, using the potter and clay image. (c) Whether one should be merciful to believers who are ignorant or addicted: Wescoe says that firmness and patience must go together, and he cites Jesus's words on the cross and "sin no more" (John 8, not given verse) and God being "long-suffering." (d) Burnout and "compassion fatigue" are discussed as serious dangers to the advocate. Wescoe concludes that even someone unconvinced that eating animals is sin should abstain, because it is contrary to God's design.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Eating animals is not sin, so it is not a topic Christians need to care about."** Wescoe says this view requires ignoring or discarding much scriptural and early-church evidence.
- **"God gave permission to eat animals after the flood."** The talk says this is the usual objection, and that earlier school sessions and live talks addressed it (the talk does not summarize that argument here).
- **"Jesus ate fish, so it cannot be sin."** Wescoe argues the passage is textually and source-critically uncertain and that Jesus was sinless, so the inference should be the reverse.
- **"Eating animals is not a salvation issue, so it does not matter."** Wescoe responds that God's will matters regardless of salvation, and that the attitude "what's in it for me" is itself wrong.
- **"Ignorance excuses believers who have not been convicted."** Wescoe says the guide now removes that excuse for readers, and that ignorance is not a fully valid excuse for going against God's instructions.
- **"Eating animals in a plain, non-survival setting is necessary."** Wescoe says that apart from a true life-and-death situation (such as a plane crash in the Andes), the exception does not apply.
