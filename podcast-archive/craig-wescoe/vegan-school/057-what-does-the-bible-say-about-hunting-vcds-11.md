## Summary

This is a recorded session of the Vegan Christian Discipleship School, a monthly group study led by Craig Wescoe, who works through a study guide section by section while participants read paragraphs aloud and ask questions. The question is "What does the Bible say about hunting?" Wescoe's answer, as he presents it, is that hunting is contrary to God's design. He argues that God made humans to be gardeners, citing Genesis 1:29-31 and 2:15 and the peaceable future of Isaiah 11:6. He also argues that the two hunters named in scripture, Nimrod and Esau, are portrayed negatively, and that the Levitical blood restrictions cannot be fully kept while eating meat. He consistently states that his approach is to "strive for what's best first." ## Key Teachings

### Creation design: gardeners, not hunters
Wescoe's opening argument, read from the study guide, is that God's design for humanity was a plant-based diet. He cites Genesis 2:15 (the man placed in the garden "to work it and take care of it"), Genesis 1:29 (every herb yielding seed and every tree whose fruit yields seed "shall be for food"), and Genesis 1:31 ("very good"). He reads Isaiah 11:6 as the restored creation in which predators and prey live together, and concludes that "there is no place for hunting anywhere in God's very good design." The participants extend this to the idea that harming animals for pleasure is "directly contrary" to the peaceable order described in Isaiah.

### The two named hunters: Nimrod and Esau
The study guide argues that the only two hunters named in scripture are negative figures. For Nimrod it cites Genesis 10:8, "a giant a hunter before the Lord," and the note that "the beginning of his kingdom was Babylon," which Wescoe says represents opposition to God throughout scripture. For Esau it cites Genesis 25 (Esau as "a skillful Hunter"), contrasted with the peaceable Jacob, and Malachi 1:2-3 ("Jacob I have loved but Esau I have hated"). The study guide supports this with two quotations: Matthew Prebble's statement that Nimrod and Esau are "enemies of God's people who are remembered for destroying life," and the Encyclopaedia Judaica's description of them as regarded "in a derogatory light." Wescoe adds that Nimrod's association with Babylon leads some to call him an "Antichrist" type; he takes this as a reason to avoid following his example. The argument is that the only named hunters are enemies of God, so imitating them is unwise. The talk does not give the wider context of the Malachi passage.

### Isaac's preference for Esau (Genesis 27:2-4)
The main objection the study addresses is that Isaac, described as a man of faith, asked Esau to hunt game for him (Genesis 27:2-4). The study guide answers that many blessed figures in scripture are flawed (Moses and Aaron at the Rock, David and Bathsheba, Peter's denial, Abraham's laughter), so Isaac's request does not prove God's approval. The phrase "the kind of tasty food I like" is read as evidence that Isaac was serving the desires of the flesh. Wescoe says Isaac "seems to have something backwards" in his evaluation, since God favored Jacob. A participant suggests the phrase functions as an "idol" in the sense of putting a desire above God's priorities, and Wescoe agrees.

### The Levitical blood restriction and the "impossibility" argument
The study guide cites Leviticus 17:13-14, which requires a hunter to drain the blood and cover it, and forbids eating blood (linked to Genesis 9:4 and Leviticus 7:26-27). The objection is that God permitted hunting under these conditions. Wescoe's reply is that the blood command cannot be fully kept while eating flesh, because "it's impossible to completely drain all the blood out of an animal." He credits Jeffrey Rud (*Why Kill for Food?*, which he dates to 1956) for the point that even kosher slaughter cannot drain all the blood. The only full way to obey the restriction, he argues, is to eat no flesh, which returns to the Genesis 1:29 diet. He calls this one argument among "dozens" and says it should not be the whole case. A participant raises the concern that this argument could seem condescending to Jews, and Wescoe responds that the argument should be presented with humility; he also points to a vegan Orthodox rabbi as a better guide for that audience.

### Sport hunting and the character of God
The study guide argues that hunting for sport or trophy hunting contradicts God's character as described in Psalm 145:8 ("gracious and compassionate, slow to anger and rich in love") and Luke 6:36 ("be merciful just as your father is merciful"). Wescoe cites Theologian Andrew Lindsay's statement that the "sheer wanness" of unnecessary hunting is "nothing less than an offense to God." He also reports, through footnote 1 drawn from Ingrid Newkirk's material, that the claim that hunting controls overpopulation is misleading because the animals are bred for hunting. Participants add examples of intentional feeding and breeding for shooting, and a report of dogs used on trapped foxes in Ontario. Wescoe calls this kind of hunting "propaganda"-based and says it is unnecessary, so it does not reflect God's merciful character.

### Necessity, alternatives, and the "high bar"
For those who say they must hunt to survive (his example is the Arctic), Wescoe says he takes the strongest form of the argument seriously and addresses it directly. He advises that people in such places should transform the land, learn irrigation and cultivation, or relocate, and he sees a true emergency as the only case that might be "somewhat justifiable... only temporarily." He cites the Romans 12:18 phrase about living peaceably "with all," reading the Greek word for "all" as not specifically "people." He also describes a vegan who lived in a northern Canadian community and bought groceries, and he endorses support for groups such as A Well-Fed World. On strategy, he states that he "always focus[es] on what is best what is the ideal" and does not aim for half-measures, although he accepts that individuals may be approached differently; he suggests starting with God's character rather than the Nimrod and Esau material.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Isaac's request proves God approves of hunting.** The study guide says Isaac was a flawed man of faith, and his "tasty food" request reflects the desires of the flesh, not God's approval.
- **Leviticus permits hunting if the blood is drained.** Wescoe says full drainage is impossible, so the only way to keep the blood command completely is to eat no flesh.
- **Hunting is needed to control overpopulation.** Wescoe, drawing on the footnote, argues that game is bred to be hunted, so the overpopulation claim is misleading.
- **Hunting is necessary wherever food is hard to grow.** Wescoe says that in such places people should relocate, change the land, or buy food; he accepts only a temporary and desperate exception.
- **The permissive will after Noah justifies hunting.** Wescoe responds that what God permits is not automatically what God wants, and the good will is the better guide.
