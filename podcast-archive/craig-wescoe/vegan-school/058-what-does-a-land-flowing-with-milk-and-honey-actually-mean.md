## Summary

This is a Zoom meeting of the Vegan Christian Discipleship School (Meeting #12) in which Craig Wescoe leads a study of whether the phrase "a land flowing with milk and honey" supports eating animal products. The question is a common objection to veganism: that God's promised land is described in terms of dairy and honey, so God must intend humans to eat them. The meeting reads through a seven-page study guide paragraph by paragraph, with participants reading aloud and Craig adding commentary.

Craig's answer is that the phrase describes abundance and fertility, not a menu. He argues that the literal reading breaks down: when Caleb and Joshua brought back a report of the land, they carried grapes, pomegranates and figs, not milk and honey, and literal milk would spoil and literal rivers of honey would be sticky and foul. He reasons that milk and honey are signs of flourishing mammals, bees, plants and water, and that this is what the phrase signals. He then connects the land to the Heavenly Kingdom through the name Joshua (Yeshua), who leads Israel where Moses could not, and reads the Torah's sacrificial and ritual laws as pointing beyond themselves to Jesus. He also argues that the honey and milk belong to bees and to calves or infants, so taking them is covetousness under Exodus 20:17. Finally, he offers two alternative readings from the study guide: that "honey" (dvash) may mean date syrup, supported by a quotation from Jod Jarvis, and that the Hebrew "milk" (halab) in Genesis 4:4 may be a homograph of "fat" or "richness."

The session ends with a recap, a closing prayer, and an announcement of the next meeting. Craig presents these as his own interpretations and as the study guide's arguments, and the participants largely accept them. The talk does not evaluate the historical or textual evidence beyond these claims.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The literal reading defeats itself
The study guide reasons that if the land were literally flowing with milk and honey, the Israelite spies would have brought back milk and honey to prove it. Instead, Numbers 13 says they brought back a huge cluster of grapes carried on a pole, plus pomegranates and figs. The meeting adds that milk would spoil within days and rivers of honey would make everything sticky, so the literal picture is "the land of rancid stench and perpetual stickiness," which does not sound like the glory of any land.

### 2. Milk and honey as signs of abundance
The study guide, read aloud at [12:38], links the products to what must be living on the land. Milk indicates flourishing mammals and the grass and herbs they eat. Honey indicates bees, which pollinate flowering plants and fruit. Those in turn indicate water and an environment fit for life, in contrast to a cursed wasteland. Craig and participants walk through this chain (bees pollinate, plants fruit, mammals eat and reproduce, mothers produce milk), and Craig suggests that the phrase is about the thriving of the whole environment, so it "doesn't mean that we're consuming the honey."

### 3. Joshua/Yeshua, the Heavenly Kingdom and the limits of the Law
The study guide argues the land symbolically points to the Heavenly Kingdom. Moses leads Israel toward the land but cannot enter, and Joshua, whose name in Hebrew is nearly identical to Jesus's (Yeshua), leads them in. The guide reads this as the Levitical law being insufficient to bring people into the kingdom, which requires following Jesus. Craig adds that a Jewish prayer names Yeshua for salvation and says the "kingdom" is ambiguous (within you, in your midst, outside you), but that the general idea is God restoring the world to its original goodness, "no cruelty no violence and no covetousness." When asked about supersessionism, Craig says people who hold that view use this example, but the reading can also be aligned with restoring an earlier Torah tradition (the "elois" and "yahs" traditions, which appear to be the Elohist and Yahwist sources, though the captions garble them).

### 4. Milk and honey are not ours: covetousness and stewardship
The study guide argues that honey is made by bees for their own survival over winter (Nicole, a participant, adds detail about bees and stingers) and milk is made for a mother's offspring. Taking them is therefore "desiring something that's not intended for you," which the group connects to the tenth commandment (Exodus 20:17). Craig argues that animals and land belong to God (citing Psalms), so the milk farmer cannot claim ownership, and that humans are stewards (Genesis 2:15), not owners. Isaiah 11:9 ("they will neither harm nor destroy") is cited for the Heavenly Kingdom. The group also draws the analogy to spouses, children and companion animals.

### 5. Honey as date syrup (dvash)
The study guide offers an alternative: dvash is often translated "honey," but it can mean a sweet syrup made by boiling dates. The guide quotes Jewish author and philanthropist Jod Jarvis on the "seven species" of Israel (Deuteronomy 8), including date honey, which are eaten at Tu BiShvat. Participants note that some non-vegan Jews hold this view too. Craig says the reading fits the spies bringing back fruit, and that the honey would be made from fruit "particularly the dates."

### 6. Halab/helev in Genesis 4:4 (Josephus)
The guide cites Flavius Josephus's commentary on Genesis 4:4, where Abel brings "milk" from the firstborn of his flock. The Hebrew word for milk (halab) and for fat (helev) are identical consonantally, and vowel points were added to the Hebrew text hundreds of years after Josephus. The guide argues the original word may have meant "fat" in the sense of richness or abundance (translated "richness" or "abundance" in several versions, including NIV, NLT and the Amplified Bible). Craig acknowledges this reading takes "the biggest leap of goodwill." He notes that a live talk he gave earlier covers Cain and Abel in more detail.

### 7. Side point: "meat" and "flesh" and plant milk
Participants discuss how the King James Bible uses "meat" for any food (Genesis 1:29 "for meat"), while Hebrew uses different words, and how "flesh" (basar) covers both living and dead animal bodies. They also discuss whether plant milks or coconut milk could count as "milk," with Craig noting that the Jewish tradition preserves date syrup but not oat milk, so he would not claim evidence for plant milk.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The phrase means God wants us to drink milk and eat honey.** The talk's answer is that the phrase describes abundance and a thriving environment, and the spies' own evidence (fruit, not dairy) shows the literal reading is a mistake.
- **"Honey" in the Bible always means bee honey.** The talk argues dvash may mean date syrup, and the speaker says the bee-honey assumption is "a contested point," not settled fact.
- **"Meat" in the King James always means flesh.** The group notes that older English "meat" meant food in general, while Hebrew uses different words for food and flesh.
