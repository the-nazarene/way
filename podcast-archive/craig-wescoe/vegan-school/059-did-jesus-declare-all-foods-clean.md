## Summary

The talk is a study session from the Vegan Christian Discipleship School, led by Craig Wescoe, on whether Jesus "declared all foods clean" in the parallel passages Mark 7:14-23 and Matthew 15:10-20. The group works through a printed study guide, with participants reading sections aloud and Craig adding commentary.

Craig argues that the passage is about handwashing, not food. Mark 7:1-3 and Matthew 15 concern disciples eating with unwashed hands, a practice he says was a human tradition of the elders with no divine instruction behind it. He reads the list of things that "defile a man" (Matthew 15:19-20: evil thoughts, murders, adulteries, thefts, false witness, blasphemies) as the real subject, and argues that killing animals and taking calves' milk fall under murder and theft. He also argues that the Torah's clean/unclean rules cannot be fully kept while eating flesh, since Genesis 9:4 forbids eating blood. He cites Acts 15:29, Revelation 2:20 and Peter's vision in Acts 10 as evidence that food prohibitions continued after Jesus, and says the NIV's "thus all foods are clean" in Mark 7:19 is a later marginal gloss that became part of the text. He reads the King James rendering as describing digestion and elimination.

The session ends with a closing read-aloud of the study guide's conclusion, a reflection on hypocrisy and the "Judas goat" image, and a group prayer. Craig notes that the study takes for granted that the Yahweh passages reflect what God said, bracketing the documentary-hypothesis view he says he holds.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The context is handwashing, not food
Craig's reading of Mark 7:1-3 and the parallel in Matthew 15 is that the dispute began with Pharisees and scribes from Jerusalem objecting to disciples eating with "defiled, that is unwashed" hands. He calls the handwashing a tradition of the elders with no divine command. On his reading, Jesus's reply (Mark 7:15, Matthew 15:11) addresses that practice. He cites Luke 6:45 ("out of the abundance of the heart his mouth speaks") to tie the heart and mouth together. He stresses that the passage never mentions which foods are permitted.

### 2. Clean and unclean cannot be kept while eating flesh
Working from Leviticus 11, Craig says the clean/unclean distinction covers which animals may be eaten, but Leviticus and other texts add more rules: no blood (Genesis 9:4), no food offered to idols, no strangled animals, no fat. Because flesh and blood are inseparable, draining blood never fully satisfies the rule, so the only way to keep every command is the original plant diet of Genesis 1:29. He admits the group has covered this before and that the Leviticus passage is confusing, and says the confusion is "frustrating."

### 3. The defilement list applies to animal use
Craig reads Matthew 15:19 as a list Jesus gives of what does defile. He argues that three items on it describe animal exploitation: murder (killing animals for their bodies), theft (taking a calf away from its mother to get the milk), and, by implication, false witness (the justifying words). The group discusses this as the "turning of the tables" in his opening summary. He suggests that "what comes out of your mouth" is the justification for violence, not the food itself.

### 4. Food prohibitions continued in the New Testament
Craig points to Acts 15:29 (abstain from blood, strangled meat, idol food, sexual immorality) and Revelation 2:20 (the teaching of "Jezebel" about idol food) as evidence that Jesus did not abolish food rules. He notes that the Greek *porneia* is usually rendered "sexual immorality" but that he thinks "defilement" is the better translation. He says the earliest witness to Acts 15 is Irenaeus, whose quotation ends with "by doing so you are walking by the Holy Spirit." On his reading, the council's prohibitions are about the Holy Spirit, loving one's neighbor, and avoiding "the table of demons," and he rejects the view that they are simply Noahide laws.

### 5. Acts 10 is about the Gentiles, not food
Peter's vision of the sheet of animals (Acts 10:11-14) is, for Craig, a case where Peter's refusal ("I have never eaten anything impure or unclean") shows he did not know of any abolition of food laws. He says Peter later explains the vision was about salvation being open to the Gentiles, and he compares the "Peter does not eat" ending to Abraham and Isaac. He presents this as one of the verses people most often cite out of context.

### 6. The NIV's "thus all foods are clean" is a marginal gloss
Craig's main textual argument: the NIV's parenthetical "thus all foods are clean" in Mark 7:19 is not in the Greek and was likely a marginal note that later entered the body of the text. The King James rendering ("purging all meats") describes food being digested and eliminated, which is continuous with the verse's first clause (Matthew 15:17 "whatever enters the mouth goes into the stomach and is eliminated"). A participant (Natalia) adds an example from a Danish Bible note that became part of the text. Craig admits he is not checking the Greek in this session, but says the translation issue is "largely why people get this misconception."

### 7. Hypocrisy and the question of "humane" treatment
Craig argues that a person who claims love while abusing animals is a hypocrite whose lips speak lies, citing Matthew 15:7-8 and Isaiah 59:3. Participants apply this to animal welfare claims, to the "Judas goat" in slaughterhouses, and to "humane slaughter." The group also raises empathy: would one want to be killed humanely? They connect this to Romans 12:2 and to the Good Shepherd.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus declared all foods clean (Mark 7:19, Matthew 15).** Craig says the passage concerns eating with unwashed hands, a human tradition, and that the disputed clause in Mark 7:19 is a translator's parenthetical, not the Greek.
- **Peter's Acts 10 vision abolished food laws.** Craig says the vision concerned salvation for Gentiles, and Peter himself refused the animals, showing he did not know of any such abolition.
- **Clean and unclean animals can be sorted by species.** Craig says the Leviticus and Genesis rules together make it impossible to avoid breaking some command while eating flesh.
- **Animal welfare regulations protect animals.** A participant argues that welfare claims in Denmark, England and the US are misleading, citing pig gas chambers and undercover footage.
- **Some Christians say Jesus ate fish, so eating animals is permitted.** A participant says pastors use this to avoid examining their own hypocrisy (Craig does not directly answer the fish argument).
