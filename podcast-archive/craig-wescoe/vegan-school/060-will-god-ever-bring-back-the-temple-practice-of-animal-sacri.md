## Summary

The talk asks whether God will ever bring back the temple practice of animal sacrifice, and it is the fourth session in a discussion series with a group of students on Zoom. Craig Wescoe, who leads the school, answers no. He argues that Jesus ended the practice once and for all by disrupting the temple and offering himself on the cross, and that believers are now the living temple offering "spiritual sacrifices" of praise, thanks, good works and generosity. Wescoe frames these as the sacrifices that are "truly pleasing to God."

His main evidence falls into several groups. First, he says every prophet opposed animal sacrifice, citing Isaiah 1:11, Psalm 40:6, Psalm 51:16-17, Hosea 6:6, 1 Samuel 15:22, Proverbs 21:3 and Micah 6:6-8. Second, he reads Jesus's temple action as quoting Jeremiah 7:11 and Isaiah 56:7 to reject the sacrificial trade (Mark 11:15-18). Third, he argues from Hebrews that Jesus is a permanent high priest in the order of Melchizedek, that animal sacrifices cannot take away sins, and that the once-for-all offering makes repetition pointless. Fourth, he argues that Ezekiel and Isaiah's passages about the temple and the peaceable kingdom were either symbolic or already fulfilled, and that Genesis 9 and Isaiah 65:25 include animals in God's everlasting covenant.

The session ends with the group reviewing the closing paragraphs on the "better covenant" and "better priesthood." Wescoe closes with the observation that if one does not want to follow Jesus, one should at least follow the prophets, and the session ends with prayer and a note that the next meeting is on the fourth Tuesday of the month.

## Key Teachings

### Jesus's temple action as the end of sacrifice
Wescoe argues that Jesus's disruption of the temple (Mark 11) rejected the animal-sacrifice system, not merely the attitudes behind it. He reads the phrase "den of robbers" as a mistranslation of *parasim*, which he says means "violence" or "tyranny," so the violence in view was done to the animals. He presents the temple's animal slaughter as commercial exploitation and says it was astonishing for Jesus to claim that the priests and scribes were selling something contrary to God. He is careful to note that a temple restored for prayer would not be a problem.

### The prophets and the question of divine command
Wescoe lists a series of prophetic passages (Isaiah 1, Psalm 40, Psalm 51, Hosea 6, 1 Samuel 15, Proverbs 21, Micah 6) and argues that they all say God does not desire sacrifice. He follows the historical-critical view that Jeremiah 8:8 ("the lying pen of the scribe") shows later scribes added the claim that God commanded sacrifice. He says he holds this "lying scribe" view himself, but allows that others might hold that the prophets were human and that a sacrifice practiced by a righteous figure was only God's "permissive will." He notes Maimonides ranked animal sacrifice low among the 613 commandments, and that Rav Kook and Akiva Gersh have associated the messianic age with vegetarianism or veganism.

### Hebrews on priesthood and the ineffectiveness of sacrifice
The longest argument uses Hebrews. Wescoe says Jesus is a priest "forever" in the order of Melchizedek (Hebrews 7:15-17, 7:23-24), so no Levitical priesthood could return without replacing him. He adds that animal blood "is impossible" to take away sin (Hebrews 10:4), and that Jesus's single offering "made perfect forever" (Hebrews 10:14). He compares the return of sacrifice to bringing back a broken computer or an old cassette player. In a later exchange he also argues from Hebrews 7 that Levi paid tithes to Melchizedek in the loins of Abraham, so the priesthood of Melchizedek is "older" and "better" rather than a supersessionist replacement.

### The temple as the body of Christ and the believing community
Wescoe argues that the body of Jesus is the permanent temple (John 2:19-21), that Revelation 21:22 says there will be no temple in the restored Jerusalem because "the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple," and that believers are the "living stones" of a spiritual house (1 Peter 2:5). Hebrews 13:15-16 is his list of the spiritual sacrifices: praise, thanks, good works and generosity. He notes that the list contains no shedding of animal blood. He cites 2 Corinthians 6:16, Ephesians 2:20-21 and Revelation 3:12 as further passages, and calls the cornerstone the one "bearing the bulk of the load."

### Ezekiel and Isaiah: symbolic, fulfilled, or incompatible with the peaceable kingdom
For the Ezekiel passage about Zadokite Levites offering a bull, Wescoe gives two readings: it is "almost certainly a misinterpretation," or it refers to a time already past, since Ezekiel prophesied before the second temple. For Isaiah 56:7 ("their burnt offerings and sacrifices will be accepted on my altar"), he follows James Tabor's proposal that the phrase is an insertion, since the verses flow without it. He also cites Ross Nichols as having made similar points. For Isaiah 11 and Isaiah 65:25 (no harming or destroying on God's holy mountain), he argues the peaceable kingdom cannot coexist with ongoing killing of animals.

### Creation covenant, animals included
Wescoe closes his argument by saying the "everlasting covenant" of Genesis 9 includes animals, which he says the text mentions five times, and that Hosea 2:18 promises a covenant with the beasts of the field. He quotes Steven Webb to the effect that animals are "freed to be what they are rather than what the ritual system turned them into." He concludes that a restoration of God's original design, which includes animals, is incompatible with a return of animal sacrifice.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That the red heifer and a third temple will bring back animal sacrifice.** Wescoe says this is a minority view within Christianity and Judaism, but it is widely pushed in some evangelical and Hebraic-roots circles, and he argues it cannot be squared with Hebrews.
- **That God commanded animal sacrifice and will restore it.** Wescoe argues that the prophets, Jesus and the Genesis covenant show God never wanted it, and that texts claiming God commanded sacrifice may be scribal additions (the lying-scribe view, which he holds himself).
- **That the Ezekiel and Isaiah prophecies literally predict a restored sacrificial system.** He argues these were symbolic, already fulfilled in the second temple era, or inconsistent with the rest of Isaiah.
- **That a Levitical priesthood could return alongside Jesus.** He says Jesus is a priest forever, so no successor priesthood is needed, and the Melchizedek priesthood is older and better.
- **That the Noah sacrifice shows God approved of sacrifice.** He reads Genesis 8:21 as a negative response to Noah's offering, and says the text gives no command.
- **That the new covenant is supersessionist.** He argues the covenant is a restoration of God's original, creation-wide promise rather than a replacement.
