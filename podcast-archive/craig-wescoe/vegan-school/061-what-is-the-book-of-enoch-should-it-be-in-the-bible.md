## Summary

Wescoe asks why the Book of Enoch is missing from most Bibles and whether it belongs in the canon. He says the main reason is that Augustine, around the year 400 by his dating, excluded it. Augustine said part of the book was genuinely from Enoch, as Jude testifies, but that since Jewish tradition had excluded the book and the authentic portions could not be identified, the whole work should be set aside. Wescoe frames the talk in four parts: scholarship before and after the Dead Sea Scrolls, first-century Jewish and early church attitudes, what the Bible says about Enoch, and a synopsis of the first 19 chapters.

His central argument is that the scholarly consensus before 1976 (that 1 Enoch was a Christian composition built on Jude and the New Testament) was overturned by the Aramaic Enoch fragments from Qumran Cave 4, which he says are older than the New Testament. On that basis he argues that Jude 1:14-15 attests to Enoch's authenticity, that Jesus' answer about the resurrection in Matthew 22 and Mark 12 depends on 1 Enoch 15, and that 2 Peter 2:4 depends on 1 Enoch 10. He then argues that anyone who rejects the whole book as pseudepigrapha must treat Jesus, Peter and Jude as mistaken. He presents three positions: reject all of it, accept all of it, or accept the first 19 chapters as authentic while leaving the rest open. He favors the third and says he wants viewers to see the evidence and decide for themselves.

The talk ends with the synopsis and the three-position comparison. It does not reach a definitive verdict on the later chapters.

## Key Teachings

### Scholarship reversed by the Dead Sea Scrolls
Wescoe says that before 1976, outside Ethiopia, the near-consensus was that 1 Enoch was a pseudepigraphon composed by Christians, a spin-off of Jude and Genesis 5-6. He credits Milik's 1976 publication of Aramaic fragments from Qumran Cave 4 with showing that the book predated the New Testament, so that "we had everything backwards." Jude's letter, he argues, is then best read as testimony to Enoch's authenticity rather than as the source of 1 Enoch. He notes that the Ethiopian Tewahedo and Eritrean Orthodox churches were the only ones including Enoch in their canon before this discovery. He also says at least four of the five sections of 1 Enoch appear among the Dead Sea fragments.

### Early Jewish and early church readings of Genesis 6
Wescoe reads the Damascus Document (which he dates between 175 and 100 BC, while the group lasted into the first century) as describing fallen "heavenly watchers" whose giant offspring were destroyed. He takes Philo and Josephus as reading Genesis 6 as angels who fathered children with women. He then surveys Justin Martyr, Athenagoras, Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, Tertullian, Commodian and Anatolius of Alexandria, arguing that each treats 1 Enoch as authoritative or as Enoch's prophecy. He reads Tertullian's trusteeship argument (Enoch to Methuselah to Noah) as a defense of the book's survival through the flood, and reads Tertullian's mention of Jewish exclusion as evidence that some Christians rejected it for that reason only. He says Commodian ties the giants' spirits to demons and idolatry, and that Anatolius's paschal reckoning draws on the calendar section, which is outside the first 19 chapters.

### Augustine's decision
Wescoe's account is that Augustine, deciding canon in City of God, accepted that Enoch left some divine writings (citing Jude) but excluded the book because Jewish scripture had not preserved it, its antiquity made authenticity impossible to verify, and it contained fables about giants. Wescoe's comment is that Augustine's reasoning throws out "the baby with the bathwater." He says the book's modern exclusion rests on that decision rather than on any finding about the text.

### Jesus, Jude and Peter on Enoch
Wescoe argues that Jude 1:14-15 explicitly quotes 1 Enoch 1:9 and calls it prophecy from Enoch the seventh from Adam. He reads Jesus' reply to the Sadducees about the resurrection (Matthew 22:29-30; Mark 12:24-25) as depending on 1 Enoch 15:3-7, which says angels do not have wives in heaven. He reads 2 Peter 2:4 as depending on 1 Enoch 10:4-6 (binding Azazel and casting angels into darkness), and Jude 1:6 as similar to 1 Enoch 10:11-13. He argues that rejecting the whole book means calling these writers mistaken.

### Synopsis: giants, demons, sacrifice and diet
Wescoe summarizes 1 Enoch 6-10 and 15-19 as the source of a fuller account of Genesis 6. Angels lusted after women and taught arts, giants were born, the giants consumed people, then animals and their blood, and the earth was corrupted (Azazel blamed for sin). The giants' disembodied spirits, he says, became the demons that cause violence, possession and idolatry. He says 1 Enoch 19:1 presents these spirits as leading people into sacrificing to demons, and that this is the origin of blood sacrifice and of eating animal flesh. He connects these motifs to Matthew 8:28-29 (the Gerasene demoniacs), Revelation 9:20-21 (idolatry, murder, magic), and the final judgment in 1 Enoch 10 and Matthew 25:41.

### Three positions and their costs
Wescoe lays out three views: the whole book is pseudepigrapha; the whole book is authentically Enoch; the first 19 chapters are authentic and the rest undetermined. He argues that the first view forces one to explain away Jesus, Peter and Jude. He says the second faces the problem of the animal apocalypse predicting Israelite history in detail, though he says it is not disproved. He favors the third view as the "safest" position, while saying the later chapters remain open.

- **"Scholars have settled that Enoch is a Christian forgery."** He argues the pre-1976 consensus was reversed by the Qumran Aramaic fragments, which he reads as showing 1 Enoch predates the New Testament.
- **"Rejecting the Book of Enoch is a harmless position."** He argues that a full pseudepigrapha verdict requires treating Jesus (Matthew 22, Mark 12), Peter (2 Peter 2:4) and Jude as mistaken or misled.
