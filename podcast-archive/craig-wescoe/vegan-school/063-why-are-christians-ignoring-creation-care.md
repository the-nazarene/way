## Summary

This is a study session from the Vegan Christian Discipleship School, recorded as a group meeting and led mostly by Craig. The question is why more Christians, and especially church leaders, are not concerned with creation care, meaning the treatment of animals and the wider creation as an expression of God's love. Craig's answer is a list of seven reasons. Five apply to Christians generally: ignorance, tradition, deception, temptation and vanity. Two apply specifically to church leaders: profit and fear. Each reason is taken from a paragraph of a written study guide that participants read aloud, and each is then discussed. The support is mainly scripture (the speaker's proof-texts, with some interpretation), plus quotations from theologians Andrew Linzey and Carl Anders Skriver, a historical claim that Augustine and Aquinas set the dominant view of animals as existing for human use, and Francis of Assisi as the main exception. Participants add personal testimony about diet, abuse and church pressure.

Craig argues that the "peaceable kingdom" vision has been neglected since early church history. He also argues that the "not salvational" objection is a real spiritual danger and not a neutral position. The talk ends with a reading that God calls ordinary people who have a willing heart, as in Isaiah 6:8, and with a closing prayer. The speakers do not test their historical or medical claims in the session. Several claims are offered as the group's own conviction (for example, that some suppression of information about farming is intentional, and that the Book of Enoch describes demons who drive violence and animal sacrifice).

## Key Teachings

### Creation care and the buried peaceable kingdom

Craig defines creation care as "embodying God's character of love in how we steward his created world," and he grounds it in 1 John 4:16 and Proverbs 12:10. The study guide, read aloud, says the peaceable kingdom vision has been "buried and ignored from very early on in church history, with few exceptions." It credits Augustine and Aquinas with the doctrine that animals, lacking reason, were placed on earth for human use, and it presents Francis of Assisi as the main exception, for whom honoring animals honored God. Craig notes that Augustine's view "really became the dominant view at the end of the 4th century." He says the prophets promise a time when this will change, and he treats the change as certain, so that the group's task is education.

### Ignorance and tradition

For ignorance, the guide cites Romans 10:3 and Matthew 6:33 and argues that most Christians either do not read the Bible or read it through inherited traditions. Craig's central claim is that churches place love on the "periphery" and should move it back to the center, from where extending love to animals would follow. He also says that some ignorance is deliberate. He describes "gag laws" and penalties against people who expose slaughterhouses and factory farms, while the people doing the abuse are not punished. This is presented as the speaker's view and is not documented in the session. A participant compares the failure to love animals with the failure to love poor people or people of other ethnicities.

For tradition, the guide cites Mark 7:8 and argues that churches have long taught that animals exist "for us to torture, kill, and destroy," contrary to John 10:10. A participant links inherited traditions to generational patterns and cites Exodus 20 on punishment passed down "to the third and fourth generation." Craig says inherited diet produces illness, and he presents the health claims of PCRM and *Forks Over Knives* through a participant's testimony. Another participant describes recovering from serious illness after going vegan. Craig also cites Genesis 1:29 and Daniel 1 as evidence that God designed a plant diet.

### Deception and temptation

For deception, the guide cites 2 Corinthians 11:3, Proverbs 3:31 and Romans 12:2. It says factory farms are "deceptively portrayed as ethical" and that slaughter is labeled humane. A participant's example is the "happy cow" marketing image. Craig raises a possible spiritual dimension and refers to the Book of Enoch, which he says describes demons who make people violent and slaughter animals as sacrifices. He connects this to the serpent in Genesis. A participant argues that animal sacrifice and meat eating are essentially the same practice. Craig then argues that Jesus, when asked about divorce, pointed back to creation, so the believer should test practices against Genesis 1 and not against Leviticus or other permissive texts. His stated reasoning is that the "deceiver" is the one who says God permits harm to animals.

For temptation, the guide cites Galatians 5:17, Romans 7:19, Numbers 11 and Proverbs 23:20. It says people may know God's will but lack self-control, and the remedy is the fruit of the Spirit in Galatians 5:23. Craig compares churches' support groups for alcohol, cigarettes and pornography with the absence of such support for animal-product consumption. Natalia links temptation with fear of standing out. Craig separates the two: deception tricks people into believing something false, while temptation lures people into acting against what they already know.

### Vanity and the "not salvational" objection

For vanity, the guide cites Proverbs 16:19 (read as "pride goes before destruction"), Luke 12:15 and Proverbs 14:12. It argues that people who wear leather or fur for appearance are acting out of vanity, and it calls such clothing among "the ugliest things you can put on your body." Tracy and another participant add that tanning chemicals pollute water and harm workers, and that fur trapping sometimes involves children.

Craig gives the most emphasis to the claim that creation care is "not a salvational issue." He argues that God gave dietary instruction at the very beginning, so the issue is central to God's purposes and not peripheral. He reads the "not salvational" stance as meaning "what's in it for me," and he cites Matthew 7:23 ("I never knew you. Away from me, you evildoers") to argue that persisting in evil is a real danger. He calls this "one of the biggest deceptions in the church."

### Profit and fear among leaders

The guide cites Zechariah 11:5 on shepherds who have no compassion for their sheep and James 2 on faith without works. It argues that leaders who teach creation care may lose donations, tithes and members. It also argues that leaders fear losing their livelihood or their families' security. Craig says that the profit motive leads some leaders to call the will of God "from the pits of hell," and he says that doing so comes close to blaspheming the Holy Spirit. He acknowledges that leaders face real pressure and says he has experienced it himself: a previous church asked him not to say that people "can't eat meat," and a participant's testimony was edited before she spoke. The answer, he says, is trust. He cites 1 John 4:18, "perfect love drives out fear," as the remedy.

### Who God chooses, and the call to "Here I am"

The closing paragraphs argue that God chooses people with a willing heart, not those with the right credentials. Craig cites Isaiah 6:8, Colossians 3:12 and Jonah, and he argues that Jesus chose fishermen and "the poor" (he cites Luke, without a verse). He reads John 15:16 and John 10:27 to argue that disciples are sent to "bear fruit that will remain," and he presents "love" as the first of the fruits. Craig also suggests that the Lord's Prayer ("Your kingdom come, your will be done on earth as it is in heaven") should be lived as a call to end cruelty to animals. He adds that bold speech should be "gentle and respectful" (citing 1 Peter), although the prophets and Jesus sometimes denounced evil sharply. The session closes with prayer and an announcement of the next meeting.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"God created animals only for human use, so they may be used however we like."** Craig argues against this through Genesis 1:29, the peaceable kingdom and Francis of Assisi, and he claims that this view has been dominant since Augustine (as stated in the talk).
- **"Creation care is a periphery issue, not a salvational one, so it can be ignored."** Craig argues that God's instruction on diet is central and that dismissing it is a real spiritual danger (Matthew 7:23).
- **"The church's neglect of animals is a modern problem caused by factory farming."** A participant suggests this as an excuse, and Craig says it does not hold, because the view traces back to Augustine.
- **"Veganism is a fringe sect."** A participant reports that she is labeled this way when she speaks, but the talk does not give a substantive rebuttal beyond her testimony.
