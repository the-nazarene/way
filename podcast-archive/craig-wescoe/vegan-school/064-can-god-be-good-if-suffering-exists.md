The guide opens with the problem of evil, which the reader traces to Epicurus and links to Darwin's loss of faith. Its answer is yes. ## Key Teachings

### Evil as potentiality rather than actuality

The guide argues that evil must be possible for good to exist, but that it becomes actual only when people "perpetuate" it. Wescoe ties this to Genesis 2–3: the tree of knowledge was a test, and the first sin "brought evil into the world" when humans ate from it. He uses Genesis 4:7 ("sin is crouching at your door") to frame the "best possible world" as one in which evil stays in potentiality. He draws on Plato and Aristotle for the potentiality/actuality language and says he himself had not framed it this way before. He also argues that the tree was a small restriction among many permitted trees, so God's prohibition was limited and loving.

### Free will and the image of God

The second argument is that God's image requires freedom. The guide reasons from 1 John 4:16 ("God is love") and Genesis 1:26 that humans are made with a loving nature, and that they must be free to act against it or else be "robots." Wescoe adds that God too is free and yet never chooses evil, which he describes as a matter of strength. He compares this to the stone-too-heavy paradox and says we should trust God because he will not choose evil. On foreknowledge, he says he would "lean toward limiting God's knowledge to actual things," because genuine freedom would otherwise be impossible, and he rejects Calvinist determinism as inconsistent with Jesus' ethical instructions.

### Angelic rebellion as an alternative source

The guide's third view is that angels, not humans, first brought corruption into the world. The text cites Genesis 3:22, Isaiah 14:12–14 (the "Lucifer" passage) and 1 John 3:8 ("the reason the son of God appeared was to destroy the devil's work"). Wescoe says "Lucifer" is not a proper name in the original, that the passage is the only one where the Bible uses that word, and that the Book of Enoch uses the name Azazel for the rebel. He links the rebellion to pride and to Jesus' refusal of Satan's offer in the wilderness. He says this view does not have to be held together with the others.

### God as "most high," not all-powerful

The guide and Wescoe argue that the Bible never calls God "all-powerful." Instead it calls him "Elyon" (most high), citing Genesis 14:18, Hebrews 7:17 and Psalm 110:4. The reading treats evil powers as "entirely separate from God" (Ephesians 6:12) and says God is "more powerful than all the other powers" without being the source of all power. Wescoe admits this is his "heretical" position and says he would drop omnipotence before dropping God's goodness or omniscience. When a participant asks whether "everything is God," Wescoe answers with Berkeley's and Aquinas's idealist/mind-of-God readings but insists that evil itself was not created by God.

### Sin destroys, God saves

A central claim is that "it is the nature of sin to destroy and the nature of God to save." The guide cites Romans 6:23, John 3:16–17, Romans 12:2 and 2 Peter 3:8–9 (God not wanting anyone to perish). It argues that Bible passages attributing destruction to God should be read as describing the consequence of sin. Wescoe offers examples: the Red Sea (the Egyptians "caused their own destruction") and the Korah rebellion, and he notes that the same passages often attribute destruction to sin in the next breath. He connects this to the name Yeshua ("he will save his people from their sins"). He also distinguishes the Elohist and Yahwist sources and says he prefers a critical reading that treats the destroyer-God language as coming from a later source.

### Temporary evil and the restoration of all things

The guide holds that evil and suffering are temporary and will be ended by God's restoration. It cites 1 Peter 5:10, 2 Chronicles 7:14, Habakkuk 2:17 and Revelation 21:1–4. Wescoe says human life is only "a blink of an eye" from eternity's perspective (citing 2 Peter 3:8), and that the tree of life in Eden was a "safeguard." He admits uncertainty about whether the new creation will still include the possibility of sin, and suggests humans may "learn our lesson" and not fall again. He also notes that the restoration is "not making it something that it's never been before," but restoring the original design.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Misconception:** God created evil or designed suffering. **Correction:** Wescoe argues God created a "very good" world, that evil only becomes actual through free human choice (or angelic rebellion), and that "the evil itself was not created by God."
- **Misconception:** God is all-powerful in the sense of controlling everything, including evil. **Correction:** The guide argues the Bible calls God "most high" (Elyon), not all-powerful, and that evil powers are "separate from God."
- **Misconception:** Darwin's theory and atheism are simply the result of scientific evidence. **Correction:** Wescoe presents Darwin's loss of faith as rooted in the problem of animal suffering, and says macroevolution is not testable, though microevolution is.
- **Misconception:** "Lucifer" is the proper name of the rebel angel. **Correction:** Wescoe says the word is a translation of "star of the light," appears in Isaiah 14:12 only, and that the rebel's name is more likely Azazel as in the Book of Enoch.
- **Misconception:** God is the destroyer of the wicked (wrath, Korah, the Flood, the Red Sea). **Correction:** Wescoe reads destruction as the consequence of sin itself, and says God's character is to save.
