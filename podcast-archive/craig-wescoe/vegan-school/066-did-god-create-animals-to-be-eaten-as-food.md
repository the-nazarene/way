## Summary

The talk is the seventeenth meeting of the Vegan Christian Discipleship School, run by Craig Wescoe through his Swords to Plowshares ministry. The question is "Did God create animals to be eaten as food?" Wescoe reads a short answer from a gray box: God did not create animals to be eaten as food. Per the description, the study guide offers a longer answer, and in the session participants take turns reading its paragraphs aloud. Wescoe leads the discussion and adds comments between readings.

The main argument rests on Genesis 1 and 2. Wescoe says the first diet God gives humans and animals is entirely plant-based (Genesis 1:29-30), that the commission to "be fruitful and multiply" applies to animals as well as humans, and that the "dominion" of Genesis 1:26 is not a license to kill, since the next verses describe food from plants. Other paragraphs build on Isaiah 11, Proverbs, Psalm 150, Revelation 5:13 and the Noah story. They argue that animals were made to live peacefully, to be named, and to praise God. The session ends with participants' thanks, a reminder that the study guide and recordings are available through the school's site, and a closing prayer. Wescoe describes his role as "a fellow disciple," not the teacher, and says Jesus is the true teacher. He encourages participants to use whichever argument persuades them most, since the arguments are not all required to stand together.

## Key Teachings

### Genesis sets the original diet as plant-based
Wescoe's central claim is that Genesis 1:29-30 shows God giving every seed-bearing plant to humans and every green plant to animals as food. He describes this as "the very good diet God specified for humans," one "completely devoid of animal products." He argues that the only diet God calls "very good" is this plant-based one, and that no passage calls eating animals very good. He acknowledges that other passages are read as permission for eating animals, and says that in other studies he argues those passages were not granted as permission.

### Dominion is not a license to kill
Participants read a paragraph attributed in the study guide to Professor Gary Francone, who argues that the dominion of Genesis 1:26 is not clearly a license to use or kill animals. The group extends this with Genesis 1:29, which they say follows directly and specifies plant food. They also call the commandment "thou shalt not kill" part of "God's entire set of instructions." The speakers present this as a principle that should govern readings of dominion.

### Animals were created to be fruitful, to be named, and to be at peace
The study guide reads Genesis 1:22 and 1:28 as giving animals the same command to multiply that humans receive. Wescoe says the "fruitful" language covers both procreation and thriving. Participants then argue, drawing on Isaiah 11:6-7, Matthew 5:9, Romans 12, Galatians 5:14, and Proverbs 3, that God intended peaceful coexistence among creatures. Genesis 2:20 is used to argue that Adam named individual animals, which participants say makes each animal "someone" rather than "something." Naming is linked to the bond people feel with pets, and participants argue that the same bond should extend to farm animals.

### Predation, cats, and the "nutrients not ingredients" argument
Participants raise predation (lions eating other animals) and obligate carnivores. Wescoe answers that predation belongs to the fallen world, and that a previous class covered it. He points to Isaiah 11:7 ("the lion eats straw like the ox") as a picture of restoration. On cats, he says studies show that "animals in general, need nutrients, not ingredients," and that nutritionally complete plant-based cat foods (he names Evolution and Amy's) can meet their needs, with added synthetic taurine where needed. A participant cites Andrew Knight's study on cat health in the chat.

### Did Jesus eat fish? Luke 24:42-43 and the manuscript question
Wescoe says Luke 24:42-43 is "the only passage in any of the gospels that depicts Jesus eating fish." He gives three reasons for doubt. First, he says the Gospel of Luke's author states at the beginning that they were not an eyewitness and compiled earlier accounts. Second, the story does not appear in Matthew, Mark, or John. Third, he says manuscripts differ, with "fish and honeycomb" in some and "just fish" in others, so the text appears altered. He describes his own approach as "source criticism" and says it leads him to see the passage as a weak basis for claiming Jesus ate fish. He adds that even if someone accepts the passage, there are "a hundred other points" that support veganism. Participants also argue that eating flesh and blood would have made Jesus a sinner, but this is a theological argument, not a textual one.

### Early-church claims about the Nazarenes, Essenes, and Ebionites
Wescoe refers to his talk "Evidence for Veganism in the early church." He says that the "earliest first, second century evidence" describes each apostle as vegan, that Jesus and the apostles opposed animal sacrifice, and that the terms Nazarenes, Essenes, and Ebionites describe the earliest followers. He does not quote these sources in this session, and he defers to the earlier talk. These are his claims as stated here, not sourced material he presents in this session.

### Animals in heaven and the nephesh question
Participants argue that Ecclesiastes says humans and animals share "the same breath and the same fate," that Revelation 5:13 shows animals praising God, and that Balaam's donkey (Numbers 22) is a Christlike figure who will be in the kingdom. Wescoe says that objections like "animals don't have a soul" come from tradition, and he attributes the distinction to Greek philosophy and to translators who render *nephesh* as "living soul" for humans and "living creature" for animals. He cites Job 12:7 ("ask the animals and they will teach you") and says a near-death account reported in an interview with John Burke suggests animals are present in heaven. He acknowledges that the Catholic tradition, which he says draws on Plato, teaches that animals do not go to heaven, and he argues for dialogue on shared ground: animals have inherent worth.

- **Cats cannot be fed a vegan diet.** Wescoe says the research shows animals need nutrients, not specific ingredients, and that nutritionally complete plant-based cat foods exist. He does not provide the research here; he refers to a presentation he gave.
