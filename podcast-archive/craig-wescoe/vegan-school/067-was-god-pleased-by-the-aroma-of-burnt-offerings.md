## Summary

This is a two-hour session of a vegan Christian study group run by Craig Wescoe, built around one lesson: whether God was pleased by the "pleasing aroma" of burnt offerings. The question is raised by a common objection, that if God loves the smell of burnt offerings, then a barbecue is fine. Most of the session walks through the study guide paragraph by paragraph, with group members reading aloud and asking questions.

Craig's answer is that the pleasing aroma was pleasing to the human senses, not to God, much as the forbidden fruit was "pleasing to the eye" in Genesis 3:6 without pleasing God. He supports this with several arguments: the Noah narrative in Genesis 8 (God never commanded the altar, and God's response in Genesis 8:21 calls human inclination evil); the Ezekiel 6:13 passage, in which an aroma is offered to idols that "cannot smell" (Psalm 115:4-6), which he says makes the "recipient is pleased" reading self-contradictory; and the prophets (Isaiah 1:11, Psalm 51:16-17, Hosea 6:6), who say God does not desire sacrifice. He presents the Ephesians 5:2 "pleasing aroma" of Christ's self-giving love as the one aroma God does accept. He also proposes a historical-critical reading, drawn from Jeremiah 8:8 and the Hilkiah scroll story in 2 Kings 22 and 2 Chronicles 34, in which the sacrificial material is later "lying pen" addition to an earlier Elohist Torah. He says he presents this alongside a traditional reading, because either way the conclusion is that God does not want animal sacrifice.

The session ends with the group discussing how to talk about this with other Christians and non-vegans. Craig suggests a "tool belt" of approaches, the group closes in prayer, and Craig says he will send links to his other talks and resources. Several claims are presented as Craig's own reading and as one of two possible readings. He labels the Yahwist/Elohist application as an unpublished, tentative view.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The aroma is pleasing to the human senses, not to God
The study guide's central claim is that the pleasing aroma was "pleasing to the human senses." Craig reasons from the Genesis 3:6 account of the forbidden fruit, which is described as "pleasing to the eye" and "desirable for gaining wisdom," yet not pleasing to God, since it was disobedience (Genesis 3:17 follows with the curse on the ground). He compares this to burnt offerings, which are described as pleasing to the nose in Genesis 8:21 but which he says God does not describe as "very good." By contrast, God calls the creation "very good" in Genesis 1, and Craig argues that God's pleasure attaches to what he calls good, not to what merely pleases the senses. (Timestamps: 53:06 to 56:23.)

### 2. Noah's altar was not commanded
Craig and a participant note that God gives Noah specific instructions throughout Genesis 6–8 ("Noah did what he said"), but no command to build an altar or sacrifice. The first thing Noah does after leaving the ark is build an altar and offer burnt offerings (Genesis 8:20). Craig reads God's reaction in Genesis 8:21 as a response to an unauthorized act, and he notes that the text never says God is pleased by the smell. He also raises Noah's later drunkenness (Genesis 9) as a parallel case of a righteous person falling short, while acknowledging that other readings are possible. (Timestamps: 08:20 to 09:52; 58:28 to 1:03:01.)

### 3. The Ezekiel idol passage creates a contradiction
Ezekiel 6:13 speaks of pleasing aroma offered to idols. Craig argues that if the Genesis passage means the recipient is pleased, then the idols would have to be pleased too. Psalm 115:4-6 says idols "have noses but cannot smell," so they cannot judge an aroma pleasing. The "recipient is pleased" reading therefore cannot be applied consistently. Craig's proposed resolution is that the aroma must be pleasing to someone other than the recipient, such as the human offerers. He acknowledges a participant's objection that God, unlike an idol, can smell. His reply is that the same Hebrew phrasing is used for both, so the consistent reading cannot make the recipient the one pleased. (Timestamps: 16:27 to 20:43.)

### 4. The prophets say God does not desire sacrifice
Craig treats the prophets as the most direct evidence. He cites Isaiah 1:11 ("I have no pleasure in the blood of bulls"), Psalm 51:16-17 (a broken and contrite heart), and Hosea 6:6 ("I desire mercy, not sacrifice"). Craig reads the wording as exclusive, not additive: God does not want sacrifice "in addition to" mercy but "instead of" it. A participant raises the objection that some prophets (Ezekiel, Isaiah) seem to anticipate a restored temple and sacrifice. Craig answers that Revelation describes no temple, that the body is called a temple in other passages, and that some Ezekiel passages were already fulfilled. He also cites Hebrews 10:4 ("it is not possible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins") to argue that the New Testament rejects sacrificial atonement, not just that it has been replaced. (Timestamps: 29:07 to 38:18.)

### 5. Two readings: traditional and historical-critical
Craig presents two options. The first, which he calls the traditional route, treats Leviticus as God's permissive will, or as God meeting people where they were, with the prophets and Jesus pointing toward God's ideal. The second rests on source criticism. Craig claims that the "Yahwist" (YHWH) material in Genesis 6–8 is secondary, and that the Noah sacrifice and "seven clean animals" detail (Genesis 7:2 versus 6:19) was added later. He ties this to Jeremiah 8:8 ("the lying pen of the scribes") and Jeremiah 7:22, which says God did not command burnt offerings in the day of the Exodus. He links this to the scroll that Hilkiah found in the temple during Josiah's reforms (2 Kings 22; 2 Chronicles 34). Craig says he has not published this research and offers it as an introduction, not a settled conclusion. He also notes that, as he understands it, German scholars of the 19th century found the YHWH/Elohim pattern held only in the first part of Genesis. (Timestamps: 1:24:56 to 1:28:38; 1:40:08 to 1:41:04.)

### 6. Jesus's love, not his blood, is the pleasing aroma
In the final paragraphs, Craig reads Ephesians 5:2 ("Live a life filled with love... a pleasing aroma to God") and Matthew 9:13 ("I desire mercy and not sacrifice") together with Jesus's temple act ("My father's house... a house of prayer"). He argues that Christ's self-giving love is pleasing to God, but that God is not pleased by blood. He compares this with Genesis 22, where God stops Abraham from sacrificing Isaac. He says Jesus's sacrifice ended the sacrificial system rather than endorsing it, and that Revelation 21:4 ("death will be no more") shows death is not God's design. (Timestamps: 1:10:52 to 1:21:52.)

### 7. Pleasure in good things versus misused pleasures
Craig and a participant argue that God does not oppose pleasure as such. The Shabbat is described as "a delight," and God gives food and creation as good gifts. The problem, in Craig's account, is misuse: pleasure in bloodshed, coveting, or burning and eating animals. He compares this to sex within marriage as a proper "design" for a good gift, and says Genesis 1 and the Edenic diet set the pattern. (Timestamps: 1:06:05 to 1:07:14; 1:15:08 to 1:18:55.)

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **God is pleased by the smell of burnt offerings, so barbecue is fine.** The aroma language describes what pleases the human senses, not God; Craig argues the "recipient is pleased" reading contradicts the Ezekiel idol passage and the prophets.
- **Noah's altar was God's instruction.** Craig says the text records no command, and God's reaction in Genesis 8:21 is critical.
- **God wants both sacrifice and mercy.** Craig reads Hosea 6:6, Psalm 51, and Isaiah 1 as presenting a choice ("instead of"), not an addition.
- **Jesus's shed blood is pleasing to God.** Craig says God is "not pleased by any shedding of blood"; the pleasing thing is Jesus's life of love.
- **Every instance of "LORD" in capitals is the Yahwist source.** Craig says this is not straightforward: the capitals show YHWH, but source attribution does not follow the usage throughout the Bible.
- **The Bible must be wholly true in every detail.** The session raises this as a pastoral problem; Craig's answer is to take individuals case by case, and a participant says she "was brought up to believe not a word of the Bible is wrong," which Craig's reading challenges.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Jesus vs. Paul:** Ephesians 5:2 and Galatians 5:17 are used as Pauline support for the "love is the aroma" reading, but Craig's main textual anchors are the prophets, Matthew 9:13 and the temple episode. He describes Hebrews as addressing a priesthood-minded audience, not as Pauline.
- **Torah and sacrifice:** The core of the session is whether Leviticus-type sacrifice texts reflect God's ideal, a concession, or later scribal addition. Craig's source-critical reading (Jeremiah 8:8, Hilkiah's scroll) is directly relevant to the repo's Torah question.
- **Kingdom on earth:** Craig and participants invoke the Lord's Prayer ("your kingdom come, your will be done on earth") and Isaiah-style peace imagery as the vision of creation restored; the "Eden ideal" is central to his reading.
- **Nonviolence and diet:** Diet is grounded in Genesis 1 and the Edenic diet, and nonviolence in the temple episode and Matthew 9:13.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Craig presents Jesus as teacher interpreting Torah by "the beginning" (the divorce teaching), and as the one who ended the sacrificial system. He does not discuss Jesus's self-understanding in depth beyond this.
- **How the Scriptures were formed:** Craig argues that some Torah material is later scribal addition, but says he is not asserting this as the only view.
- **Resurrection, afterlife, and hell:** Not discussed, apart from Revelation 21:4 on death being removed.
