## Summary

This is a Discipleship School session from the vegan-school channel, hosted by Craig Wescoe with a small group, most of whom join by video. The question, from the title and opening, is why Deuteronomy 12:20 ("you may eat as much meat as your heart desires") and the Numbers 11 quail story seem to give God's permission to eat animal flesh, which vegan readers are often told settles the matter. The session is built as a rebuttal guide: the opening paragraph states the opposing argument at its strongest, and the rest of the study answers it.

Craig's answer is that these passages describe a test and a rebuke rather than an approval. In Numbers 11 the people crave meat, are given it, and are struck with a plague while the meat is still in their teeth; the site is named for the "grave of craving." Deuteronomy 12 is read as a conditional permission addressed to a second generation who are warned against repeating that rebellion. Craig then argues that the blood prohibition makes flesh-eating impossible to do without disobedience, that Genesis 1:29 sets a plant diet as "very good," that Galatians 5 and Philippians 3:19 frame craving as serving "the flesh" or a god of appetite, and that Micah 6:8 and related prophetic texts describe God's real demand as mercy. The closing paragraph urges believers to "crucify" these desires (Galatians 5:24) and to aim for the "A+" rather than the minimum permission.

The session ends with a prayer and a discussion of outreach (showing slaughterhouse footage, church tables, and the group's study resources). Susan raises a principled objection that killing is wrong whatever the welfare conditions, and Craig agrees. Craig also advises new readers to set aside Leviticus for now and says the group will cover Luke 24:42-43 later. The reasoning throughout is the speaker's and the group's; the transcript does not give primary-source checks on most of the claims.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Deuteronomy 12 and Numbers 11 read as a test and warning, not a grant
Craig reads the "eat as much meat as you desire" language as a conditional allowance inside a covenant context: the second generation is told to be faithful to God, drive out idolatry, and not repeat the first generation's mistakes. He points to Deuteronomy 12:12 for the joy of worship in the land, and to Deuteronomy 30:19 for the choice between life and death. He reads the desire clause ("if you crave meat") as the focus, so the permission concerns craving, not a general entitlement. He concedes that some consequence still follows. The Numbers 11 narrative is quoted in pieces (11:18, 11:20, 11:33, 11:34): the complaint, the meat "until it comes out of your nostrils," the plague, and the naming of the grave of craving. Participants agree that God is displeased with the craving.

### 2. The blood prohibition as the decisive limit
Craig's central argument is that flesh cannot be separated from blood, so eating flesh always violates Deuteronomy 12:23 and Genesis 9:4 (and, he adds, Leviticus and Acts 15). He quotes an author, Jeffrey Rudd, who argues that ceremonial throat-cutting and bleeding cannot remove all blood, since blood remains in the arteries and tissues and the quarters still contain fat. Craig presents an analogy: a catheter bag emptied until it stops dripping would still not be safe to drink from through a straw. He concludes that the only way to avoid the blood command is to abstain from animal flesh, which returns to the original diet of Genesis 1:29.

### 3. Genesis as the baseline diet
Craig and the participants treat Genesis 1:29-31 as the "very good" diet of fruit, seeds and herbs. He notes that Genesis 1:30 assigns green herbs to animals, and that Genesis 9:3-4 expands human diet to include animals while keeping the blood prohibition. He uses Genesis 1:29 and 1:31 as God's own valuation of the plant diet.

### 4. Flesh and Spirit, appetite as idolatry
Using Galatians 5:16-17, Craig argues that craving meat is a case of the flesh opposing the Spirit, and that God's will (not harming creatures) and the desire for meat pull in opposite directions. He ties this to the first commandment (Exodus 20:3) and to Philippians 3:19, reading "whose god is their appetite" as idolatry. Proverbs 23:20 ("gluttonous eaters of meat") is read as forbidding association, not just excess. Craig distinguishes desiring what God calls good (fruit, a smoothie) from desiring what he calls not good.

### 5. Sacrifice and slaughter
Craig argues that the prophets opposed animal sacrifice (he cites "mercy, not sacrifice" and "obedience... not burnt offerings," and says Jeremiah denounced "lying scribes" for inserting sacrificial law). He says Jesus' temple action ended the sacrificial system. He then argues that modern slaughter is sacrifice in substance, since eating meat is offering animals to the appetite or "belly." Luke (a participant) claims the Arabic and Hebrew words for sacrifice and slaughter are the same; Craig extends this to say the distinction is weak. These are presented as the speakers' readings, not as settled philology.

### 6. Micah 6:8 and mercy as the criterion
Micah 6:8 ("act justly, love mercy, and walk humbly") is presented as the summary test. Craig and Natalia discuss translation variants (mercy vs faithfulness), and Craig recommends checking several translations and the Hebrew on Bible Hub or Blue Letter Bible. He argues that if mercy is the goal, the desire for meat is the thing to be "crucified" (Galatians 5:24), since Luke 6:36 ("be merciful") and Proverbs 3:31 ("do not choose any of his ways" of violence) show the same orientation.

### 7. Paul as witness, and the abuse versus killing debate
Craig reads Paul as sympathetic to abstention: Romans 14 ends with "I will never eat meat again," 1 Corinthians 8 says eating meat can cause a brother to stumble, and 1 Corinthians 10 is read as calling craving an evil. He denies that Paul is anti-vegan. Participants push further: Susan argues that killing is wrong whatever the treatment, while others, including Craig, emphasize industrial abuse. Craig agrees with Susan that a life is taken regardless of welfare, and the group reaches agreement that abuse is part of the case, not the whole.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Deuteronomy 12:20 and Numbers 11 give God's permission to eat meat.** Craig reads them as a conditional allowance tied to craving and a warning against repeating Israel's rebellion, with a plague in Numbers 11 as the result.
- **You can eat meat if you drain the blood.** Craig argues that blood remains in the tissues, citing Rudd and the catheter analogy, so flesh-eating still breaks Deuteronomy 12:23 and Genesis 9:4.
- **Paul was against vegans or favored meat-eating.** Craig reads Romans 14, 1 Corinthians 8 and 10 as Paul's own abstention and criticism of craving, not as a defense of meat.
- **Sacrifice is a thing of the past, distinct from slaughter.** Craig argues that eating meat is sacrifice to the appetite, and that the Hebrew and Arabic words overlap.
- **Vegans cherry-pick verses.** Craig and Natalia claim the opposing side cherry-picks, and that the group reads passages in context.
- **Free-range or humane meat is acceptable.** Susan and Craig agree that killing a sentient being is wrong regardless of treatment, though Craig also stresses industrial abuse.
