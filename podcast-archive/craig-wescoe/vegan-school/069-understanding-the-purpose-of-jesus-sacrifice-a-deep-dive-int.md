## Summary

The talk asks a follow-up question to the claim that God never wanted animal sacrifice: if so, what was the point of Jesus' death? The speakers cite Jeremiah, Hosea, Amos, Isaiah and Micah for the view that God did not desire animal sacrifice, and they treat Hebrews 9:22 ("without the shedding of blood, there's no remission") as the traditional problem the question has to answer.

The answering speaker's main reply is that "it depends on which audience you're talking to." He argues that Hebrews was written to a Torah-observant audience that believed only animal blood could atone, and that for them Jesus is the once-for-all sacrifice and a high priest "of the Melchizedek order," superseding the Levitical priesthood. For a wider audience, he says, Jesus' death ends a sacrificial system God never wanted, restores true worship, and is shown to be effective by the resurrection. He supports this with Jesus overturning the temple tables, John 1:29, the Genesis 22 "God will provide" passage, and John 10:18. He also argues against a "bloodthirsty Father" reading, citing "I and my father are one."

The talk then turns to Cain and Abel, which the speaker reads as a non-sacrificial offering, and to the origin of sacrifice, which he places in Genesis 6 as expanded by the Book of Enoch. The talk ends with a discussion of Augustine's reasons for leaving Enoch out of the canon, which the speaker disputes, citing Jude and 1 and 2 Peter. The conversation closes with "we're satisfied so far" and the promise of more sessions. Throughout, the speakers are offering positions and open questions, and they say so; the Cain and Abel reading is explicitly offered as "more alternatives," not as the text's clear meaning.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Hebrews is written to a specific audience that still believed in blood atonement
The speaker reads Hebrews 9:22 as stating the accepted belief of its readers, and Hebrews 10:4 (blood of bulls and goats cannot do it) as the author pushing back on that belief. Jesus is presented as the "once-for-all" sacrifice and as high priest forever, "not the high priest of the Levitical order" but of the Melchizedek order, prophesied in the Psalms. The speaker adds that Levi was "in Abraham's loins" when Abraham tithed to Melchizedek, so the Levitical priesthood is subordinate to the earlier one. The speaker presents this as a reading of Hebrews' own argument aimed at its audience, not as a universal statement.

### 2. Jesus ends a sacrificial system God never desired
The speaker cites Jeremiah 7:22 ("I did not command you to do these animal sacrifices") and says Jesus, by overturning the temple tables and calling the place a "den of violence," is quoting Jeremiah 7. The caption wording here is garbled and should be checked in the primary text. In this reading, Jesus is "putting an end" to the system and restoring worship "the way it's supposed to be from the beginning." The speaker also says that Israel and the pagan world both adopted animal sacrifice, and that the Babylonian-period criticism of Judaism reflects this. He links this to a claim about Jeremiah and Hilkiah finding an earlier Torah scroll, which he himself calls "maybe neither here nor there."

### 3. Rejecting a "bloodthirsty Father" picture
The speaker says the Father is not one who needs his Son's bloodshed, and that "Jesus says, I and my father are one. They're not opposite." He describes the cross as a way "to be that lifeline for everybody," including those who held the older view of atonement. He also suggests that God "accommodated" the belief that blood was needed by offering a final sacrifice himself.

### 4. Genesis 22 and the "lamb" who will be provided
The speaker notes that Isaac asks "where's the lamb," not "ram," and that Abraham answers "the Lord will provide." He reads the ram in the thicket as not the lamb, and reads John 1:29 ("Behold the lamb of God"), together with John 3:16, as the fulfillment. He also links Ishmael's objection to "only son" as a point he sets aside. The speaker's account is that God, having stopped Abraham, says he will do the sacrifice himself.

### 5. Willing self-offering and the resurrection as proof
Citing John 10:18 ("Nobody can take my life. I lay it down of my own accord"), the speaker argues Jesus' death was voluntary, unlike animal sacrifice, which is "done by force." He argues that the resurrection shows Jesus' power over death, and that "you can't resurrect unless you die," so the death was needed for the resurrection to demonstrate that power. He pairs this with John 11:25 and John 14:6 for the "way" to God.

### 6. Cain and Abel: a non-sacrificial reading
The speaker reads Genesis 1:28-30 (fruitfulness, dominion, plant-based food for humans and animals) and Genesis 2:15 (tending the garden) as God's instructions. He suggests Cain brought "the fruit of the ground" and Abel brought the first-born of the flock with its milk, using a point about how Hebrew was written without vowels so that the word could be read as "fat" or "milk." He says Josephus describes Abel bringing milk, and argues that the offering was a showing of obedience and care, not a slaughter. He is explicit that this is "not saying this is exactly what the Bible says," and offers it as an alternative. He also admits it could be "baby dedication," which still sounds like sacrifice.

### 7. The origin of sacrifice in Genesis 6, as read through Enoch
The speaker says Genesis 6 describes the sons of God, the Nephilim, and a world filled with "corruption and violence." He argues that the Book of Enoch expands this: giants consumed labor, vegetation, human flesh and animal blood, and their disembodied spirits possessed humans to receive sacrifices. He takes Jesus' "days of Noah... eating and drinking" (New Testament, chapter not given) as confirmation that eating involved violence, and says the "marrying" refers to the sons of God and daughters of men. He treats Jude's quotation of Enoch and references in 1 and 2 Peter as supporting Enoch's authority. He says he has written a study edition of Enoch.

### 8. Augustine's canon decision and the "measuring stick"
The speaker summarizes what he presents as Augustine's reasoning at the end of the fourth century: Jude's testimony, uncertainty about parts of Enoch, and the decision to follow the Hebrew canon's exclusion of it. The speaker answers that the New Testament is also outside the Hebrew canon, and that Jesus, Jude, Peter and the Gospels of Mark and Matthew treat Enoch as authoritative. He concludes that using the canon "as a measuring stick," Enoch measures up better than any other extra-biblical text.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **God wanted animal sacrifice.** The speaker argues that Jeremiah 7:22 and the prophets show God did not desire it, and that Jesus' temple action confirms this.
- **God needs his Son's blood to forgive.** The speaker rejects the "bloodthirsty Father" picture and says "I and my father are one."
- **Hebrews is a universal statement that blood is required.** The speaker reads it as addressed to a specific audience that held that belief, and as arguing against it.
- **Cain and Abel shows God approving a slaughtered animal.** The speaker argues Abel's offering was the first-born of the flock with its milk, an act of obedience, though he admits this is an alternative reading.
- **The Book of Enoch is outside all authority.** The speaker argues that Jude, Peter, Mark and Matthew treat it as authoritative and that Augustine's exclusion reasoning does not hold.
