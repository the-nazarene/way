## Summary

This is Class #20 of the Vegan Christian Discipleship School, a Zoom session led by Craig Wescoe, with class members reading the study guide's paragraphs aloud. The question is whether Jesus ate lamb at Passover, a claim often used to justify eating meat. Wescoe's short answer, given at 01:54, is that "there is no direct evidence anywhere in scripture of Jesus ever eating lamb," and that the combined evidence "overwhelmingly suggests he did not eat lamb at Passover."

Wescoe first lays out the traditionalist case: Exodus 12 makes the Passover lamb a mitzvah, Luke 2:41-42 and Mark 14:12 show Jesus keeping the feast, and a sinless Jesus must therefore have eaten lamb. He then sets out the counter-evidence. He reads Jeremiah 7:22-23 as denying that God commanded sacrifices at the Exodus, and links this to a "lost" Torah scroll found under Josiah (2 Kings 22:8). He argues that Jesus's temple action, which quotes Jeremiah 7:11, targets the Sadducean slaughter system, and that the Last Supper uses bread, not lamb, for Jesus's body. Finally he argues that the Essenes, whom Jesus never criticizes, rejected animal sacrifice and flesh-eating, and that Jesus, his family and his disciples are best understood as close to them.

The session ends with a closing prayer. Wescoe acknowledges that the Essene identification is contested among scholars and that archaeology is disputed. Several participants say they now feel equipped to discuss the topic, and one says she is "closer than I've ever been" to thinking Jesus was an Essene. Wescoe also says the vegan conclusion holds even if Jesus did eat lamb, since he would have ended the ritual.

## Key Teachings

### The traditionalist case, stated fairly
Wescoe presents the opposing view on its own terms so it can be checked. He cites Exodus 12:6 and 12:14, which describe the lamb sacrificed and eaten each year as a lasting ordinance. Luke 2:41-42 has Jesus's parents going to Jerusalem for Passover, and Mark 14:12 has the disciples asking where to prepare the Passover. A participant notes that in Jewish law such commands count as mitzvot, not optional custom, so omitting the lamb would be breaking the law, and Wescoe agrees this is the usual reasoning. He also notes that Mark calls it a "custom," which he says might suggest it was not a mitzvah.

### Jeremiah and the "lying pen of the scribes"
Wescoe reads Jeremiah 7:22-23: "For I did not speak to you fathers or command them in the day that I brought them out of the land of Egypt concerning burnt offerings or sacrifices." He reports that the high priest Hilkiah found a Torah scroll in the temple during Josiah's reign (2 Kings 22:8), and that Jeremiah then wrote against the sacrificial system and accused the scribes of producing "a deception." Wescoe reads the Torah history as the Elohist text being combined with the Yahwist text by Ezra, and says Jeremiah's scroll lacked the sacrifice material. A participant (Jonathan) reports that Jeremiah 33:14-26, a passage affirming an eternal priesthood and sacrifices, is absent from the Septuagint and the Dead Sea Scrolls, which Wescoe takes as evidence the sacrificial passages were later additions. He also says Psalm passages show similar additions.

### The temple action and the "den of violence"
Wescoe argues that the temple disruption in all four gospels targets the sacrificial killing itself, not just the money-changers. He notes that Luke does not mention money-changers at all. He cites Matthew 21:12-13 and says the "den" quotation comes from Jeremiah 7:11, the chapter that denies God commanded sacrifice. He also points out that Jesus quotes Jeremiah rather than Proverbs about dishonest scales, which he takes as a signal that the issue is violence against animals. He makes a translation argument: the Hebrew *parets* (Strong's number given as 6530) is translated "violence" in three of six uses, but English versions render Jeremiah 7:11 as "robbers" or "thieves." Wescoe argues that the translators obscure the bloodshed sense. A participant also refers to the 2014 film *Christ Killer* (as transcribed) and its claim that Jesus freed animals in the temple.

### The Last Supper: bread instead of lamb
Wescoe says the Last Supper accounts mention bread and wine but never lamb. Citing Matthew 26:26, he argues Jesus identified his own broken body with the Passover, rather than a lamb on the table. He adds that Paul (1 Corinthians 5:7), Peter (1 Peter 1:19) and John the Baptist (John 1:29) all call Jesus the lamb, so a physical lamb at the meal would have created "two lambs." A participant raises a counterargument that even if a lamb was present, Jesus was inaugurating a Melchizedek order (Genesis 14:18; Hebrews) that replaced the Levitical sacrifice. Wescoe says he is sympathetic to this reading, that it still leads to the same ethical conclusion, and that he does not regard it as the strongest argument.

### The Essenes: three groups and the silence about them
Wescoe describes three Jewish groups in Jesus's time: the Pharisees (scribes and synagogue teachers), the Sadducees (temple priests running the sacrifices), and the Essenes (communal, monastic). He argues Jesus harshly criticizes the Pharisees and Sadducees (Matthew 16:12; Matthew 5:20) but never criticizes the Essenes. He concludes that, of the three, Jesus aligns with the Essenes, citing Acts 4:32 on shared possessions, swearing oaths, and anger as parallels to Essene practice. Wescoe presents a comparison appendix, drawing on Philo, Josephus and Hippolytus, that he says shows "almost word for word" similarities. Wescoe also argues that Essene primacy is shown by Pliny the Elder, Josephus and Hippolytus, all of them outsiders, with Pliny treated as a hostile witness whose admission carries weight.

### Flesh-avoidance among the Essenes, the family and the disciples
Wescoe cites several sources for the claim that the Essenes rejected animal sacrifice and flesh-eating: Eusebius (transcribed as "Phoenius"), Philo of Alexandria, Josephus and Jerome. He then argues that Jesus's family and immediate circle also abstained. He cites the Proto-Evangelium of James on Mary's upbringing, a source on James the brother of Jesus who "drank no wine... nor did he eat flesh," and a source on "Judas Thomas," as well as a fourth-century claim that all the disciples abstained. He links abstention from wine to the Nazarite vow (Judges 14, Daniel 1, Proverbs), while noting it is a larger topic he has treated elsewhere.

### Psalms and later additions
Wescoe reads Psalm 51:16-17 and Psalm 40:6, which he says contain "you do not desire sacrifice" but then, in Psalm 51:18-19, a line about rebuilding Jerusalem's walls and offering sacrifices. He argues this is an anachronism pointing to the Babylonian-period editing associated with Ezra and Nehemiah. He says the earlier sources "do seem to suggest" David belonged to the same movement as Jeremiah.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus ate lamb at Passover, so Christians may eat lamb.** Wescoe says the gospels never mention lamb at the Last Supper or any Passover meal Jesus eats, so the claim goes beyond the text.
- **The temple action was only about money-changers' dishonesty.** Wescoe says all four gospels record the event, Luke omits money-changers, and Jesus quotes Jeremiah 7:11 about violence rather than the Proverbs about dishonest scales.
- **The Essenes were an obscure desert offshoot.** Wescoe argues the earliest outside sources describe them as the most ancient group, and that Jesus never criticizes them.
- **The Essene Gospel of Peace is an ancient source for the Essenes.** Wescoe rejects it as lacking any ancient manuscript, citing the claim that it was found in the Vatican and the translator's inconsistent accounts, and says he relies only on historical sources.
- **Archaeology (animal bones at Qumran) disproves Essene vegetarianism.** A participant says the archaeological debate is ongoing and that one smaller site appears entirely vegetarian; Wescoe agrees the evidence is hard to reconstruct.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** The talk cites 1 Corinthians 5:7 as an early identification of Jesus as the Passover lamb, but does not contrast Paul's teaching with Jesus's own. Paul appears only as a witness to the lamb symbolism.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Wescoe cites a source saying James never ate flesh or drank wine, and links the Jerusalem community in Acts 4:32 to Essene-style common ownership.
- **Essenes and Nazarenes:** Wescoe treats Essene and Nazarene as closely related terms, possibly overlapping, and cites the Nazirite vow as a link. He acknowledges that the two may be identical or overlapping.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet and nonviolence:** The central argument is that the Elohist and Yahwist Torah sources differ on sacrifice, and that Jeremiah 7 and the temple action oppose the animal-slaughter system.
- **Gospels and canon:** Wescoe argues that Ezra combined the Torah sources and that sacrificial passages in Jeremiah and the Psalms were later additions, supported by Septuagint and Dead Sea Scrolls variants.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Wescoe argues Jesus identified his own body with the lamb and proposed a new ritual, and in the counterargument, a Melchizedek priesthood.
