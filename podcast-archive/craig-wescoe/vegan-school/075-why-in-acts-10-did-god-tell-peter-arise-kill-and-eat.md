## Summary

The class asks why, in Acts 10, God tells Peter to "arise, kill and eat," and whether the vision approves eating animals that the Torah calls unclean. Craig Wescoe, the teacher, argues that the text states its own purpose: the vision shows that salvation is for Gentiles as well as Jews. He points to Peter's explanation to Cornelius (Acts 10:28, 34-35) and to the Holy Spirit falling on the Gentile household (Acts 10:44-46). He adds that no passage in Acts or elsewhere shows Peter or another disciple eating unclean animals or teaching that it was permitted, so the "two purposes" reading has no textual support. The supporting arguments include the "impure or unclean" wording, a reading of the Clementine homilies, and a claim about how the early church handled table fellowship with non-vegetarian candidates.

Wescoe then develops a second line of argument. He describes God as a teacher who sometimes gives instructions that contradict his own will in order to test the heart, citing Genesis 22 and Jeremiah 17:10 and using the refining-fire image. On this reading Peter "passed the test" because he did not kill, and Cornelius's household was saved. The session closes with questions about self-examination, repentance, and the prayer "your kingdom come." Several participants add personal experience of eating with non-vegans, and the group moves on to the wider ethics of compassion toward animals. Throughout, the positions are Wescoe's and the class's; the talk does not settle the historical questions about the early church, and Wescoe presents several of them as "reasonable evidence" rather than proof.

## Key Teachings

### The vision's stated purpose is the inclusion of Gentiles
Wescoe's central claim is that the text explains itself. Peter says to Cornelius that God "has shown me that I should not call anyone impure or unclean" (Acts 10:28), and Peter then declares that God "welcomes those from every nation who fear him" (Acts 10:34-35). Wescoe reads the outcome as confirmation: the Holy Spirit falls on the Gentiles (Acts 10:44-46). He also cites Paul in Romans 3:29 ("Is God the God of Jews only?") as an echo of the same understanding.

### Against the "two purposes" objection
Wescoe answers the claim that the vision also permitted eating unclean animals by pointing to the absence of evidence. If the apostles had begun eating such animals, that would show up in the text, and it does not. He concedes that the text supports the salvation reading "strongly" and the second purpose "not at all." He also notes that Mark 7 is often cited as making all foods clean, but argues that Peter's puzzlement in Acts 10 is hard to square with that reading.

### The language of "impure" and "unclean" may be broader than Levitical law
Wescoe notes that the Greek-derived phrase in the English "impure or unclean" might cover more than the Levitical categories. He cites the Clementine homilies, where Peter is said to eat only "pot herbs and bread," and suggests that the vision may be about animal flesh in general. He is careful to add that the group should not assume this, and he flags that his own paragraphs assume the clean/unclean reading "for the sake of argument."

### God tests the heart, and Peter passed
Wescoe reads the vision as a test with an instruction contrary to God's stated will (Deuteronomy 14:3 is cited as the contrary instruction in the study guide). He compares it to Genesis 22, where God tells Abraham to sacrifice Isaac and then stops him, and to Jeremiah 17:10 ("I the Lord search the heart"). He uses the refining image of a goldsmith removing dross to describe how God shapes character. On his reading, Peter's refusal and his puzzling-out are the passing response, and God's lack of displeasure is evidence of that.

### Table fellowship and the Acts 15 decree
This was the most extended argument and the one Wescoe hedged most. He reports from the Clementine homilies and recognitions that candidates for conversion ate separately from baptized members, who were bound by the Jerusalem council's rules in Acts 15. He says there is "pretty reasonable evidence" for this practice, and he cites Paul's letters on Peter eating with Gentiles as support. He also cites an essenic practice of not eating with outsiders, which he says is "documented" in a previous study, as further evidence that Jesus's circle was Essene-like. Wescoe repeatedly says this is a matter of conscience and that he is not requiring the practice. He distinguishes sharing a table physically from participating in the food itself, which he ties to the "table of devils" language of Acts 15 and to Proverbs 2.

### Kingdom, repentance, and the narrow path
In the closing sections Wescoe argues that the vision's point is to call people to God's "very good" will from creation onward, and that the call is repentance. He quotes Matthew 4:17 ("Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand") and the narrow-path saying, and he frames the "superior path" as the one God laid out, not the speaker's own superiority. He opposes the reading that the vision moves people away from the kingdom, asking, "Is that how you view Jesus? No."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That Acts 10 shows God approved eating unclean animals.** Wescoe says the text states its own purpose (Gentile inclusion), and that nothing in Acts or elsewhere shows the apostles eating unclean animals or teaching it.
- **That Mark 7 made all foods clean before Acts 10 happened.** Wescoe argues that if this were so, Peter's puzzlement in Acts 10 would make no sense.
- **That the vision is necessarily a voice from God.** A participant notes the text says "a voice" and that Peter interprets it as God's; Wescoe agrees that a vision is not the same as hearing God directly.
