## Summary

This is a group Bible study from the Vegan Christian Discipleship School, led by Craig Wescoe, on the question "How does one overcome the temptation to consume products of animal cruelty?" It opens with a round of personal experiences: several participants describe going vegan gradually, one describes cheese cravings, and one describes a long path from vegetarianism. Wescoe says he took six years to go fully vegan, and he presents the study partly for people who find the change hard. The session follows a written study with five headings, which participants read aloud in turn: living with conviction, practical steps, trusting God for strength, showing yourself grace, fleeing from temptation, praying with others, and being motivated by love. Practical steps (veganizing favorite foods, searching for recipes online) are framed as secondary to the spiritual tools.

Wescoe's central claim is that the strongest help against the temptation is motivation by love for God and his creatures, not willpower alone. He supports each step with scripture: James 4:17 on acting against known good, Romans 7:19-20 and Philippians 4:13 on relying on God's strength, Matthew 6:12 and 26:41 on forgiveness after failure, 1 Corinthians 10:13 on the "way of escape," and James 5:16 on prayer with others. For the final section he draws on 1 John, Job 12, Psalm 91, Hosea and Isaiah 11:9 to argue that animals are meant to trust humans, that violence toward them "raids" God's peaceable kingdom, and that God's will is for humans to eat the plants God designated in Genesis 1:29. The study does not present its interpretations as settled; several participants add their own experience, and the group treats the conclusions as a shared Christian conviction.

The session ends with testimonies (one participant says veganism has improved her health and relationship with God), a closing prayer for hearts to be transformed, and a hope that God's kingdom will come on earth.

## Key Teachings

### Conviction and consistency
Roslin reads the opening paragraph, which argues that once a person is convinced that kindness, goodness and mercy to animals is right, they should align their conduct with that conviction. The study cites James 4:17 for the claim that acting against a known good is sin. Wescoe frames the study as a guide to overcoming the gap between conviction and action. The argument rests on this single verse and on the assumption that animal cruelty is a moral failing that believers should recognize.

### Trusting God for strength
Nicole reads this section, which calls believers to rely on God rather than their own ability. Proverbs 3:5 is cited for trust, and Romans 7:19-20 is presented as Paul's own admission that he keeps doing what he does not want to do. Luke 18:27 ("What is impossible with man is possible with God") and Philippians 4:13 are used to argue that God supplies the strength. In discussion, a participant calls cheese cravings "withdrawal symptoms," citing "quesomorphines" (the transcript term, possibly garbled) as an addiction. Wescoe and the group treat the craving as a physical habit that should be resisted through prayer.

### Forgiving yourself without excusing a habit
Roslin reads that believers should forgive themselves when they fall short, citing Matthew 6:12 and Matthew 26:41 ("the spirit is willing but the flesh is weak"). Wescoe qualifies this: the grace is not a license. He says the believer must be "vigilant not to make a habit of falling short," and must confess the mistake and resolve not to repeat it. He adds that the animal has suffered far more than the person who ate the product, so self-mercy should not replace mercy toward animals.

### Fleeing from temptation and the "person of peace"
Ann reads this section, which uses 1 Corinthians 10:13 ("the way of escape") and Ephesians 4:27 (do not give the devil an opportunity). It also cites Philippians 3:18-19 on minds set on earthly things and Romans 12:2 on renewing the mind. The study quotes Jerome on Adam's "belly" and expulsion from paradise. The practical application includes planning ahead: bringing vegan food to events, eating beforehand, or keeping a snack in a bag. For social pressure, Wescoe argues that some people misuse Jesus's instruction to "eat whatever is put before you" and Paul's "don't ask any questions." His correction is that the instruction is to seek out the "person of peace," so one may decline animal products offered by people who are not peaceable, while accepting what a person of peace provides. He suggests explaining one's convictions gently so the host is not offended. No verse is given for the "person of peace" or "eat whatever is put before you" sayings.

### Praying with others
Roslin reads this section, which cites James 5:16 on confessing sins and praying for one another, John 14:14 ("If you ask anything in my name, I will do it"), Philippians 4:13, and 1 Thessalonians 5:11 on encouraging one another. Wescoe argues that non-vegan Christians will often pray for someone's struggle even if they do not share the conviction, and that this creates a form of witness. He links the promise in John 14:14 with the request for strength, arguing that a request for strength to avoid what is "contrary to your will" is within God's will.

### Love as the strongest motive
Ann reads the final section, which quotes 1 Corinthians 16:14 ("Do everything in love"), John 14:15 ("If you love me, keep my commandments"), and 1 Corinthians 13:13. The section also quotes a passage from author Danielle Nuenberger describing abstaining from animal products as resisting "selfish human appetites that wage violence against the peaceable kingdom." Wescoe calls love "the number one most important and most powerful" motive, supported by 1 John 4:8-16 and 1 John 4:18 (perfect love casts out fear). He describes how a dog's love for her puppies convinced him to go fully vegan, and he cites Job 12:7 ("ask the animals and they will teach you") and Psalm 91:4 (refuge under God's wings) to argue that God's love extends to all creatures.

### Kingdom, violence and the "raiding" argument
In the late section Wescoe ties the study's conclusion to Matthew 11:12 (the kingdom "has been subjected to violence and violent people have been raiding it") and Matthew 6:10 ("your kingdom come, your will be done on earth as it is in heaven"). He reasons that heaven has no killing, so earth should have none either. He describes a "narrow path" of peace versus a "wide path" of violence. He also cites Genesis 1:29 and Genesis 9 for God's dietary instruction and for God's promise to animals, Hosea 2:18 (the "lie down in safety" promise, which Wescoe reads as "Hosea 21:18" in the caption), and Isaiah 11:9 ("They shall not hurt or destroy you, nor my holy mountain"). He argues that animals were designed to be friendly toward humans and that fear of humans is a consequence of human violence, citing Habakkuk 2:17 (the transcript says "218 or 217") and 1 John 4:18.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Going vegan must happen overnight, or the person is a failure.** Several participants and Wescoe describe gradual transitions (vegetarian first, then vegan; six years in Wescoe's case). Wescoe's view is that people should "not give up" and that the process can take "however long it takes," while still encouraging a quick change where possible.
- **Scripture requires Christians to eat whatever is put before them.** Wescoe argues that Jesus's and Paul's teachings on eating are misused, and that the instruction is to accept food from a "person of peace," not from anyone. He says believers may decline animal products without an unloving tone.
- **Vegan food is hard to find or expensive.** Participants reply that vegan versions of most dishes exist online, that chili made with rice and beans is cheap, and that "animal products are actually the most expensive part of chili."
