The session works through a written study guide of seven paragraphs, with participants reading aloud and Craig adding commentary. Craig carries most of the argument. He presents a "complete gospel" that includes the restoration of creation, and he calls resurrection and forgiveness an incomplete account of the good news when they are preached alone.

The Hebrew word study, the Eden diet argument, and the claim that repentance stops end-times disasters in Revelation are Craig's readings, not settled scholarship.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The Hebrew word *basar* as "good news" from a messenger to a king
Roslyn reads the study guide's paragraph: *basar* means good news, linked to a runner who brings news to a king (2 Samuel 18:19). The "beautiful feet" of the messenger come from Isaiah 52:7, which Paul quotes in Romans 10:15. Isaiah 61:1 then changes the audience: the good news is for "the poor" and for the captives. Craig draws out the point that Jesus reads this passage in Luke 4:21 and says it is fulfilled "in your hearing." The gospel of peace, in this reading, is good news for those who are captive and poor, including animals "that live confined." The talk gives no Hebrew lexicon or source for the meaning of *basar*, so readers should check that claim in a lexicon before relying on it.

### 2. The Greek *euangelion* and *euangelizo* as reconciliation of all creation
Craig separates the verb (*euangelizo*, "to evangelize") from the noun (*euangelion*, "the message"). The study guide cites Acts 10:36 ("the good news of peace through Jesus Christ") and 2 Corinthians 5:18-19 on the "ministry of reconciliation." Craig argues that reconciliation runs back to the severance in Eden, so the gospel must restore what was broken there. He then cites Matthew 28 ("the whole world") and Mark 16:15 ("every creature") to argue that the scope of the good news is all creation. He asks participants whether limiting salvation to humans "is selling God short" and diminishes "his lordship of the whole creation."

### 3. The Fall as the start of the gospel story
Susan reads the study guide's account of Adam and Eve trusting the serpent's lie (Genesis 3:4), citing Romans 5:14 that sin and death entered through one man. Craig develops this with 1 John 3:8 ("to destroy the works of the devil") and John 14:6. He argues that Jesus "exposes" the serpent's lie that sin does not lead to death, so the Fall and redemption are one story. The study guide quotes theologian Andrew Linzey: the story of the Fall is "inextricably related to the story of redemption." Craig also says that if there were no death before the Fall, there was no killing before it, which supports his reading of the Eden diet.

### 4. Genesis 1:29-30 and the Eden plant diet
Participants and Craig read Genesis 1:29-30 (seed-bearing plants and fruit as food for humans, and "every green plant" for beasts and birds) and Genesis 2:15 (keeping the garden) as evidence of an original, non-violent design. Craig reasons that since death entered at the Fall, the pre-Fall diet was plant-based, and that the restored relationship with animals would look like Isaiah 11. One participant raises the 80/10/10 diet and the ratio of fruit to vegetables; the group does not assess the health claims. Craig offers an ecological argument that eating fruit and cutting herb flowers "aids" plants and does not harm them. That argument is his own, with no source given in the session.

### 5. Animals' souls, heaven, and the Noah covenant
Responding to a participant whose church friend said animals "don't have a soul," Craig says the Hebrew *nephesh chayah* is used of animals in dozens of passages and of humans with the same term. He argues that God's promises to animals (Hosea 2:18, Isaiah 11, Genesis 9) would be pointless unless they were fulfilled after death, so animals will be in heaven. On Noah, Craig says God makes a covenant with the animals explicitly, mentioned "five times," and the group discusses how people fixate on Noah, his family and "mankind," overlooking the animals. Craig says he has taught this at an Adventist Sabbath school and that people "read it but their eyes are not open to it."

### 6. The kingdom "on earth as in heaven," resurrection, and the good and faithful servant
Luke reads the study guide's paragraph linking Daniel 2:44 (an everlasting kingdom that crushes earlier kingdoms), Matthew 6:10 (the Lord's Prayer), John 5:28-29 (resurrection of life or judgment), Romans 6:23, John 3:16 and Mark 1:15. Craig adds that Daniel 1 shows vegetables and water producing better health, and he asks whether we want to be "cast out" or hear "good and faithful servant" (Luke 12:42-43, read by Anne). He argues that "faithful" means doing what God called "very good" at creation, which he equates with stewardship of animals and a non-harmful diet. He also argues against passive waiting: Jesus wants his followers "actively doing the things that he commanded us to do."

### 7. Repentance, Revelation, and end-times fear
In the paragraph on "a return to the beginning," Jonathan argues that the gospel is "framed entirely" as resurrection and eternal life and omits the reversal of the original sin. Craig reads Romans 8:19 ("the creation waits") and cites Jeffrey Rudd on Isaiah's vision as "not a far-fetched vision." On Revelation, Craig says that between the disasters the text repeats "they did not repent," and that the disasters stop when people repent, based on Mark 1:15 and 2 Chronicles 7:14. He cites Jonah and Nineveh as a model: repentance can change the outcome. Craig says this reading comes from his 2013 blog post "Greater than Jonah." He also argues that small committed minorities (he suggests around 3%) have historically changed culture, and he cites the women's suffrage and anti-slavery movements as examples.

### 8. Judgment, condemnation, and the test of fruit
Responding to a participant who worries about judging others, Craig separates condemnation (wishing punishment, as in "burn in hell") from righteous admonition, which "instructs" people and corrects them. He reads "Do not judge that you not be judged" (Matthew 7) as a warning against condemnation, not against telling people how to live, since the Sermon on the Mount itself sets out how to live. He also cites Romans 8:1 (no condemnation in Christ), Jesus's test that people are known by their fruit, and the Epistle of John on "those who make a practice of sinning." Isaiah 66:3-4 and Hosea 6:6 (quoted by Jesus: "I desire mercy, not sacrifice") are used to argue against animal sacrifice.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Creation care is "not a salvation issue" and secondary to preaching the gospel.** Craig argues the gospel is good news for all creation, citing Mark 16:15 and the reconciliation texts, so limiting it to humans shrinks the message.
- **Animals have no soul and will not be in heaven.** Craig says the Hebrew *nephesh chayah* is used of animals and that God's promises to them (Hosea 2:18, Isaiah 11, Genesis 9) only make sense if they are fulfilled after death.
- **God gave humans permission to eat meat after the Flood, so the Noah covenant settles the question.** The participants argue that the covenant explicitly includes the animals, "five times," so the passage does not settle the matter in favor of meat-eating.
- **Veganism is demonic (1 Timothy 4).** A participant reports this claim; the group treats it as disinformation that the truth of Jesus exposes, but Craig does not directly exegete 1 Timothy 4.
- **The earth will be burned up, so environmental stewardship does not matter.** A participant suggests the "elements burned with fire" passage may be symbolic, and that the promise of inheriting the earth would not be compelling if everything were destroyed.
- **Jesus's "do not judge" forbids any telling people how to live.** Craig separates condemnation from admonition, saying the Sermon on the Mount itself instructs how to live.
- **The end times are inevitable and cannot be changed.** Craig reads Revelation as showing that repentance stops the disasters, and that Jonah's Nineveh shows God can relent.
