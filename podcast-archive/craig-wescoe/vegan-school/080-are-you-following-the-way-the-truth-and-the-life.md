## Summary

The talk asks what it means that Jesus is "the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6), and whether following him requires a particular way of living, including how one treats animals. The unnamed host argues that "the way" is a narrow path of peace and nonviolence, that "the truth" means hearing Jesus' voice and acting in love "in deed and in truth" (1 John 3:18), and that "the life" means giving life rather than stealing, killing, or destroying. His main evidence is a chain of proof texts: Proverbs 3 ("her ways are ways of peace"), Matthew 7:13-14 (the narrow gate), Acts 9:2 and Acts 24:14 (the earliest believers called "followers of the Way"), Proverbs 12 on the life of animals, Romans 14:17 (the kingdom is not food and drink), and Hosea's "I desire mercy, not sacrifice." He insists that the "original teachings of Jesus," not later creeds or traditions, define discipleship, and he reads the Good Samaritan parable (Luke 10:25-37) as a call to show mercy to animals.

The talk ends as it began: a call to repent, with the parable of the house built on sand (Matthew 7:24-27) set against the faithful servant who is welcomed into the kingdom. The host presents his conclusions as the plain reading of scripture, but several of his bridging claims are asserted without verses or sources. These include the temple action read as liberating animals, the claim that Acts was written to an "Archbishop of Antioch, Theophilus," and the claim that the first believers were called Nazarenes and Ebionites. ## Key Teachings

### 1. "The way" is a narrow path of peace, and violence is its opposite
The host reads Proverbs 3:13-18 ("all her paths are peace," "she is a tree of life") and Proverbs 3:31 ("do not envy the oppressor... choose none of his ways") as establishing that the "way" of God is the way of peace and that its opposite is violence. He extends this from human relations to animals, asking whether food and clothing choices cause violence. He links this to Matthew 7:13-14, where the narrow gate leads to life, and to Matthew 11:12 ("the kingdom of heaven suffers violence"), which he reads as a warning against taking the kingdom "by violence."

### 2. The earliest followers were called "followers of the Way," so discipleship means following Jesus' original teaching
The host says the first believers, before being called Christians in Antioch (Acts 11, as he reads it), were known as Nazarenes, Ebionites, and above all "followers of the Way." He cites Acts 9:2 (Saul seeking "any who were of the way") and Acts 24:14, and says James "was in charge of the church" at the beginning. His argument is that to be a follower of the Way one must follow Jesus' original teachings "and not what maybe other people later on say about him." He says the labels Protestant, Catholic, and Orthodox "came centuries and centuries later." The talk does not analyze Paul's theology; it uses Saul's pre-conversion persecution only to establish the name.

### 3. "The truth" means hearing Jesus' voice and acting on it, including the liberation of animals
Drawing on John 18:37 ("everyone who is of the truth hears my voice"), John 8:32 ("the truth shall set you free"), John 16:13 (the Spirit of truth), and John 4:23-24 (worship "in spirit and truth"), the host argues that truth is practical and that those who are "of the truth" will free animals rather than confine or kill them. He says Jesus "disrupted" the temple and "set the animals free" in John 8, though the talk does not cite a verse for the temple action. He also reads Hebrews as saying that animal blood cannot reconcile humans to God.

### 4. "The life" means giving life, and the commandments express that life
The host reads John 10:10-11 ("the thief does not come except to steal and to kill and to destroy... I am the good shepherd") against the treatment of animals, and Deuteronomy 30:19 ("choose life") alongside the narrow and broad ways of Matthew 7. He cites Proverbs 12:10 ("a righteous man regards the life of his animal") and Proverbs 12:28 ("in its pathway, there is no death") to argue that eating animals contradicts the path of righteousness.

### 5. Romans 14 is about the kingdom, not food, and mercy outweighs sacrifice
He reads Romans 14:17 ("the kingdom of God is not food and drink, but righteousness and peace and joy in the Holy Spirit") and Romans 14:15 ("do not destroy with your food the one for whom Christ died") as opposing those who quote Romans 14 to defend eating animals. He cites Proverbs 21:21 ("he who follows righteousness and mercy finds life") and Hosea's line "I desire mercy, not sacrifice," which he says Jesus quotes "multiple times" and which he takes as a call against shedding animal blood.

### 6. Eternal life comes through keeping the commandments, and the parable shows who the neighbor is
In Luke 18:18-20 the host stresses Jesus' answer, "Keep the commandments," and rejects the idea that this is "works" theology, saying instead that one must stop resisting the Spirit and bear fruit. He ties the commandments to Deuteronomy 6:5 (the Shema) and Leviticus 19:18, and to the covetousness and murder of animals. In Luke 10:25-37 he reads the priests and Levites as those who avoid mercy and the Samaritan as the model, and he argues that the "neighbor" includes animals, citing Genesis 1 (animals created before humans).

- **Eternal life is a matter of faith in the cross alone.** He argues that Jesus' answer to the rich ruler is "Keep the commandments," and that the commandments are the way to eternal life.
- **Romans 14 permits eating whatever one likes.** He says Romans 14:17 defines the kingdom as righteousness, peace, and joy, and that the passage warns against destroying others with food.
- **Animals are not our neighbors.** He argues from Genesis 1 that animals were created before humans and are part of the neighbor command.
- **Jesus endorsed animal sacrifice.** He reads Hosea's "mercy, not sacrifice" as Jesus' rejection of shedding animal blood, and reads Hebrews as denying that animal blood reconciles.
- **Later creeds and traditions define Christianity.** He says the first followers were "followers of the Way" and that Protestant, Catholic, and Orthodox identities came centuries later.
