## Summary

The class asks what the Torah's distinction between clean and unclean animals was for, given that Genesis 1:29 gives a plant diet and that a common objection to veganism reads Leviticus 11 as permission to eat the clean animals. Craig Wescoe works through a 13-page study in three steps: first the common view and two historical answers (Tertullian's "test and first step" and Maimonides' hygiene view), then the textual evidence (Genesis, Exodus, Numbers, and the Noah passages), then a pair of competing origin theories. He concludes that the Bible gives no explicit reason for the distinction, and that the most consistent reading of the early texts treats "tahor" as "pure" in the sense of being without violence.

Wescoe's own preferred explanation is a "two Torahs" theory: an earlier Elohist layer that never mentions clean and unclean, and a later Yahwist layer, woven together by Ezra, that adds the clean/unclean list and a sacrificial rationale. He says this view is best supported by the literary and historical evidence, while acknowledging it conflicts with a traditional view of scriptural inerrancy. He explicitly says that his audience's own view determines which argument to use, and he does not require listeners to accept the historical-critical account.

The talk ends with a "takeaway" and a closing prayer, not with a single settled answer. Wescoe argues that the permission to eat clean animals is impossible to keep because of the blood prohibition (Genesis 9:4), and that the modern animal industry makes any claim to kosher meat untenable. He invites participants to see "eat like the clean animals" as the reading that fits Exodus and Numbers. He also says that a separate study by him covers the New Testament corruptions he believes occurred, and that a further talk argues that first-century Christians were vegan. Those last claims are asserted in this session, not argued.

## Key Teachings

### The common objection and the two historical answers
The study begins by naming the objection: if God wanted no animals eaten, why would he separate clean from unclean rather than forbid all animals? Wescoe rejects that reading. He first offers Tertullian's view (the distinction was a stepping stone toward full abstinence, starting with partial abstinence and moving to "absolute fasts") and Maimonides' view (the distinction was hygienic, since pig flesh carried greater disease risk than fish). Wescoe says he does not hold either view, and he uses them as the common positions his audience will meet.

### Tertullian's "test and first step" and the gradual-abstinence analogy
Wescoe reads Tertullian as saying that God's laws for a stubborn people imposed food restrictions to train them toward "perpetual abstinence" that would make "absolute fasts" easier. Wescoe uses this as a model for advocacy: start people by cutting out one animal product, then another, rather than endorsing partial consumption. He cites Ezekiel 33:11 as a parallel call to turn from ways of death.

### Maimonides, hygiene, and the blood problem
Maimonides, as the study presents him, holds that unclean foods are "unwholesome" and that the distinction is a health rule, not a moral one. Wescoe notes that the view explains Isaiah 66:17 (warning against eating pig flesh and rats) but says this is not his position. He also points out that the blood restriction in Genesis 9:4 cannot be fully met even by someone eating clean animals, so the permission reading collapses under its own rule. He says even Daniel and his companions (who ate vegetables in Babylon) illustrate the fuller path of abstinence.

### Two Torahs: Elohist and Yahwist layers
Wescoe's preferred account divides the Pentateuch into an earlier Elohist (E) layer, where God (Elohim) tells Noah to bring one male and female of every animal "to keep them alive" (Genesis 6:19), and a later Yahwist (J) layer, where Yahweh orders seven pairs of clean animals and one pair of unclean (Genesis 7:2). He argues the Yahwist layer glorifies the temple sacrificial system, which Jeremiah opposed (Jeremiah 7:22, which he reads as denying that God commanded burnt offerings). He says Ezra joined the two layers and that Hilkiah's discovery of an older Torah (Kings and Chronicles) is part of the background. He concedes this view conflicts with inerrancy and advises using other arguments with traditionalists. He also argues that even in the Yahwist text Noah's altar is his own initiative (Genesis 8:21), not a divine command.

### The Noah anachronism and the origin question
Wescoe points out that the clean/unclean distinction first appears in Genesis 7:2 but is not explained until Leviticus 11. Noah is given the category without explanation, which scholars would call an anachronism. He concludes that the text does not say whether the distinction was God's invention, pre-existing human practice, or a later addition. Reasons the text never gives are speculative. He lists several as equally plausible (testing humanity, a sign of rest and completion tied to the name Noah, faster reproduction of scavenger-free species, and cuddling for warmth) and argues that opponents of veganism also assert reasons the text does not state.

### "Tahor" as pure rather than clean: Exodus, Genesis, Numbers
Wescoe argues that the word is better rendered "pure" in its earliest uses. In Exodus (25, 28, 30, 31, 37, 39) it describes pure gold for the tabernacle, and Wescoe notes that translators render it that way in nearly every English Bible. Read this way, the pure animals in Genesis 7 are those not corrupted by violence (Genesis 6:11: "all flesh has corrupted their ways"). Genesis 1:30 describes the animals as herbivores. He adds that the corruption was not the animals' fault but the angels' and then humanity's, and cites Zephaniah 1:3 and Ezekiel 34:25 for judgment on violent beasts. In Numbers (5, 9, 18, 19), he says all eight uses of the term refer to humans, which he reads as meaning "pure" in the moral sense. From this, he argues the original sense is "which animals to eat like" (the herbivores, which Job 12:7 invites us to learn from), not "which to eat."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Leviticus 11 as permission to eat clean animals.** Wescoe argues that the blood prohibition (Genesis 9:4) makes the permission impossible to fulfill, so the clean list cannot function as a clean license.
- **Clean/unclean as a purely hygienic rule (Maimonides).** He presents this as one common view and says he does not hold it. He notes that Isaiah 66:17 does not establish the hygiene reading.
- **God commanded Noah's sacrifice.** He says the Yahwist text has Noah build the altar on his own initiative (Genesis 8:21), and the Elohist text gives the animals' survival as the purpose (Genesis 6:19).
- **"Clean" means "ritually clean" in Genesis.** He argues the earliest uses (Exodus, Numbers) mean "pure," in the moral sense.
- **Kosher meat exists in the modern world.** He argues that modern animal agriculture (antibiotics, cruelty, blood in milk) makes any claim to kosher animal products untenable.
