## Summary

This is a recorded session of the Vegan Christian Discipleship School, a study group meeting in which participants read prepared paragraphs and then discuss them. The question is whether animals can give and receive love, and what the Bible says about it. The group treats the answer as a way to respond to two common objections to veganism: that animals lack reason, and that they act only from "instinct," which is taken to justify killing and exploiting them. Craig, who guides the discussion, argues that love is the Bible's "holiest attribute" (1 John 4:8), so the right question is whether animals can love, not whether they can reason.

The main evidence is a sequence of texts. Matthew 23:37 and Psalm 91:4 use a mother hen or bird's care as a picture of God's love, which the group reads as showing that the analogy only works if such animal love is real. Balaam's donkey (Numbers 22) is presented as a case of costly, Christlike love toward an abuser, since the angel says the donkey would have been spared regardless. Psalm 150:6, Isaiah 66:23 and Revelation 5:13 are read as including animals in praise. Proverbs 12:10, 2 Samuel 12:3 and Psalm 145:16 show animals being cared for by righteous people and by God. Genesis 2:15 (the Hebrew words *abad* and *shamar*) is used to define human dominion as protective service. Craig also argues that the Hebrew word for "all flesh" covers animals and that translators have added a human-only distinction. Much of the argument is supported by personal anecdotes about pets and farm animals, and the Hebrew claims are asserted in the session rather than shown from a lexicon.

The session ends with a reading from 1 John 4:16, group testimonies about how the course has equipped them, and a closing prayer. Craig ties the discussion to Satan's "steal, kill, and destroy" (John 10:10) and to the image of the peaceable kingdom (Isaiah 11), and argues that Christians who eat animals are "embodying Satan instead of God." Most of the claims in the session are offered as the group's reading, not as settled scholarship.

## Key Teachings

### Love as the holiest attribute, so cognition is not the benchmark
The study guide paragraph read at [11:09] argues that the common claim that animals are "cognitively inferior" and therefore exploitable is "contrary to scripture," since 1 John 4:8 makes love the essence of God's character. Craig adds at [10:46] that even if animals lacked human reasoning, that "shouldn't matter compared to their ability to give and receive love." The group concludes that the relevant question is whether animals can give and receive love.

### Reason vs. instinct as a double standard
At [07:35]–[08:17] a participant says that when a pig solves a puzzle or a crow uses a stick, people call it "instinct," but when humans do the same thing they call it reasoning. Craig adds that the philosophical tradition (Descartes and Kant, as the captions render them) treated animals as incapable of reasoning in a way that justified exploitation, and that no Christian would accept the same logic for a human with limited cognitive ability.

### The mother hen and God's love (Matthew 23:37; Psalm 91:4)
The study guide reads Jesus's image of gathering Jerusalem's children "as a hen gathers her chicks" as echoing Psalm 91:4. At [15:41]–[16:30] the group argues that if the mother bird's love were "mechanical instinct," then God's love would be shallow by comparison. Because God uses the analogy, the group reads the analogy as proof that animal love is genuine. A participant adds that Psalm 91:4 links the image to faithfulness, which people readily see in dogs.

### Balaam's donkey (Numbers 22; Proverbs 25:21-22; Romans 12:21)
The study guide retells how the donkey turns aside from the angel three times, is beaten each time, and then speaks (Numbers 22:28). The angel says, in the study guide's reading, that he would have killed Balaam and spared the donkey. The group argues from this that the donkey acted from love rather than self-preservation, and that the story illustrates loving one's enemy (Proverbs 25:21-22; Romans 12:21). Craig links it to Jesus's "Forgive them" on the cross. Participants also note animals' forgiveness and loyalty, including dogs at graves and elephants mourning their dead.

### Animals in worship (Psalm 150:6; Isaiah 66:23; Revelation 5:13)
The study guide reads "Let everything that has breath praise the Lord" (Psalm 150:6), Isaiah 66:23's "all flesh" coming to worship, and Revelation 5:13's "every creature" praising the Lamb as proof that animals can love God. At [42:16] Craig argues that the Hebrew word for "flesh" (captioned "kbaser") covers all creatures, including animals in the ark story, so translations limiting it to humans are "translator's bias." He says the same happens with *nephesh*, rendered "soul" for humans and "living creature" for animals.

### Animals receive love (Proverbs 12:10; 2 Samuel 12:3; Psalm 145:16)
The group reads Proverbs 12:10 ("The righteous care for the needs of their animals") and the story of Nathan's parable (2 Samuel 12:3), in which a poor man's lamb "was like a daughter to him," as showing that animals can be the object of real bonds. Craig argues at [47:19] and [49:45] that the parable only works if the bond is real, and that David's repentance depends on it. Psalm 145:16 is read as God feeding every living creature. Participants give anecdotes about cats and emotional support animals to support the point that animals can receive love.

### Stewardship and dominion (Genesis 2:15; Psalm 36:6; John 10:11; Genesis 4; Isaiah 11)
The study guide says Genesis 2:15 uses *abad* (to serve) and *shamar* (to keep, protect, care for) for humanity's role in the garden, and contrasts this with Cain's "Am I my brother's keeper?" Psalm 36:6 and John 10:11 ("the good shepherd") are read as the model of protective care. Craig says that Christians fall short of this role and should, like the good shepherd, protect animals instead of acting like the thief. Predation is attributed to the fall. A participant refers to an earlier class (which Craig offers to link) arguing that predation was not part of God's original design. Craig cites Isaiah 11 (the peaceable kingdom) as the true picture of nature and says that "leave nature alone" is not adequate responsibility.

### Language: "someone" not "it"
At [28:00]–[30:22] a participant argues that English has only "something" or "someone," so animals who are not things should be called "someone," "her" or "him." Craig chose a translation that uses "she" for the donkey for this reason, and says "there is power in language": calling animals "it" makes it easier to mistreat them. The group treats this as a practical instruction to change how Christians speak.

### Permission, Genesis 9 and Job; Satan's "steal, kill, and destroy"
Craig argues that the phrase "given into your hand" in Genesis 9 (about animals being given to humans) is used as a justification for treating animals as anything humans want. He compares it to Job 1-2, where Job is "handed over" to Satan, who is limited in what he can do. At [1:00:56] Craig argues that if humans do the opposite of God's shepherding love with animals, they are "embodying Satan instead of God" and should consider what that means for their hope of eternity. Participants then extend John 10:10 ("steal, kill, and destroy") to the animal industry, including the stealing of milk, eggs, wool and young, and the destruction of forests and health. Craig also mentions Genesis 9:16's "everlasting covenant" as including all animals and all people.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Animals lack reason, so humans may kill and eat them.** Craig argues that the premise is faulty, since love (not cognition) is the holiest attribute, and that the same logic would be unacceptable for humans with limited cognition.
- **Animal behavior is "just instinct," while human behavior is reasoned.** The group argues this is a double standard, since the same behaviors (puzzle-solving, tool use) are called reasoning in humans and instinct in animals.
- **Animals cannot love or be loved.** The group reads the mother-bird imagery, the donkey, 2 Samuel 12:3, and pet anecdotes as showing real love from and toward animals.
- **"All flesh" and "soul" in the Bible refer only to humans.** Craig argues that the Hebrew makes no such distinction and that translators have introduced one.
- **Predation is part of God's original design.** A participant argues from an earlier class that predation came after the fall, and Craig cites Isaiah 11 as the picture of God's intended peace.
- **Genesis 9 gives humans permission to do whatever they want with animals.** Craig argues that "given into your hand" is the same phrase used of Job's being handed to Satan, and that treating animals with cruelty means "embodying Satan."
- **Animals are "it," so they are not moral persons.** The group argues for "she/he/someone" language and says that speech shapes how animals are treated.
