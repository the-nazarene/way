## Summary

Craig Wescoe leads a study of Genesis 4:3-5 for a vegan Christian study group, asking why God favored Abel's gift and not Cain's. He addresses a common objection: that God's acceptance of Abel's animal offering shows God wants animal sacrifice and so rejects veganism. The talk runs through a study-guide summary, a set of paragraphs read aloud by participants, and open discussion. Craig carries most of the argument, and participants add examples and objections.

Craig's answer has three main parts. First, the prophets (1 Samuel 15:22, Isaiah 1:11, Hosea 6:6, Psalm 40:6, Jeremiah 7:22-23) and Hebrews 11:4 are read as saying God does not require or desire blood sacrifice, so bloodshed cannot be the reason for acceptance. Second, the text itself is read as lacking an altar, killing, blood, or burning. Abel's gift is then argued to have been a living firstborn lamb, possibly presented with its mother's milk, which Craig links to a claimed Hebrew confusion between *chalab* ("fat") and *chalav* ("milk"), reported through Josephus. Third, the difference between Cain and Abel is located in the quality and faithfulness of the gift (the "firstborn of the fat" against "some of the fruit"), grounded in the Genesis 1-2 design for humans as gardeners and caretakers of animals.

The talk also acknowledges that the Cain and Abel story is absent from six of seven early Adam/Eve witnesses and is found only in the J source. Craig says this could suggest a later layer, but for the study he assumes the account is historical and argues from the traditional text. The talk ends with a discussion of how to respond to environmental and carnivorous objections, a warning against "angry" vegan advocacy, and a closing summary by a participant. The final takeaway is that the offering is "whatever we bring to God," and that the living sacrifice is a life lived according to God's design (Romans 12:1-2; Micah 6:8). Craig's conclusions are presented as his reading, and he says the evidence "strongly favors" the living-lamb interpretation, not that it is proven.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The common objection and the text of Genesis 4:3-5 against Genesis 1:29

The study frames the popular argument as: God accepted Abel's animal offering and rejected Cain's fruit, so God wants bloodshed and rejects a plant-based view. Craig reads Genesis 4:3-5 alongside Genesis 1:29, where God gives "every herb that yields seed" and "every tree whose fruit yields seed" as food, and argues the diet given at creation cannot be overturned by a reading of this passage. Participants also note that the text never says Abel killed the animal, and Craig says there is no altar mentioned at all.

### 2. The prophets and the rejection of sacrifice as God's desire

Participant Roslin reads a sequence of prophetic texts: 1 Samuel 15:22 ("obedience is better than sacrifice"), Isaiah 1:11, Hosea 6:6 ("mercy, not sacrifice"), Psalm 40:6, and Jeremiah 7:22-23. Craig's argument is that if the prophets consistently say God did not command or delight in sacrifice, the reading that God accepted Abel's blood offering for that reason is "inconsistent" with the wider witness. He adds that the study's reading is that Abel's acceptance has to rest on some other reason.

### 3. Hebrews 11:4 and the inner motive of faith

Craig and participants argue that the New Testament reason given in Hebrews 11:4 is that Abel's offering was made "by faith," and that Cain's was not. Discussion distinguishes "faith" as trust in propositions from "faithfulness" as obedience in action, and Craig uses the phrase "faithfulness to your spouse" to explain. He reads Abel's faith as doing what God instructed (tending the animals, not eating forbidden fruit, not murdering). Craig also reads Genesis 4:7 ("If you do what is right, will you not be accepted?") as God's warning to Cain, which he links to Cain's failure to bring his best. A participant reports that mainstream rabbinic teaching, as the speaker understands it, also places the reason in "first fruits" or bringing one's best rather than in desire for blood.

### 4. Textual history: the Adam literature and the J source

Participant Jennifer reads a paragraph listing six early Adam/Eve witnesses (Armenian, Georgian, Latin, Greek *Apocalypse of Moses*, and Slavonic texts), none of which contains the Cain and Abel offering. Within Genesis, the story is attributed to the J (Yahwist) source and not to the E (Elohist) layer. The paragraph cites Peter's description of a later corruption and Jeremiah 8:8 ("the lying pen of the scribes") as possible witnesses to later insertion. Craig explicitly says the study will assume the account is original, but notes that if it is a later layer, the debate may be "kind of pointless." A participant says an AI tool (Gemini) confirmed the J-source attribution. The talk does not resolve the source-critical question.

### 5. The Hebrew phrase "firstborn of the fat" and the milk reading

Participants read a paragraph saying the phrase "the firstborn of the fat of his flock" appears only once in Scripture, making it hard to compare. Craig reads "fat" as "abundance" and contrasts Abel's "firstborn of the fat" with Cain's "fruit of the ground," which lacks a qualifier of quality. Luke then reads a paragraph reporting that the first-century historian Flavius Josephus wrote that Abel brought milk. The paragraph argues that *chalab* (fat) and *chalav* (milk) are written identically in the consonantal Hebrew, that vowel points came hundreds of years later, and that the lamb was therefore presented with the mother's milk (the mother's "bosom" is mentioned). Craig adds that milk would have been "stored in the mother's teeth," meaning the mother and nursing lamb would have been brought. Participants say they find this "plausible" and compare it to an Arabic cognate, but the speaker's claim about the vowel points and Josephus is reported, not checked against a source in the talk.

### 6. No altar, no blood, no burning: the living-lamb and baby-dedication readings

Nicole reads a paragraph citing author Ryan Hicks: the text mentions no altar, no killing, no draining of blood, and no burning. Craig argues the text fits a living lamb presented to God. He ties this to the Eden setting, noting God is described as walking with and speaking to the first humans (Genesis 3:8) and to Cain, so the gift would be offered to a God "physically in their presence," more like a baby dedication than a burnt offering. He links this with Exodus 13:12 (every firstborn "sanctify to me") and says the offering might be a "blessing of the animals." Other participants suggest the firstborn could be dedicated for service rather than slaughter. Craig says the text describes "the first creature that needs to be blessed," not a sacrifice.

### 7. Stewardship, dominion, and the offering as service

Building on Genesis 1:26 ("dominion"), Genesis 1:29 (food for humans and animals), and Genesis 2:15 (tilling and keeping the garden), Craig argues that Cain and Abel were carrying out the two jobs God gave humanity: tilling the ground and guarding the animals. He then reads Exodus 35:21-29 (offerings of labor and material for the tabernacle) to argue that an "offering" can be service and not only a slaughtered animal, and cites Romans 12:1-2 for the "living sacrifice" idea. Micah 6:8 closes the study. Participants discuss environmental objections (crop deaths, animal feed grown on cropland, rewilding) and Craig explains a utilitarian argument that eating animals fed on crops creates more crop-related deaths. He also suggests the messaging question of whether 50% or 10% of people going vegan would tip the culture; he says that justice movements typically need a "vocal minority of about 5 to 10%."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **God wants animal bloodshed, so he accepted Abel's animal offering.** Craig argues the prophets (1 Samuel 15:22; Isaiah 1:11; Hosea 6:6; Psalm 40:6; Jeremiah 7:22-23) reject this as God's desire, so the blood reading cannot be the reason.
- **Abel killed and burned a lamb on an altar.** Craig argues the text mentions no altar, killing, blood, or burning, and that a living animal presented to God is at least as plausible.
- **Cain was rejected for farming or for offering plants.** Craig reads Cain's rejection as tied to the quality and faithfulness of his gift ("some of the fruit" vs. "the firstborn of the fat"), not to his occupation or diet.
- **The Bible endorses slaughter as the normal form of offering.** Craig says Christians and rabbinic teaching in the talk agree that "sacrifice" in the passage concerns giving one's best, and that "sacrifice" and "slaughter" are not synonymous in the text. (Note: a participant later says "sacrifice is slaughter" in the Bible, and Craig appears to agree in the closing section; the talk is internally mixed here.)
- **Being vegan is not enough, or is a sign of self-righteousness.** A participant describes this fear; Craig responds that the answer is humility, doing one's best, and not comparing oneself to others.
