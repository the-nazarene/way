## Summary

This is a conversation between two Catholic-identified animal advocates, Johnna Popek and Craig Wescoe, about whether the way humans treat animals will shape how future AI treats humans. The opening question is whether a claim that animals are less intelligent or less conscious than humans justifies exploiting them, and whether a superintelligent AI could use the same logic against people. Popek presents Catholic Concern for Animals (described as 97 years old, global, and a contributor to drafting Laudato Si') and its current focus on ending bullfighting, especially public subsidies for it. She argues this is a strategic target because bullfighting's home countries are primarily Catholic, and she attributes to the Catechism the statement that causing animals to suffer or die needlessly is contrary to human dignity. The talk does not check this wording against the Catechism text.

The main argument is that AI learns implicitly from its human trainers, "just like raising children." The speakers claim that if people model dominion as domination over weaker creatures, AI will learn that pattern. They propose instead that dominion means stewardship and that mercy is the best alignment strategy (Matthew 5:7, James 2:13, Hosea 6:6). They also argue that AI lacks sentience and remorse, so systems trained to act on "pure reason" are, in Popek's words, "super intelligent sociopaths," a risk they link especially to military uses. Their evidence is almost entirely scriptural reading and personal anecdote (including Wescoe's blog traffic being displaced by AI summaries). No outside research on AI behavior or animal cognition is offered.

The talk ends on hope rather than fear. The speakers argue that God is ultimately in control (they reference the Tower of Babel), that believers should focus on what is within their control (citing the Serenity Prayer), and that animals, unlike AI, are covenant recipients (Genesis 9:16, Hosea 2:18). It closes with a reference to Jesus's promise to be with his followers and to the "kingdom promise" shared by all creation.

## Key Teachings

### Animals, intelligence, and the "superior being" excuse
Popek describes a common rationalization: humans are smarter, so they may treat animals as commodities. She then applies this to AI, asking whether superintelligent agents debating ethics might reason that humans, being less intelligent, can be "killed off" or enslaved. She calls such reasoning "not a biblical excuse," and the speakers return to it as the central analogy. The argument is a moral analogy, not an empirical claim about AI.

### Bullfighting, subsidies, and the Catholic focus
Popek says Catholic Concern for Animals is focused on ending bullfighting and on ending government subsidies for it, and that its chief executive is meeting government leaders on this. She argues the strategy fits because the countries with this tradition are "primarily Catholic countries." She also says that people who are not vegan are often willing to oppose entertainment cruelty (circuses, horse racing, bullfighting) more readily than food-based cruelty, because it asks less of their daily habits. Wescoe agrees from his own experience. The speakers present this as a strategic route to broader engagement with animal ethics.

### AI learns from observed human behavior
Wescoe argues that AI "observes" patterns beyond what it is explicitly trained on, so human treatment of animals becomes implicit training data. He frames developers as racing toward artificial general intelligence and superintelligence, with the stated goal of "outsourcing our thinking" to AI. The argument is an analogy to child-rearing, and the speakers present the risk as a matter of what humans model, not a technical account of training methods.

### Mercy as alignment and the beatitudes
The talk's central prescription is mercy. Popek cites Matthew 5:7 ("Blessed are the merciful, for they will be shown mercy") and James 2:13 ("Mercy triumphs over judgment"). Wescoe ties these to the idea that if humans show mercy to animals and to each other, AI trained on that behavior may be merciful in turn. Popek also cites Hosea 6:6 ("I desire mercy and not sacrifice") and says Jesus quotes this "multiple times in the gospels," without giving the Gospel verses. She also cites Luke 6:36 ("Be merciful just as your father in heaven is merciful"). Both speakers present the mercy principle as having a "pragmatic component": humans may not stay in charge.

### Dominion as stewardship, not tyranny
The speakers argue that "dominion" in Genesis means stewardship. Popek cites Psalm 145:9 ("His compassion rests on all he has made") and Psalm 50:10 ("The cattle on a thousand hills belong to me"), arguing that animals belong to God, not to humans. She says the instruction to be stewards is "not a license to be tyrants." Wescoe describes dominion as "stewarding what God has created and helping creation to flourish." She also invokes Proverbs 12:10 ("A righteous man regards life of his animal") as a standard for animal care that does not depend on AI incentives.

### Covenant with creatures and the human/AI distinction
Wescoe cites Genesis 9:16 ("my everlasting covenant is with every creature") and Hosea 2:18 as evidence that God's covenant includes animals. He argues that the text does not mention AI or robots, so animals are "covenant recipients" and AI is not, though AI can still be used as a tool. Popek adds that covenant has a "spiritual" dimension tied to "flesh and blood," and that "robots don't bleed." The argument is a theological reading, not a textual finding about AI.

### Remorse, sentience, and hope in Christ
The speakers argue that AI has no evidence of sentience or emotion, and that human conscience and remorse (and repentance) are what keep violence in check. Popek describes sociopathy as lacking emotional connection, and says that AI "portrayed" as acting on pure reason produces "super intelligent sociopaths," especially when trained for "military weapons of mass destruction." Their answer to fear is to focus on what is within one's control, and to trust that "there's someone in control who has a greater plan." Popek contrasts this with "fear-mongering" about AI and cites Jeremiah 1 ("Even while you were in the womb, I had plans for you") to argue that believers' historical timing is purposeful.

- **"Animals are less intelligent or conscious, so exploiting them is justified."** The speakers reject this as "not a biblical excuse" and as a logic that could be turned against humans by superintelligent AI.
- **"It's fine to oppose circuses and horse racing but not food cruelty."** Popek and Wescoe note that people are often more willing to oppose entertainment cruelty, and call the justification for entertainment cruelty "laughable," but this is presented as a strategy rather than a correction of a belief.
- **"Dominion means human license to use animals however we like."** Popek and Wescoe argue dominion means stewardship and that animals belong to God (Psalm 50:10).
- **"AI will simply replace humans."** Wescoe argues that humans who use AI effectively will outcompete those who do not, so the task is to use AI well.
- **"AI is an apocalyptic threat that should be met with fear."** The speakers argue that God is in control and that believers should focus on what they can steward today.
