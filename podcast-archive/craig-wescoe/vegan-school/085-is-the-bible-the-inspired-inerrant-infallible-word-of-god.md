## Summary

Craig, with guests Chris and Kathy, takes up the question in the title by splitting it into three parts: what "the Bible" refers to, what "the word of God" means, and whether the Bible is inspired, inerrant and infallible. Craig says at the outset that the group is "kind of brainstorming" and that the views are their own, not those of any group they belong to. The conversation is informal and mostly unscripted, and the three speakers often defer to one another.

On the first part, they note that "the Bible" can mean the Protestant 66-book canon, the Catholic canon with the Apocrypha, or the larger Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo canon. Craig argues that the Protestant canon was fixed late in the 4th century, under Augustine's use of Athanasius's list and the Roman imperial church, and that other books, including 1 Enoch and books cited inside the Bible itself, were then set aside. For the second part, Craig argues that the Bible often presents "the word of God" as God's speech delivered through prophets, and that the Bible as a whole is a narrative about that word. He concludes that the book is "mostly descriptive, not prescriptive," so its narrative parts are not automatically God's words. On inspiration, the speakers cite 2 Timothy 3:16, Acts-era and Luke 1:1-4 statements, and the Holy Spirit's role in the Gospels. They read Luke's prologue as a researcher's account rather than a claim of direct dictation.

On inerrancy, the strongest evidence presented is Jeremiah 8:8-9 (the "lying pen" of the scribes), the apparent conflict between Jeremiah 7:22 and the sacrificial laws of Exodus, the documentary hypothesis, and a list of contradictions and textual variants in the Gospels and Samuel/Chronicles. The talk ends with a split conclusion. Craig says the word of God is "inherent, inspired and infallible," but that the biblical text is harder to call inerrant, since the original wording is uncertain. Chris and Kathy are more open about this. The closing prayer and the remark that Jesus tied salvation to the Gospel's message rather than to inerrancy close the discussion.

## Key Teachings

### The canon was fixed by a historical process, not by the text's self-claims
Craig argues that "the Protestant Cannon was established at the end of the 4th century," attributing it to Augustine's use of Athanasius's list under the Roman imperial church (he dates the Roman Emperor's role to 325). In his account, this decision labeled other widely used books non-canonical, "varying degrees of heretical," including sources later found behind some canonical books. He notes that Chronicles cites about 75 source texts, that 1 Kings 11:41 points to "the book of the acts of Solomon," and that Enoch is called scripture in Jude (as Craig's list in the transcript presents it). Chris and Kathy agree that some extra-biblical books, especially 1 Enoch, should be studied, and Kathy says she now thinks Enoch "should be included in the Protestant Canon."

### "The word of God" is God's speech, usually through prophets, and the narrative is about it
Craig reads the Bible's phrase "the word of the Lord came to" a prophet (e.g., Jeremiah) as God speaking, with the prophet relaying the message. He contrasts this with John 1:1-3 and Genesis 1:1-3, where the Word is the means of creation. He proposes that the word of God is the "main character" of a larger narrative, while the narrative itself is not identical with that word. Kathy and Chris agree; Chris adds that the "Living Word" is Jesus. A borrowed phrase, which Craig attributes to Frank Turk, is that the Bible is "mostly descriptive not prescriptive." The speakers also raise a lighthearted test: if the Bible is the Word that "became flesh" (John 1:14), the book itself cannot literally be that Word.

### Inspiration: 2 Timothy 3:16 and the researcher model of Luke
The speakers ask what "all scripture is God breathed" (2 Timothy 3:16) can mean. Chris notes that if Paul wrote it, scripture at that time was likely the Old Testament and not the Gospels, some of which were probably not yet written. He also reports a translation option, "all God-breathed scripture," which would make only some scripture inspired. Against a dictation model, they cite Luke 1:1-4, where the author says he "carefully investigated everything" and wrote "an orderly account." Kathy adds that Kings and Chronicles also appear to be historical rather than prophetic works. The speakers do link inspiration with the Holy Spirit (Luke 3:22, John 3:5-7, John 14:26 as paraphrased), but they read it as a "real living active thing" in the believer's life and not as a guarantee about every written word.

### Inerrancy: Jeremiah 8:8-9, the sacrificial conflict and the documentary hypothesis
Craig's main evidence against inerrancy is Jeremiah 8:8-9, which says the "lying pen of the scribe has falsified the Torah." He also cites Jeremiah 7:22, which says God did not command burnt offerings when bringing Israel out of Egypt, against the sacrificial laws of Exodus. Craig suggests the prophets, Psalms, Amos and Hosea raise similar doubts about the sacrificial system. He points to the documentary hypothesis and to paired doublets (two flood accounts, two Abraham-and-Sarah stories, two versions of Joseph's capture) as "good manuscript evidence or literary evidence" for Jeremiah's claim. He labels this a theory, not a settled result.

### Contradictions and manuscript variants
Craig lists problems: the staff/no-staff instruction for the disciples (Matthew 10 vs. Luke 9, as he frames it), the instruction to meet in Galilee versus staying in Jerusalem, two different Joseph genealogies (Matthew 1 and Luke 3), God versus Satan inciting David's census (2 Samuel 24:1 and 1 Chronicles 21:1), and Paul's statements about his Jerusalem visits against Acts. On manuscripts, he lists Septuagint vs. Masoretic genealogies (with figures differing by a hundred years per generation), the Alexandrian versus Western readings of the post-resurrection meal (fish vs. fish and honeycomb), the woman caught in adultery (John), "Levi" vs. "Matthew," "Mary" vs. "Martha" in John, and the baptism voice in Luke (citing Psalms 2). Craig says these show that "the original text" is uncertain, even if the original authors were inerrant. He also notes that the Quirinius census date and Mark's attribution of a Malachi line to Isaiah are factual slips, which he suggests were a common ancient practice of naming the more authoritative source.

### Inerrant vs. infallible, and what salvation requires
Kathy, drawing on Greg Boyd, says infallible means God will "meet his mark" and not fail, which allows for error in the written word, and Chris says a former pastor drew the same line. They cite Isaiah 55:10-11 (God's word "will not return to me empty"). Craig concludes that the living Word is "inherent, inspired, and infallible," but that the Bible's text is harder to call inerrant. Chris concludes that inerrancy is a "man-made Doctrine" and argues that Jesus "never made that a Salvation issue," pointing out that any of the four Gospels could lead a reader to Jesus as Messiah.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Bible means the 66-book Protestant canon.** The speakers say "the Bible" can refer to several canons (Hebrew Bible, Protestant, Catholic, Ethiopian Orthodox), and that the Protestant one was fixed late and by a historical process.
- **Every word of the Bible is the word of God.** Craig argues the Bible is mostly descriptive, so narrative content is not automatically God's speech.
- **The Bible was dictated word for word to its writers.** Using Luke 1:1-4 and the Kings/Chronicles sources, the speakers say at least some authors worked as researchers.
- **The Bible is inerrant.** The speakers cite contradictions, variant manuscripts and Jeremiah 8:8-9 to challenge this, and Craig says the original text is uncertain.
- **Believing the Bible is inerrant is required for salvation.** Chris argues Jesus never made this a salvation issue.
