Craig Wescoe, with Kathy asking questions, gives a fast overview of church history from the first century to the present, saying he will spend more time on first and second century Christianity in later sessions. The talk's central claim is that early Christianity was not one unified movement. Wescoe argues that by the middle to late second century there were several distinct groups, including Marcionites, Valentinians, Ebionites, Sethians and an emerging "Proto-Orthodoxy," and that the Jewish-Christian split was gradual, not the result of a single doctrinal break. ## Key Teachings

### 1. Early Christianity was largely Jewish, and the split was gradual
Wescoe says that through the first century Christianity was "primarily a Jewish thing," with Gentile believers present but a minority, and that the Jerusalem church was often seen as a sect of Judaism. He ties the Jewish revolts of 70 AD and the Bar Kokhba revolt to both histories. He mentions a practice of pronouncing a final blessing that functioned as a curse on believers, which he says is preserved mainly in Jewish sources, as one possible factor in the split (the transcript does not name the blessing). He argues the separation was driven partly by political pressures, not only by disagreement over whether Jesus was the Messiah, and that the two groups stayed in fellowship for centuries. Wescoe says the Ebionites identified as Jewish, while Marcionites and later groups identified as non-Jewish, and that the break "had to occur sometime" between the second century and the fourth, without a clear date.

### 2. Second-century diversity and the labels used for it
Wescoe describes several second-century groups, each linked to key figures: Marcionites (Marcion of Pontus, whom he calls the Marcionites' founder, using Paul's letters and an early form of Luke without the birth narrative), Valentinians (associated with the Johannine material, including the "I am" sayings), Ebionites (under James, focused on ethics and doing God's will, and concerned with Torah), and Sethians (focused on *gnosis* and an elaborate cosmology, and seeing the Creator god as evil). He says scholars now avoid the blanket term "Gnostic" because the Nag Hammadi texts allow more precise distinctions. He is uncertain whether Marcion held that the Old Testament God was evil or that a separate evil creator made the world. He also says the Sethians' view of the Genesis serpent as a benefactor is the origin of the name "Ophites."

### 3. Using early writers as witnesses to lost and surviving texts
Wescoe treats the church fathers mainly as sources for reconstructing texts:
- **Justin Martyr**: He says Justin's *First Apology* and *Dialogue with Trypho* are the earliest reliable witnesses to documents in existence. Wescoe argues Justin took his birth narrative from the Infancy Gospel of James, and that this was the source for Matthew 1-2 and Luke 1-2 (he says not all scholars agree). Justin's "Memoirs of the Apostles" is one of the earliest references to gospel material, and Wescoe thinks Justin used a Greek translation of the Gospel of the Hebrews, which lacked a birth narrative.
- **Gospel of the Hebrews**: No manuscript survives. Wescoe says it can only be partly reconstructed from quotations and from comparisons such as the Gospel of Thomas parallels with Q-type material.
- **Irenaeus**: He says Irenaeus's *Against Heresies* is the most extensive surviving heresiology and may build on Justin's lost work, and that Irenaeus is the first to set the fourfold gospel (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John) as the authoritative canon, rejecting texts such as the Gospel of Mary, Gospel of Judas, Gospel of Thomas and Gospel of Truth.
- **Tertullian**: He credits Tertullian with the first use of the word "Trinity" in Latin, and says Tertullian was wrong that Marcion cut the Gospel of Luke. Wescoe argues that comparative and linguistic work shows Marcion used an earlier form of Luke.

### 4. Constantine, Nicaea and the state-church relationship
Wescoe says Constantine's conversion is questionable: he reports the vision story but says there is little record of Constantine practicing the values Jesus taught, and he calls himself a Constantine skeptic. The Edict of Milan provided toleration for all religions. The Council of Nicaea (325) is, in Wescoe's words, often seen as the major turning point. He says the bishops gathered to agree on official belief, that Constantine cared mainly that they agreed, and that the precedent of emperor-called councils meant the emperor now ran the church, marrying church to state. He describes the four major churches as Antioch, Alexandria, Constantinople and Rome after the empire's division in 330.

### 5. Persecution, book burning and the Edict of Thessalonica
Wescoe describes Diocletian's book burning in the early fourth century as a setback because texts had to be copied by hand and materials were costly. The Edict of Thessalonica (380) under Theodosius made the state-approved form of Christianity the only legal form and ordered other Christian literature confiscated and burned, with death as a possible penalty. Wescoe reports that church records call those who surrendered texts "traitors" (*traditores*), and that disputes arose over whether to readmit them. He says groups that refused to conform met underground, and that the Council of Constantinople (381) and the Theodosian Code completed the state-enforced orthodoxy.

### 6. Chalcedon and the split of the Oriental Orthodox churches
Wescoe describes Emperor Marcian's Council of Chalcedon as the third major turning point. The official reason given was the nature of Christ, but Wescoe says the actual cause was Marcian's attempt to subordinate the Alexandrian and Antiochene churches to imperial authority. The Oriental Orthodox churches, which he names (including the Coptic Orthodox Church of Alexandria, the Armenian Apostolic Church, and the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church), refused and stayed separate. He argues the Christological dispute is largely semantic. He notes the Henotikon of Emperor Zeno, a reconciliation attempt that Rome rejected, and he describes the papacy's ecclesial structure as different from the conciliar structure in the East.

### 7. The canon was assembled over centuries, not dropped from heaven
Wescoe argues that the Bible's canon came together over about four centuries and was formalized at the Council of Carthage under Augustine's influence. Augustine, he says, adopted Athanasius's proposed New Testament list (from the 360s) without changes, and used the Rabbinic Judaism Old Testament canon, excluding the Book of Enoch on that basis. He presents this as a political decision with stated reasons that Augustine himself gave. Kathy and Wescoe then argue that the church became detached from its Jewish foundations and that this affects how Paul, Jesus and James are understood, with Wescoe pointing to the cyclical reading of Torah in Jewish tradition versus linear Greek thinking (Kathy's framing, which Wescoe accepts in part). He notes that Hellenization pressured both Judaism and Christianity.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"The true church has always been unified from Jesus through the apostles into the second century."** Wescoe says the record shows multiple groups with different beliefs and practices in the second century, and that denominations often read their own history backward.
- **"Judaism and Christianity split simply because some rejected Jesus as Messiah."** Wescoe says the split was more complicated and gradual, with political factors and shared fellowship for centuries.
- **"Peter established the church and was the first pope."** Wescoe calls this anachronistic, since the term "pope" came later and Rome's primacy developed over time.
- **"The Bible's canon was delivered complete at the start."** Wescoe says the canon took about four centuries and was shaped by councils and political decisions.
- **"Constantine's conversion was a turn to following Jesus's teaching."** Wescoe, who calls himself a Constantine skeptic, argues the evidence shows a political alignment, not an adoption of Jesus's ethics.
- **"Islam existed at the time of Christianity's beginning."** Wescoe says Islam emerged in the early seventh century.
