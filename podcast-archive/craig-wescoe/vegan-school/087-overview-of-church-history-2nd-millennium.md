## Summary

The talk is part two of a survey of church history, covering roughly the late first millennium through the 20th century. Craig Wescoe carries most of it, with Kathy asking questions and offering reactions, so the positions below are Craig's unless marked otherwise. The organizing question is how Christianity moved from the Jesus movement and the Jerusalem church to the state-linked, divided Christianity of the present, and what the reader should make of that path.

Craig's central argument is that the fourth-century decision to tie the church to imperial power, from Constantine's councils through the Edict of Thessalonica (380), was the turning point. He traces later events as consequences: the 451 split of the Oriental Orthodox churches (which he presents as a refusal to keep yoking the church to the state), the 1054 Catholic-Orthodox schism (which he says was partly a power struggle over papal authority, not only a dispute over the filioque), the Crusades, inquisitions, pogroms, and the blaming of Jews for Jesus's death. He treats the Protestant Reformation as a response to these abuses and to wider access to the Bible after the printing press. Late in the talk he argues that the 20th-century discoveries of the Dead Sea Scrolls and the Nag Hammadi library let scholars recover earlier Jewish and Christian forms, including the Ebionites and Gnostic groups, that the later orthodox narrative had buried. He cites the Dead Sea Scrolls and Nag Hammadi as the most important discoveries in recent church history.

The talk ends with a question-and-answer segment. Craig says the churches that refused to join the councils, such as the Ebionites, may have the strongest claim to having stayed close to Jesus, and that the test of the church is its fruit rather than institutional membership. ## Key Teachings

### The fourth-century yoking of church to state as the turning point
Craig argues that the Edict of Milan (313) gave freedom of religion, but that under Constantine and the councils of Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381), along with the Edict of Thessalonica (380), the state-approved form of Christianity became the only permitted religion. Dissenting Christians, Jews and pagans faced persecution, and literature could be ordered handed over under penalty of death. He calls the Council of Nicaea one of the biggest events in church history, because the emperor ordered the bishops to agree on official beliefs. He sees the fourth-century settlement as the origin of the state-linked church and the later Bible canon. He is sympathetic to the desire to end persecution but holds that the alliance cost the church its fidelity to Jesus. Kathy largely agrees, saying the church should not have yoked itself together at all.

### Schisms: Chalcedon (451) and 1054
Craig says the Oriental Orthodox churches broke away at Chalcedon in 451 after roughly 125 years of state alignment, and that they remain in communion with one another and not with Rome or Constantinople. He presents the 1054 split as formally about the filioque, using John 15:26 ("the Spirit of Truth who proceeds from the Father") and John 20:22 ("Jesus breathed on them"). He argues the real conflict was about papal authority: Rome's pope had absolute power, while Constantinople's bishops voted collectively, so the dispute was a power struggle as much as a doctrinal one. Craig says the Christological dispute at Chalcedon (over Christ's human and divine natures) also seems to him largely semantic, since people on both sides still struggle to say what the difference is.

### Crusades, inquisitions and pogroms
Craig describes the Crusades (late 11th to late 13th century) as violent campaigns against Jews and Muslims in the Middle East, against Muslims in the Iberian Peninsula, and against pagan peoples in northern Europe, justified as protecting the Eastern Orthodox church. He says the Latin Crusaders sacked Constantinople in 1204 (he gives "124," which should read 1204) and that the Greek Orthodox church carried out a pogrom against Latin-speaking Catholics in Constantinople. He describes the inquisitions as violence and imprisonment against anyone labeled heretical, and says these groups (the Cathars, Waldensians, Hussites, Beguines and others) opposed the Crusades and the church's wealth. He ties this to the Christian movement's history of self-correction, and says the church's violence contradicts the commission to "Go Make Disciples of all the nations."

### Blaming the Jews for Jesus's death
Craig argues that the medieval blaming of Jews for Jesus's death, drawing on the Gospel of John, and the blaming of Jews for the 1348 Black Death, were unjustified. He makes three arguments: Jesus and his first followers were Jewish; the Gospels present Jesus forgiving his killers; and Roman soldiers, not Jewish people as such, carried out the crucifixion. He concedes that Jewish groups were involved. He says the Sadducees, who ran the Temple under Herod and whose money-making sacrificial system Jesus disrupted, were the ones pressing for his death, while the Pharisees (the ancestors of rabbinic Judaism) were not. Kathy pushes back that a faction of his own people did want him killed, which Craig accepts. He says this will be examined further in the discussion of first- and second-century texts.

### The Jesus movements of the 12th and 13th centuries, and the Reformation
Craig groups a range of 12th- and 13th-century movements under a loose label, "Jesus movement," without claiming they were true followers. Within the Catholic church he names the Friends of God (Gottesfreunde) in Germany and Switzerland, and John Wycliffe and the Lollards, who questioned church wealth and papal authority. He presents the Reformation as beginning with the printing press (1454), which allowed Bibles to be affordable and read at home, and with Luther's 95 Theses (1517), which attacked indulgences and asserted the authority of Scripture over the pope. He notes that Luther wanted to remove the Epistle of James because it seemed to promote works-based righteousness, and ties that to anti-Jewish feeling. He also says the Council of Trent (1545–1563) was the Catholic response, reaffirming that good works matter for salvation, citing James 2 ("faith without works is dead").

### Dead Sea Scrolls and Nag Hammadi
Craig presents the 20th-century discoveries as the most important recent developments in church history. He says the Dead Sea Scrolls show the Judaism of the century before Jesus, and that the Book of Enoch predated the New Testament, which he calls groundbreaking. He says Nag Hammadi lets scholars read Gnostic texts directly (Valentinians, Marcionites, Sethians) rather than relying only on heresy writers' accounts. He argues this lets scholars reconstruct first-century Christianity more reliably, and that together the two discoveries produced an explosion of scholarship comparable to the printing press.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"The church is just Catholic and Protestant."** Craig says this ignores the Eastern Orthodox, the Oriental Orthodox and the groups that never joined the councils, and that Protestants often inherit a Catholic-centered view of history.
- **"If it is in the Bible it is perfect and beyond question, and everything else has no authority."** Craig argues the Bible's formation was a historical process with source texts and contested books, so Scripture cannot be treated apart from history.
- **"The Jews killed Jesus, so Jews may be persecuted."** Craig says Roman soldiers carried out the crucifixion, Jesus forgave his killers, and the Sadducees, not Jews as a whole, pressed for his death.
- **"The Jews rejected their Messiah."** Craig says thousands of Jewish people accepted Jesus, and that the separation of Judaism and Christianity owes as much to later developments on both sides.
- **"The Eastern-Western split was over a trivial doctrinal point."** Craig presents the filioque as the stated reason, but says the underlying conflict was about papal authority.
- **"Purgatory and indulgences are biblical."** Craig says Purgatory is not biblical and that indulgences have no basis in Scripture. He notes Catholic teaching on Purgatory may have changed, but says he is not Catholic and suggests the reader check this.
