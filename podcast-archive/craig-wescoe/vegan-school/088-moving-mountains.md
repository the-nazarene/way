## Summary

Craig Wescoe asks how a saying about moving mountains changed as it passed through the Gospels. He proposes that the saying began as a teaching about reconciliation through forgiveness and became, in Mark, a teaching that faith lets a person curse and wither trees. His main evidence is a comparison of parallel passages: Gospel of Thomas 48, then Luke 17:3-6, Matthew 17 and 18, and Mark 11:20-26. He reads each later version as shifting the saying further from forgiveness toward faith, prayer and cursing. He also argues that Thomas 48 preserves a form close to Jesus's words, and he treats Thomas as an early source that sometimes preserves older material than the canonical Gospels.

The talk ends with Craig's reconstruction of an earlier source. In it, Jesus teaches forgiveness and reconciliation, and the "keys to the kingdom" (Matthew 16:19) are understood as forgiveness. Craig says this is "plausible" and that the pattern is "very telling." Kathy agrees it is plausible but does not test the argument against other evidence. The talk is a presentation with a guest responding, not a debate, and the conclusions are offered as a proposal. ## Key Teachings

### Thomas 48 as the earliest form: reconciliation, not faith
Craig takes Gospel of Thomas 48 ("if two make peace with each other in this one house they will say to the mountain move away and it will move away") as the baseline. He reads it as being about people who are estranged, and says that making peace removes any obstacle. He describes Thomas as a sayings gospel with no narrative and about 114 sayings, found in the Nag Hammadi library. He notes that scholars have long recognized that about half its sayings look like the canonical Gospels and half look strange. He argues that the half that parallels the synoptics, especially the material shared by Matthew and Luke that Mark lacks (the "Q material"), often preserves the earlier form. Craig says most scholars agree that Thomas uses an early source that may capture Jesus's original words. He admits that this view is controversial.

### Luke 17: faith enters the saying
Craig reads Luke 17:3-6 as preserving the forgiveness context: rebuke a brother who sins, forgive him if he repents, even seven times a day. The disciples then ask for more faith, and Jesus answers with the mustard seed and the mulberry tree. Craig marks the changes from Thomas in color: the mountain becomes a mulberry tree planted in the sea, and faith as a mustard seed is inserted. He argues that Luke still keeps the forgiveness context but has begun to make the saying about faith. He also suggests that if Luke's forgiveness passage is followed directly by Thomas 48, the sequence reads naturally, which he takes as evidence that Luke is the next step after Thomas.

### Matthew: from forgiveness procedure to prayer-agreement and doublets
Matthew 18:15-22 has a procedure for reconciliation (go alone, then take one or two others, then the congregation), followed by "if two of you on Earth agree about anything they ask for it will be done" (18:19), and then Peter's question about forgiving seventy times seven. Craig says Matthew keeps the forgiveness frame but turns the two-person agreement into a promise that prayer will be answered, and that "if he doesn't do it for us we must not have prayed hard enough." He then discusses Matthew 17:19-21, where the disciples cannot cast out a demon "because of your unbelief," and argues that Matthew is using doublets to rework earlier material. In his reading, the "unbelief" replaces the unwillingness to reconcile, so that the original context of two people's desire to be unified gets displaced by faith. He also cites Alan Horvath's view that faith is a covenant relationship with God, which Craig finds helpful against a vaguer, subjective sense of faith.

### Mark 11: cursing the fig tree and faith as psychic power
Craig argues that Mark 11:20-26 is the furthest stage. The fig tree withers after Jesus has cursed it, and Jesus tells the disciples to have faith in God, not to doubt, and to speak to a mountain. Craig says forgiveness is attached as an afterthought ("if you hold anything against anyone forgive them"), so that the central point becomes getting what you want by believing and cursing things. He calls this the saying's transformation into "psychic powers to curse trees if you believe hard enough," and he says that the Jesus of this version is turned into "a sort of sorcerer doing Conjuring." He treats Mark's verses 25-26 as the part most likely to be preserved, because they still say forgiveness is required.

### Binding, loosing and the keys to the kingdom as forgiveness
Craig links the "binding and loosing" in Matthew 18:18 and the "keys of the kingdom" in Matthew 16:19 to the forgiveness context. He argues that "bind" means refusing to reconcile and "loose" means both parties agreeing to forgive. He supports this by pointing to Matthew 6 ("if you forgive others... your heavenly father also will forgive you"), Luke 6:37 ("forgive and you will be forgiven") and John 20:22-23 ("if you forgive anyone's sins their sins are forgiven"). In his conclusion, "forgiveness is the key to the kingdom of heaven" is the core of Jesus's teaching, and he says the Kingdom works through reconciliation that requires forgiveness. He says the key to the kingdom "gets obscured" as one moves through the sources. Craig says this idea has been discussed in his earlier work.

### Reconstructed source and Craig's view of Jesus's words
Craig presents a reconstructed source saying, which he says is "probably approximately what Jesus was saying": take heed to yourselves; if your brother sins, rebuke him and forgive him; if two make peace in this house they will say to the mountain move away; for where two or three are reconciled in my name there I am in the midst of them. He describes his method: once the original saying is identified, the order of the Gospels reveals how it was rearranged. He is careful to say that Thomas 48 is not Jesus's exact words, because it is a translation (he suggests a chain of Hebrew to Greek to Coptic to English) and not in Jesus's own language. He still holds it to be the closest access to the saying. He argues that the "apologetic perspective" that every word of the Gospels is Jesus's cannot be assumed, and that Source criticism tests this against the literature.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Popular belief:** that Jesus taught that faith can literally move mountains, walk on water, or wither trees by spoken command. **Craig's correction:** the earliest saying (Thomas 48) is about two estranged people making peace; the mountain is their quarrel, and the later faith and cursing material is a transformation of it.
- **Popular belief (as Craig frames it, the "typical apologetic perspective"):** that every word in the Gospels is Jesus's words and nothing has changed. **Craig's correction:** this cannot be assumed, and the literature itself shows development through comparison of parallel passages.
- **Popular belief:** that a prayer will be answered if two people agree, so unanswered prayer means insufficient faith. **Craig's correction:** Matthew 18:19 originally concerned two people agreeing to forgive, and the prayer-promise reading is a later shift.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. later development:** The talk's core method is to find the earliest form of a saying and trace how it was reworked. Craig treats Thomas 48 as closest to Jesus, with Luke, Matthew and Mark as successive stages.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Craig reads "keys to the kingdom" as forgiveness and says the kingdom operates through reconciliation, which requires forgiveness. This is the talk's only kingdom claim, and it rests on his reconstruction.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Craig reads Mark's Jesus as turned into "a sort of sorcerer doing Conjuring," and reads Matthew 18:20 ("I am there in the midst of them") as Jesus's presence among reconciled people.
- **How the Gospels and canon were formed:** The talk is an extended example of Source criticism, using Thomas, Q, and the doublets in Matthew to argue for a sequence of redactions. It is not a survey of canon history.
- **Forgiveness and reconciliation:** The talk's ethical point is that forgiveness toward others is the condition for receiving forgiveness from God (Matthew 6, Mark 11:25-26, Luke 6:37).
- **Not addressed in this talk:** Paul, James, the Jerusalem community, the Nazarenes and Ebionites, the Essenes, Torah, sacrifice, diet, nonviolence, resurrection, afterlife and hell.
