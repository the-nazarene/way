## Summary

The talk asks how the many second-century Christian groups (Irenaeus's "Proto-Orthodoxy," the Ebionites, and the Marcionite, Valentinian, Basilidean and Saturninian "Gnostic" schools) relate to the Jerusalem church under James and to the first century. The presenter, speaking with Kathy, starts from the middle of the second century because the sources are clearer there, relying above all on Irenaeus's *Against Heresies* (c. 180) as a "road map" of groups, teachers and texts, and then works backward. He describes a "Simon" lineage running from John the Baptist through Simon, Cerinthus and Marcion, and a "Paul" lineage running from Paul through Theudas to Valentinus, plus a Peter/Clement succession at Rome and an Antioch succession running through Ignatius. He argues that the key break came after the Bar Kokhba revolt, which he places roughly 60–65 years after 70 CE. In his telling, the Jerusalem bishops had Hebrew names until Judas, Greek names from Marcus onward, and several major second-century movements began right after that war. He presents this as an overview, not a settled account, and repeatedly flags his own speculation.

The longest argument concerns Marcion. The presenter says Marcion was probably not teaching that the Creator is evil, but was building on a reading of Genesis that separates an "Elohist" source (Elohim, Genesis 1) from a "Yahwist" source (Yahweh, Genesis 2); he says the Ebionites and the Gnostic systems share this basic reading. He also argues that the canonical Gospel of Luke is probably Marcion's gospel with material added later, not the reverse. To date Luke and Acts, he argues that Irenaeus's account of Luke as Paul's companion does not fit the evidence. Luke and Acts were, on his reading, composed around 169 for Theophilus, who became bishop of Antioch that year, and possibly by a Gaulish circle linked to Irenaeus's predecessor Pothinus. He notes that Paul quotes Jesus only a few times, which he thinks is hard to square with Luke's claims.

The talk ends without a conclusion. The presenter says his main interest is in source criticism and the relationships between ideas, and that the Jerusalem-to-Rome transition is the frame he finds most useful. He and Kathy agree on a set of follow-up topics: the Clementine homilies on how to read scripture, the Gospel of Truth from Nag Hammadi, Luke and Acts' sources, and the Elohist/Yahwist reading of Torah. The presenter speculates about backlash against Jews and Christians after the revolt, and says plainly that this is speculation.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The two lineages and the Jerusalem church
The presenter draws two main chains. One runs from John the Baptist to Simon, then to Cerinthus and Marcion, and to Menander and his followers (Saturninus and Basilides). The other runs from Paul to Theudas to Valentinus. He places the Ebionites as the continuation of the Jerusalem church, with James as the first bishop (he cites Gospel of Thomas saying 12 and Acts 15 for James's role). He says the Jerusalem bishops were of Hebrew descent until Judas, and that after Judas every bishop had a Greek name, starting with Marcus (about 10:32). He admits he has not found a clear historical explanation for this change and treats it as a sign that something significant happened to the Jerusalem church during or after the revolt.

### 2. Bar Kokhba as the turning point
The presenter's central claim is that the Bar Kokhba revolt, which he says leveled Jerusalem and barred Jews from living there, marks the break between a Jewish form of Christianity and the Greek-influenced forms that expanded afterward (about 09:24 and 12:32). He cites Justin Martyr (writing in the 150s) as saying Jews were still barred from Jerusalem in his time. He links the timing to Marcion and Valentinus both teaching in Rome, to Pothinus founding the church in Gaul, and to the start of Irenaeus's line. He later says the Ebionites "came first" and that the Proto-Orthodoxy came third, after the Gnostics (about 1:26:27). He also speculates that the revolt produced anti-Jewish backlash that led some Christians to distance themselves from Judaism, and says he does not know that this happened.

### 3. Marcion, Genesis, and the two sources
The presenter argues that Marcion's teaching is usually misreported. He thinks Marcion was reading Genesis in a way that distinguishes the Elohist God of Genesis 1 from the Yahwist God of Genesis 2, and that this reading goes back to Jesus and to the Ebionites. He says Irenaeus and Tertullian (who wrote against Marcion) presented Marcion as teaching that the Creator was evil, and that his opponents may have misrepresented him. Marcion's *Antitheses*, which listed contradictions in the Old Testament, is lost; the presenter says only a few fragments survive, through his opponents' quotations. He calls this reading "a basis for" the wider Gnostic systems and says most of them interpret Genesis.

### 4. Marcion's canon and the Luke question
The presenter says Marcion's canon had a shortened Gospel of Luke and ten of Paul's letters (without the Pastorals). He says Irenaeus claimed Marcion cut Luke down, but that linguistic analysis points the other way: canonical Luke looks like Marcion's gospel with later material added. He points to material Justin attests in the 150s from the Gospel of James, the Acts of Pilate and the Infancy Gospel of Thomas, which Irenaeus says Luke alone contains. He concludes that the evidence fits better with the added material coming after Marcion and before Irenaeus, roughly 155–180.

### 5. Luke, Acts and Theophilus
Luke 1:1–4 is addressed to "most excellent Theophilus." The presenter says Irenaeus presents Luke as Paul's companion, but that this does not fit the record: Paul quotes Jesus only five or six times across his letters and does not mention most of the miracles Luke contains (about 1:21:44). He proposes that Luke and Acts were written around 169, when Theophilus became bishop of Antioch, possibly by someone in Irenaeus's Gaulish circle around Pothinus. He admits this is "theoretical" and that the identity of the author is open.

### 6. Simon, Peter, and the Roman succession
The presenter notes that Simon (Acts 8, Simon Magus) and Peter are sometimes treated as one figure and sometimes as two, and that Basilides is said by one source to have learned from Glaucus, a disciple of Peter, rather than from Menander (about 34:30). He describes the Clementine tradition, in which Clement succeeds Peter directly at Rome, and the Irenaean tradition, in which Linus and Anacletus stand between them. He says the Linus and Anacletus names look invented to lengthen the gap. At Antioch he argues that the Antiochene successor "Evodius" in Irenaeus's list was originally the woman Evodia of Philippians 4:2–3, whose name was changed to a male name. He says this is a scholarly argument he accepts, not his own.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That "Gnostic" names one movement.** The presenter says the groups should be treated separately, with their own leaders, origins and followers.
- **That Marcion taught that the Creator God was evil.** The presenter says Marcion was probably reading Genesis through two sources, Elohist and Yahwist, and that his opponents may have misrepresented him.
- **That canonical Luke came first and Marcion cut it down.** The presenter argues the reverse, based on linguistic analysis.
- **That non-canonical texts are all late and derived from the New Testament.** The presenter says the Gospel of Truth existed by the time of Irenaeus and may predate some New Testament texts.
- **That Jesus taught secret teachings to individual disciples.** The presenter says these texts are Greek philosophical and do not sound like Jesus.
- **That Peter had nothing to do with Simon the Gnostic and was simply the first bishop of Rome.** The presenter says the Simon/Peter identification is confused in the sources, and that the Linus and Anacletus succession looks invented.
- **That Luke was Paul's companion and that Paul was the source of Luke's material.** The presenter says Paul's letters show little knowledge of Jesus's miracles or parables.
- **That the Proto-Orthodox view of church history is the historical default.** The presenter says the Proto-Orthodox story was constructed to claim Jesus, Paul and the Apostles while defining itself against both the Ebionites and the Gnostics.
