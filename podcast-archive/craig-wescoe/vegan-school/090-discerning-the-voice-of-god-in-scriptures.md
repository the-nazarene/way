## Summary

The talk asks how a reader can tell which parts of Scripture reflect God's voice if, as Jeremiah 8:8 says, "the lying pen of the scribes" has falsified the Law. Craig Wescoe opens with Jeremiah 7 and 8, noting that Jeremiah denies God commanded burnt offerings at the Exodus while the Torah as we have it (Exodus, Leviticus) does command them. He argues that the Clementine Homilies, which he reads as a Jerusalem-linked or Ebionite teaching of uncertain date, give a method for separating the false material. Wescoe proposes that the Homilies' Book 2 (from about chapter 38) was a private, "esoteric" teaching for disciples, while Recognitions Book 1 was the public version.

The core argument, as Wescoe presents the Homilies' Peter, is that any passage portraying God as evil, ignorant, changeable, violent, or sacrificial is a later falsification, and that rejecting a true statement about God is less dangerous than believing a blasphemous one. Wescoe then applies this rule himself. He says God's name Yahweh marks the "counterfeit" voice and Elohim the true one, and he claims that when the Genesis and Exodus stories are separated into two sources, each reads as internally consistent. He says this mostly confirms the older two-source documentary hypothesis, which he notes had been extended to four sources. He identifies the lying scribes with the temple priests and, in Jesus's time, the Herodian Sadducees.

The talk ends after a long reading session, with Wescoe and Kathy stopping for the evening. Wescoe promises further work on the Noah flood and animal sacrifice texts in a separate course and signs off. Most of the claims here are the speaker's own readings of the Homilies and of Genesis, and the transcript does not settle whether the Homilies are a first-century text or a second-century creation.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Scripture contains deliberate "lying pen" additions
Wescoe reads Jeremiah 8:8 as an admission that scribal hands altered the Law. He argues that Jeremiah 7 denies that God commanded sacrifice at the Exodus, so either Jeremiah is right that scribes added the sacrificial material, or the Torah is accurate and Jeremiah is lying. He says many translators soften Jeremiah 7 by adding words (around 04:41), which he takes as evidence of how much is at stake for an inerrancy view. He accepts Jeremiah's side and treats the sacrificial law as a human addition.

### 2. The Homilies' rule: what is spoken against God is false
Wescoe reads Clementine Homilies Book 2 (from chapter 38) as stating that "the scriptures have had joined to them many falsehoods against God," which the Homilies attribute to the wicked one working through the scribes. The rule is that anything "spoken against God" is false, and Wescoe extends it to the righteous figures (Moses, Noah, Abraham, Jacob, Adam) whom the scriptures portray doing evil. He cites the Homilies' argument that it is safer to be wrong by rejecting something true about God than by believing something blasphemous about him.

### 3. Public versus private teaching
Wescoe's own proposal is that Recognitions Book 1 was intended as public teaching and that Homilies Books 2 and 3 were a private, "esoteric" teaching for disciples. He ties this to the Homilies' concern that revealing falsehoods in Scripture to the "unlearned multitudes" could drive people from faith. He distinguishes two audiences: pious readers who already trust God's character and do not need the method, and those troubled by the violent God of certain passages, who do.

### 4. Divine attributes as a test list
The Homilies (as read aloud) list attributes that cannot belong to the true God: changing purpose, lying, envy, hardening hearts, blinding, weakness, injustice, loving war, changing his mind, delighting in fat and sacrifice, dwelling in a tabernacle. For each, Wescoe identifies a scriptural passage. He cites Pharaoh's hardened heart, the flood, and Genesis 1 and 2. He also reads the Homilies' attribute list of the "good" God (creator, long-suffering, merciful, bringer of immortality, "dwelling in the souls of the good"), and takes it as a counterweight: Death is not the work of the true God. He says he is working through the Torah marking each passage as true or counterfeit.

### 5. Yahweh versus Elohim, and the two-source reading
Wescoe's working method is that the counterfeit God is usually called Yahweh and is the destroyer, curser, and sacrificer, while Elohim is the one who creates, saves, and blesses. For the flood he identifies one account of forty days and nights of rain (attributed to God as destroyer) and another of 150 days in which violence and wickedness cause the flood. He says that when Yahweh and Elohim material are separated, each story is consistent, and that this largely supports the older documentary hypothesis of two sources (scholarship later proposed four). He describes this as an ongoing project, not finished results.

### 6. The lying scribes and Jesus as the "true Prophet"
Wescoe identifies the lying scribes in Jeremiah's time as the priestly temple scribes who prescribed the Levitical sacrifices, and in Jesus's time as the Herodian Sadducees who profited from animal sales at the temple. He reads the temple cleansing as Jesus disrupting this trade, and he links the Homilies' "be ye good money changers" saying (which he says appears in the Hebrew Matthew, not our Greek Gospels) to the call to discern true from counterfeit. He identifies Jesus, in the Homilies, as the "true Prophet" promised in Deuteronomy, who teaches disciples things "plainly spoken" but not written.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That every word of Scripture is equally the word of God.** Wescoe argues that the Homilies and Jeremiah show otherwise: Scripture mixes a true voice with falsified additions, and readers need a method to tell them apart.
- **That translators have not altered Jeremiah 7.** He says some translations add words so that Jeremiah does not appear to deny that God commanded sacrifice.
- **That God commanded animal sacrifice and violence.** He says these passages are the lying-pen additions and that the true God, Elohim, is the one who blesses and saves.
- **That God hardens hearts, changes his mind, or lacks knowledge.** He says these passages describe a counterfeit God; he reads the change-of-mind passages (such as Noah and the flood) as God withdrawing and letting the consequences of sin run their course.
- **That the Homilies' dialogue form shows Peter speaking Greek philosophy.** He says the form reflects Clement's training as the writer, not an invented Peter.
- **That free will and divine foreknowledge are incompatible.** Kathy raises the Calvinist problem; Wescoe says God knows the goal will be reached while people keep real choices along the way, and he aligns this with open theism.

- **Torah and sacrifice:** The core argument is that the sacrificial law is a later falsification (Jeremiah 7 and 8 read against Exodus), and that the true Torah is the walk with God in righteousness before the sacrificial system was added.
