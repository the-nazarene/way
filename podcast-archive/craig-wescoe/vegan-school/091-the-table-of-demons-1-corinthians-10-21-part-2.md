## Summary

This is the second part of a conversation between Craig and Chris on the "table of demons" in 1 Corinthians 10:21. Paul mentions the table only briefly, so they turn to the Clementine Homilies, reading and commenting on sections from Homilies 9 and 11 and on a short passage about Peter's simple diet and Clement's family. The question is what the Homilies mean by eating at the table of demons, and how that bears on food, sacrifice, illness, and the early Jerusalem community's rules.

Their answer, offered as their reading rather than settled fact, is that the Homilies treat sharing meat, blood, and sacrificial meals as a way of inviting demons into a person, through a "table fellowship" that shapes desire, worldview, and even health. They tie this to the Jerusalem decree of Acts 15, which they say appears in an older form in the church writers Irenaeus, Tertullian, and Cyprian, including a Golden Rule clause that later manuscripts dropped. Chris argues that Paul is best read as agreeing with James and Jesus, and that building doctrine on Paul's letters first is "not starting in the best place." Craig adds that the Homilies' claims about disease are meant as "not necessarily all" cases, and both disclaim medical advice while pointing to PCRM and Dr. Milton Mills.

The talk ends with a summary: they say James's decree makes abstaining from the table of demons central to following Jesus, and that modern Christians largely ignore it. They close by agreeing to take up John's relation to the Synoptics next week.

## Key Teachings

### Demonic deception works through food, desire, and "consent"
Starting from Homily 9.12, Craig and Chris read the idea that people "consent to the evil thoughts suggested by the demons as if they were the reasonings of their own souls." Craig explains that possession is not felt as possession: "you don't recognize that it's a demon like you think that these thoughts are my own thoughts." In his reading, eating flesh and blood invites demonic activity, and the person's identity then gets tied to the demon's. Chris reads 9.13 as showing that deception can even come "by love" (their example is saying "I love cheese"). Both say the answer is to resist, since the desire belongs to the demon and not to one's true nature as a child of God.

### Eating meat means sharing in the violence behind it
In the discussion after 9.12, Chris argues that the Homilies' phrase "the material part of the food being united to the body" should be read as uniting oneself with the thing itself: "we're sort of uniting ourselves with this cruelty and the violence." Craig and Chris both connect this to factory farming, with antibiotics, tumors, and disease in meat, and to Numbers 11 and Psalm 106, where craving flesh brings plague and "leanness." They say this is why Acts 15 treated the issue as non-negotiable.

### Sacrifice as a demonic deception
In 9.13 and 9.14 the Homilies describe people who think that gods demand sacrifices and that bad events (drought, locusts, the sun failing to rise) show the gods are displeased. Craig reads this as the origin of the sacrificial system. He links the claim to 2 Corinthians 11:14 and to Paul's equation of idols with demons, and to the Book of Enoch, Revelation, and Leviticus, which he says people use to justify sacrifice "over Jesus and all the prophets." On Craig's reading, the remedy is to withdraw power from these beings by refusing to worship them, not by offering more sacrifices.

Chris adds that in 9.14 demons "command them to eat with them," which he takes as the table fellowship of Paul's warning: the sacrifice is offered to the demon and then eaten by the worshiper together with it. Only grace and faith can break that bond, and Chris says the person must actually want to be freed. Craig and Chris both use the parable of the lost sheep (Luke 15) for this.

### Illness: demonic influence, but "not necessarily all of them"
Discussing 9.12 and 9.14, Craig and Chris say the Homilies link many sicknesses to demonic activity, and Craig explains the Homilies' view of false remedies: a sacrifice seems to work, a pill or aspirin seems to cure, and the underlying cause remains. Craig says this is "not necessarily all of them," since some conditions are genetic or inherited. Chris says that scientific consensus holds most of such illnesses to be lifestyle-related. Both repeatedly disclaim medical advice and recommend PCRM and Dr. Milton Mills, and they cite the Blue Zones as examples of whole-food plant-based living. Craig also says that suicide and depression should not be read as proof of demonic possession, comparing the link to smoking and cancer, which is a link and not a certainty.

### The Jerusalem decree in Irenaeus, Tertullian, and Cyprian
Chris reads Acts 15 in a form he attributes to Irenaeus (Against Heresies 3.12.14), with support from Tertullian (about 200) and Cyprian (about 250). The form includes abstaining from "meats offered to idols and from blood and from fornication," and then adds "whatsoever you do not wish to be done to you do not ye do to others." In this version, which the speakers treat as older, the ending is "walking in the Holy Spirit" rather than the later "Farewell." Craig says this clause "puts a whole new understanding" on the decree, and they read it as summing up what being a follower of Christ means, with the Golden Rule and the table of demons as one teaching. Chris says that Irenaeus is among the earliest witnesses to the book of Acts, and both say the Golden Rule version appears only in some later manuscripts. (The speakers draw this from a website during the recording, and Craig says he will send the text next week.)

### Paul's letters are hard to read; use the clearer Jerusalem material first
Chris says Paul's letters may have been edited, that the pastorals and some others are doubtful, and that Paul often "writes in a dialectic style" where it is unclear which view he holds. He also notes that Koine Greek has no punctuation and no spaces between words. He argues that people who base doctrine on Paul and then conclude Paul contradicts James and Jesus are starting in the wrong place. In his reading, 1 Corinthians 8 and 10 show Paul mediating between two views: "an idol is nothing," but "let's still not eat it for the sake of unity." Chris cites 1 Corinthians 8 for "I will never again eat meat" and 1 Corinthians 11 for "follow my example as I follow Christ," which he uses to say Paul also agrees the matter is serious.

### Peter's frugality, Clement's family, and the table separation
The "Peter's frugality" passage describes Peter and Andrew living on "bread and olives and rarely pot herbs," which Chris and Craig take as a lifelong vegan diet and a simple, poor life. They read this as fitting a Johannine account in which Andrew first follows John the Baptist (John 1:35-42), in contrast to Mark's account where Peter and Andrew are called as fishermen (Mark 1:17). Craig suggests that Peter and Andrew may have been "orphans" and that Jesus calls Simon "son of John," which they agree is worth rereading. They also link the Ebionite-style sharing of possessions (Acts 4) and Clement's choice to become a servant to Peter with the rich young ruler, and with Jesus's "I came not to be served but to serve."

They then read Peter's words to Clement's mother, who is told not to eat with her son until she shares his faith. Chris explains that the Homilies separate believers from Gentiles at table because of impurity, and that a newly baptized convert must be cleansed of demons first, like a quarantined animal at a sanctuary. They connect this to Paul's account of Antioch (Galatians 2:11-14), where Paul confronts Peter about eating with Gentiles, and they say the Homilies give fuller background for that dispute. Chris explains the "Thrice blessed invocation" as the three names El, Elohim, and Ruach Elohim, which he says the Homilies use to distinguish their reading of God from Yhwh; he flags this as not mainstream and open to evidence against it.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Paul's letters settle the question of eating meat and sacrifice.** Chris argues that Paul is mediating between two views and that the clearer teaching comes from James and Jesus.
- **Faith alone means works don't matter.** Chris and Craig say Paul's own "good works" follow grace, and that James's "dead faith" makes the same point.
- **Illness is always demonic.** Craig says the Homilies link many sicknesses to demonic activity but "not necessarily all of them," and that genetic and inherited conditions exist.
- **"Chastity" means total sexual abstinence.** Craig argues that in the Homilies the word may be better translated "fidelity" or "faithfulness," especially for married women.
- **The Thrice blessed invocation is Trinitarian.** Chris says the three names are El, Elohim, and Ruach Elohim, not the later Trinity, and flags this as not mainstream.
- **Peter and Andrew were called as fishermen in the Gospels.** Chris reads John 1 as placing them first as followers of John the Baptist, and says the synoptic call story conflicts with this.
