## Summary

Craig Wescoe, a vegan pastor and former university instructor in New Testament ethics, reviews the documentary *Christspiracy* in a YouTube video of about 31 minutes. He says the film argues that Jesus opposed animal sacrifice and the Jerusalem Temple's slaughter system, that this has been obscured, and that Jesus and his earliest followers were vegan. He organizes his review around 18 points the film makes and marks 17 as ones he agrees with. He presents himself as a committed disciple since 2003 and says his research focuses on early Christianity and source criticism.

Wescoe's main supporting evidence comes from the Gospels and Hebrew Bible. He cites Daniel 1 for the diet of vegetables and water, Genesis 1 for a vegetarian creation, and Jeremiah for the "den of thieves" saying. He argues that the Greek/Hebrew word usually rendered "thieves" (parim) is better translated "violent ones," so the Temple was a den of violence rather than a place of mere cheating. For the early-church evidence he relies on a quotation attributed to the Ebionites in Epiphanius (Panarion 30.16), which he cites via Keith Akers' book, and on the film's interview with Robert Eisenman on the name "Nazarene." He also says the Ebionites were the Jerusalem community under James. He asserts that the Gospels' fish-eating scenes come from later material, which he says the film answers with extra-biblical sources.

The talk ends with a call to action: he asks viewers to take up animal liberation as discipleship, to stop mocking religious people in animal-rights movements, and to "let's all become Disciples of Jesus." He recommends his own live talks, study guides and a video titled "Evidence for Veganism in the Early Church." He also names one point where he disagrees with the film: its claim that Paul was anti-vegetarian.

## Key Teachings

### The Temple as slaughterhouse and Jesus's prophetic critique
Wescoe agrees that the film correctly shows the Temple as a working slaughterhouse, with animals bought and sold, throats cut, and blood drained into channels under the Temple. He argues this is the context for Jesus's action. He says the Temple money-changers are not mentioned in the Lukan account, and that Jesus quotes Jeremiah, not Proverbs' "dishonest business with weighted scales," so the target is animal sacrifice, "in alignment with all the prophets that came before him." He adds that the Temple priests and Herodian-appointed Sadducees profited from sacrifice, which he compares to the modern kosher and halal trade.

### "Den of thieves" and the word *parim*
Wescoe's central linguistic point is that the Hebrew word translated "thieves" or "robbers" in Jeremiah (which he cites as 7:1) is parim, which he says is better rendered "violent ones." He therefore reads Jesus's saying as "a den of violence." He notes that the talk's team has a live talk on this word and treats it as the film's most striking claim.

### Diet from Daniel and Genesis
He endorses the film's claim that Daniel was "10 times wiser and healthier" on a diet of vegetables and water, citing Daniel 1, where Daniel and his companions refuse the king's food and drink. He also says that Genesis 1 contains no killing: humans and animals are given plants as food (Genesis 1:29), so "everybody is vegan in God's very good design." He suggests that the Daniel Fast is what led the filmmaker Cam to these questions.

### Dominion as stewardship
Responding to the film's Point 9, Wescoe argues that the "dominion" of Genesis 1:26-28 is stewardship, not a license to kill animals. He says the very next verse (Genesis 1:29) gives plants as food, and that this is "the exact opposite" of a license to kill.

### Nazarene as a title, not a town
He agrees with Robert Eisenman, interviewed in the film, that no early source (he mentions Josephus and about 200 places Josephus names) mentions a town called Nazareth. He reports that the word "Nazarene" is better understood as "the righteous one" or "the devoted one," and links this to John 14 ("the way, the truth and the life"), which he takes as a description of Jesus rather than of a town.

### The Ebionite quotation and the Jerusalem community
Wescoe calls the quotation "I came to destroy the sacrifices, and if you cease not from sacrificing the wrath of God will not cease from you" a verifiable early saying. He reports that Keith Akers' *The Lost Religion of Jesus* cites it from Epiphanius, who describes the Ebionites (which Wescoe identifies with the Jerusalem Church under James). He says it is unclear whether the quotation came from a lost text of Matthew or from another source, but argues that it fits the opposition to sacrifice in the Epistle to the Hebrews and other first-century texts.

### Paul: the one disagreement
Wescoe disagrees with the film's claim that Paul was anti-vegetarian, and it is the one point of the 18 he rejects. He says there is some evidence for the claim but that it rests on a misreading. He names 1 Timothy 4, Romans 14 and 1 Corinthians 10 as the passages people use, and says his team has addressed them in live talks. He says he wishes the film had left the point out, because it may make Christians dismiss the message if they think Paul, "who wrote half the New Testament," opposed vegetarianism.

### Cognitive dissonance and "humane" meat
Wescoe endorses the film's argument that many Christians and non-vegans hold contradictory beliefs about animals, which he calls hypocrisy in Jesus's terms. He cites the film's interview with psychologist Melanie Joy and the story of the roadkill-eating YouTuber "Food for Louie," who later became vegan. He also rejects free-range, grass-fed and regenerative farming as "not better" and says the film shows the farm-practice claims to be largely false advertising.
