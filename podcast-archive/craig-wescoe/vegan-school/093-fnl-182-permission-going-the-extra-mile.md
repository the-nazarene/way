## Summary

Wescoe, adapting a blog post he wrote about nine years earlier, asks whether Christians actually have God's permission to kill and eat animals. Many people, he says, argue from Genesis 1:26-28 (human "dominion"), Genesis 9:3 (God's grant of "every moving thing"), Mark 7:19 (the marginal gloss "thus Jesus declared all foods clean"), and 1 Timothy 4:4 (every creature is good if received with thanksgiving). He argues that each of these, read with its following verses and its context, does not grant permission. Genesis 1:29 gives humans and animals a plant diet, Genesis 9:4 forbids eating flesh with its blood, and Mark 7 is about handwashing traditions and lists "evil thoughts," murder and other defilements that come from within.

Wescoe then turns to the Jerusalem Council (Acts 15:28-29), where, he says, the decree to abstain from idol meat, blood, strangled things and porneia makes the diet a condition of being a follower. He reads Revelation 2 and Paul's statements in Romans 14, 1 Corinthians 8 and 1 Corinthians 10 as showing that Paul himself abstained out of love. He concludes that the real question is love rather than permission, citing Matthew 22:34-40, 1 John 4 and the Good Samaritan parable.

The second half of the talk is a live question-and-answer session in which Wescoe reads chat comments, responds on cannibalism and the "dogs" analogy, mentions his upcoming events and resources, and closes with a prayer for God's kingdom to come "on earth as it is in heaven."

## Key Teachings

### 1. Dominion is qualified by the plant diet (Genesis 1)
Wescoe reads Genesis 1:20-25 (animals created and blessed, told to multiply) and 1:26-28 (humans made in God's image with dominion). He argues that the common claim that dominion grants permission to kill is answered by 1:29, where God gives "every herb that yields seed" and "every tree whose fruit yields seed" as food, and gives the animals "every green herb" for food. For him, dominion is followed immediately by specific dietary instructions, so the reading of dominion as permission is "the exact opposite" of the text.

### 2. Noah's sacrifice and the blood prohibition (Genesis 8-9)
He reads Genesis 9:3 ("every moving thing that lives shall be food for you") together with 9:4 ("but you shall not eat the flesh with its life, that is its blood"). He says the blood prohibition is the immediate next verse, so the grant cannot be read as unlimited. He links this to Genesis 8:20, where Noah builds an altar and burns clean animals after the flood, and to Genesis 8:21, where God's heart response mentions the imagination of man's heart being evil "from his youth," which Wescoe connects to Genesis 6:5. He concludes that God does not call the sacrifice "very good," as he did in creation.

### 3. Mark 7: handwashing, the marginal gloss, and the list of defilements
Wescoe argues that Mark 7:19's "thus Jesus declared all foods clean" is a translator's marginal gloss, not the text. He reads Mark 7:1-13, where the Pharisees question disciples eating with unwashed hands, and Jesus answers with Isaiah's words on lip-service and "the commandment of God" versus "the tradition of men." He says the dispute is about hand-washing, not about which animals to eat. He then reads Mark 7:20-23 and counts seven items from the list that he says apply to killing animals: evil thoughts, murder, theft, covetousness, lewdness, pride and foolishness. (He lists "adulteries, fornications" as a separate item and counts murder as one of them.)

### 4. 1 Timothy 4:4 and the instruction to "declare good"
He reads 1 Timothy 4:4 ("every creature of God is good, and nothing is to be refused if it is received with thanksgiving") as a reference to things God himself declared good in Genesis 1, which in his reading are the plants, not animal flesh. He adds that interpreters should check an author against the rest of the teaching, and he says that if Paul contradicted the disciples, the reading of Paul is probably mistaken.

### 5. The Jerusalem Council and Revelation 2 (Acts 15; Revelation 2)
Wescoe reads Acts 15:28-29, where James's decree includes abstaining from "things offered to idols," "blood," "things strangled," and "porneia." He treats these as "necessary" conditions for being a follower, and he identifies the strangled item with slaughter by throat-cutting or suffocation. He says Irenaeus's "earliest account" of the council adds the golden rule, which he applies to animals. He then reads Revelation 2:14 and 2:20 (Balaam and Jezebel, eating things sacrificed to idols) to show that the same food prohibition runs through the book. He says that the permission reading would set Paul against the other apostles.

### 6. Paul's own abstention (Romans 14; 1 Corinthians 8 and 10)
Wescoe reads Romans 14:15 and 14:21 ("It is good neither to eat meat nor drink wine...") and 1 Corinthians 8:13 ("I will never again eat meat") as Paul's own resolution, made out of love for brothers who abstain. He reads 1 Corinthians 10:23-24 ("all things are lawful for me, but all things are not helpful") as Paul's acknowledgement that appeal to lawfulness is not the aim. He concludes that Paul is not granting permission, and that Paul himself points toward edification and love.

### 7. Love as the criterion (Matthew 22; Luke 10; 1 John 4)
He reads Matthew 22:34-40 (love God, love neighbor) as the summary of the law, and 1 John 4:8, 4:16-19 as saying the one who does not love does not know God. He reads the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25-37) to define "neighbor" broadly and reads Isaiah 11:9 ("nothing will hurt or destroy") as the end state, which he ties to the Lord's prayer for God's kingdom to come "on earth as it is in heaven." He presents this as the consistent answer to "what would Jesus do."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Dominion means permission to kill and eat animals.** Wescoe argues that Genesis 1:29 immediately gives a plant diet, so dominion is qualified rather than granted.
- **God permitted all flesh after the flood (Genesis 9:3).** He says the next verse, 9:4, forbids eating flesh with its blood, and that Genesis 8:21 shows God does not approve of the sacrifice.
- **Jesus declared all foods clean (Mark 7:19).** He says this is a translator's marginal gloss, and that Mark 7 is about handwashing traditions, not food.
- **1 Timothy 4:4 gives permission to eat anything if thanked for.** He reads "every creature of God is good" as referring to what God declared good in Genesis 1, which he identifies as plants.
- **Paul permitted eating meat.** He reads Romans 14 and 1 Corinthians 8 as Paul's own choice to abstain for love, and 1 Corinthians 10:23-24 as a rejection of appeals to lawfulness.
- **Christians have no obligation on diet.** He argues the Acts 15 decree lists abstention from blood and strangled things as necessary for being a follower.
