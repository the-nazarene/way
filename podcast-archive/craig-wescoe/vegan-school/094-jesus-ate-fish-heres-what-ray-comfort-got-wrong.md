## Summary

This is a reaction video. Craig Wescoe and Steven Lee August respond to a Ray Comfort video that lists nine foods Jesus allegedly ate or recommended: eggs, butter, honey, lamb, fish, salt, veal (the "fatted calf"), figs and bread. Comfort's case rests on quoting a food word from a gospel passage and then citing health benefits. The question the speakers take up is whether those passages show Jesus eating or endorsing these foods at all. They argue they do not. Most of the passages are parables or comparisons (Luke 11, Luke 15), and where a meal is described, the text names bread, not lamb or fish. The speakers say the Hebrew word translated "honey" means date syrup, and that Luke 24's "fish" appears in only some manuscripts and is absent from Luke's apparent source, Marcion's gospel, which says only "food." They also argue that John places Jesus' death on the preparation day, before Passover, so the last supper cannot have been a lamb meal.

The speakers keep a traditionalist framing for most of the talk, saying that a textual-history argument would lose Comfort's audience. They also acknowledge that they are not health experts and that their main concern is ethical (animal exploitation), with Genesis 1:29 as the "original diet." Steve claims, without naming sources, that Jesus and the apostles were Essenes who ate bread at Passover. Craig gives his own practice of abstaining from grapes as a Nazarite-style symbol. The talk ends with a running tally: Craig and Steve give Comfort roughly one correct item (salt) out of nine, and Steve says he respects Comfort's zeal. The video is mostly argument over texts, with a long section on health claims that the speakers largely set aside.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Food words in parables and comparisons are not dietary instructions
The speakers say the eggs, fish and bread in Luke 11 come from a comparison about prayer. Craig notes that the parable's point is that God gives more than an annoyed neighbor does. Craig points out that Matthew's version of the saying (Matthew 7:11) calls the people who give eggs "evil," which he argues makes the verse a poor basis for an egg recommendation. Steve adds that Jesus' use of a cultural image does not endorse it, using the slavery parable in Matthew 18 as the parallel. The same argument is applied to the "fatted calf" in Luke 15: Craig says the story is a teaching about repentance and the heavenly Father, not a menu. The speakers also note that Luke 15 is a Luke-only passage, so it has only one witness.

### 2. The Passover lamb and last-supper timing
Comfort reads Luke 22:7-8 as proof that Jesus ate lamb. The speakers answer that none of the four gospels says Jesus ate lamb or that lamb was on the table, and that the lamb is an inference. Craig says Paul, in 1 Corinthians 5, mentions keeping the Passover with only bread. Steve argues that John places Jesus' death on the preparation day, when the Passover lambs were slain, so the meal could not have been a Passover meal. He says harmonizing this with Luke requires treating Luke's "Passover" as the eve of the feast, and says the speakers favor John because the historical evidence supports it. Steve then says the apostles were Essenes, who (he claims) ate bread rather than lamb at Passover, which he uses to explain why Jesus described his body with bread. Steve states this with confidence but does not name the sources. Craig says the timing question has been debated for a long time and that he does not think it matters much for vegan outreach.

### 3. Luke 24: "fish" in a proof-of-flesh scene
The speakers argue that Luke 24:41-43 does not say fish. Craig notes that Jesus asks for "food" and that the text reports a piece of fish and honeycomb without saying he ate both. Steve says that study Bibles show two manuscript groups, one reading "fish" and one "fish and honeycomb" or "honeycomb" alone, so the original wording is uncertain, and the passage is one that "was corrupted." Craig explains the scene: the disciples think they are seeing a ghost, and Jesus eats to show he is flesh and bone. He says the point is the resurrection, not a fish diet. The speakers then cite Marcion's gospel as the source Luke used, via Tertullian as the earliest witness, and say Tertullian reports "food" without naming a kind. Craig concludes that Luke may have said "fish" to make the story relatable to fishermen, and he presents this as a reasonable inference from the premises, not a settled claim.

### 4. Lexical and textual points on honey, butter, salt, figs and bread
- Honey: Steve says the Hebrew term usually rendered "honey" in the land-of-milk-and-honey phrase refers to date paste or date syrup. Caleb's spies brought back figs, pomegranates and dates (Numbers 13), so the phrase describes fruitfulness, not bee products. Craig adds that describing a food does not recommend it.
- Butter: Craig argues that Isaiah 7's "butter and honey" describes what a child eats, and that a newborn's food is milk from his mother, so the verse is a prophetic sign, not a dietary command. He also notes that the same Hebrew word is translated differently in different versions.
- Salt: Craig and Steve agree that Jesus probably ate salt, but say the Mark 9 saying is about character and not about sodium. Steve says they have "one out of seven" correct so far.
- Figs: The speakers joke that Figs are the one food on the list that is vegan.
- Bread: Steve says bread is the food Jesus mentions most, and that he "daily broke bread with the apostles," with the eucharist as the example.

### 5. Genesis 1:29 as the baseline diet and the ethical framing
Craig argues that God's "very good" diet is in Genesis 1:29, and that animal products are outside that diet. He describes the egg and dairy industries, saying they involve killing male chicks and "spent" dairy cows, and he rejects the idea that a Christian needs health reasons to avoid such products. He also argues that eating animal products is a matter of conscience and that the Christian's reasons are not only about nutrition. The speakers say that Isaiah 11, which Craig cites as describing the food of the future age, speaks of plants. Craig suggests that the "vegan diet before sin" argument is a loophole, asking why anyone would use a pre-sin practice to justify something that came only after sin entered the world. The speakers repeatedly decline to argue nutrition, saying they are not health experts.

### 6. Paul, the table of demons, and the Jerusalem Council
Craig says that Paul introduces "the table of the Lord" and "the table of demons" but "doesn't elaborate extensively," so the meaning depends on historical context. He says the Clementine Homilies describe the table of demons at greatest length as the eating of the flesh and blood of creatures, which the speakers treat as idolatry. The speakers then describe what they call earliest attestations of the Jerusalem Council, which they say set minimal requirements: abstain from the table of demons and love your neighbor as yourself. Steve says Acts 15 is not the version they are using. Craig says Nazarite vow symbolism shapes his own practice: he abstains from grapes as a sign of not eating the blood of creatures, but he regards animal products as much more important to avoid and says he will not criticize someone who eats a grape.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mentioning a food means Jesus approved or ate it.** Craig and Steve argue that parables and comparisons (Luke 11, Luke 15, Matthew 18) use cultural images without endorsing them.
- **The last supper was a lamb Passover meal.** The speakers say no gospel states lamb was eaten, and John's timing puts the death before the Passover meal.
- **Jesus ate fish in Luke 24.** The speakers say the word is uncertain in the manuscripts, and the earliest source they name reports only "food."
- **"Honey" in the land of milk and honey is bee honey.** Steve says the Hebrew term refers to date paste or syrup.
- **Butter in Isaiah 7 is a diet for the child.** Craig says a newborn eats milk, so the verse is a sign, not a diet instruction.
- **Health studies justify eating these foods.** The speakers say veganism is an ethical question, and they decline to argue nutrition, though Craig notes that cholesterol and "superfood" claims keep changing.
