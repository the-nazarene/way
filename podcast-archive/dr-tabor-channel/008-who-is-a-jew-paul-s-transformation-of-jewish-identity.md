## Summary

Tabor asks whether Paul redefined what it means to be a Jew, and whether that redefinition still shapes Jewish identity today. He begins with Philippians 3, where Paul calls his audience "the true circumcision" and presents himself as a "Hebrew of Hebrews," then turns to Romans 2. Tabor argues that Romans 2 makes three moves that many scholars misread: God judges without partiality (Jew and Greek alike); there is a "double Torah," the written law and the law written on the heart; and the real Jew is defined inwardly rather than by ethnicity or outward circumcision. He calls the argument in Romans 2 a "setup," because Romans 3 says "none is righteous," so the ideal standard it describes is one no one meets. Romans 9-10 then makes Israel's membership depend on confessing Jesus as lord, with unbelieving Jews described as broken branches. Tabor ties this to 1 Corinthians 7, where Paul says "the appointed time has grown very short," to explain how Paul could ask believers to keep their social situations while expecting those distinctions to dissolve.

Tabor's main evidence is close reading of the Pauline letters, supported by two modern parallels: a Pew Research Center survey of American Jews on whether Jewishness is ethnic, cultural or religious, and a 1989 Israeli Supreme Court ruling on messianic Jews under the Law of Return. He says the tension between ethnic and faith identity is "still with us," especially in Jewish-Christian relations. He does not settle the question; he says he lacks time to "sort this out" and closes by saying Paul "stuck a spoke through that wheel" in the first century.

The talk is a conference presentation (Conference on Jewish Christian Relations, Elon College, November 17, 2013). An unnamed introducer gives Tabor's biography at the start, and Tabor carries the rest of the talk. ## Key Teachings

### 1. Philippians 3: circumcision redefined
Tabor opens with Philippians 3, where Paul warns against those who "mutilate the flesh" and claims "we are the true circumcision." Paul then lists his own credentials: circumcised on the eighth day, of Israel, of Benjamin, a Hebrew of Hebrews, and a Pharisee as to the Torah. Tabor reads this as Paul "recasting identity" for a mostly Gentile audience in Philippi, where the true identity is no longer defined by the flesh.

### 2. Romans 2: no partiality and the "double Torah"
Tabor says Romans 2 makes three points. First, God judges "with no partiality": the Jew comes first, and the Greek too, according to works. Second, Tabor describes a "double Torah." He explicitly distinguishes this from the rabbinic oral/written Torah. Here Paul speaks of the written Torah for Jews and the Torah written in the heart for Gentiles who "do by nature what the torah requires," becoming "a torah to themselves." Tabor calls this "a double standard" that "applies to all humankind." Third, Paul argues that the true Jew and true circumcision are inward ("a matter of the heart"), not ethnic or literal. Tabor says the traditional reading of this passage, and his students' reading, reverses ordinary categories: an uncircumcised person who keeps the Torah "will condemn you" who have the written code and break it.

### 3. Romans 2 as a "setup"
Tabor does not take the chapter at face value. He argues that the ideal standard it describes is hypothetical ("it could work that way in an ideal world"). Romans 3 then says "none is righteous, no not one," so no one, including observant Jews, actually does the law. Tabor says Paul uses himself as an example of this failure. He describes the Gentile-righteousness argument as having "a certain hellenistic logic," which makes it persuasive while setting up the universal condemnation that follows.

### 4. Romans 9-10: Israel redefined by confession
Tabor argues Paul makes Israel's identity internal and also "contingent upon confessing Jesus as lord." Unbelieving Jews who "do not bow the knee" are "broken off of the tree of israel," described as "dying branches" that "can get re-grafted again." Tabor notes that Paul is not saying Jews lack belief in God, since "they believe there's one god," but that they have not accepted Jesus as the Messiah. He quotes the confession "believe that god raised them from the dead" as the basis of salvation.

### 5. The apocalyptic framework
Tabor says Paul's radical claims ("neither jew nor greek... there's not even going to be male or female") make sense only within an expectation that the present world is ending. He cites 1 Corinthians 7, "the appointed time has grown very short," and the phrase "the schema of this world," which he takes to mean the social configuration of Jew/Gentile, male/female, bond/free. Paul's practical advice (stay in your condition; marry if you must, but it would be better not to) follows from that expectation. Tabor warns that when the expected transformation did not come, the church moved to a "realized eschatology" in which believers live "already not yet" in a world that has not changed.

### 6. Modern parallels: the Pew survey and the Law of Return
Tabor describes a Pew Research Center survey of American Jews that asked whether Jewishness is ancestry and culture, religion, or both. In his reading, about a quarter said both, and he reports that 22 percent say they have no religion and 68 percent say one need not believe in God to be Jewish. He also reports that 94 percent say a Jew can work on the Sabbath. The caption figures for the other categories are garbled, so these numbers need checking. He then points to a 1989 Israeli Supreme Court ruling on the Law of Return, which he says concerns whether someone who confesses Jesus as Messiah can still claim Jewish status (Tabor is hedging here; his summary is brief). He uses these cases to show that the ethnic-versus-faith question is still open.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Romans 2 as Paul affirming Gentiles who live by conscience apart from the Torah.** Tabor argues the chapter is a "setup": the ideal standard is hypothetical, and Romans 3 says no one meets it.
- **That the "double Torah" means the rabbinic oral and written Torah.** Tabor says he is not talking about the rabbinic distinction; Paul's double standard is written law versus the law written on the heart.
- **That Paul's inward circumcision is a special Christian idea without roots in Judaism.** Tabor says "does not the torah even say moses says circumcise your heart," implying the idea draws on Torah itself (a claim he states without a reference).
- **That Jewish identity is simply ethnic or simply religious.** Tabor uses the Pew survey and the Law of Return to show that the answer is mixed and still contested.
