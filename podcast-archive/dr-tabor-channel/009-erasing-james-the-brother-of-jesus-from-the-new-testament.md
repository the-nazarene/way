## Summary

Tabor asks how James, the brother of Jesus, came to be nearly invisible in the New Testament even though Paul treats him as a central figure, and why a figure so prominent in the early sources is unknown to most students. He opens with the 2002 announcement of the "James son of Joseph, brother of Jesus" ossuary, describes the forgery trial that ran until 2012, and says the inscription could be the first first-century material reference to Jesus of Nazareth. He then turns to the texts. He argues from one saying in the Gospel of Thomas that James was regarded as the rightful leader after Jesus, and from Acts and Galatians that Luke-Acts downplays James while still presenting him as leader. He reads Paul's letters as independent evidence for James's authority and for the Jerusalem church's mission.

Tabor's central claim is that Luke, writing late in the first century, muted James, Mary, Mary Magdalene and John the Baptist because Paul was Luke's hero and the Acts narrative became the standard history. Tabor presents this as his own theory ("I'm going to give you my theory as to why I think that happened"), and he admits that the sources are patchy. He notes that Luke's source Mark names Jesus's brothers and that Luke removed the names. He also says Hegesippus and Josephus report James's death in 62 or 63 (he gives 63), and that the Jerusalem church had a worldwide mission to the circumcision.

The talk ends with the Vatican painting of Peter and Paul, where James is absent. Tabor suggests the artist was conveying that Jesus chose James, not Peter, as successor. He acknowledges this contradicts Matthew 16 and closes by inviting a question. The trial and soil-chemistry material is presented as a side thread, and Tabor's reading of it is his own.

## Key Teachings

### The ossuary and its disputed authenticity
Tabor describes the "James son of Joseph, brother of Jesus" inscription (presented in Aramaic, which he reads as yaakov bar yosef akhui di yeshua) and the surrounding debate. He cites Andre Lemaire's epigraphic judgment that it was valid and says Eric Meyers denounced it at the 2002 SBL meeting for lacking provenance. Tabor reports that Oded Golan was cleared of forgery charges, and that the judge "pronounced not guilty" without arguing that the inscription itself was authentic. He presents the trial testimony on the patina (including Chris Rollston, whom he says still thinks it is a forgery) as the main basis for accepting authenticity, and he says Herschel Shanks's headline "Brother of Jesus Inscription Is Authentic" reflected that view.

### The soil study and the Talpiot tomb
Tabor says a soil-geochemistry study led by Rea Shimron, testing blind samples from some 30 or 40 Jerusalem tombs, concluded that the James ossuary "likely comes from the talpio tomb." He says the study got little attention and that he has not pushed it in major media. He also mentions a DNA test he has commissioned but withholds the results. He is clear that this is his reading of the study, and he says that if the inscription refers to Jesus of Nazareth it would be the first first-century material reference to him.

### Which James? Sorting the New Testament figures
Tabor separates several James figures: James son of Zebedee (the "son of thunder" who is later beheaded by Herod Agrippa in Acts 12), James son of Alphaeus ("James the less" or "James the younger"), James the brother of Joseph, and James the brother of the Lord. He argues that Jesus's brother James is the one who matters and that students' unfamiliarity with him is the problem. He also describes the three Catholic and Protestant readings of "brothers of Jesus" (children of Mary, children of Joseph's brother Clopas, or children of Joseph by an earlier marriage) to set up his argument that the brother is a historical figure, not a cousin.

### The Gospel of Thomas saying
Tabor cites a saying in which the disciples ask who will lead after Jesus leaves, and Jesus replies: "go to james the just for whose sake heaven and earth came into being." He credits April DeConick for dating the core of Thomas to a Jerusalem-linked Judeo-Christian stratum, and he reads the saying through the Jewish idea of righteous people who sustain the world (Genesis 18, the ten righteous; the thirty-six hidden righteous of later Jewish tradition; Malachi 4:6). He presents the saying as evidence that James was regarded as the one for whose sake the world stands.

### Luke-Acts mutes James while presenting him as leader
Tabor argues that Luke presents James as in charge (Acts 15, where James issues the decision on Gentiles, and Acts 21, where Paul reports to James and the elders) but never identifies him as the Lord's brother. He says Luke removed the names of Jesus's brothers that appear in Mark 6:3 and that Acts 1:14 is the last mention of Mary. He reads the same pattern for Mary Magdalene, John the Baptist (Luke does not explicitly say Jesus was baptized by John) and Peter, who fades by Acts 10-11. He dates Luke-Acts to the 90s or later, citing Arthur Droge and Talbert, and says that by then James had been dead for decades and that no church histories like Hegesippus or Eusebius existed to correct Luke.

### Paul's letters as independent evidence
Tabor says Paul is the earliest witness to James's authority. In Galatians 1, Paul says he "saw none of the other apostles except james the lord's brother." In Galatians 2, James's representatives come to Antioch, Peter withdraws from eating with Gentiles, and Paul rebukes him. Tabor also cites 1 Corinthians 9 (Paul becoming "as a Jew" to Jews) and Galatians 2's "pillars" (James, Cephas and John) who gave the right hand of fellowship. He notes that Paul lists an appearance of the risen Jesus to James (1 Corinthians 15, which he does not cite by chapter in the talk but which he says is in the early tradition) and that Paul mentions the Lord's brothers travelling with their wives. He says the Jerusalem church saw its mission as going to the circumcision worldwide.

### Successor and the Vatican painting
Tabor closes with the 1st-century succession story from Hegesippus, James's death by being thrown from the temple pinnacle and then stoned and beaten (he says Josephus also has a passage on James's murder), and the painting of Peter and Paul with Jesus and no James. He suggests the painting's artist may have meant that Jesus chose James, not Peter. He acknowledges that this contradicts Matthew 16.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"James" in the New Testament means James the fisherman.** Tabor says students assume this, but the James who leads the Jerusalem church is Jesus's brother, and James son of Zebedee is beheaded in Acts 12.
- **Peter and Paul were the leaders of early Christianity.** Tabor argues James was in charge in Jerusalem, as Acts 15 and 21 and Galatians 2 show, even though Luke-Acts downplays him.
- **The brothers of Jesus must be children of Mary.** Tabor lists three interpretations (children of Mary; children of Joseph's brother Clopas; children of Joseph by an earlier marriage) and argues the brother is historically a full brother, citing the name statistics.
- **The James ossuary is a forgery.** Tabor says Golan was acquitted and the judge did not find forgery, though Tabor acknowledges some scholars, including Rollston, still doubt it.
- **Luke's account is the definitive history of early Christianity.** Tabor argues Luke wrote late, without earlier church histories to check against, and that Acts reshapes the record around Paul.
