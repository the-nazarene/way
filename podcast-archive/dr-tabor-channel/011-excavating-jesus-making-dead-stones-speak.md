## Key Teachings

### Jesus as a builder, not a carpenter
Tabor argues that the Greek *tekton* means builder more broadly than "carpenter," and cites the English word "architect" as its relative. He notes that Joseph is also called a *tekton* and that Jesus's sayings refer often to building houses, foundations and stones, but never to making a plow or wooden objects. His conclusion is that a stonemason is "a good guess," which he says "would fit *tekton*." He presents this as an inference and says "I can't prove it."

### Sepphoris, its destruction in 4 BC, and Jesus's work
Tabor describes Sepphoris (which he calls Cephas, using the Hebrew "Sippuri") as the capital of Galilee, about four miles north of Nazareth. He says Josephus called it "the jewel of all Galilee" and that Roman forces burned it in 4 BC after rebels, led by one named Judas, broke into its armory when Herod the Great died. He reasons that rebuilding under Herod Antipas would have offered work to a young stonemason from Nazareth. He sketches the daily walk from Nazareth over the hill and says "I'm picturing jesus every morning at 4 or 5 a.m walking to cephas," while noting this is conjecture. He also suggests a link between the theater at Sepphoris and the Sermon on the Mount's "hypocrites," which in Greek he says means "play actor."

### Capernaum as a base, and Peter's house
Tabor says Jesus, after his baptism and temptation, moved his mother and brothers (Joseph having apparently died or disappeared) to Capernaum, which he takes to be Jesus's strategic headquarters on the Sea of Galilee. He argues that an excavated first-century house under the later octagonal church is "good reason" to be considered Peter's house. He ties it to Mark 2, the paralytic let down through the roof. He reconstructs a roof with beams, straw and mud to illustrate the story, and notes the Jesus boat (dated to the first century by carbon dating, in his telling) as a reference to Peter's fishing life in Luke.

### John the Baptist at Suba, and the proposed Jesus's last winter
Tabor describes a cave at Suba (near Jerusalem) excavated with Shimon Gibson from 2000 onward. He says the cave's walls carry an image he identifies as John the Baptist, with a staff, hair in a bun, and a platter with a head, and that the cave was a large public *mikveh* with first-century pottery. He says "John the baptist has his cave been found" and that a major publication was planned. He also argues that John 3's Ainon near Salim is where Jesus baptized "in the north" before he crossed the Jordan to the wadi Cherith (Elijah's hiding place), which he says he and students identified east of the Jordan as "wadi Yabis," where he places Jesus's last winter, December 29 CE. These are identifications he offers and promotes; he does not present them as settled.

### Golgotha on the Mount of Olives
Tabor argues that Jesus was crucified on the summit of the Mount of Olives rather than at the traditional Holy Sepulchre site. His reasons include Josephus's statement that the Romans crucified "outside the gate of the city" so everyone could see, the mass crucifixions in the revolt, and a path up and down the Mount of Olives. He says "I didn't come up with this" but that he is convinced. He cites the crucified man Yochanan's heel-bone nail (ossuary) as evidence of crucifixion practice. He admits that "90 percent of scholars" disagree with him.

### The family tombs: Talpiot, James, Caiaphas and Simon of Cyrene
Tabor describes the Tomb of the Shroud (a freshly robbed tomb in 2000 with a preserved shroud and an ossuary family DNA profile), then the ossuary inscribed "Joseph son of Caiaphas" (which he says is "most likely" the high priest's). He discusses an ossuary he associates with Simon of Cyrene, linking "Simon" and "Alexander" on an inscription to Mark's name for Simon's sons. He discusses the James ossuary ("James son of Joseph, brother of Jesus") and calls it controversial. He discusses the Talpiot tomb with names "Jesus son of Joseph" and "Mary," calling it a long shot but noting "no other tomb" out of 900 has a fitting cluster of names. He says that if the James ossuary and the Talpiot material belong together "it would pretty well be what we call a closed case." He later says a DNA study shows "affinity between the james ossuary and yeshua" and that he does not "stand or fall" on the identification.

### Resurrection as a transformed body, and Jesus's possible marriage
In the Q&A, Tabor argues that the earliest Christian view of resurrection was not a revived corpse. He cites Paul's seed analogy: what is sown is a physical body that returns to dust, and what rises is a glorified, spiritual body. He says Paul described the seed analogy and admitted "I have no idea" what the risen body would be like. He argues that Paul's own report of his encounter (Acts 9) and the appearance stories are consistent with an epiphany. He concedes that many disagree and that he is "not talking about whether there was a resurrection." On marriage, Tabor says "I've changed my mind, I think he was," mainly because Paul praises celibacy while citing Jesus only for other points.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus was a carpenter.** Tabor argues *tekton* means builder, and that Jesus was probably a stonemason, not a woodworker.
- **The Holy Sepulchre is the site of the crucifixion.** Tabor says most scholars favor it, but he argues for the Mount of Olives.
- **Resurrection meant a revived corpse.** Tabor says the earliest view, based on Paul, was a glorified, transformed body, and that the tombs need not be contradicted by this view.
- **Jesus's family tomb would be in Nazareth.** Tabor argues that Jewish burial law required burial where the person died, and that Joseph of Arimathea would have provided the tomb.
