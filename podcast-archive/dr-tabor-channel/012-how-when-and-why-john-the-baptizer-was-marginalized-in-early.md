## Summary

Tabor asks how John the Baptist came to be marginalized in early Christianity, and what he was in his own time and place. He argues that the canonical "master narrative" pushed John into a subordinate role as the forerunner of Jesus, and that careful reading of older or non-canonical material can recover John as a leading messianic figure. His main lines of evidence are Josephus's *Antiquities* 18.116-119 passage on John's execution, which Tabor treats as largely authentic; the Q sayings preserved in Matthew and Luke, which he takes as the earliest tradition; a 14th-century Hebrew Matthew (Shem Tov version) that he says preserves readings free of later Christian theology; the Gospel of John's account of Jesus and John baptizing in Judea; and a cave at Ein Karem that he and Shimon Gibson excavated in 2000, which he identifies as a John the Baptist site with first-century ritual use. He also draws on the Mandaeans, who trace their faith to John. Tabor presents his reconstruction as a proposal, not settled fact, and says several items (the Jesus-John "twin movement," the cave identification) are still awaiting academic publication.

The talk ends with Tabor's personal reflection. He says that John, Jesus and James were murdered figures, that Jesus must have grieved deeply for his cousin, and that Jesus "picked up the gauntlet and he carried it on" until his own crucifixion. He closes by saying he does not want John or Mary to "fade."

## Key Teachings

### 1. "Baptist" as a translation problem
Tabor opens by saying "Baptist" conjures Christian denominations and misses the sense of the Hebrew *Yokanan hamathir*, which he renders as "John the dipper," linked to Jewish ritual immersion. He stresses that John's baptism was "a baptism of repentance for the remission of sins" and that a later theology which asks who can forgive sins is anachronistic for John's own day. He describes the baptism as flowing-water immersion, distinct from the *mikvah* ritual purity bath, and says Josephus frames it as purifying "your soul" and not just the body.

### 2. Josephus's testimony and the Herodian background
Tabor treats the Josephus passage (*Antiquities* 18.116-119, which he cites as "18 119") as the most reliable source on John. He reads it in a stripped-down form, removing the phrases he regards as Christian additions, and argues that no part of the John testimony is interpolated. He says he has students read it from *The Jewish War* and from his own translation. He sets the John account against the war between Herod Antipas and the Nabatean king Aretas, which he says Josephus connects to John's death. He gives two reasons Josephus reports for the execution: John's "great influence" and the fear of a revolt. Tabor accepts that the marriage dispute with Herodias may also have been a factor, and he links the Gospel of Mark's birthday-party story to this. He portrays John as a mass revival preacher and a popular, famous figure. He notes that Josephus places the beheading at Machaerus, though the method is not described in the text.

### 3. Jesus as a disciple of John: the "one movement" thesis
Tabor's central claim is that the Jesus movement "picks up from the john the baptist movement" and that they formed "one movement" in Judea and Galilee under the Herods, with branches such as Qumran and the John movement. He argues from the Gospel of John that several of the twelve were first disciples of John, that Jesus was baptized by John and joined his movement, and that the two ran a joint baptizing mission in Judea (John 3:22-26 is implied, though he cites only the last verse of John 10 and the Judean setting). He says that the Gospel of John's own verse about Jesus not baptizing himself indicates the awkwardness of this picture. He presents the "two messiahs" model, priestly (John) and Davidic (Jesus), and cites the Qumran expectation of a priestly messiah. He says his view is "heretical to most people" and that he does not claim one is greater than the other, while also stating that once John is arrested Jesus "picked up the gauntlet."

### 4. The Q material and John's ethical teaching
Tabor relies on the "saying source" (Q), which he takes as the earliest strata in Matthew and Luke, and which Mark lacks. He treats Luke 3 (the crowds asking "what should we do," the two-coats teaching, the tax-collector and soldier instructions) as Q material that Matthew omits to downplay John. He argues that Luke 7's "what did you go out to see" passage contains John's exalted status ("more than a prophet") and that the statement "none is greater than John" is a Christian interpolation, which he says would need a Q text untouched by Christian copyists to confirm. He also proposes that the Lord's Prayer in Q (Luke 11) is what Jesus taught "as John taught his disciples." He describes John's austerity (a Nazirite-like life, eating what grows naturally, possibly celibate) and compares it to Josephus's own teacher, whom the transcript calls "venus" (presumably Bannus), with Eden-style diet.

### 5. Hebrew Matthew and the textual evidence for an earlier John
Tabor cites George Howard's work on the Shem Tov Hebrew Matthew, which he says is a 14th-century text (copied by Ibn Shaprut) and preserves readings independent of the Greek Matthew. He lists three variants: the Hebrew omits the phrase "none has risen greater than John"; it reads "all the prophets and the lost spoke concerning john," where Greek reads "all the prophets and the law prophesied until John"; and it has Elijah "will come and save all the world," where the Greek says Elijah "does come and restore all things." He argues that the Hebrew readings present John as the one who introduces the Davidic messiah, which he regards as the original view. He acknowledges the variant "could even be a scribal mistake" and notes the Hebrew Matthew's "shaky" textual history.

### 6. The "fading" of John across the Gospels
Tabor presents a sequence: in Mark, Jesus is baptized with no objection; in Matthew, John protests ("I need to be baptized by you"); in Luke, John is in prison before Jesus's baptism and the heavens open "when jesus had also been baptized"; in John's Gospel, John becomes the witness who does not baptize Jesus and says "behold the lamb." He calls this a "successive fading" in which John becomes the one who points to the main figure. He says Acts 2's 3,000 baptisms "for the remission of sins" raise the question of whether the apostles were baptized by John, and he says the later view that there is "christian baptism" and "john baptism" does not hold up.

### 7. The Mary-Elizabeth connection and the Elijah expectation
Tabor reads Luke 1:39-80 as placing Mary in the hill country of Judea at Ein Karem, the village he identifies as John's home, and suggests that Mary's kinswoman Elizabeth was priestly, so Mary has a priestly relative. He imagines that the two boys grew up together and that Mary attended John's birth. He argues that Jesus's withdrawal after John's death (Mark 6) reflects his study of Isaiah's suffering servant passages, and that in the transfiguration Jesus tells the disciples that "elijah has come" and they "did to him whatever they pleased." He identifies Malachi's Elijah passage as the prophecy of the day of the Lord, and he reads Zechariah 13 ("awake, O sword, against my shepherd") as referring to John's sword death, with the sheep scattered and two-thirds of the land cut off as referring to the Roman war.

### 8. The Ein Karem cave and the Mandaean connection
Tabor describes the cave excavation at Ein Karem (begun March 2000 with Shimon Gibson and a UNC Charlotte team). He says the walls bear an image of John holding a staff, with Nazirite hair bound up, and a basin for pouring water, and he says the image's face was later defaced, perhaps by Muslim iconoclasts or Byzantine iconoclasts. He reports first-century Roman pottery in the lower layers, and steps down to a bedrock "foot" shape that he interprets as a site for foot anointing before immersion. He links the Mandaean rite (a shallow-water immersion with a staff, followed by pouring water over the head) to the cave's use. He describes the Byzantine coin image as showing Elizabeth fleeing with the infant John, which he connects to the cave. He calls the identification "incontrovertible" but also says a formal academic volume is "going to publish" later. Herschel Shanks, he says, "was never convinced of this" but may come to accept it.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Baptist" means the same thing as the Christian denomination.** Tabor says the title is better rendered "John the dipper" or "John the baptizer," linked to Jewish immersion practice.
- **John was only a forerunner with no independent role.** Tabor argues John was a major messianic leader in his own right and that Jesus was his disciple.
- **Jesus never joined or worked with John.** Tabor says the Gospels of Mark, Matthew and Luke, and especially John, show a joint mission and that the later church faded this.
- **The Josephus testimony on John is wholly reliable and uninterpolated.** Tabor argues it is an authentic account, while the Jesus passage (the Testimonium) contains Christian additions.
- **Jesus's messiahship required a single messiah.** Tabor says Qumran and his reading support a two-messiah model (priestly and Davidic).
- **The Hebrew Matthew is just a late copy of the Greek.** Tabor argues it preserves earlier, independent readings, while acknowledging its uncertain textual history.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor does not discuss Paul. His focus is on the pre-Pauline and pre-Gospel John movement, the Q material, and Jesus's joining it.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Not discussed beyond the closing reference to James being murdered alongside John and Jesus.
- **The Nazarenes/Ebionites/Essenes:** Qumran is mentioned for its priestly messiah expectation and its teacher who anticipated suffering. Tabor treats Qumran as a branch of one messianic movement.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet and nonviolence:** John's austere diet (he "eats food that comes right out of the ground") and his ethical instructions in Q (sharing with the poor, not being violent to soldiers) are covered. Tabor describes John's ritual immersion as spiritual purity, not ritual purity.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Tabor says John's message was a universal message of the kingdom of God that would "fill the earth," and that Israel's purpose was to be a light to the nations.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** Not discussed.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor argues Jesus saw himself as a co-messiah with John, and that Jesus's messianic anointing came at his baptism. He admits these are contested views.
- **How the Gospels and canon were formed:** This is the talk's main methodological concern. Tabor argues the Gospels were "theological visions" that faded John, and that Hebrew Matthew and Q preserve earlier readings.
