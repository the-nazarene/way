## Summary

Tabor asks what happens to a messianic movement when its leader dies and its expected end-time events do not arrive. He presents the Dead Sea Scrolls as the earliest documented case. Using the Community Rule (1QS), the Damascus Document (CD A and B), and the Habakkuk pesher, he reconstructs a community whose Teacher of Righteousness is dead, whose group has split under a rival called "the Liar," and which expects the messiahs to come within about 40 years. He argues the community computed this window from Daniel 9's 490 years, which he says makes the Teacher's career fit a tenth jubilee. He adds that Josephus describes several violent messianic claimants around 4 BC and 6 AD, and that the Gabriel Revelation stone, which he links to Israel Knohl's work, shows a hope of raising a slain prince after three days. He holds that "dead messiahs are the norm," and that Zechariah 13:7 gave the community a way to read the Teacher's death as scripture-fulfilling.

Tabor then moves to the New Testament. He reads Mark 13 as an expectation of the Son of Man's coming within a generation, which he says was not fulfilled, and he reads Luke 21 as an interim-time reinterpretation that places the end at an unknown point. He casts Paul as an apocalyptic thinker, citing 1 Corinthians 7, and ends with the Branch Davidians as a modern case of the same pattern. He presents these as his reading of the evidence, not as a settled account. He closes by saying that dead messiahs "never return at least when the community expects."

## Key Teachings

### 1. The Community Rule and the Teacher's group before and after his death

Tabor reads the Community Rule (column 8, cited from Geza Vermes's translation) as a founding text in which the group goes into the wilderness to "prepare the way," citing Isaiah 40:3. He stresses that this earlier text names no single leader. The Damascus Document (CD) has two manuscripts: in manuscript B, the group looks back on "20 years" of groping "like blind men" before the Teacher arrived, and in manuscript A there is no mention of the Teacher's arrival. He treats the two versions as a kind of "synoptic" pair that shows the story being rewritten. From [12:26] onward he reads CD column 19 as dating the Teacher's death: "from the day of the gathering in of the teacher" to the destruction of the wicked is "about 40 years." He notes that the phrase "about" is not exact, and that he prefers the more literal "gathering" to "death" in translation.

### 2. The 490-year scheme and the "about 40 years" window

Tabor says the community used Daniel 9's 70 weeks of years, which equal 490 years, and that they counted this as ten jubilees. On his reading, the Teacher arrived in a particular week of the tenth jubilee, leaving "just a little more than 40 years to go." He estimates a career of about nine years, and says he would date the Teacher to the first century BC, "maybe around 75 to 50," while admitting the Teacher's name is not known. He cites 11Q Melchizedek as also using the jubilee framework. He suggests Paul's "the appointed time has grown very short" and John the Baptist's "the time is fulfilled" may reflect similar calculating, though he calls this speculative.

### 3. Zechariah 13:7 and the dead shepherd

At [20:12], Tabor says manuscript B adds a quotation of Zechariah 13:7 ("smite the shepherd and the sheep shall be scattered") that manuscript A lacks. He reads this as the community's way of making sense of the Teacher's death: scripture predicted a smitten shepherd, so the scattering of followers is also predicted. He also says that if Allegro is right the Teacher may have been crucified, but he prefers to "just have him dead." He notes that Mark quotes the same verse on the way to Gethsemane and then narrates an arrest and crucifixion. He explicitly denies that this means Jesus had Qumran links or copied the scroll. He argues that such messianic texts were "in the air" across Palestinian messianism from the Maccabees to Bar Kokhba, as Eisenman has argued (per Tabor).

### 4. Josephus's messiahs and the Gabriel Revelation

Tabor reads Josephus (Jewish War, book 2, and Antiquities) as describing a series of messianic claimants who were killed. These include Simon, a royal slave who burned the palace at Jericho and was cut down by Gratus; Athronges, whose hopes rested on physical strength and who had four brothers; and Judas the Galilean, who in 6 AD led a revolt over taxes. He calls Josephus's accounts political and rhetorical. He discusses Israel Knohl's book (named in the talk as "Knoll") on messiahs and resurrection in the Gabriel Revelation, which he associates with a stone found in Jordan that may mark the site of Simon's death. The text commands that the "prince of princes" be raised "by three days." Tabor says there is no evidence in Josephus that the raising happened. He suggests "three days" may be metaphorical, drawing on Hosea 6:6, and says he argued this in *The Jesus Dynasty* that Jesus used the same idea.

### 5. Habakkuk pesher: the delay and the three stages of apocalypticism

At [37:18] Tabor reads the Habakkuk pesher (1QpHab, from cave one) as the community's response to delay. Habakkuk's "the vision will tarry" is applied to "the final age," which "shall be prolonged." The commentary identifies the one who "doubts the vision" as someone whose "soul is puffed up," and who says he is tired of waiting and will marry and buy land. The commentary also speaks of faith and suffering among "those who observe the torah." Tabor names a three-stage pattern: (1) acute imminent expectation, shown in Paul's 1 Corinthians 7; (2) recalculation because the end is taking longer; (3) acknowledgment that the timing is God's and not human. He presents Luke's Acts 1:6 question and answer as stage three, and Mark 13's "this generation" as stage one.

### 6. Mark 13, Luke 21, and Paul as apocalyptic

Tabor dates Mark as the earliest Gospel and reads Mark 13's "truly I tell you this generation will not pass away" as a fresh expectation that "was not fulfilled." He argues that the community "lived with that" through 70 and into the 80s or 90s. He says Luke 21 rewrites the desolating sacrilege as Jerusalem surrounded by armies, and that its "until the times of the Gentiles are fulfilled" makes the interim open-ended. He says he is "almost willing" to date Luke into the second century, following Arthur Droge. He reads Paul's letters as shaped by the crisis under Caligula and Nero (per Josephus), and says "you cannot understand Paul except... as the apocalyptic Paul." He ends by saying "what they most expected to happen did not happen," while Rome "triumphed," and that Matthew's "lo I am with you always" reflects a mission-based response to delay.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Dead messiahs are unique to Jesus.** Tabor says "a dead messiah is something new only jesus is a suffering messiah" is a misreading; dead messiahs are the norm, citing Josephus and the Damascus Document.
- **Jesus copied the Dead Sea Scrolls community.** He says the parallels do not mean Jesus was part of Qumran or copied the scroll, but that the texts were widely available in the messianic culture of the period. He mentions that some people found the parallels so striking that they called it copying "like cheated or something."
- **The Dead Sea Scrolls only became usable after 1992.** Tabor says the general parameters of the movement can be drawn from scrolls published in the 1950s, and the 1992 release mainly completed the picture.
- **Jesus taught that the time of the end could not be known.** Tabor says the saying "it's not for you to know the times and the seasons" reflects Luke's despair over calculations, not Jesus's view; he guesses Jesus "would have said it's coming" soon.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor reads Paul as "the apocalyptic Paul," shaped by the Caligula and Nero crises, with 1 Corinthians 7 showing imminent expectation. He raises, but does not settle, whether Paul's "appointed time" language reflects calculations learned from James or Jesus.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor speculates that James may have learned end-time calculations from Jesus and passed them on. He presents this as a possibility, not a finding.
- **The Nazarenes, Ebionites, and Essenes:** The talk is centered on the Qumran (Essene-associated) community and its Teacher. Tabor says he is "not claiming" the Teacher's group is the whole of the movement, and he presents the Qumran identification as controversial.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet, nonviolence:** The Community Rule's halakhic disputes over purity and divorce are discussed. Tabor notes that the group accused Jerusalem's priests of charging for sacrifices, disqualifying animals, and profaning the temple.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** The expectation of the Son of Man's coming and a "righteous kingdom of God" within about 40 years is central to the talk.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** The Gabriel Revelation's three-day raising of a slain prince is discussed as a resurrection hope; Tabor links it to Hosea 6:6 and to Jesus's "third day" language, which he reads as metaphorical.
- **How the Gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor dates Mark earliest and reads Luke 21 as an interim-time rewrite of Mark 13, with Luke possibly into the second century.
