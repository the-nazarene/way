## Summary

Tabor's 2009 lecture, given as part of a Biblical Archaeology Society seminar, works through the basic questions about the Dead Sea Scrolls: when they were found, where, what they contain, and who wrote them. He treats the Qumran settlement and the scroll caves as one connected find. He argues that the scrolls were written by the group the ancient writer Josephus calls the Essenes, and he says he generally agrees with the scholarly consensus while adding his own details. His main evidence is the site's layout and finds (a pantry of matching cups, a large male cemetery oriented north-south, what he reads as pottery production, and latrines placed outside the camp), the scrolls' own content (the Community Rule, the Habakkuk Pesher, the War Scroll, and the Isaiah scroll), and the 68 CE ostracon from Qumran. He also argues that the Qumran group and the Jesus movement were two wings of one Jewish messianic movement in Palestine, which he sees as spanning the Maccabees to Bar Kokhba.

Tabor separates two questions: whether the scrolls belong to Qumran, and whether the group that wrote them is known from other sources. He answers yes to both and says Josephus's descriptions of the Essenes do not match the scrolls' theology, so the sources should not be read as identical. He ends by urging listeners to read Flint and VanderKam, Magness, and the scrolls themselves, and to use the Biblical Archaeology Review archive for the continuing debate.

## Key Teachings

### The scrolls, the caves, and the Qumran connection
Tabor gives the basic numbers: the scrolls were found between 1947 and 1956 in 11 caves near Qumran (he notes that Charlesworth now says only 10 held scrolls). He describes the surviving material as a library of more than 900 documents representing as many as 350 separate works, many known only from fragments. Cave 4 alone, he says, contains about 520 texts in about 15,000 fragments. He calls the Qumran connection "appears to be connected" and compares it to finding family records in a backyard near the family's cemetery. He acknowledges naysayers, naming Norman Golb among them, and says he thinks their view is incorrect.

### Qumran as a sectarian center, not a villa or fortress
Tabor reads the site as a spiritual headquarters with strict ritual purity rules. He points to a pantry with about 48 cups and 47 saucers, all alike and undecorated, which he reads as a sign of purity concerns and self-supply. He argues for pottery manufacture, citing the clay facility, and rejects the country villa and military fortress readings. He also describes a cemetery of about 1,100 shaft graves, mostly male, with no jewelry, weapons, or grave goods, and buried north-south with the feet toward Jerusalem. He suggests the orientation may reflect expectation of light from the north, based on Isaiah 9. He presents this as a guess he cannot prove. He notes that the few female burials were later Bedouin burials. He also identifies latrines northwest of the camp and links them to a rule that waste must be kept outside the camp.

### Identifying the group with the Essenes
Tabor says the group that wrote the scrolls is "otherwise known as the essenes," which he takes from Josephus, Pliny, and Philo. He stresses that Josephus's portrait does not match the scrolls on doctrine. Josephus, he says, describes the Essenes without apocalyptic or messianic content, and he suggests Josephus was writing for a Roman audience and framing Jewish groups through familiar Greek philosophical categories (Pythagoreans, Stoics, Epicureans). Tabor says the sociology, organization, and rituals match closely, citing the sacred meals and communal life that Flint and VanderKam list. He also notes unresolved differences, including pacifism, celibacy, slavery, sacrifice, and the calendar.

### One messianic movement with two wings
Tabor argues that the Qumran group and the Jesus movement belong to the same "messianic movement of palestine from the maccabees to barcokba." Both shared expectations that God would redeem his people, gather the lost tribes, raise up a messiah, bring judgment, and raise the dead. They differed in how to live that out. He places Qumran on one wing with strict Torah observance, and places Jesus, John, and James on a more open wing, with contact with Samaritans and women and a less strict approach to purity. He lists "seven parallels," including separation and preparing the way in the wilderness, waiting for the messiahs, a new covenant, a baptism distinct from ordinary mikvah, and the community as the temple. He also suggests that John the Baptist's baptism and a Jesus baptizing near Qumran are possibilities, though he presents the latter as speculation.

### The 68 CE ostracon
Tabor describes a 16-line inscribed potsherd found at Qumran in January 1996, written in the "year two" of the revolt and dated to 68 CE. It records a man named Khoni giving his land in Jericho to the yahad, which Tabor glosses as "together" and compares to "church" in Paul's letters. He says the text captures the moment when the Roman Tenth Legion was approaching Jericho. He notes that Norman Golb and others have questioned whether the word reads yahad, but Tabor says he is "pretty sure" it does.

### Ritual law and the Sabbath latrine dilemma
Tabor uses the Qumran latrine rule as an example of how far this group pushed purity law. The Sabbath limit of 2,000 cubits meant that a Qumran member needing a toilet had to either break the Sabbath distance rule or go inside the camp, which the latrine rule forbade. He presents this as the kind of strictness that Josephus reports for the Essenes. He contrasts it with Jerusalem, where he says the Pharisees redefined the "camp" with an eruv-like string around the city. He concludes that the Qumran group "would have hated" Jesus's approach to Torah.

### Burial, resurrection, and the north
Tabor reads the north-facing graves as evidence of resurrection belief: the dead would rise facing the light of the Lord appearing in Jerusalem. He says the group is "taking the resurrection very literally." He ties the north orientation to the messianic expectation drawn from Isaiah 9, which he says was also used by early Christians. He again flags the reasoning as a hypothesis.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The scrolls are unconnected to Qumran.** Tabor says the naysayers (Golb and others) are wrong, and that the scrolls and site belong together.
- **Qumran was a country villa, military fortress, or monastery.** He argues it was a sectarian spiritual center with pottery production and a large cemetery, and he says the monk comparison (de Vaux) only holds in a general sense.
- **The Essene portrait in Josephus matches the scrolls.** He says it does not, because Josephus left out apocalyptic and messianic content and wrote for a Roman audience.
- **Women were buried in the Qumran cemetery.** He says the few female burials were later Bedouin, so the cemetery is essentially male.
- **The scrolls are controlled by a denominational agenda.** He says the team of Tov and Ulrich works with the same historical method and no theological agenda.
- **Jesus was an Essene.** He says Jesus "wasn't in this scene," though he finds many parallels with the movement.
