## Summary

Tabor sets out to explain how ancient Jewish groups counted time, arguing that calendar disputes were among the most consequential conflicts of the late Second Temple period because the feasts, Sabbaths and Jubilee cycles all depended on the calendar. He first sets aside the modern printed Jewish calendar, which he describes as a calculated system fixed after the fall of the Temple, and focuses on the two competing calendars of the time: a solar-lunar system tied to new moons and the seasons, and a solar calendar of 52 weeks found in the Dead Sea Scrolls and the book of Jubilees.

Tabor's central argument is that the solar calendar, with its fixed 364-day year and weekday-aligned festivals, gave the Essene or sectarian groups a sense of divine harmony that the moon-based system lacked, and that this difference was strong enough to motivate schism. He cites the Damascus Document's language about "the exact determination of the times" and the Habakkuk pesher's account of a priest who attacked a group on its Yom Kippur fast. He also reports, without endorsing it, a scholarly suggestion (attributed to Jaubert and Pixner) that Jesus may have followed the Essene calendar, which would explain differences between John and the Synoptics on the timing of Passover. He says he has "never been convinced" of this.

The talk ends with Tabor's proposed "observed" calendar, based on two rules: sight the new moon to begin each month, and decide on a thirteenth month by checking whether the barley will be ripe for Passover. He presents this as the approach of the Karaites (whom he calls "the keritz" in the captions), and closes by likening the rabbinic and Karaite divide to a Catholic/Protestant contrast between tradition and text.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Two competing calendars in Second Temple Judaism
Tabor says the calendar was contested between "two competing calendars": a solar-lunar system and a solar system. The solar-lunar calendar is anchored in Genesis 1:14, where the sun and moon serve as "signs, seasons, days and years," and where the word for seasons (moadim) is also the word for festivals or appointments. He argues that the Torah's festival dates (Leviticus 23) are "divine appointments" that require knowing the correct day of the month. The printed modern Jewish calendar, which he calls the "Hillel calendar," is not the subject; he says it is a calculated system that often differs from the actual new moon by a day or two or more.

### 2. The arithmetic problem
Tabor walks through the astronomical mismatch. A solar year is about 365.24 days; twelve lunar months are about 354 days, leaving roughly 11 days of drift per year. He illustrates this with Ramadan, which he says moves about 11 days a year relative to the seasons. To keep Passover in spring, a solar-lunar system must intercalate. In the rabbinic scheme he describes, a 19-year cycle contains seven leap years with an extra (thirteenth) month, placed in years 3, 6, 8, 11, 14, 17 and 19. He notes this "sounds kind of cumbersome" but says it roughly works.

### 3. The Essene (Jubilees) solar calendar
In the solar system, Tabor argues, the word for "month" (he gives the Hebrew as "kodesh or kadosh") means "new" rather than "moon," so Exodus 12's "first of the months" can be read as a new 30-day period rather than a lunar month. The sectarian calendar then has 12 months of 30 days, giving 360 days, with extra days added (he says 31-day months appear in months 3, 6, 9 and 12). Every date falls on a fixed weekday every year, so, in his words, "every day of the month corresponds to a day of the week forever." New Year falls on a Wednesday, the first Wednesday after the vernal equinox, which he links to Genesis 1:14 and the creation of the luminaries on the fourth day. Shavuot always falls on a Sunday, the 15th of the third month. Tabor says scholars disagree on how the group handled the leftover quarter-day; some think they ignored it, which he notes would have been a benefit to an apocalyptic group that read the seasons' drift as a sign of the end.

### 4. The sectarian motive: harmony, schism, and conflict
Tabor argues that the solar calendar's sense of harmony helped make it a marker of sectarian identity. He reads the Community Rule and the Damascus Document as describing a "remnant" that "held fast to the commandments" and, in Damascus Document column 3, a people who "unfolded to them his holy sabbath and his glorious feasts." He cites column 16 for the phrase "the exact determination of their times." He speculates that the teacher of righteousness straightened out the calendar, and that the "corrupt priests in Jerusalem" who used moon-based reckoning were judged for it.

He reads the Habakkuk pesher (Habakkuk 2:15) as describing a "wicked priest" who, on the Day of Atonement, "appeared before them to confuse them and cause them to stumble on the day of fasting." Tabor takes this as evidence of a calendar dispute turning violent: groups keeping the feast on different days could be attacked on a fast day. He draws a comparison to the 1973 Yom Kippur War, when an attack came during a national fast. He acknowledges the group's solar calendar did not last, and he attributes its failure to the deep tradition, expressed through many references to the new moon, that the new moon was to be observed.

### 5. Passover, the Gospels, and a speculative Essene link
Tabor reports the suggestion, attributed to Annie Jaubert and Bargil Pixner, that the discrepancy between John and the Synoptics about the timing of the Passover meal could reflect different calendars, with Jesus perhaps following the Essene calendar, so that the Passover meal fell on a Tuesday night while others kept it on Thursday or Friday. He describes the sociological difference of celebrating on different days, including Yom Kippur, but says he has "never been convinced that that's the case."

### 6. Early Christian Easter controversy
Tabor sketches the second-century dispute over setting Easter. Churches in the East kept Easter as the Sunday after the Jewish Passover, observed by watching what the Jews did; Tabor says Christians "had to ask the Jews when Passover was." He describes Asian churches tracing their practice to John, Polycarp and Papias, and the bishops of Rome, who in the West pressed to tie Easter to the vernal equinox. He says the Western churches excommunicated the Asian churches, and that the great church eventually accepted a floating date, with small dissident groups keeping Passover.

### 7. Proposed observed calendar (two rules)
Tabor proposes a calendar that needs no printing or calculation. Rule one: sight the new moon to begin each month, counting twelve months per year. Rule two: after twelve, decide whether to add a thirteenth month by checking the barley harvest in the land of Israel. If the barley will be ripe in time for the Passover waving of the first-fruit sheaf, no month is added; if it will not be ripe within about fifteen days, a thirteenth month is added. He says this method keeps the seasons aligned, and that it is "the calendar Abraham used," the calendar he thinks the ancients generally used, and the one the Karaites return to. He does not claim the Karaites are a direct continuation of anything; he presents them as a group trying to follow the text more literally, in contrast to rabbinic tradition.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The printed Jewish calendar tracks the actual new moon.** Tabor says it is a calculated calendar, so its dates can differ from the observed new moon by a day, two days, or more. Observed and printed calendars are different systems.
- **The Essene calendar was simply an odd, outlier practice.** Tabor argues it was a coherent system with a real rationale (harmony with creation and the seasons) that motivated schism and even conflict, as the Habakkuk pesher suggests.
