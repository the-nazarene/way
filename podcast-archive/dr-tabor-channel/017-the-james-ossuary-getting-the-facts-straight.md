## Summary

Tabor asks whether the "James ossuary," an inscribed limestone bone box publicized in October 2002, could be the burial box of James, "brother of Jesus," and whether the charge that it is a modern forgery holds up. He says the public debate has been distorted by media coverage and by scholars who dismissed the object as "too good to be true" without evaluating the evidence. He argues the object is plausibly authentic, relying on comparable finds (the Caiaphas and Simon of Cyrene ossuaries), a statistical study of name frequencies by Camille Fuchs, and his reading of the forgery case, including what he calls the one original patina in the phrase "brother of Jesus." He concedes that provenance is unknown, that he once misstated measurements, and that the case is unresolved. He ends by reporting that Hershel Shanks has said only "Could it be?" and that no scientific evidence yet shows forgery. Tabor says soil-chemistry testing may eventually settle the question.

## Key Teachings

### The "too good to be true" objection is not a sound reason to dismiss the ossuary

Tabor says scholars such as Robert Eisenman and Eric Meyers, along with much of the academy, reacted with "TGBT" (too good to be true) and treated the ossuary as not worth serious attention. He answers that finds of this kind do occur. He cites the Joseph son of Caiaphas ossuary (publicized in 1990, now in the Israel Museum), which bears a name tied to the high priest of the Jesus trial, and an ossuary found in 1941 by Sukenik and Avigad in the Kidron Valley, inscribed for "Alexander son of Simon, the Cyrenian," which he links to Mark 15:21. He calls the Simon ossuary "stronger than the James ossuary" because of the place name. He also notes that the Dead Sea Scrolls were once dismissed as impossible.

### Name statistics and the Fuchs study

Tabor uses the Rahmani catalog (roughly 900 ossuaries in the Israel state collection, about 200 inscribed) and Tal Ilan's catalog of Jewish names in Palestine, 200 BC to AD 200. He reports that Joseph occurs in about 7.9% of the male names, Jesus about 4%, and Yakov/Jacob about 2.1%, and says the proportions are similar across both catalogs. He says Fuchs (Tel Aviv Department of Statistics, published in the *Polish Journal of Biblical Research*) modeled a roughly 100-year ossuary period using population, death, birth, and family-size variables. Fuchs's result for the combination James / son of Joseph / brother of Jesus is about 1.71 expected persons, which Tabor glosses as "one and three quarters." He treats this as leaving the identification possible but not certain, and he argues that a person in this family would be unlikely to list a brother unless the brother was notable. He also notes that "brother of" is attested on only one other ossuary, which he calls rare.

### Rebutting the three forgery charges

Tabor says the government's case, as he reads it, rests on three things: that the people involved are lying; that the inscription itself is inauthentic (he says no epigrapher has claimed this); and the patina. On the first, he says the accusations are about personality and motive and he does not believe the people he deals with are lying. On the inscription, he rejects the argument in the Byrne and McNary-Zak volume that Golan pasted letters from three other inscriptions, saying a letter-by-letter comparison shows the letters differ and that the argument is "not strong at all." On the patina, he says that in the trial testimony the only original patina that everyone agrees on is in the phrase "brother of Jesus," which he says changes the forgery question. He also presents a photograph of the ossuary on a shelf with a 1970s-era book and Kodak paper labeled "expire 376," which he says is an authentic-looking photo taken before the controversy, though not conclusive.

### Physical evidence and open questions

Tabor says he once suggested in *The Jesus Dynasty* that the James ossuary might have come from the Talpiot tomb, and he admits this brought him trouble. He says he re-measured the ossuary and that its dimensions are "much closer" to the Talpiot Jesus ossuary than published figures suggest, though not exactly the same, and that measurement varies by where one measures. He shows a photo of the ossuary before cleaning and notes bone material inside, which Oded Golan told Time he had collected; its present whereabouts are unclear. He describes Israeli scientists testing soil and chemical signatures from different tombs in double-blind trials, and he says this may resolve the question.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The ossuary was the work of sensational media (Shanks's hype).** Tabor says the hype came mainly from later television and other coverage, while the original *BAR* article was balanced.
- **"Too good to be true" means it cannot be authentic.** He cites the Caiaphas, Simon of Cyrene, and Dead Sea Scrolls finds to argue such finds occur.
- **James and Jacob are different people.** He says "James" is an English rendering of the Greek Jacobos and Aramaic Yakov, so the name is the same.
- **Jesus was a very common name.** He says about 4% of named males in the sample were Jesus, which is not especially common.
- **Shanks has declared this the bone box of Jesus' brother.** Tabor says Shanks has only asked "Could it be?"
- **Roman Catholic teaching on Jesus' brothers.** Tabor says Catholic dogma holds they were cousins, or children of Joseph from a prior marriage (he gives both variants), which he says is why some people did not recognize James as Jesus' brother.
- **The forgery is proven.** He says the forgery case rests on three arguments, none of which he finds conclusive.
