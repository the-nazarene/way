## Summary

Tabor asks how the Dead Sea Scrolls' copies of Hebrew Bible books differ from the traditional Hebrew text (the Masoretic Text, MT), and what those differences mean. He uses the Dead Sea Scrolls Bible edition by Abegg, Flint and Ulrich as his working text and presents this as an overview for a course he is teaching, with a set of reading recommendations along the way. He notes that about 40% of the scrolls are copies of Hebrew Bible books, that scholars have been comparing them for about 75 years, and that the MT itself has variants, since the standard BHS apparatus shows them on every page. The Esther fragment, which he says was long thought missing, has now been identified (he points to an article at thetorah.com).

His main body is a walk through several passages where the scrolls read differently from the MT: Deuteronomy 5:12 (the Sabbath tied to creation), Deuteronomy 32 (the "sons of Elohim" and the divine council), Isaiah 53 (first-person "we," "his people," and "his soul will see light"), and Malachi 3 (what he reads as two messengers, pointing to a group that expected two messiahs). Some of these readings, he says, agree with the Septuagint or the Samaritan Pentateuch. He then describes the scroll-writing community as an apocalyptic, messianic, "new covenant" group with baptism, separatism and a solar calendar. He links its expectations to a missed 40-year deadline and to Sabbath rules that he says the Jesus movement did not follow.

The talk ends without a formal conclusion. Tabor says the variants should be treasured rather than feared, promotes an upcoming trip to Qumran and Masada, and closes with a Sabbath greeting. The overall tone is an enthusiastic lecture, and the arguments are offered as Tabor's readings, not as settled results.

## Key Teachings

### Variants are the normal state of the Hebrew Bible, not a flaw
Tabor says that even the MT has variant readings, which the BHS apparatus records at the bottom of each page. He compares this to Bart Ehrman's experience with New Testament variants, and warns against swapping one fundamentalism for another. He argues against "Bible code" searches on the grounds that the text being searched is only one copy of the MT. He treats the three main witnesses (MT, Septuagint, Samaritan Pentateuch) plus the scrolls as separate textual traditions.

### Deuteronomy 5:12: the Sabbath grounded in creation
Tabor reads the scroll version of Deuteronomy 5:12 (4QDeuteronomy) as including the creation rationale ("For in six days the Lord made heaven and earth..."), which the MT lacks at that spot. He says Exodus 31 gives the same creation reason, and he notes that the Moses Scroll also does this. He allows two readings: a harmonization of the two reasons (Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5), or a older tradition that the later MT dropped.

### Deuteronomy 32: "sons of Elohim" and the divine council
Deuteronomy 32:8 reads "according to the sons of Elohim" in the scroll, against the MT's "according to the number of the children of Israel." Tabor argues that Deuteronomy 32:43 in the scroll ("bow down to him, all you gods") supports a heavenly-beings reading that parallels Psalm 82. He credits Michael Heiser's work on the *benei elohim* for this background.

### Isaiah 53: first person, "his people," and "see light"
Tabor reads several Isaiah 53 variants from the Great Isaiah Scroll (from Cave 1): "we despised him" in verse 3 (MT: "he was despised"), "his people" in verse 8 (MT: "my people"), and a plural "with rich people his tomb" in verse 9 that he links to the MT's odd plural "deaths." The most important reading for him is verse 11, where the scroll says "his soul will see light." He says the MT has no equivalent and that the Qumran group might have read this as resurrection, given the reference to seeing offspring. He also says some of these readings agree with the Septuagint, which suggests the Greek translators had a text closer to the scrolls.

### Malachi 3: two messengers, two messiahs
In the scroll version of Malachi 3, Tabor reads two figures: a messenger who prepares the way, and "the Lord whom you seek" who comes to his temple, with "the messenger of the covenant." He says this fits the group's belief in two messiahs (a priestly one and a Davidic one), and he cites Zechariah 4 and Zechariah 6 for similar pairings. He also presents a three-figure scheme from the scrolls: a prophet like Moses, a teacher figure who "inaugurated" the covenant, and the two messiahs. He says the group thought their teacher of righteousness had come, and he points to the Habakkuk Pesher as evidence that they read prophecy through their own history.

### The community: apocalyptic, messianic, separatist, and a missed deadline
Tabor describes the scroll group as "an apocalyptic messianic New Covenant group baptizing," which he thinks also describes the Jesus movement. He argues that Josephus's portrait of the Essenes is shaped for a Roman audience and should not be used to define them. He says the sectarian scrolls (about 30% of the collection) are the group's own writings. He reconstructs the group's timeline from the Pesher and Daniel 9's "70 weeks": the teacher is placed in the tenth jubilee week, the end is expected about 40 years after his death, and when it does not come the group decides God has prolonged the time. He compares this to Second Peter's account of God's patience. He suggests that followers of John the Baptist and Jesus may have had parents or grandparents who knew the teacher.

### Sabbath strictness and the Jesus movement
Tabor argues that the scrolls take the Sabbath more strictly than the Pharisees and the Jesus movement. The scrolls forbid rescuing an animal from a pit and require meals to be prepared before the Sabbath. Tabor says the scroll group would have been appalled by the disciples plucking grain (Mark 2 and Matthew 12). He cites Jesus's saying that the Sabbath was made for people, and he presents John the Baptist and James as more accommodating than the scroll group. He also reports a joint finding with a colleague that the Qumran latrines lie about 1,000 to 2,000 cubits from the camp, as the scrolls describe. He says the nearby cemetery is mostly male, which he takes as a sign of a possibly celibate community.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The scroll group wrote all the scrolls.** Tabor says only about 30% are sectarian works by the group. Others, like Jubilees and Enoch, are not by them, and about 40% are Hebrew Bible copies.
- **The Hebrew Bible is a single, fixed text.** Tabor argues that the MT itself has variants, that the Septuagint and Samaritan Pentateuch differ, and that the scrolls add a further tradition.
- **Esther was missing from the scrolls.** Tabor says a fragment has now been identified, though he cautions that the claim is recent and the article is a popular one.
- **The Bible code works on the MT.** Tabor rejects Bible code searches, because the text being searched is only one copy of the MT.
- **Josephus gives an accurate picture of the Essenes.** Tabor argues that Josephus wrote for Vespasian and presents the group in terms of Greek philosophical schools, so the scrolls' self-description should be used instead.
- **The Jesus movement and the scroll group are the same.** Tabor says they share features (wilderness preaching, baptism, sacred meals, a council of twelve) but differ sharply on Torah interpretation, especially the Sabbath.
