## Summary

Tabor asks whether the Gospel of John's geographical details can be tied to specific places in Jordan and Israel, and whether they point to a place where Jesus and, later, the Nazarene community took refuge. He presents this as the most satisfying of his discoveries from some 70 trips to the Holy Land, based on work he began in 1992 and four later trips. He argues that scholars often discount John for its theology, but that its "narrative geographical framework" may be historically useful. He follows John's account of Jesus baptizing near Aenon ("anan salim") in the north while John the Baptist baptized further north, and that Jesus left Jerusalem in winter 29 CE and "went away again across the jordan" to the place where John had baptized (John 10:40). He links this to the Elijah tradition at the Wadi Cherith (1 Kings 17), to Eusebius's report, relying on Hegesippus, that the Nazarenes fled to Pella during the Jewish War, and to Dead Sea Scroll references to hiding places and a "place of refuge." The talk ends with Tabor's own caveat that the evidence is textual and geographic rather than proof. He closes by asking the audience what they think of the "Jesus hideout" idea and by suggesting that Revelation 12's woman who flees "into the wilderness to her place" may echo the same tradition.

Most of the talk is Tabor's own lecture; there is no guest or second speaker. He presents the material as a working theory he still finds "highly plausible," and he says he has not proven it.

## Key Teachings

### 1. John's geographical framework and the early joint baptizing campaign

Tabor argues that John's gospel contains a travel framework that scholars often overlook because they discount the gospel theologically. He points to John's three (possibly four) Passovers, which imply a ministry of roughly three and a half years, compared with Mark's sense of a much shorter period. He highlights material found only in John: Jesus's baptizing alongside John the Baptist in the Judean area, and John 3:22–24 placing John baptizing at Aenon near Salim, with "many waters." Tabor says Jesus and his new followers, including fishermen who later appear in Mark, worked in a "joint" campaign before John's arrest. He reads John 3:26 and 4:1 as indicating that Jesus baptized more people than John, though not personally. He also cites Josephus (Antiquities 18) on John's popularity and Herod's fear of it, to argue that a large Jesus-led campaign would have drawn Roman or Herodian attention.

### 2. Locating Aenon near Salim: Tel Salim and Wadi el-Yabis

Tabor describes a map-based discovery made in one afternoon in 1992. He identifies "anan" (Aenon) near Salim with the Tel Salim, which he says has never been dug. He notes that the Madaba map places this site in the north. He points to a border between Perea/Galilee and the Decapolis, which he says lies just across the Jordan from Tel Salim, so that crossing over would take someone out of Herod's jurisdiction. About seven kilometers south of Pella is the Wadi el-Yabis, which Tabor says Gaetano Palumbo and others connected with ACOR have surveyed for prehistoric remains. He describes the wadi as rugged, with springs, waterfalls, and caves, and presents photographs of water channels and high caves he says could be used for hiding.

### 3. The Brook Cherith, Elijah, and John 10:39–40

Tabor's central textual argument is John 10:39–40. He notes that John places a confrontation in the temple in winter (the Hanukkah/Feast of Dedication period), that Jesus "escaped from their hands," and that he "went away again across the jordan to the place where john had been baptizing and he remained there." He treats this as the key clue that Jesus later went east of the Jordan. He compares this with 1 Kings 17, where Elijah is told to hide in the "brook Cherith" east of the Jordan and is fed by ravens. Tabor argues that the wadi (Wadi el-Yabis) is the Cherith, that Jesus, as a Galilean who knew local sites, might have known this tradition, and that John's account of Lazarus (which he places in the following chapter) may explain why Jesus later returned to Judea. He says Mark does not mention these movements and that he does not see John and Mark as contradictory on this point.

### 4. The Pella flight of the Nazarenes

Tabor presents Eusebius (fourth century), relying on Hegesippus, as reporting that the Nazarenes left Jerusalem around 67–68 CE, during Titus's siege, and fled to Pella across the Jordan. Epiphanius is also cited as repeating the tradition. Tabor notes that many scholars have discounted the Pella tradition, naming S.G.F. Brandon and Gerd Lüdemann (transcribed as "Garrett Ludemann") as examples. Their objections include that a group led by Simon (identified as James's successor after James's death in 62 CE) would not have gone to a Greco-Roman city that Jews had attacked. Tabor answers that the precise geographic name is evidence in its favor, and that Eusebius's main purpose was theological (the Jews who crucified Jesus were punished, while those who followed his warning escaped). He argues that Pella was probably a district the refugees were in, rather than the city itself, and that a fleeing group would likely avoid entering a city.

### 5. Qumran, the Essene quarter, and the pottery connection

Tabor links three sites he says he has spent his career on: Qumran, the Essene quarter on Mount Zion in Jerusalem, and Wadi el-Yabis. He argues that the Essene quarter might have become a Nazarene stronghold, and that the group which fled Jerusalem may have carried pottery typical of Qumran. He cites the Copper Scroll's list of hiding places, some across the Jordan, and mentions "Kolit" (also in the Babylonian Talmud as an area Alexander Jannaeus defeated east of the Jordan). He cites the Damascus Document's references to the "teacher" fleeing to his place of refuge, and Stephen Goranson's 1991 SBL paper arguing that "land of Damascus" refers to a region east of the Jordan, which Tabor says Jerome also reflects when describing Ebionite-type groups. He describes Mount Zion probes by Bargil Pixner and a Mount Zion project of his own, and proposes chemical analysis of pottery to trace origin. He says scrolls would not survive in the wet Gilead climate, and that the best hope is pottery and possibly inscriptions.

### 6. Apocalyptic flight texts: Mark 13 and Revelation 12

Tabor argues that Mark 13 (the "desolating sacrilege" passage, with its instruction to those in Judea to "flee to the mountains") reflects a memory that the followers of Jesus would leave the city before the 70 CE destruction. He suggests the mountains could be the mountains of Gilead, through which the wadi cuts. He then reads Revelation 12 as possibly echoing the same tradition, since the woman "flees into the wilderness to her place" and is "nourished" for 1,260 days (he links the "fed" language to Elijah's ravens). He calls this the most speculative part of his case and says it is included for discussion.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **John is historically unreliable because it is theologically high.** Tabor argues scholars often discount John for its theology, but that its geographical and chronological framework (three Passovers, a joint baptizing campaign before John's arrest, Jesus's flight across the Jordan) may preserve historical information.
- **The Pella flight is a legend because a Jewish group would not flee to a pagan city.** Tabor says this objection overlooks that the name is specific, that Eusebius's purpose was theological rather than fabricating a location, and that the refugees may have been in the Pella district rather than the city.
- **Mark's account implies Jesus's public ministry lasted only a few weeks or months.** Tabor argues Mark gives no clear duration and that John's three-and-a-half-year timeline is a useful counterpoint.
