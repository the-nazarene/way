## Summary

Tabor asks whether there is credible archaeological evidence, dating to before 70 CE, that Jewish followers of Jesus in and around Jerusalem left behind in material form. He focuses on the Jerusalem necropolis and its ossuaries (bone boxes), which he says yield about 590 inscriptions out of 704 known from the period, most of them from tombs. He presents the scholarly consensus, which he attributes to Rahmani, Cotton and Jonathan Price, that Jewish ossuaries show no distinctly Christian inscriptions or symbols, and says that he and filmmaker Jacob Avicii (rendered "Simca" in places in the captions) examined many of these pieces firsthand at the Israel Museum, the Rockefeller, and other collections.

His main evidence is a set of inscriptions and images he reads as Christian or Jesus-related: several ossuaries bearing the name Jesus or cries to Jesus, charcoal crosses (on an ossuary from the Mount of Olives and one from Pompeii), a fish with the word "Yeshua" in a circle, and, most importantly, a ossuary from a tomb about 200 feet from the Talpiot "Jesus tomb" showing a Greek inscription ("dios yao upso agba," which he admits he has not fully resolved), a Hebrew "yonah" (Jonah) written beside a fish-like image, and a Jonah-and-fish scene. He links the Jonah image to the "sign of Jonah" in the Q material, which he dates as early as about 50 CE, and argues that the image shows a first-century resurrection hope.

The talk ends with Tabor stating that he will present a formal paper on the ossuary at the SBL meeting the following day, and that the material should be reconsidered. He says that if early followers left evidence of faith in tombs, it would most likely concern resurrection hope, the cross, or salvation in Jesus. He acknowledges that his readings are contested and that most of his colleagues would dismiss the scratches as random.

## Key Teachings

### The tombs as the main body of early Jerusalem inscriptions
Tabor argues that the best place to look for early inscriptional evidence is the tombs. He says over 900 tombs have been uncovered, that there are roughly 2,000 to 3,000 ossuaries, and that most of the 704 known inscriptions from the period (590) come from tombs. He quotes a writer who called this material "the domain of the dead." He contrasts the older catalog (Rahmani, which covered only the Israel state collection up to 1989) with Hannah Cotton's later volume, which he says is needed for the full corpus.

### The consensus against early Christian marks, and Tabor's challenge to it
Tabor states that most scholars hold that Jewish ossuaries lack Christian inscriptions and marks, and that Rahmani and Jonathan Price concluded that supposed Christian items were over-interpreted, mainly by Bagatti and others. He presents his project as marginal by his colleagues' estimate ("99 percent") but says that Shimon Gibson, Hershel Shanks (whom he calls open to controversial theories) and others have engaged it. He also says that the cross was assumed not to exist in the first century, and he uses the Epistle of Barnabas (which he dates "usually around 100 A.D.") to suggest that early Christians already read the T-shaped cross symbolically. He argues the consensus should be revisited.

### The name "Jesus" and the question of who the inscriptions refer to
Tabor lists 21 references to Jesus (Yeshua) in several languages across known ossuaries, and he says that roughly half clearly cannot refer to Jesus of Nazareth (for example, "Jesus son of Simon" or "Jesus son of Deutscius"). He cites what he says is about 4.4 percent frequency for the name, and compares it to his own name "Jimmy" versus "James." He argues that the most evocative cases ("Jesus a lot," "Jesus woe," "Jesus rise up") may reflect followers thinking of Jesus when burying their dead, not the name of the deceased. He notes that the readings are uncertain and charcoal-written inscriptions are fading.

### The Talpiot tomb near the "Jesus tomb" and the Barsaba family
In the 1945 Talpiot tomb, Tabor notes three names: Simon, Miriam ("of Simon, probably wife"), and Matthew Barsaba. He points out that Matthew bar Saba appears only in Acts in the New Testament, as a rare name, which he says makes it worth noting without proving any identity. He also reports a charcoal "Jesus" inscription (read with "iota omicron") on one of its ossuaries. He describes a 1981 probe of a tomb about 200 feet from the "Jesus tomb," in which the team, using a robotic camera, found further inscriptions: "Mara," an uncertain name read as possibly "Ionas" or "Julia," and a four-line Greek epitaph on the front of one ossuary.

### The epitaph and the Jonah imagery
Tabor's reading of the Greek epitaph is "dios yao upso agba," which he takes as "wondrous" (dios) plus a form of the divine name (IAO), with "upso" meaning "to lift up," as in John 12 and Acts 5, where Jesus or God is "lifted up." He says "agba" remains unresolved and proposes that it could be a Hebrew term for the Torah lifted up, making the inscription bilingual. He notes that epitaphs are rare on ossuaries, and that those known say the same thing: a curse against moving the bones.

### The Jonah image and the "sign of Jonah"
On the same ossuary, Tabor describes a figure he identifies as a large fish spitting out a human figure, a Jonah image; he also considers that the shape could be a vase. He reads the Hebrew letters beside it as "yonah" (Jonah) and says that a seaweed-wrapped head fits Jonah 2. He cites Graydon Snyder's catalogue of catacomb motifs, where Jonah appears about 100 times in fourth-century Rome, and argues that the gap between that and first-century Judea is bridged by the Q source. He points to Matthew 12:39-40, "as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the fish...", and argues that "we've got it back to the period of the life of jesus" if the saying comes from Q, which he dates as early as about 50 CE.

### Reinterpreting the fish and Jesus-marked images
Tabor reports a fragment from Beit Shemesh (Figueras's publication) that reads "yeshua" inside a circle, which Figueras suggested might be a fish. Rahmani and Price called it a circle and an over-interpretation. Tabor says the Jonah image makes him more convinced that the circle is also a Jonah reference, and that a scratched fish with two letters might be iota-eta, the first letters of "Jesus." He also mentions a mariamne lid with three scratched fish, which he admits most colleagues would call random.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Early Christians left no material trace before the late second or third century.** Tabor argues that the tombs of Jerusalem may hold inscriptions and images from before 70 CE that point to Jesus-followers, though he says the matter is contested.
- **The name "Jesus" was so common that inscriptions with it prove nothing.** Tabor says the name's frequency is around 4.4 percent, but he argues that a name like "Jesus" alone is less telling than context such as "Jesus woe" or "Jesus rise up."
- **The cross did not exist in the first century.** Tabor challenges this with the Epistle of Barnabas, which reads a T-shaped cross symbolically, and with the Mount of Olives and Pompeii marks.
- **Ossuary decorations are purely decorative and non-symbolic.** Tabor argues that some personalized marks on ossuaries may be symbolic, and that Jewish ossuary art is not uniformly non-symbolic.
- **Jonah imagery in Christian art began only in the fourth century.** Tabor argues that the sign of Jonah in the Q material shows the imagery goes back to the first century, and that the ossuary scene may be an early instance.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor's central claim is that Jesus-followers in Jerusalem left traces in tombs before 70 CE, and that the Talpiot names (Simon, Miriam, Matthew bar Saba, Mariamne) and the Jesus inscriptions could link to Jesus's family. He does not claim a specific link to James, but he says the people who buried Jesus and his family may have been the ones who left these marks.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** The talk's main thread is whether the Jonah image and "lifted up" inscription express resurrection hope before 70 CE. Tabor argues that burial marks would most likely express resurrection hope, the cross, or salvation in Jesus.
- **Gospels and canon:** Tabor uses the Q source (which he dates as early as about 50 CE) and Matthew 12's "sign of Jonah" to argue for first-century Jonah-resurrection tradition, and treats Matthew as expanding Q.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor does not address Jesus's self-understanding directly. He treats the inscriptions as evidence of how followers remembered and honored Jesus after his death.
