## Summary

Tabor asks how and when Satan came to be called "the god of this world," the phrase Paul uses in 2 Corinthians 4:4. He notes that Genesis presents Yahweh as the creator of the world, so he treats the change as a major shift in the history of ideas: from an ancient cosmology in which humans are at home on earth to a dualistic Hellenistic cosmology in which the material world is a prison and Satan rules it. He presents this as the framework behind Jesus' apocalyptic movement and Paul's letters, which he says readers of the New Testament often take for granted.

His main evidence is a survey of New Testament Satan texts. He reads Mark 3 (binding the "strong man"), Luke 10:18 ("I saw Satan fall like lightning"), John 12:31 and 14:30 ("the ruler of this world"), and many Pauline passages, including 1 Corinthians 5:5, 2 Corinthians 12:7, and Romans 16:20. He sets these against the Hebrew Bible's council scene in Job and the picture of Sheol in Job, where the dead rest without torment. He then turns to Hellenistic material: the planetary spheres, the compartments of Hades, Plato's *Timaeus*, Empedocles' lament about the "dreadful place," and the Greek gold-leaf "lamellae" for the dead. He credits his teacher Jonathan Z. Smith with the concepts of "locative" and "utopian" religion. He also quotes Frank Moore Cross (in an interview with Hershel Shanks) on the contrast between the Hebrew Bible's world-affirming outlook and the New Testament's. Tabor concedes that the proposed origin of this dualism is unclear ("I don't know how it happened but I know when it happened"), and he leaves the question of any direct eastern influence open.

The talk ends with a closing question rather than a formal conclusion: why none of the Satan texts he surveyed fit the key ideas of the Hebrew Bible. He suggests viewers take that up for themselves. The last minutes promote his book *Paul's Ascent to Paradise*, his blog, and the BAS Library, which the description says the lecture was made available through.

## Key Teachings

### Two cosmologies: the ancient (Hebrew Bible) and the Hellenistic (New Testament)
Tabor argues that the Hebrew Bible's cosmos has three levels: God in the heavens, humans and living creatures on earth, and the dead in Sheol. Sheol is "silent dark deep," and the dead "are there" with no torment, "waiting basically for the resurrection" (28:00). In Genesis 1, creation is "good good good good good," and humans belong on earth (31:08). Death outside Eden is explained as humans coming of age through the knowledge of good and evil, not as Satan ruining God's plan. He contrasts this with the Hellenistic model, which he calls dualistic: planetary spheres, a dark low earth, and a Hades divided into compartments (29:35–31:08). He says the transformation runs from roughly 300–200 BC into late antiquity and that "Plato the timaeus" and Neoplatonism (Plotinus, third century AD) supply the developed form (32:41–34:16).

### Satan's role in the Hebrew Bible versus the New Testament
In the Hebrew Bible, the "sons of God" (*bene elohim*) meet with God, and the figure Tabor calls "the prosecutor" tests Job, under God's authority (28:00). Tabor says Satan is not "up here flying around causing all these things to happen." In the New Testament, by contrast, Satan rules the world, and God's kingdom is "sacking" his house (07:46). Tabor treats exorcisms as signs that Satan's kingdom is breaking up: "if it is by the finger of god that i cast out demons then the kingdom of god has come upon you" (10:52). He also argues that the phrase "the kingdom of God is at hand" should not be read as a later second-coming expectation. Its original sense, he says, is God's rule breaking into a world held by Satan (10:52).

### Paul's version: Satan as the god of this world and the blinding of minds
Tabor says Paul treats Satan as the powerful ruler behind the political powers. He claims that the "rulers of this world" would not have crucified Jesus had they understood God's plan (01:33). He reads 2 Corinthians 4:4 as the source of "the god of this world has blinded the minds of unbelievers," and he links this to Romans 8:29–30, where a sequence runs from predestination to calling, sanctification, and glorification (17:09). He treats 1 Corinthians 5:5 (handing a sinning man over to Satan) and 1 Corinthians 7:5 (marital abstinence leaves room for temptation) as examples of the community as "a circle of protection" against Satan's power (18:42–20:16). He suggests that Paul's "thorn in the flesh" may have been sexual temptation, although he marks this as a guess, and that Satan "hindered" Paul's travel plans (21:49–23:22).

### The Dead Sea Scrolls, Enoch, and predestination (a question Tabor sets aside)
Tabor says the New Testament does not settle whether humans are predestined, and that the debate has run from Augustine to Calvinism (15:35). He notes that the Dead Sea Scrolls seem to treat a built-in human nature as a matter of predestination, and he ties this to the Book of Enoch. He says he is "not so sure" about the New Testament view and sets the question aside.

### The Hellenistic afterlife and "the gnostic" soul
Tabor presents the gold-leaf tablets as evidence that by the 300s AD the dead were expected to identify themselves to guardians below and be let out of Hades. He reads this as salvation through knowledge of one's heavenly origin (35:36–37:10). He says Jesus is "a kind of ... redeemer" in a "certain gnosticism" sense (38:44). He also notes that many Christians speak of "dying and going to heaven," which he calls more Hellenistic than biblical, because Revelation describes a new heaven and new earth, with God coming down to earth (40:17).

### Method, sources, and the ending
Tabor says he aims for a "history of ideas" approach (00:01), and he surveys the texts "as rapid fire as possible" (06:11). He recommends three books (Pagels, Heiser, Almond) for a range of perspectives on Satan (04:39–06:11). He ends by asking viewers to consider why none of the Satan texts fit the Hebrew Bible's central concepts (see description and close of talk).

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Satan as a red, pitchfork-wielding devil.** Tabor says the biblical texts do not describe this figure, and he chose Dante's illustrations to avoid it (01:33).
- **Satan fell long ago and therefore rules the world.** Tabor notes the "tradition" that Satan fell long ago, but reads Luke 10:18 as referring to the fall of Satan's kingdom, not a distant past event (09:19).
- **"The kingdom of God is at hand" means the second coming.** Tabor says the phrase originally meant God's rule breaking into a world held by Satan, and that the second-coming reading is "much later" (10:52).
- **Hell as torture in the Hebrew Bible.** In Sheol the dead "are not being tortured"; they are waiting (28:00).
- **Death as a natural part of creation.** Tabor argues that Genesis presents death as a consequence of humans coming of age, not as part of God's original plan (31:08).

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught.** Tabor treats Jesus' exorcisms and the kingdom language as an earlier layer, and Paul's theology of Satan, sin, and deliverance as a developed layer that uses the Hellenistic cosmology (10:52; 17:09). The talk claims that both are "thoroughly immersed" in the dualistic world view, which the description states.
- **The Kingdom on earth.** Tabor reads "let your will be done on earth as it is in heaven" as a request that God's rule replace Satan's current rule (10:52).
- **Resurrection, afterlife, and hell.** He contrasts Sheol (no torment, waiting for resurrection) with the Hellenistic Hades of compartments and descent (28:00–37:10), and he notes that Revelation's new earth undercuts the idea that the soul's destiny is heavenly escape (40:17).
- **Torah.** He cites the Torah's prohibition on uncovering a father's nakedness as the basis for calling the Corinthian man's relationship incest (18:42).
- **Who Jesus thought he was.** Tabor describes Jesus as "a healer and an exorcist" (10:52). He suggests Jesus is a "kind of" redeemer, with a "certain gnosticism" in Paul's "knowledge of Christ" (38:44).
- **How the gospels and canon were formed.** Tabor notes that only John does not describe Jesus' exorcisms as binding Satan, and that the Synoptics do (07:46). He does not develop the wider canon question.
