## Summary

Tabor asks whether the messianic titles and scriptural "predictions" attached to John the Baptist and Jesus in the gospels were added after the men's deaths, or whether these figures may have developed their own messianic self-identity by reading Hebrew Bible texts. He frames the standard scholarly view (which he attributes to Crossan and "everybody else" in Jesus studies) as the position that the gospel writers, especially Mark, read texts like Zechariah and Isaiah backward onto events such as John's beheading and Jesus' crucifixion. He calls the opposite view a minority position, which he holds alongside Michael Wise and Israel Knohl.

His main evidence is the Dead Sea Scrolls, especially the Habakkuk Pesher and the Thanksgiving Hymns, which he reads as autobiographical writing by the Teacher of Righteousness who found himself in Isaiah 53 and related texts. He adds parallel cases: David Koresh and Moses Gabori, both of whom he says found their roles in scripture. He also argues that Deutero-Isaiah's servant and anointed-figure texts gave Jesus a self-understanding that could be "documented" in the way these modern cases were. He reconstructs John's death as the trigger for Jesus' sense of mission, with Zechariah 13 offering the text he thinks Jesus found.

The talk ends by shifting to apocalyptic disappointment. Tabor describes "realized eschatology" as the way communities (Adventists, Jehovah's Witnesses, Mormons, and the Gospel of John) keep faith after failed predictions. He closes by noting that N.T. Wright seems to be moving toward a more allegorical reading of the Son of Man coming, and by quoting Crossan's view that Christ's coming is realized in egalitarian community. Tabor presents much of this as his own hypothesis, saying "i don't know this for sure."

## Key Teachings

### 1. The "chicken or egg" question about prophecy and events
Tabor sets up two positions. In the standard view, the gospel traditions "import and impose these textual categories onto the figures of john and jesus long after their deaths" to explain shocking deaths. In the alternative, the figures themselves "read the text and saying that's me." He stresses that the standard view assumes no genuine prophecy, and he says that this assumption is why scholars take the first position. He says that "the issue would be did things happen to the figure and then the followers went to the text and justified what happened," and asks whether the sequence could have run the other way.

### 2. The Teacher of Righteousness and the Thanksgiving Hymns
Tabor draws on Michael Wise's *The First Messiah* and Israel Knohl's *The Messiah Before Jesus*, which he says "independently... argue the same thesis that the qumran teacher read himself into the thanksgiving hymns." He describes the Hymns as largely autobiographical and calls them "the most valuable material on the planet" for the self-consciousness of a messiah. He notes that the Teacher claims to be anointed of the spirit (Isaiah 61:1), and he says the Teacher "quotes isaiah 53 and he says my life is going to be poured out as an offering." He reads Habakkuk Pesher's line about faith in the Teacher as evidence that the Teacher's suffering shaped his followers' faith. He describes three figures in the Scrolls and related texts: the prophet and two anointed ones, one of whom is "anointed by the spirit" in a way higher than oil. He ties this to Zechariah 4's "two anointed ones" and says the middle figure "basically runs the show on earth."

### 3. Deutero-Isaiah's servant texts as a self-reading resource
Tabor argues that the servant and prophetic "I" in Isaiah 48:16, 49:1-6, 50:4 and 11 can be read by a candidate as describing himself. He notes that the "I" and "you" shift in these passages and that readers must ask "who's speaking who's the i and who's the you." He says Mark's secrecy motif could derive from Isaiah 49's "hid me away" language, though he does not think the secrecy is simply a literary device. He argues that Jesus' "blessed are the poor, blessed are the meek" echoes Isaiah 11, and that Isaiah 50:4-6 would let Jesus anticipate being "spit upon" and struck. He says Jesus "can read isaiah so he says we're going to jerusalem."

He is explicit that this is not his theological view of the Ebionites, whose ideas about the Spirit he describes as "not my view."

### 4. Parallel modern cases: David Koresh and Moses Gabori
Tabor says he studied the self-identification of David Koresh (Waco, 1993) and Moses Gabori (Jerusalem, 1926). He says Koresh found "all sorts of things that fit the siege at waco in the bible" and that the texts are "generic enough that they fit," with a "generally singular task" such as "gather the tribes of israel bring light to the nations." Gabori, whom he calls "the first messiah of the modern period" after Bar Kokhba, took another man's wife by reading Hosea 3 as a command to himself. Tabor uses these cases to argue that finding oneself in scripture is a documented human pattern, not something unique to Jesus. He says, "i think that's a much more believable scenario."

### 5. Reconstructing Jesus' response to John's death
Tabor proposes that John's execution devastated Jesus, who had expected them to "work together and bring the kingdom." He suggests Jesus found Zechariah 13 ("strike the shepherd and the sheep will be scattered," "awake o sword against my servant") and applied it to the death of the Baptist. He argues that Mark's Elijah saying ("elijah did come but he suffered all that was written of him") preserves a tradition that Jesus thought something "was written of a guy" who would be beheaded. He thinks Mark moved this material to Gethsemane to make Jesus, not John, the shepherd. He also notes Zechariah 12 ("they will look on him whom they pierced") as another text Christians could use. He is clear that this is "imagining" and that "i don't know all of this."

### 6. Realized eschatology as the way communities survive failed prophecy
Tabor argues that when predictions fail, groups either "throw the thing whole thing out" or move toward "realized eschatology." He points to Adventists, Jehovah's Witnesses, and Mormons as examples of groups that "set so many dates that have been wrong" and then reinterpret. He ties the Gospel of John's "the hour is coming yes and now is" to this pattern. He says realized eschatology is "so seductive because it is not falsifiable right now." He suggests N.T. Wright is moving toward allegory on the Son of Man's coming, and he reads Daniel 7 as describing the people of the Most High receiving power, which "doesn't lead to disappointment." He ends by quoting Crossan's view that the coming of Christ is realized in egalitarian community.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Passion predictions are necessarily vaticinia ex eventu.** Tabor argues the standard view assumes no genuine prophecy, and he proposes that figures like Jesus may have read texts and expected their own fates before they happened.
- **Jesus faked his death (Schonfield's *The Passover Plot*).** Tabor calls the faked-death idea "ridiculous," though he admires the book's account of how Jesus might have read texts about his fate.
- **Mark's secrecy motif is purely literary.** Tabor says he doesn't think it is, arguing instead that the secrecy may reflect Isaiah 49's "hid me away" language and a sense that the revelation must wait.
- **Isaiah 53 was first applied to Jesus by the Christian church.** Tabor cites the Habakkuk Pesher and Thanksgiving Hymns to argue that the Teacher of Righteousness read Isaiah 53 about himself before Christianity, though he notes that "the messianic interpretation of isaiah 53 was discovered by the christian church" is the standard view he is contesting.
- **Non-literal fulfillment is merely a modern evasion.** Tabor presents realized eschatology as an old pattern in groups facing failed predictions, not a recent invention.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor's central claim is that Jesus' messianic self-identity may have been built by reading Isaiah's servant texts and Zechariah, rather than invented by later writers. He stresses that this is his hypothesis.
- **Jesus and the Dead Sea Scrolls:** The Teacher of Righteousness is his main comparison. He sees parallels in the autobiographical Hymns, the suffering motif, and the figure who is "anointed by the spirit."
- **John the Baptist and Jesus:** Tabor reconstructs John's execution as the event that shaped Jesus' sense of mission, with Zechariah 13 as the text Jesus is supposed to have applied.
- **The Kingdom on earth and apocalyptic expectation:** His discussion of Daniel, Zechariah 14, and the failed siege expectation sets up his account of how communities shift from literal expectation to realized eschatology.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** Only briefly touched, via Daniel 12's resurrection and John's "hour is coming." Not a major thread.
