## Summary

Tabor asks where the events of Jesus's last week took place, and how recent archaeology in Herodian-period Jerusalem bears on the traditional sites. He moves through the temple, the Last Supper, the trials, the crucifixion, and the burial, noting at each stage where scholars disagree. He describes the lecture as a tour of "all the possibilities," though he states his own preferences clearly.

His main positions are these. He favors Bargil Pixner's identification of a Mount Zion building as the upper room of Acts 2 and the Last Supper, and he thinks Pixner's evidence is convincing. He is uncertain where Caiaphas's house stood, but says the trial before Caiaphas was an illegal, late-night meeting held before Passover. He accepts Shimon Gibson's and Pixner's location of Pilate's praetorium in the Antonia fortress area, and he uses the Roman governor's *bema* as support. For the crucifixion he argues for the Mount of Olives, citing Josephus on the visibility of crucifixions and Hebrews 13 on sacrifice "outside the camp," an argument he credits to Ernest Martin. For the burial he argues that Joseph of Arimathea's tomb was a temporary burial made in haste before the Sabbath and Passover, and he suggests a tomb on the Mount of Olives as a candidate, while calling it "not saying it's the tomb." He also presents the "shroud tomb" he excavated in the Hinnom Valley as a demonstration of how a burial might have been wrapped and preserved.

The talk ends with a discussion of the burial. Tabor notes that the Gospels differ on whose tomb Jesus was laid in, and he says he has tried in his book to give all the possibilities rather than settle the question. He also jokes about James Tabor's own changing proposals for the tomb's location. Much of the material is illustrated with images and maps, and the talk is essentially a monologue.

## Key Teachings

### The upper room on Mount Zion as the Last Supper site

Tabor says Pixner's argument that the "Church of the Apostles" on Mount Zion preserves an authentic first-century building is persuasive to him. Pixner, he says, suspected a first-century Essene guest house, and the building was kept through the Byzantine and Crusader periods. Tabor links it to Acts 2, the gathering of about 120 people in an upper room when the Spirit came, and he says the room "fit" that scene. He also notes a large mikveh outside the building and a Jewish tradition that places David's tomb nearby, which he connects to Peter's remark in Acts 2 that David's sepulcher "is with us to this day." He explains that a wealthy friend of Jesus (possibly Joseph of Arimathea, though he says he does not know who it was) would have supplied the room, which then became the movement's headquarters, where James, Mary and the brothers lived, as Acts 12 suggests. He prefers this upper-city site to Gibson's proposal of a lower-city Last Supper, which Gibson grounds in Jesus's association with the poor.

### Trial before Caiaphas: an illegal night meeting before Passover

Tabor says he does not know where Caiaphas's house was. He reports Pixner's view that it was near the temple, close to the burnt house and priestly mansion, and that Jesus was tried in a private home. He describes the trial as a secret meeting in the early morning hours, not a session of the Sanhedrin, staged by Caiaphas's henchmen who wanted Jesus removed before Passover. His reasoning is that Jesus's popularity, with thousands coming to hear him all week, made a public arrest risky before the festival. He points to the Caiaphas ossuary, found in 1990 during road construction near Abu Tor, and says the inscription is readable, though ossuary names were for family use and not public announcement.

### Pilate's praetorium and the Antonia fortress

Tabor argues that Pilate's judgment took place outdoors at a raised platform in the Antonia fortress area, not in Herod's palace. His evidence includes a bedrock platform, steps going up to a gateway that he says is no longer there, pavement from the first century, and a comparison with the *bema* (raised platform) at Corinth. He notes that Jewish leaders would not enter the praetorium to avoid defilement before eating Passover, so Pilate came out to them. He credits Gibson for the identification and says that Gibson's sense of the site shapes his own view. He also describes the "Behold the man" scene as the likely setting, with Jesus shown in a doorway above the crowd.

### The crucifixion on the Mount of Olives

Tabor argues for the Mount of Olives over the Church of the Holy Sepulchre and Gordon's Calvary. His first argument rests on Josephus, who says the Romans liked to crucify "over against the wall of a city," and who reports that during the siege the Romans crucified 500 men a day on the Mount of Olives. Tabor's inference is that the site was chosen for visibility: "it's the only place where everyone in the city can look and see no matter where you are." His second argument, which he says comes from Ernest Martin, relies on Hebrews 13:10-13. He reads the phrase "outside the camp" as a technical term for the east side of the city, linked to the red heifer rite and the Mount of Olives. He says the author presents Jesus as suffering "outside the gate," in parallel with sacrifices burned outside the camp. He adds that Jesus would have ridden down the southern road from Bethany and walked up the northern road carrying the cross, which he links to the "stumbling" of the Gospel account.

### The burial as a temporary, rushed burial

Tabor argues that Jesus's body was first placed in a nearby tomb as an emergency measure, because Passover and the Sabbath were approaching within hours and handling a corpse would defile those who took part. He says he raised this with Dan Bahat, who held that Jesus was buried in the Holy Sepulchre, and that Bahat agreed the body would have needed reburial somewhere else if it was not there. Tabor says, "this is an initial burial a burial of haste although i don't think it was here," adding that he thinks the rush explains the reason Joseph gave in Mark. He notes that Mark says Joseph bought linen and laid the body in a rock tomb "because the sabbath was fast approaching," which suggests Joseph did not own the tomb beforehand.

### The Gospel accounts of Joseph's tomb

Tabor contrasts the four accounts. Mark describes Joseph of Arimathea as a respected council member "waiting for the kingdom of God." Matthew calls him a rich man and "his own new tomb." Luke says Joseph had not agreed to the council's plan, and that no one had been laid in the tomb. John mentions a garden tomb "in which no one had ever been laid," and adds Nicodemus. Tabor stresses that Mark, Luke and John do not say the tomb was Joseph's, and he says, "please note he doesn't say it's joseph's tomb." He proposes a tomb on the Mount of Olives as a candidate and mentions a grave there he visited, though he says he cannot say it is the tomb. He describes a burial linen reconstruction based on the shroud he found in the Hinnom Valley, in which a body was wrapped with a separate head covering and placed in a tomb.

### The temple money changers and the Tyrian shekel

Tabor argues against the common explanation for Jesus overturning the tables. He says he was taught that the priests only allowed "holy" money without images, but in fact the temple required the Tyrian silver coin, which was 99.9% silver and guaranteed, and which worshippers had to use to pay the temple tax. Coins from across the Roman world were brought in and might be fake or of unknown origin, so pilgrims had to exchange them. In his view, the priests were not concerned with images but with silver quality. He presents the picture of the Tyrian coin as an illustration of the point.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Tabor challenges the idea that the priests banned pagan money from the temple.** He says the priests wanted the Tyrian silver coin because of its guaranteed silver content, and the images were not the concern.
- **Tabor challenges the assumption that Joseph of Arimathea owned the tomb.** He says Mark, Luke and John do not say the tomb was Joseph's, and Mark's wording suggests an emergency burial.
- **Tabor challenges the idea that the Holy Sepulchre is the only traditional site with support.** He presents the Mount of Olives and Gordon's Calvary as alternatives, while noting that the Holy Sepulchre remains the site of pilgrim tradition.
- **Tabor challenges the Gordon's Calvary identification.** He says archaeologists object that the tomb there is an Iron Age tomb, which does not fit the newly hewn tomb of John's account.
