## Summary

Tabor asks whether Mary Magdalene and Mary of Bethany, the sister of Martha and Lazarus, are the same woman. The question comes from the anointing stories: Mark 14:3-9 and John 12:1-8 describe a woman anointing Jesus at Bethany, and Luke 7:36-50 describes an unnamed "woman of the city who is a sinner" anointing him at a Pharisee's table. Tabor also notes that Luke 8:2 introduces Mary Magdalene and Luke 10:38-42 introduces Mary and Martha. He frames his method as reading Mark first, because he judges Mark the earliest gospel, and then reading John and Luke as expansions of or reactions to Mark.

Tabor does not settle the question. He lays out the parallels (the house or dinner setting, the costly nard, the head and feet anointing, the hair, the complaint about the money, the reference to burial) and treats John as "completing" Mark. He says he tends to resist the identification, mainly because John would have named Magdalene if he meant her. He then raises the possibility that a later reader would have known Mary as Magdalene. He also points to a 300-denarii price he calls a year's wages, and to Mark's naming of Magdalene at the tomb as the reason the anointing for burial connects to her. He cites Jeffrey Butz and a peer-reviewed article by Bevins as arguments for identification, and Grenville Kent as also favoring it, while admitting Luke 7 is the main obstacle.

The lecture ends with Tabor saying Luke's sinful woman is probably a separate story, possibly one Luke did not know to be Mary of Bethany. He stops at about the 42-minute mark to take questions. The transcript ends mid-thought, with his point that anointing is what makes Jesus "the messiah" in Mark's terms, since Mark's Jesus is the one who dies on the cross.

## Key Teachings

### Mark first, then John and Luke as commentary
Tabor's method, which he says he uses with students, is to start with Mark because "invariably matthew is editing and luke is editing deleting adding reacting to mark." He illustrates this with the baptism narrative, where he says Matthew's public announcement differs from Mark's personal revelation. He then reads the Bethany anointing in Mark 14 alongside John 12, which he treats as partly parallel and partly supplementary: "mark and john... this is a lot of similarities." He says "i don't suggest you just mush them together like like a web but we can read these and be instructed by both."

### The Mark and John parallels
Tabor lists the points he sees in common: Bethany; a dinner; "pure nard" in an alabaster flask; a value of about 300 denarii; the anointing of the head in Mark and the feet in John; the wiping with hair; a complaint about waste and the poor; and Jesus's response about burial. He reads John's Judas as an eyewitness detail and identifies Judas as the group's money-keeper. He argues that the anointing of head and feet together stands for the whole person, and cites the anointing of priests. He reads "for the day of my burial" as connecting the anointing to Jesus's death.

He also argues that the 300-denarii figure should be read as a year's wages for a day laborer, not by modern silver prices, which he calls "totally bogus."

### Why Tabor resists the identification
His main reservation is that John "knows who mary magdalene is and if he meant if he meant to say this is mary magdalene then he would have said it." He adds that the gospel's first readers knew these people, and that "you're reading it 1900 years later." He offers the example of Mark 15 (Simon of Cyrene, father of Alexander and Rufus) to argue that Mark assumed readers knew the families. Tabor says "mark expects his readers to understand" the same way with Mary and her sons (Mark 6:3 and Mark 15-16), so the three names given for Mary as mother could be read as one woman known differently.

### Luke 7 as the hard case
Tabor says the identification is "the problem is luke 7." He notes that the early church fathers (third to fifth centuries, per his account) and Pope Gregory identified the sinful woman with Magdalene. He then says he does not make her a prostitute: "i'm not going to make her a prostitute but maybe she had not lived a devoted life." He treats the "seven demons" as a sign of strength of opposition rather than a sign of madness, and cites Paul's "messenger of satan" (2 Corinthians 12) as a comparison.

He argues Luke's special material (Luke 10:51 through 18:14, per his reading) has no chronology, that Luke skips Mark's anointing story while including other Markan material, and that this suggests Luke was "just giving us additional material" and may not have known Magdalene and Mary of Bethany were one person.

### Luke 10 and the Martha-Mary story
He reads Luke 10:38-42 as a teaching story where Mary sits at Jesus's feet "with the men listening" and Jesus commends her "better part." He calls it a story that shows women's intellect and spirituality as equal, and argues that the setting in Galilee (not Bethany) is odd. If the Mark and John anointing stories did not exist, he says, "we'd just say well man i wonder who they were what an interesting story."

### Scholars supporting the identification
He mentions Jeffrey Butz (whose article he posted on his blog), a peer-reviewed article in Catholic Biblical Quarterly by "Bevins" (transcribed as "mary bevins," so the spelling should be checked), and Grenville Kent's article that "thinks they're likely the same person." He presents these as arguments, not conclusions, and says Butz's paper "leaves it all open."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The woman in Luke 7 is Mary Magdalene.** This is the traditional reading (Tabor cites the early church fathers and Pope Gregory). Tabor says he "tend[s] to resist" it and treats Luke 7 as a separate story.
- **Mary Magdalene was a prostitute.** Tabor rejects this: "i'm not going to make her a prostitute."
- **The 300-denarii price equals a modern amount based on silver.** Tabor calls the modern silver-price conversion "totally bogus" and says the value should be read as a day laborer's year of wages.
- **"Leper" in the Gospels is a mild skin disease, not leprosy.** Tabor says skeletal evidence from the Tomb of the Shroud, which he dates to 2000, shows leprosy (Hansen's disease).
