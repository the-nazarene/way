## Summary

Tabor asks whether the Talpiot tomb in Jerusalem, discovered in 1980, could be the family tomb of Jesus of Nazareth, and whether the "James ossuary" (the bone box inscribed "James son of Joseph brother of Jesus") might have come from it. He presents this as an update to the argument of his earlier books, *The Jesus Dynasty* (paperback edition, which he dates to 2006) and *The Jesus Discovery* (2012). He says the James ossuary's provenance is "a stronger possibility" of coming from Talpiot than before, though not yet demonstrated, since the dealer Oded Golan "is not clear on which dealer he got it from."

His main evidence is onomastic and archaeological. He lists the Talpiot ossuary inscriptions (Yeshua son of Joseph, Mariamne/Mara, Matia, Yose, Judah son of Yeshua, and the James ossuary's inscription), and argues that the combination of names, weighted by statistics on known Jerusalem names, makes the identification "pretty sure." He reports a preliminary DNA comparison, attributed to Chuck Greenblatt and Kathy Reichs, which he says indicates that Mariamne is "not the mother or the sister" of the Yeshua in the tomb. He also argues that a "temporary tomb" explanation fits the Gospel burial accounts. Against the common objections (names are common; Jesus was unmarried and had no son), he offers his own reasoning, and he says the 2010–2011 re-examination of a second, sealed tomb nearby revealed a Christian symbol and so "the earliest testimony of faith in the resurrection of jesus."

The talk ends on Tabor's closing claim: that the proximity of the second tomb to the family tomb "increases the likelihood that the other tomb is the tomb of jesus." He frames the evidence as open to investigation, saying "we're not trying to make it come out one way or the other." The talk is almost entirely Tabor speaking, with no host or guest.

## Key Teachings

This talk is not a teaching of Jesus. It sets out Tabor's archaeological case, so these subsections are his arguments.

### The James ossuary as a possible first physical link

Tabor says that if the James ossuary "indeed held the bones of james the brother of jesus," it "would be the first physical link an archaeological link to jesus." He notes that James is "actually quite rare" as a name among the roughly 2,538 individuals he says can be identified from the period, so a "james brother jesus" inscription would weigh heavily. He describes the inscription's reading as "james son of joseph brother of jesus," and reports that Shimon Gibson's drawing is accurate. He also cites an earlier question, whether the words "brother of jesus" were a later forgery, and says that "if you kept up with the latest that's looking more and more unlikely." He treats the provenance (which dealer, which shop, whether it came from the robbed tomb or Talpiot) as still unresolved.

### Ossuary practice and the robbed Hinnom tomb

Tabor describes the Second Temple (Herodian) custom in Jerusalem of collecting bones into limestone ossuaries, some inscribed with names, as making these inscriptions "an invaluable asset." He says the Israeli collection holds about 600 ossuaries, and that a new corpus covers roughly 650 inscriptions from Jerusalem. He uses a robbed tomb in the Hinnom valley, which he and Gibson found about twelve hours after it was looted, as an example: thieves broke ossuaries, took the inscribed fronts, and sold them to tourists. He says this shows why names that appear in the New Testament were valuable on the black market. The tomb yielded a burial shroud, the first example of Hansen's disease found in a burial, and "the first jewish male hair" sample from this period, which he says is why he calls it the "tomb of the shroud."

### The Talpiot inscriptions and the name statistics

Tabor says the 1980 Talpiot tomb had ten ossuaries, six with names. He lists them as Yeshua son of Joseph ("Yeshua bar Yosef"), James, Yose, Matia, Mariamne (also read Mara), and Judah son of Yeshua. He gives reasons for the identifications: "Yose" is a rare nickname form of Joseph; "Mara" is the Aramaic term for "the lord (feminine)"; and "Yehudah bar Yeshua" would be "judas son of jesus," who is "not a figure known to us in the new testament." He argues that the sequence of names (a father Joseph, a mother Mary, a brother James) makes the identification much more probable, using a hypothetical model of a first-century family burial in which Joseph, Mary, James, Joseph, Simon and Judah would be placed. He also says that the 1980 tomb is a "family tomb" of this type, and that "if you got two with names you would be pretty happy four you would be ecstatically happy six."

### Mariamne, Migdal, and the DNA test

Tabor discusses the name "Mariamne" (Mary, with the Greek article in the Gospel usage "Mary the Magdalene"). He suggests the byname could refer to Migdal, a town on the Sea of Galilee that he says is being excavated, and whose name means "tower." He cites Luke 8:1-4 for Mary as a woman of means who supported Jesus. He also offers a Hebrew wordplay (*migdal* linked to a participle meaning "the one who raises the child") as a possibility, and says that the Mary "could be the mother of the child." He reports that a preliminary DNA comparison by Greenblatt and Reichs "indicated to us that mary omni is not the mother or the sister" of the Yeshua tested, which he says raises the possibility that she was a wife. He calls the idea that Jesus had a wife and child "probably the main objection people have of historically to the talpiot tomb."

### The temporary-tomb burial argument

Tabor's own theory is that Jesus was crucified near Jerusalem, not at Talpiot, and that there were "two burials of jesus." He reads John's account as describing a "temporary tomb of emergency": since the Sabbath (and Passover) was approaching, the body was laid in a nearby unused tomb and "block[ed] up until the holiday's over," and then moved to a permanent burial. He says this would explain the empty tomb reported in John, citing Mary Magdalene's question in "john 21" ("where have they taken him"). He states that he is "not talking theology here," but is describing "how you would bury a jew if it's passover afternoon when they die."

### The second tomb and the first Christian symbol

Tabor says that a second tomb "less than 200 feet away," sealed and untouched, was found when a pipe in the first tomb led to an intact chamber. Working under an Israel Antiquities Authority license, and with permission from a religious authority, the team drilled down and used a remote camera (built, he says, by an associate of James Cameron) to film seven ossuaries, which he says had not been disturbed. He reports that they found "the earliest christian symbols ever discovered and the first christian symbol ever found in first century jerusalem," which he says is "not a cross." He reads this as "the earliest testimony of faith in the resurrection of jesus." He says that if the second tomb's occupants believed Jesus was raised, and they lie "200 feet away from his quote tomb," this raises questions about how early believers understood resurrection, and he cites 2 Corinthians 5 for Paul's image of "new clothes" and a "permanent building." He ends by saying the proximity increases the likelihood that the Talpiot tomb is Jesus's.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"The names are common, so the tomb can't be identified."** Tabor argues that the combination of names (Jesus son of Joseph, Mary, James, and others) is statistically rare, so a "james brother jesus" inscription "pretty well settles things."
- **"Jesus was never married and had no children, so this can't be his tomb."** Tabor says this is the main historical objection, but he argues that a wife and child are possible, pointing to the DNA result and the Mariamne byname.
- **"Finding the bones of Jesus would be needed to rule out the tomb."** Tabor says this is the main theological objection, not the historical one, and his argument is historical.
- **"Early Christian resurrection faith and the Talpiot tomb cannot coexist."** Tabor suggests that theologians may not have worked this through, and that early believers may have distinguished the old body from the new one, citing 2 Corinthians 5.

- **James and the Jerusalem community:** The talk's central claim is that the James ossuary may link James the brother of Jesus to the Talpiot tomb. Tabor says James is "the leader of the jerusalem church," but he does not discuss James's teaching.
