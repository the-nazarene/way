## Summary

Tabor asks what the burial evidence in the Herodian period can tell us about Jesus' family, and whether a Jerusalem-area tomb, the Talpiot tomb excavated in 1980, could plausibly be a "Jesus family tomb." He frames the family as a royal "dynasty" of David's line, not just a family. He argues that Jesus was succeeded by his brother James, then by Simeon, then by Jude, and that these relatives led the Jerusalem community and were hunted under later emperors. He presents this as a contrast with Herod's tomb at the Herodium, which Ehud Netzer has identified, and he notes that the Herodium is about six miles south of Jerusalem, within sight of the Talpiot tomb.

Much of the talk is an evidence review. Tabor argues that the James ossuary is authentic, though he acknowledges he is not an expert, and he discusses the illicit antiquities trade, a robbed tomb in the Hinnom Valley that he links speculatively to the ossuary, and other inscribed finds (Caiaphas, Simon of Cyrene, Simon bar Jonah). For the Talpiot tomb he works through the inscribed names, their frequency in Tal Ilan's lexicon, and a hypothetical list of who would have been buried there. He rejects the common objections that the names are too common, that Jesus would not have had a rock tomb, and that the family would have buried him in Nazareth. He also reports that DNA testing did not establish a connection between "Yeshua" and "Mary Omni" but could not rule out Mary Magdalene.

The talk ends with Tabor's account of Paul's view of resurrection (2 Corinthians 5) and Revelation's image of the sea giving up its dead. He suggests that resurrection could be a re-clothing of the person rather than the reconstitution of bones. The talk is cut off mid-thought at the end, and Tabor presents all of this as his own hypothesis, not as settled fact.

## Key Teachings

### The "dynasty" of Jesus' relatives
Tabor's central framing is that Jesus' family was royal, of David's line, and that his relatives were treated as a dynasty. He says that when Jesus died, "he's succeeded by his brother James," then "when james dies he's succeeded by shimon varklofas," and then "jude barclophas," whom he says might be brothers or cousins of Jesus. He argues that church fathers called these leaders "related to jesus according to the flesh," and that the Eastern church revered this family. He says the sources name relatives, not children of Jesus, and that the idea of Jesus' children comes from Dan Brown and similar works. He cites Eusebius for records of royal-line people being arrested, and says that by the 50s, 60s, 70s, and 80s "it became pretty dangerous to be part of the royal family," and that two grandsons of Jude were arrested and tortured in Hadrian's time. Tabor also says the Jerusalem leadership was marginalized in the later Pauline tradition, and he uses a painting of Peter and Paul to illustrate that James was "essentially ignored."

### The James ossuary
Tabor describes the ossuary inscribed "Yaakov bar Yosef, brother of Yeshua" as the first physical artifact that could be connected to Jesus. He says he is "with those who say that it's authentic," citing his own reading and his interviews with Oded Golan, whom he says he does not believe forged it. He acknowledges that the inscription may have been written in two hands, with the "brother of Yeshua" tag added later. He describes the Old City antiquities shops, the claim that everything was found before 1978, and the illicit trade in robbed tombs. He then describes the robbed tomb in the Hinnom Valley that his team found in 2000, which he says "was the only tomb robbed" and which he suggests may be the source of the ossuary. He reports that an Israeli government case against Golan refers to Golan obtaining the James ossuary around the summer of 2000, but says he does not know the evidence behind that date. He says Shimon Gibson "is quite convinced" of the link, and that patina studies were planned.

### Other inscribed finds and the case that such finds are possible
Tabor notes that Jerusalem's road construction and building have produced notable finds: the Caiaphas ossuary, which he says "many scholars" and he himself find convincing as the ossuary of the high priest who presided over Jesus' crucifixion; an ossuary of "Simon of Cyrene," whose son Alexander carried the cross, which he says was found under a table in the basement of the Hebrew University; and an ossuary on the Mount of Olives reading "Simon bar Jonah," which Stephen Pfann now reads differently. His point is that a famous person's ossuary could turn up in Jerusalem.

### The Talpiot tomb: names, statistics and the hypothetical tomb
Tabor describes the 1980 Talpiot tomb, with its ten ossuaries (six inscribed), and argues that the inscriptions are a private family cataloguing system. He reads the inscriptions as Yeshua bar Yosef, Yose (a rare nickname that appears in Mark for Jesus' brother Joseph), Mariamne/Mara, Yehuda bar Yeshua, Matya, and Yoseh/Joseph. He presents statistics from Tal Ilan's lexicon of Jewish names (2,538 male and 320 female names, with Joseph at 8.6 percent, Jesus at 3.9, Judah at 6.5, Mary at about a quarter), and says that combining the names so that a grandfather, father and son line up eliminates most alternatives. He draws a stadium analogy to show how the odds narrow as criteria are added. He argues that "Mariamne" written with the nu, which he says appears in Greek literature only for Mary Magdalene (in Hippolytus and the Acts of Philip), is significant, while noting that Stephen Pfann and Jonathan Price read the inscription as two women, Mary and Martha. He also discusses Jane Schaberg's work on spiritual marriage as a possible context.

### Hypothetical occupants and the two burials
Tabor proposes who would belong in a Jesus family tomb according to Jewish burial practice: Jesus (died about 30), Mary, and possibly other relatives who died before 70, such as James, with their wives and children. He ties the cutoff to the destruction of Jerusalem in 70, which he calls the terminus for peaceful burial there. He describes two burials for Jesus: a temporary one because the Sabbath was approaching, and a secondary ossuary burial about a year later. He argues that Joseph of Arimathea's tomb is probably a Matthean addition ("only matthew says this"), noting that Mark and John say the tomb was chosen out of haste, and that Luke does not name Joseph's tomb. He suggests the rich-man's-tomb motif is an attempt to fulfill Isaiah.

### DNA and the caution against overreading
Tabor reports that the DNA results showed no connection between Yeshua and Mary Omni, which he says means Mary Omni could be Mary Magdalene but is not necessarily Jesus' mother or sister, since only a shared mitochondrial lineage would establish that. He says the Israel Antiquities Authority cleaned the ossuaries for display, so the remains are not fully available to test, and that the presence of human material in an ossuary does not mean it was not reused.

### Resurrection as re-clothing rather than resuscitation
In the final section Tabor argues that Paul describes resurrection as putting off the body and being re-clothed, citing "we put off the body" and "lest we be found naked" in 2 Corinthians 5. He says the seed image in Paul is one of a new life emerging from a discarded husk. He uses Ezekiel 37 to illustrate the bones image, then says resurrection could be understood as a new bodily state in which the old remains are not needed, drawing on the Revelation phrase about "the sea gave up the dead." He says the Talpiot DNA results do not decide the question either way.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Jesus family had children.** Tabor says the sources name brothers, cousins and other relatives, not children of Jesus, and that the idea of children comes from Dan Brown and similar works.
- **"Jesus of Nazareth" is only a spiritual title, not a royal lineage.** Tabor argues the family was royal, of David's line, and that the Roman authorities treated it that way.
- **The James ossuary is a forgery.** Tabor says he is with those who call it authentic, though he acknowledges the inscription may have been written in two hands.
- **The names are too common to mean anything.** Tabor argues that frequency statistics and name combinations (a grandfather, father and son) narrow the possibilities sharply.
- **Jesus would not have been buried in a rock tomb in Jerusalem because he was poor, and his family would have taken him to Nazareth.** Tabor calls these the main objections and says they are weak.
- **Mary Omni is a common name.** Tabor says the "Mary Omni" form comes from Winston's English translation of Josephus, not the Greek, and the Greek form "Mariamne" with the nu is rare.
