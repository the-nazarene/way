## Summary

Tabor asks why Mary, the mother of Jesus, is so widely known yet so little known, and why the New Testament says so little about her. He presents this as a lecture drawn from his forthcoming book *The Lost Mary*, given as part of a Biblical Archaeology Society seminar. He builds his case from a short list of New Testament passages about Mary, from family-related details in the Gospels and Acts, and from archaeological work at Sepphoris, Nazareth, and Mount Zion in Jerusalem. Tabor argues that Mary was a Davidic and possibly priestly woman from a family of several children, that she was widowed early, and that she remained in Jerusalem as a leader within the early movement alongside her son James. He says her later image as a sinless, heavenly figure grew up over a much more earthly and Jewish woman.

Tabor's main textual claim is that Mary fades out of the Gospels in sequence. Mark gives the Nazareth scene and the crucifixion. John never names her. Luke, in his view, begins to remove the family from the story, while Acts keeps James visible but leaves Mary only in the upper room. He reads the Nazareth "who is my mother" passage as a positive statement about a larger spiritual family, which he says is usually read negatively. On the archaeology, he describes first-century remains at Sepphoris, the excavations of Ken Dark at Nazareth, and the Mount Zion upper-room complex, which he associates with Mary's last years. He admits that several points, including her lineage and the size of the family, rest on inference from "little strings," and that the Byzantine and medieval traditions he discusses are not, in his view, historically reliable as to specific locations.

The talk ends with Tabor's speculation that Mary taught Jesus and James in a household setting, and that the moral teaching of James and the Sermon on the Mount reflect her voice. He closes with a brief thanks and no formal conclusion beyond that.

## Key Teachings

### The Gospels fade Mary out in sequence
Tabor says Mary appears in only about nine main New Testament passages. Mark has the Nazareth scene and the crucifixion. Matthew follows Mark and adds a birth narrative. John never names her, though she appears twice in the Gospel and at the crucifixion. Paul calls Jesus "born of a woman" without naming her. Tabor argues that the decline begins in Luke-Acts, where Luke changes Mark's material: "Is this not Joseph's son?" replaces the fuller family-naming of Mark, and the women at the cross and tomb are listed without the family context Mark gives. He says Acts can drop Mary but cannot drop James, so Mary is present whenever James is.

### The Nazareth scene is a positive text
Tabor argues that the scene in which Jesus' mother and brothers arrive at a crowded house in Capernaum and Jesus says, "Who is my mother and my brothers and sisters? Those who hear and do the word of God," is usually read negatively but should be read as inclusive. In his reading, Jesus tells the crowd that the family outside is welcome, and that everyone who hears the word belongs to the same family. He also treats Mark 6:3 ("Is this not the carpenter, the son of Mary, the brother of James, Joseph, Judas, and Simon?") as the key verse for reconstructing the family.

### A family of eight, with Joseph likely dead early
From Mark 6:3 and the reference to "his mother and brothers," Tabor reconstructs Jesus, four named brothers including James, and three sisters, for a family of eight. He says it is traditional that Joseph died early, which would make Mary a single mother. He presents the family size and her widowhood as his own likely reconstruction rather than something the texts state.

### Davidic and possibly priestly lineage
Tabor says that he believes Mary had Davidic descent and possibly priestly connections, and that his book develops this at length. He acknowledges that many scholars would reject this. In this lecture he sets the question aside, saying "I'm not going to emphasize in this lecture because it would be a separate lecture."

### Sepphoris and a possible urban upbringing
Tabor reports his excavation work with Jim Strange at Sepphoris, where he says first-century remains lie about a meter below the surface, including mosaics and a wall of a first-century house. He cites Josephus for describing Japha (Yafia) as the largest town in Galilee, and he argues that Nazareth is a suburb of that region. He proposes that Mary grew up in or near Sepphoris, which he calls an urban background, and that she then married Joseph of Nazareth. He cites a Byzantine tradition placing a family house on the southern slope of Sepphoris, but describes that tradition as legend in its details.

### Mary in Jerusalem after the crucifixion
Tabor argues that Mary spent the rest of her life in Jerusalem. He cites Acts 1 and 2, where Mary is with the apostles and the brothers in the upper room, and says that later art of Pentecost always places her at the center. He discusses the Mount Zion complex, which includes a Byzantine structure he believes preserves the upper room. Citing Bargil Pixner's work, he suggests that the buildings below and above reflect a first-century space, and he says he thinks the courtyard and well are where James and the brothers lived. He rejects the tradition that Mary went to Asia Minor with John, saying "This doesn't even make sense to me," and he identifies James as the beloved disciple in his view.

### Why Mary was marginalized
Tabor gives two reasons. The first is a shift away from interest in the earthly Jesus and his family, which he ties to Paul's statement "we don't know Jesus after the flesh" (2 Corinthians). The second is growing emphasis on sexual purity and celibacy, which he says led to revering Mary as a virgin and downplaying her human role. He also points to the Protoevangelium of James, which he uses to argue that Joachim and Anna were not celibate, and says that the later focus on Mary's holiness muted her human side.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mary as a virgin removed to heaven, detached from earthly life:** Tabor says the heavenly queen image in Marian art has overshadowed her human life as a Jewish mother and widow.
- **The Nazareth scene as Jesus rejecting his family:** Tabor argues Jesus is saying the crowd is also family, and that the scene is positive about Mary.
- **Mary as a temple girl raised in a vestal-like life in Jerusalem:** Tabor says he respects the legend but does not think it historical, and places her upbringing at Sepphoris.
- **Mary going to Asia Minor with John:** Tabor says this tradition does not make sense to him and that she stayed in Jerusalem.
- **Mary's sexuality and family as unimportant:** Tabor argues that later emphasis on celibacy and sanctity muted her role as a mother of several children.
