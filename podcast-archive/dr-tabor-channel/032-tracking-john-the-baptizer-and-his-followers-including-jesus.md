## Summary

Tabor asks whether the followers of John the Baptist (Yochanan HaMatvil, "John the Dipper") can be traced in the sources, and whether Jesus belonged to that movement. He argues they can, using a mix of texts (Luke's infancy story, a Q saying, a Hebrew Matthew variant, Malachi as read in a Dead Sea Scrolls copy, and Josephus in both the Greek and Slavonic versions), an archaeological site (the Suba cave west of Jerusalem), and comparative material from the Mandaeans of Iraq and Iran. He presents the Suba findings as a stratified sequence of rock-cut water installations and large numbers of deliberately broken jugs, which he links to ritual washing in the first century, though he is careful to say the cave is not proven to be John's.

The talk's central interpretive claim is that the Dead Sea Scrolls version of Malachi 3 describes two figures, a "Lord" (adon) who comes to the temple and a "messenger of the covenant," and that this supports a "two messiahs" pattern in which John and Jesus fit. Tabor says this is the chapter his book draws the most questions about. He reads Josephus's Antiquities passage on John as sympathetic and independent of the gospels, and argues that the Slavonic Josephus may preserve older material about John's ascetic lifestyle. He also suggests that a practice of ascetic avoidance of meat and wine survives in the tradition about James, and he contrasts it with Paul's position in Romans 14.

The lecture closes with an open-ended legacy argument: Tabor traces a line from John through James's family and the Ebionite tradition, with a tentative guess about the next family leader after James, and ends with footage of Mandaean baptismal rites filmed at Harvard. ## Key Teachings

### John's ethics and the Q / Hebrew Matthew evidence
Tabor says John's ethical teaching survives in variant forms. The "two shirts" saying ("he who has two shirts let him give to one who has none") appears in Luke's version of Q, and Tabor notes that the Hebrew Matthew (Shem Tov) parallels Luke's Q rather than the Greek Matthew. He cites a second case, the "good gifts" saying, where Luke has "the holy spirit" and Greek Matthew has something more general, and argues that Hebrew Matthew favors the Lukan wording. He presents this as evidence that Hebrew Matthew may preserve readings from an earlier form of Q. He credits George Howard for pointing this out, and says he expects more examples. He also uses Matthew 23 to argue that Jesus, in the Hebrew Matthew version, tells the crowd to follow the teaching of "Moses' seat" (the Pharisees and sages) but not their deeds, with "ordinances" in place of the Greek wording. Tabor connects this to the question of how the Torah was interpreted (halacha) and draws a parallel with James's rendering of a decision in Acts 15.

### The Suba cave and John's water rites
Tabor describes the Suba cave, west of Jerusalem near Ein Karem, which the Byzantine-period wall drawings associate with John, and which he says is about a kilometer from the traditional Ein Karem sites. The excavation found a stratified fill from the Iron Age through the modern period, which Tabor says is unusual for a cave. The early Roman layer (he dates it roughly 50 BCE to 150 CE) contains thousands of small water jugs deliberately broken, which he estimates at about 40 vessels per square meter, and a foot-shaped stone that he thinks was used for pouring liquid. Visitors would descend steps, perhaps undress, purify themselves, break a jug, and enter the water. He also reports Iron Age phases: a rock-cut corridor, standing stone pillars, and an Iron Age jug in a sealed chamber found in a later season. Tabor is explicit that identifying the cave as John's is an inference from the Byzantine evidence and its location, not a finding. He presents the "breaking of jugs" as the most important datum for the first-century rite and notes that critics have offered other explanations.

### The Mandaeans as a surviving John community
Tabor argues that the Mandaeans, a small community in Iraq and Iran that he says call themselves followers of John, preserve the practice of breaking vessels and ritual washing in flowing water. He says Mandaean contacts told him they have the same kind of jugs and break them in sacramental rites, especially weddings. He compares the rabbinic practice of breaking or marking vessels, and says the Mandaean explanation (vessels broken so they can never be used for common purposes) is simpler than the rabbinic explanation for the breaking of the glass at Jewish weddings. He argues that the Mandaeans understand their rites as effective, not merely symbolic, and compares this to Paul's language about the body and blood of Jesus. He does not claim a direct line of descent, but suggests the Mandaean tradition is a window onto the first-century movement.

### Malachi 3 and the "two messiahs"
This is the talk's core interpretive argument. Tabor says the first "messiah" in the Bible was Aaron, anointed with oil, and that anointed priests and kings both count as messiahs. He reads a Dead Sea Scrolls copy of Malachi 3:1 (the transcript gives it as "4q12a," which appears to be a garble; check the primary publication) in which the text reads "the Lord whom you seek ... will suddenly come to his temple" and "the messenger of the covenant." He notes that the Hebrew reading differs from the Masoretic text and the Septuagint, and he argues the Scrolls' "Lord" is a figure named "adon" (master or leader) rather than the divine name, and that the "messenger" is a separate figure, so two figures are sent by the Lord. He connects this to John as the messenger who prepares the way (Malachi 3:1, as read in Q and Hebrew Matthew) and to Jesus's entry into the temple during Passover week. He says the community rule and the Damascus Document, which he has on his website, speak of messiahs of Aaron and Israel in the plural. He acknowledges that this is not a lecture on the Scrolls' messiahs and that the reading depends on a heavily damaged fragment.

### Josephus on John, the Essenes, and Jesus
Tabor argues that Josephus's account in Antiquities 18 (which he calls paragraph 119 in his numbering) is sympathetic to John and independent of the gospels. Josephus calls John "a good man" who urged baptism for "purification of the body," and he says Herod feared John's influence and had him killed, which Tabor compares with Mark's birthday-party narrative. Tabor suggests Josephus's silence about John, Jesus, and James in the Jewish War may reflect his disdain for popular "brigands" and his wish not to link these leaders to them. He also reads Josephus's description of the Essenes in the Jewish War as sounding like a quietist, pacifist group, and says Josephus probably knew of the more radical apocalyptic side of the group that wrote the Scrolls. On Qumran, Tabor reports that Joe Zias (transcribed as "Zios") found an all-male cemetery and that soil sampling around the site found an area of ancient human waste that matches the purity rules in the Scrolls and Deuteronomy. He says this evidence is forthcoming in the journal "Dead Sea Discoveries," and he expects it to appear in BAR.

### The Testimonium Flavianum and the Slavonic Josephus
Tabor goes through the Greek Testimonium (Antiquities 18.63-64) and argues that many phrases ("if it be lawful to call him a man," "he was the Christ," "he appeared to them alive again the third day") are likely Christian additions, leaving a core about a wise teacher who drew Jews and Gentiles, was condemned by Pilate, and whose followers remained. He cites John Meier's discussion of the passage. He then discusses the Arabic (Pines) version, which he treats as less embellished, and the Slavonic version of Jewish War, which includes an extended account of a man in animal skins who summons Israel to baptism and freedom, speaks of a king who will set them free, and is brought before Archelaus. Tabor says the Slavonic material's political language and its details (e.g., the "heavenly sickle" rebuke of Herod) do not look like Christian interpolation to him, and that it may describe John's asceticism, including a diet of honey and "wafers" and abstention from wine and unleavened bread.

### Legacy: James, asceticism, and the Ebionite trajectory
Tabor reads Hegesippus (his "main account" of James) as describing James as abstaining from wine and strong drink and as vegetarian, which he sees as a continuation of John's asceticism. He connects this to the Ebionite gospel's account of John eating honey and wafers. He cites Romans 14 as evidence that a group in Paul's communities practiced such abstention and that Paul, while defending freedom to eat meat and drink wine, labels abstainers "weak in faith." He suggests the line continues through James's successor (a family member he thinks was chosen for his pedigree), then into the desert fathers and the Gospel of Thomas. He states this is a trajectory he sees, not something the sources state outright.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Christians believe in only one messiah.** Tabor says the Malachi 3 reading in the Dead Sea Scrolls describes two figures sent by the Lord, a "Lord" and a "messenger," and that he treats John and Jesus as fitting this pattern. He says he gets asked about this chapter more than anything else.
- **Josephus never mentions Jesus outside the New Testament.** Tabor argues the Greek Testimonium is heavily interpolated, but that a core summary (a wise teacher, Pilate's condemnation, followers who persisted) is plausibly Josephan.
- **Jesus told people to ignore the Pharisees completely.** In the Hebrew Matthew reading he presents, Jesus tells the crowd to follow the Pharisees' teaching ("according to their ordinances") but not their deeds.
- **Breaking the glass at a Jewish wedding is explained only by mourning the Temple.** Tabor says the Mandaean practice of breaking vessels so they can never be used for common purposes may be a better parallel, and that the rabbinic explanation may not go back far.
- **Archaeology can't say anything about John.** Tabor says Suba's first-century water installations and broken jugs are the first material evidence that could correlate with the texts, though he does not claim the cave was John's.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor sets Paul's Romans 14 position (freedom to eat meat and drink wine, with abstainers called "weak in faith") against the ascetic tradition he attributes to John and James.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor reads Hegesippus as describing James as an ascetic (no wine, vegetarian) and presents James's Acts 15 decision as rabbinic in style. He also suggests a family successor after James, which he presents as a guess.
- **Nazarenes, Ebionites, Essenes:** The Ebionite gospel's account of John's diet and the Slavonic Josephus are used together as evidence for a continuing ascetic line. Tabor argues Josephus's Essenes description may be softened and that the Scrolls group was more radical.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet, nonviolence:** The Hebrew Matthew reading of Matthew 23 (halacha, Moses' seat) and the diet and wine material (James, Romans 14) bear directly on Torah practice and diet.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Tabor says John's message ("repent the kingdom of god is here") and Josephus's account of a king who would set Israel free bear on the political reading of the kingdom message.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** The two-messiah reading of Malachi 3 is Tabor's main argument for placing Jesus and John as a pair of anointed figures.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Q, Hebrew Matthew, Luke's wording of Q, and Acts 19 are used to argue that the John movement persisted and that later gospel writers responded to it. Tabor says Hebrew Matthew "should inform some of our discussions of q."
