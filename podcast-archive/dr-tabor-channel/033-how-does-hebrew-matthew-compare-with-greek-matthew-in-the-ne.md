## Summary

Tabor asks whether the Hebrew text of Matthew preserved by the Spanish Jewish writer Shem Tov ibn Shaprut (c. 1400) is an independent witness to an early Hebrew gospel, rather than a back-translation of the Greek Gospel of Matthew. He begins with the ancient testimonies that link Matthew to Hebrew: Papias (through Eusebius) on Matthew's *logia* "in the Hebrew language," Irenaeus on a written gospel "among the Hebrews," and later statements by Origen, Eusebius, Epiphanius and Jerome. He then turns to Shem Tov's text, which survives in a manuscript in the British Library and was edited by George Howard. Tabor follows Howard in arguing that Shem Tov's Matthew is not a translation of the Greek. His evidence is linguistic (wordplays and Hebrew style), agreements with Codex Sinaiticus against the Byzantine text, and agreements with Luke's version of Q, the Gospel of Thomas and the Pseudo-Clementines. Tabor calls this a "foggy window" onto an earlier tradition and says he finds the case convincing, while noting Howard's view has not been accepted by everyone.

The talk then works through several Shem Tov readings: the Great Commission without a Gentile mission or baptismal formula (Matthew 28:19-20); the mountain where the risen Jesus appears, and "some doubted him"; a very exalted view of John the Baptist in Matthew 11, in which "all the prophets" and "the law" speak "until John" rather than being superseded; and a reading of Elijah's coming in Matthew 17. He also discusses Epiphanius's quotation from the Gospel of the Ebionites, where John eats "raw honey" and a honey cake rather than locusts. Tabor concludes that Shem Tov may preserve a hebrew source (possibly underground in Jewish circles), and that the Greek tradition should not be automatically preferred. The talk ends with pointers to handouts and to the Gordon and Johnson study, and with an announcement of a later lecture on John the Baptist. Tabor's Mount Tabor identification is one he rejects as a late tradition.

## Key Teachings

### The ancient testimonies point to a Hebrew Matthew
Tabor opens with the patristic evidence. Papias, via Eusebius, reports that Matthew "collected the oracles in greek" (*logia*) "in the hebrew language." Tabor says no one has satisfactorily explained what that means: a Hebrew original of our Greek Gospel, or a collection of sayings that could relate to Q or the Gospel of Thomas. Irenaeus (dated by Tabor to the 170s) says Matthew "also issued a written gospel among the hebrews." Origen, Eusebius, Epiphanius and Jerome repeat versions of this. Tabor notes that Eusebius's tone becomes more dogmatic about the four gospels as the centuries pass.

### Shem Tov's text is a polemic that preserves Matthew in Hebrew
Shem Tov ibn Shaprut wrote *Even Bohan* ("the touchstone"), an anti-Christian polemic from a Jewish standpoint, written in the period of the Spanish disputations. Tabor says Shem Tov quotes the whole Gospel of Matthew in Hebrew, which is what makes him valuable. Tabor reads Shem Tov as a refutation of Celsus, comparable to Origen's preservation of Celsus, though the comparison is his own gloss.

### Howard's argument: not a translation of the Greek
George Howard's edition (Hebrew and English, with commentary) is Tabor's main guide. Tabor says Howard argues that Shem Tov's Hebrew is not translated from the Greek, and that Tabor finds this "extremely convincing," especially on linguistic grounds (wordplays and Hebrew style). Tabor acknowledges that Howard's work relied on few manuscripts, and that Nehemia Gordon and Keith Johnson have since examined all extant copies (see Books & Resources).

### Textual affinities: Codex Sinaiticus, Luke's Q, Thomas and the Pseudo-Clementines
Tabor says Shem Tov often agrees with Codex Sinaiticus against the Byzantine text, which would put some of its readings in an old text tradition. He notes that Shem Tov's Matthew sometimes gives readings of Q as they appear in Luke, and also agrees with the Gospel of Thomas and with readings in the Pseudo-Clementines (second-third century Jewish-Christian writings that champion Peter and James and condemn Paul). Tabor says that if Shem Tov's readings come from "some older source that we don't have," that is what makes the text important.

### The Great Commission without the Gentile mission or baptismal formula
In Shem Tov, Matthew 28:19-20 does not include "go into all the nations" as a mission to the Gentiles (which Tabor links to Paul) or the Trinitarian baptismal formula. Tabor reads the commission as sounding like a rabbi sending disciples to teach what he taught. He says Eusebius quotes Matthew's Great Commission without the baptismal formula, and that the Pseudo-Clementines seem to know the same reading. He says he does not think the nations are meant in this text.

### Mountain, doubt and the Galilean appearance
Shem Tov's Matthew 28:16-17 says the disciples went to "the mountain where they had prayed," which Tabor takes as the mountain of the transfiguration. Tabor says the Greek makes no such link. He also argues that Shem Tov reads "some doubted him" (not just "some doubted"), so the doubt is directed at Jesus. Tabor says the mountain is probably a peak south of Caesarea Philippi, not Mount Tabor (see Misconceptions).

### John the Baptist: "greater than a prophet," and "until John"
Tabor treats Matthew 11:2-15 (drawn from Q) in detail. In Shem Tov, John's question is "Are you the one who is to come" and Jesus' reply is followed by "blessed is he who is not perplexed in me." Tabor says Jesus' statement that "among those born of women none has arisen greater than John" stands in Shem Tov without the Greek qualifier "the least in the kingdom of god is even greater than john," which Tabor links to later supersessionist readings. He also argues that Shem Tov's Matthew 11:13 reads "all the prophets and the law spoke until John" (the Hebrew "odd" versus Greek "until"), so the Law and Prophets end with John. Tabor says this high view of John does not survive in Mark, Matthew or Luke in this form, and that it is important for his later lecture on John.

### Elijah and John in Matthew 17
In Shem Tov's version of Matthew 17, the disciples ask why the scribes say Elijah must come first, and Jesus replies that Elijah "has already come and they did not know him and they did to him according to their desire." Tabor says this identifies Elijah with John, who is already dead, and that the disciples' question reflects an expectation of two figures (drawing on Malachi and the Dead Sea Scrolls). He notes that the Greek and Mark have parallel "all that was written of him" language.

### The Gospel of the Ebionites and the honey cake
Tabor cites Epiphanius's quotation from a Gospel of the Ebionites (or Hebrews), which says John's food was "raw honey that tasted like manna" and "pancake cooked with oil" (or "honey"), where the Greek reads "locusts and wild honey." Tabor points out that the words differ by one letter in Greek. He notes that Epiphanius rejects the variant and accuses its users of changing "the word of truth into a lie." Tabor relates the manna and honey imagery to a figure who restores the way of the Lord in the desert. He says Shem Tov has "locusts," so this variant is not in Shem Tov.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mount Tabor was the site of the transfiguration and resurrection appearance.** Tabor says the mountain of the transfiguration is more likely a peak south of Caesarea Philippi, since the chronology of Mark does not fit Mount Tabor, which he calls a late (Crusader or medieval) tradition.
- **Greek Matthew was originally Hebrew.** Tabor says it is unclear what Papias meant, and that Shem Tov's text is not a translation of the Greek, not that the Greek Gospel was written in Hebrew.
- **Modern English New Testaments give you one ancient text.** Tabor says eclectic editions are composite texts chosen by committee, so readers are not reading any single manuscript.
- **"Some doubted" means the disciples had weak faith.** In Shem Tov the Hebrew reads "some doubted him," which Tabor says makes the doubt directed at Jesus.
- **The least in the kingdom is greater than John (supersessionism).** Tabor says this qualifier is absent from Shem Tov, and that without it John is the greatest of those born of women, with the Law and Prophets ending with John.
- **John ate locusts and wild honey.** Tabor reports that Epiphanius's Gospel of the Ebionites says "raw honey" and a honey cake, a reading that Tabor says is not in Shem Tov.
- **Jesus Christ was his first and last name.** Tabor says Shem Tov calls him simply Jesus, and that his students assume "Jesus Christ" is a full name.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor points to Shem Tov's Great Commission (no Gentile mission and no baptismal formula) as a version that does not match the Pauline mission, and mentions the Pseudo-Clementines' condemnation of Paul.
- **The Ebionites/Nazarenes:** Tabor relates the Gospel of the Ebionites fragments and the Pseudo-Clementines to a Jewish-Christian tradition that may have preserved Hebrew readings. He assigns the Nazarenes and Ebionites handout as homework.
- **Jesus' view of John the Baptist:** Tabor's main theme in the Matthew 11 and 17 material is a very high view of John, as the last of the prophets, and he says this is a view that has been lost.
- **Kingdom on earth:** Tabor reads the kingdom of heaven as "trying to break in" in Matthew 11:12, with ill-advised people "spoiling it or rending it," but his main focus is textual.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor's central claim is that Shem Tov may preserve an independent Hebrew tradition, and he argues that Greek readings should not be automatically preferred. He also explains the editorial choices in modern translations.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor reads Shem Tov's Matthew 17 as a voice from heaven identifying Jesus as "my son my beloved," with the disciples then asking about Elijah. He does not develop a christological argument.
