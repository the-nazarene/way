## Summary

Tabor asks who Jesus's "band of brothers" were, whether they were members of his family, members of the Twelve, or both. He starts with the list of the Twelve, noting that the Gospels give little or nothing about several of them, and then turns to Mark's list of Jesus's brothers (James, Joses/Joseph, Judas and Simon), which Matthew edits and Luke drops. Tabor argues that the obscure apostles James, Judas and Simon (sons of Alphaeus/Clopas in his reading) may be Jesus's own brothers. He proposes that Mary was married to Joseph and then to Clopas, so that Joseph's line and Clopas's line merge, and that Jesus's father was someone other than Joseph. He calls this a hypothesis that cannot be proven without DNA evidence. He presents the four standard Christian positions on Mary's children (Protestant, Eastern Orthodox, Roman Catholic and Western views) and argues that the Western view should be adjusted.

The second half concerns James the Just. Tabor relies on Hegesippus (as relayed by Eusebius) for James's death in 62 AD and on the James ossuary, whose inscription he reads as "James son of Joseph, brother of Jesus." He discusses the forgery case against Oded Golan and the Kidron Valley tombs, including the tomb once called the tomb of James, and the tomb he and Simon Gibson studied in 2000, which he suspects may be the source of the ossuaries. He argues that James and then Simon led a family "dynasty" in Jerusalem for about a century, that the community fled to Pella and later returned to Jerusalem, and that texts such as the Didache, the Gospel of the Ebionites and the Q and Thomas traditions preserve a "lost christianity" distinct from the Pauline tradition. He ends by contrasting that tradition with Paul's influence, which he says was not widely taken up until Augustine and later thinkers.

Tabor presents this as a set of possibilities and hypotheses, not settled results. He says, for example, that "it's a hypothesis that i don't know that it can be proven."

## Key Teachings

### The Twelve and the obscure apostles

Tabor notes that most people can name Peter, Andrew, James, John, and possibly Philip, Bartholomew, Matthew and Thomas, but few can name the last three (James son of Alphaeus, Judas also called Thaddeus, and Simon the Zealot/Canaanite). He says the Gospels give at least one anecdote for most of the Twelve, but "there's no stories about these at all" for the last three. He uses this gap to ask whether the obscure apostles could be Jesus's brothers. He connects Alphaeus with Clopas, suggesting the Hebrew "khalaf" with a "cat" is very similar or the same word. He also says the British Broadcasting Corporation (BBC) series on the disciples offered only a five-minute program on these three unless it could be framed around the brothers of Jesus, which led to a two-hour treatment.

### Mark's list of brothers, Matthew's and Luke's changes

Tabor identifies Mark's list of Jesus's brothers (James, Joses, Judas and Simon, with unnamed sisters) as the earliest record. He says Matthew "fixes" the text by writing "son of Mary and Joseph" and changing the name to Joseph, while Luke "is not real interested in the family" and omits it. He argues that Mark's "son of Mary" is an old and unusual formulation, since in Jewish practice a person is normally identified by the father's name. He says the variant tradition "gets thick" at this point, and calls the phrasing "a problem" that scribes tried to fix. He cites the Greek name Joseph as an affectionate nickname for Joses.

### The question of Jesus's father and the Mary family

Tabor says the question "who is the father" is distinct from "who is the mother." He states his "working assumption as a historian" that Jesus had a father, and says he would write "unknown" on a birth certificate. He proposes that Mary was the wife of Joseph and later the wife of Clopas after Joseph "dies childless." He acknowledges the problem that this makes Clopas the father of four boys and also of girls, and that some names are repeated (both Mary, both Joseph, both Simon). He says Izaman (Eisenman) "talks about this" and that he himself "essentially developed" the idea, though Eisenman does not go as far as Tabor. He also says the Protestant and Eastern positions, and the Western view that Joseph was celibate, each reflect theological assumptions about sexuality. He criticizes the Hellenistic (Greek) idea that celibacy is high and sexuality low, contrasting it with a Jewish view that sexuality within marriage is holy and childlessness a curse.

### James the Just, Simon, and the "dynasty"

Tabor reads the Hegesippus account, relayed by Eusebius, as saying James was thrown from the temple corner, then beaten and stoned in 62 AD. He says Simon, called "son of Clopas" and a "cousin of the Lord" in later literature, took over. He argues that "cousin" is a loose term, and that if Clopas is the father of James, Joses/Joseph, Judas and Simon, then Simon is Jesus's brother. He describes the group as a "dynasty" because it was interested in the bloodline connection with Jesus and believed his spirit was with them. He notes Simon's long life (he says Simon died in the reign of Trajan, near 100 years old) and presents the family leadership as lasting almost a century. He adds that "the one thing that herschel shanks has done" on the Biblical Archaeology Review website is compile the reports on the James ossuary.

### The James ossuary and the Kidron tombs

Tabor reads the ossuary inscription as "Ya'akov bar Yosef, ahui d'Yeshua" (James son of Joseph, brother of Jesus). He reports the Israel Antiquities Authority case against Oded Golan, which argues the words were added to a genuine ossuary to increase its value. He says Golan claims he had it since the 1970s and "never really noticed" the inscription. Tabor says he does not want to accuse anyone of lying, and that the more interesting question is where the ossuary came from. He describes the "traditional tomb of James" in the Kidron Valley, which historians and archaeologists do not think is James's tomb, and the "Tomb of the Shroud" in the Hinnom area, which he and Gibson studied in 2000. He suggests that the ossuaries may have been stolen from that tomb between 2000 and 2002, so that it could be the tomb of the Jesus family. He says only DNA (mitochondrial DNA on the tested ossuaries) could settle the question, and that a volume of about 200 pages on the tomb is forthcoming.

### Pella, the Didache and the "lost christianity"

Tabor says the Nazarenes fled to Pella when the Romans surrounded Jerusalem, and suggests they lived in a wadi across the Jordan where Jesus himself had hidden. He cites Revelation's woman who flees to the wilderness, and 1 Kings 17 where Elijah is fed by ravens in the brook Cherith (which he locates in the same wadi). He says the community returned to Jerusalem after the war, probably around 73 AD, and that Bargil Pixner's identification of a first-century guest house on Mount Zion supports this. He presents the Didache, which he says "differs in many ways from pauline christianity" and "will remind you a lot of the dead sea scrolls," with its "two ways" teaching. He notes that the manuscript he discusses is a late copy with interpolations, including a trinitarian baptism formula. He also cites the Gospel of the Ebionites handout, in which James is at the Last Supper, and says Q, the Hebrew Matthew and the Pseudo-Clementines form part of the picture. He calls this tradition "a kind of a lost christianity or forgotten christianity," and sets it against the Pauline tradition, which he says was mostly admired rather than practiced until Augustine.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Dan Brown's book is non-fiction.** Tabor says the *Holy Blood, Holy Grail* claim to be non-fiction has been "fairly thoroughly refuted now by historians," though he praises Brown for raising the questions.
- **The Twelve are all well known from the Gospels.** Tabor says the last three apostles (James son of Alphaeus, Judas, Simon) have almost no stories attached to them.
- **Jesus's brothers are simply Joseph's children from Mary.** Tabor reviews the four standard positions and argues that the Western view should be revised, and that Jesus's father may not have been Joseph.
- **Luke and Matthew are the same on Jesus's family.** Tabor says Matthew edits Mark's phrasing and Luke omits the family entirely.
- **Paul's vision was the dominant force in the early church.** Tabor argues that Paul was mostly admired, not applied, until Augustine, and that the Jerusalem tradition was a distinct and "forgotten" alternative.
