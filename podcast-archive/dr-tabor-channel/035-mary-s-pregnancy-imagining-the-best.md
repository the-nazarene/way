## Summary

Tabor asks whether the claim that Jesus had a human father other than Joseph must be read as slander, gossip, or immorality. He argues it need not be. He opens with a hypothetical ("let's say that this is the father of Jesus and he was a Roman soldier") and says that critics who hear "Tabor says Jesus' father was a Roman soldier" assume the worst without knowing the circumstances. In his view, the only evidence is the label "son of Pantera," which he says is a name Roman soldiers used and which is "the story going around." He presents this as the sole datum and says nothing else is known.

Tabor then offers alternative readings that he presents as speculation. The relationship might have been a love story, the father might have been a young man, or he might have come from the tribe of Joseph and been among Greek mercenary troops with Israelite heritage. He says he wanted to defend Mary's privacy and asks why the assumption of a horrible story is "universally taken" for granted.

The clip ends with Tabor saying he tried to make this point in his book but did not get it across. He does not reach a conclusion about the actual paternity. The clip is a short, speculative defense of reading the tradition charitably.

Tabor's central argument is epistemic. He says "you know nothing about the circumstances of the story," so the assumption that a Roman father would be a horrible thing, including the assumption of rape, is unwarranted. He says "The point is we don't know anything about that."

### "Son of Pantera" as the only datum
Tabor identifies "son of Pantera" as the one piece of information. He says it is "a name that's used by Roman soldiers," and treats the soldier link as a rumor rather than established fact. He does not explain the label's history or cite any source for it in this clip.

### Speculative positive readings
Tabor proposes several alternatives, introduced with "What if": a love story "of the ages," a father from "the lost tribes of Israel," or a father from "the tribe of Joseph" who served among "Greek mercenary troops" and had "Israelite heritage." He frames these as possibilities, not claims, and says "Maybe he's the one that... was chosen to be the father."
