## Summary

Tabor asks when and how a dualistic view of the cosmos, in which the human self is a heavenly being trapped in a mortal body, displaced the older Hebrew view that this earth is the human place and death is a quiet end in Sheol. He calls the new outlook "Enochian Judaism," after the *Book of Enoch*, which he treats as the channel through which the idea reached Second Temple Judaism and early Christianity. He argues that this was "the most fundamental shift in human thought until the enlightenment" (01:33), a shift he locates between Abraham and Jesus.

His evidence runs in stages. He sets out the Hebrew three-level cosmos and reads Psalms, Job, and Ecclesiastes as witnesses to the older view. He then compares Gilgamesh and the *Odyssey* to show that death as a shadowy existence was widespread in the ancient Near East and Greece. The turning point, in his account, is a set of gold "prayer for the dead" tablets from Greek-world tombs, dated to roughly 350–400 BC, whose deceased claim, "my race is of Heaven alone." He follows this with Plato, Empedocles, and Cicero's *Republic*, the Maccabean martyr accounts, Ben Sira, Wisdom of Solomon, and *1 Enoch*, and finally Paul's transformed "spiritual body." He ends by saying that Judaism, Christianity and Islam were all reshaped by this view, though he notes that some would call it an enlightenment and others a pollution by Greek ideas.

### Key Teachings

### 1. The older Hebrew cosmos: three levels, and Sheol as silence

Tabor describes a three-level universe: heavens above (the Lord's), the "Dome of the earth" with its sun, moon and stars, and the Good Earth given to humans, with chaos waters on each side. Below lies Sheol, "the world of the dead." He argues that the dead there are not punished. The image is sleep in the dust, and the Hebrews "never had the idea that shio uh you're punished or any kind of Hellfire." He ties this to the poetic cosmology of a psalm about God dividing the sea and crushing Leviathan, and to Psalm 115, which says "the dead do not praise Yahweh." He contrasts the Hebrew "animated self" (nephesh) with the dust that returns.

### 2. Job, Psalms and Ecclesiastes as witnesses to the old view

Tabor argues that Job is "actually the greatest reinforcer of this view," not a text of hope beyond death. Job's wish to be hidden in Sheol is a wish, not a belief, and the book's ending, where God answers from the whirlwind, does not affirm an afterlife. He reads Job 19's "I know that my redeemer lives" in the JPS translation as Job wanting his words inscribed in stone so that a redeemer-advocate will someday vindicate him, not as a resurrection hope. He cites Psalm 88 ("my life draws near to shio") and Ecclesiastes' uncertainty about whether the spirit goes upward or downward. He reads David's words after his child's death (2 Samuel) as a statement that the dead do not return.

### 3. The Mesopotamian and Greek parallels

Tabor claims the view was "ubiquitous" across the ancient world. In the Gilgamesh epic, Siduri the barmaid tells the hero that "when the gods created humankind death for humankind they set aside life in their own hand," and advises him to enjoy his life. The plant of youth is lost to a serpent, echoing Genesis. In *Odyssey* 11, Achilles tells Odysseus he would "choose if I could live on Earth to serve as a Hired Man" rather than "be Lord over all the dead." Saul's summoning of Samuel's shade (1 Samuel 28) is cited as the same shadowy afterlife. He notes the Caiaphas tomb (found in the 1990s), where a Greek coin was found in a burial, as evidence of a Greek custom among the high priestly family.

### 4. The gold tablets: the earliest evidence of the shift

The core archaeological argument. Tabor describes 17 gold tablets from tombs in Crete, Thessaly and Italy, rolled into tubes and placed by the ear or hand of the dead, dated "350 maybe 400 BC." They give instructions for the soul in Hades: avoid the "lake of memory" and drink from the cold water that flows from the lake of memory, and confess "I am a child of Earth and starry heaven but my race is of Heaven alone." Tabor calls this "the first time that I've been able to document in Mediterranean Antiquity" a claim that the soul's essence is heavenly and the body is not the self. He argues that Hebrew texts show no such resurrection or heavenly-soul material at this time, so the shift starts in Greece. He also says the confession implies ethical living, since "the Guardians would know if you were falsely confessing."

### 5. Plato, Empedocles, Cicero: the reversal of life and death

Tabor treats Plato's account of Socrates' death, Empedocles' lament that he "wept when I saw this Dreadful Place" (being born into this world), and Cicero's *Republic* Book 6 (the Dream of Scipio, where the elder Africanus tells his grandson that "that life of yours which men so call is really death") as steps toward a reversal in which "life is death and death is life." He points to Hellenistic tomb inscriptions that give the death date as the birth date, reflecting the view that death was birth into true life. He calls the Phaedo's idea of the soul's immortality a Platonist commonplace and says the "irresistible" idea "conforms to our sense of worth" and answers the question of why good people suffer.

### 6. From the Maccabees and *Enoch* to Paul and later Judaism

Tabor reads 2 Maccabees 7 (the seven sons who say they will be raised with new bodies) as the first explicit resurrection argument, provoked by Antiochus Epiphanes' persecution and the problem of theodicy. He reads Ben Sira as still holding the old view and Wisdom of Solomon as teaching that "we're born for eternity." His main text is *1 Enoch*, especially the Similitudes (chapters 37–72), where Enoch ascends to a seventh heaven, sees the righteous dwelling there, and in chapter 72 is invited to sit on God's throne. Tabor argues Paul's "third heaven" experience (2 Corinthians 12) shows the same pattern, and that Paul's "resurrection" means transformation into a heavenly "spiritual body" (1 Corinthians 15), not resuscitation. He says Paul "wants to preserve the personal identity," unlike the Stoic or Buddhist "drop" image. He contrasts this with Luke 20's "like angels" and with Luke 16's Abraham's bosom. He calls the result "Enochian Judaism" and says Judaism, Christianity, and Islam were all transformed by it, though Orthodox Judaism still affirms the world to come and even Kabbalistic reincarnation.

### Misconceptions Addressed

- **Job is about hope for an afterlife.** Tabor argues Job reinforces the old view. The "I know that my redeemer lives" line is a wish for an advocate, read in the JPS translation, and the book's ending does not affirm an afterlife.
- **Hebrew Sheol involves torment or Hellfire.** Tabor says the Hebrews had no such idea. Sheol is silence and sleep in the dust, and while Greeks "developed a little bit of that," Hebrew thought did not.
- **Sadducees denied life after death because they were "sad."** Tabor says this Sunday-school memory is wrong; the name relates to *zadok* (righteousness), and the sect's view is a theological position, not a mood.
- **Paul's "resurrection" means the corpse getting up.** Tabor says Paul uses the word for transformation into a heavenly, incorruptible state.
- **Paul is mainly about justification by faith (the "Luther Paul").** Tabor says Paul's main concern is that humans are destined for a transformed heavenly life.
- **Jews don't believe in life after death.** Tabor says that is a mistake: Judaism has the world to come (Olam Haba), and Orthodox rabbis and Kabbalists affirm it, though secular Judaism has shifted.
