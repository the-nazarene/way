## Summary

Tabor asks why the two figures closest to Jesus, John the Baptizer and Jesus' brother James, have been so thoroughly forgotten in later Christian memory, and why the family of Jesus was played down or erased. He argues that Jesus' movement was a dynasty, with leadership passing by bloodline from Jesus to James, and then through the wider family, rather than only a set of followers. He presents this as a reading he is "not dogmatic about," not as settled fact. His evidence includes the family lists in Mark and Matthew, Mary's ambiguous family relations in John 19:25, the Gospel of Thomas saying 12, Luke-Acts's treatment of James, Clement of Alexandria, Hegesippus (as quoted by Eusebius), the pseudo-Clementine Recognitions, and Josephus's account of James's death.

The talk moves from the family questions (who the brothers were, and whether Mary's sister was married to a Clopas) to the leadership structure of the Jerusalem community: a council of 12, an inner three of James, Peter and John, and a body of 70. Tabor reads this as a "government in exile" awaiting the Son of Man. He then follows the succession after James's death in about 62 CE, through Simeon son of Clopas and Jude, to a list of later names that he thinks is probably a list of holders of a council seat rather than a run of successive bishops. The talk ends with the Bar Kokhba revolt, the scattering of the Jerusalem community (later called Ebionites), and the Roman church's preference for Paul and Peter. Tabor closes by saying he wants to rehabilitate John, Jesus and James in Christian memory.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The "Jesus Dynasty" thesis
Tabor says he moved from calling this the "Jesus family" to calling it a "dynasty." He compares it with Herod's attempt to found a dynasty (including his marriage to a Hasmonean princess), the Julio-Claudian line (where adoptions substituted for blood ties), and the Nazarene community's expectation that its leadership would pass through the family. He notes that leading a community through one brother is "not quite a dynasty unless we can trace it further," and says he will try to trace it.

### 2. The brothers of Jesus and the Mary question
Tabor reads the list of Jesus' brothers in Mark and Matthew as James, Joseph, Simon and Judas, and says the names (common in Galilee, linked to the Hasmonean family) point to a family with revolutionary sympathies. He reviews the three standard positions (full, half or step brothers, or cousins) and explains that the perpetual virginity of Mary created pressure to make them half-brothers from a previous marriage of Joseph, or cousins via Joseph's brother Clopas. Tabor says the Clopas view was favoured in the West because it kept Joseph chaste. He grants that Joseph likely had a brother named Clopas, but argues for a different reading: John 19:25 lists "Mary the wife of Clopas" at the cross, and Tabor proposes that she is Mary the mother of Jesus. He says it would be "very unlikely" for two sisters, both named Mary, to marry brothers and name their sons the same names, so the simplest reading is that Mary was married to Clopas after Joseph's death, and that the brothers were Jesus' full siblings. He says this is argued in his book.

### 3. James in Paul, Mark and Luke-Acts
Tabor argues that Paul's list of the "so-called pillars" (James, Peter and John in Galatians 2) puts James first, and that Paul calls him "James the Lord's brother." He says Luke "doesn't want to put it on the front burner" because Luke's hero is Paul. In Acts 1:13 Luke lists Peter and John first and then "James the son of Alphaeus," and Tabor reads this as a veiled reference to James the brother of Jesus, identifying "Alphaeus" with "Clopas" as a variant name. He notes that Acts 1:14 mentions "his brothers" among the women and believers, and that the other gospel "Hebrew Gospel" quoted by Jerome places James at the Last Supper. In Acts 15, James makes the decisive ruling about Gentiles, which Tabor says Luke records without naming James's role until that point. Tabor argues Luke "doesn't deny it" but does not emphasise it.

### 4. The Gospel of Thomas saying 12 and Clement of Alexandria
Tabor quotes Gospel of Thomas saying 12, in which the disciples ask who will lead them after Jesus leaves, and Jesus answers that they should go to "James the just, for whose sake heaven and earth came into being." He says he does not think Jesus said this, but that the saying reflects the memory of the Jerusalem community. He credits April DeConick with tracing the Gospel of Thomas to early Palestinian Jewish Christianity. He then cites Clement of Alexandria (as preserved by Eusebius) for the statement that Peter, James and John, after the Ascension, chose "James the just as overseer of Jerusalem." He presents this as evidence that James's leadership was widely known in early Christianity.

### 5. The Council of 12, the inner three and the 70: a "government in exile"
Tabor cites the Dead Sea Scrolls' Community Rule, which describes a council of 12 men with three priests, and argues the Jesus movement had a similar structure: a council of 12 with three prominent members (James, Peter and John), and a larger body of 70 sent out in Jesus' final year. He calls the 70 a "Nazarene Sanhedrin" and cites Acts' references to elders. He reads the structure through Daniel 7 and Matthew 19's "twelve thrones," describing a movement expecting the Son of Man to vindicate the saints and depose the "other dynasties," including Herod and Rome. He distinguishes this from armed revolt: he thinks they expected God to act supernaturally, and cites Brandon's argument that Jesus was a Zealot as one he learned from but ultimately does not accept for this group.

### 6. The death of James and the succession
Tabor presents Josephus's account (Antiquities 20) of Ananus son of Ananus, who convened the Sanhedrin and had James the brother of Jesus stoned. Josephus says the "fair-minded" inhabitants of Jerusalem were offended. Tabor says the same priestly family that killed Jesus also killed James, and that the Talmud curses the family. He quotes a longer passage attributed to Josephus that appears in Eusebius and Origen, saying the Jews' punishment came "to avenge James the just." He then cites Hegesippus (second century, quoted by Eusebius) on the church passing to James "the brother of the Lord," and on the surviving family members of Jesus gathering after James's death to choose Simeon son of Clopas, whom Tabor describes as the cousin or brother of the Lord. Tabor notes the age problem with Simeon (who would have been very old) and suggests that the Jude who succeeded him might have been Jude the son of James. He treats the later list of 12 successors (Eusebius and Epiphanius, down to Hadrian) as a list of holders of a council seat, perhaps prepared by Richard Bauckham, rather than a continuous run of bishops.

### 7. The Ebionites and the pseudo-Clementine Recognitions
Tabor cites the pseudo-Clementine Recognitions (a fourth-century text he thinks preserves second-century material) urging readers to accept no teacher "unless he bring from Jerusalem the testimonial of James the Lord's brother." He reads this as a dynastic principle of authority and sets it against Paul's claim to have received revelation directly from the risen Jesus. Tabor says the first 200 years of the movement included a wing (later called Ebionites, though he doubts they used the name) that gave James priority over Paul. He notes that the Ebionite position was not necessarily anti-Paul, but that it kept James in a higher place.

### 8. Dispersal after Bar Kokhba
Tabor says the Bar Kokhba revolt (135 CE) "utterly decimated" Judea and that the surviving Jesus followers, family-led, dispersed to Jordan and the Decapolis, where they became known as Ebionites and "kind of became more rigid." The Orthodox church in the West followed Paul, and by the third and fourth centuries James had been effaced and Mary had become the perpetual virgin. He says the book is meant to restore John, Jesus and James to Christian memory.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Peter, Paul and John were the leaders of the early church.** Tabor argues that Paul himself names James first among the pillars and that James was the head of the Jerusalem community, which Luke downplays rather than denies.
- **Luke falsified the early history.** Tabor says Luke is "not falsifying" but "deemphasizing," because he treats Paul as the central apostle.
- **Gospel of Thomas saying 12 records Jesus' own words.** Tabor says he doubts it is verbatim Jesus but thinks it reflects early Jerusalem tradition.
- **Mary's perpetual virginity and the brothers of Jesus are settled.** Tabor argues the Clopas-marriage reading of John 19:25 is more natural than the step-brother or cousin readings, but says he is not dogmatic about it.
- **The Nazarene movement was an armed revolt.** Tabor argues that the group expected a supernatural move by God and was not stockpiling weapons, though he says it still had "fairly high ambitions."

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **James and the Jerusalem community:** This is the talk's central subject. Tabor argues James succeeded Jesus, led the Council of 12, and was killed in about 62 CE, and that later succession passed to family members.
- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor frames the dispute as a choice between Paul's heavenly revelation and the Jerusalem apostles' direct contact with the earthly Jesus, and says the first 200 years of the movement did not treat this as simple.
- **The Nazarenes and Ebionites:** Tabor identifies the Jerusalem community with the Nazarenes and says it scattered after Bar Kokhba and became known as Ebionites, which he thinks is a name given by enemies.
- **Torah:** Tabor refers to the Torah's "be fruitful and multiply" commandment and the seven laws of Noah in the Acts 15 decision, but does not develop a separate argument.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Tabor reads the Council of 12 and the 70 as a "government in exile" that expected the Son of Man to empower the saints and depose Herod and Rome.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Not developed in this talk; Tabor's argument is about succession and family rather than Jesus' self-understanding.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor argues that Luke's narrative choices and later Christian memory (Mary's perpetual virginity, the Roman church's focus on Peter and Paul) shaped what was remembered about James.
