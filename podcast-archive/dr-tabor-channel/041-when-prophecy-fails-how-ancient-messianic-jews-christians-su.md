## Summary

Tabor opens with a passage from Josephus's *Jewish War* that says the revolt against Rome was driven "more than anything else" by "an ambiguous oracle found in their sacred scriptures" that one from Judea would rule the world. He argues the question for this talk is which prophecy that was, and what Jewish and early Christian groups did when the predicted end did not arrive. He links Tacitus and Suetonius to the same rumor and proposes that the oracle is the 70 weeks prophecy in Daniel 9.

He then follows two second-temple movements that he says counted down Daniel's 490 years: the Qumran (Dead Sea Scroll) community, using its Damascus Document, Community Rule, pesher on Habakkuk, pesher on Psalm 37 and a Melchizedek text, and the circle around John the Baptist and Jesus, using Mark 13 and Paul's letters. Tabor's evidence is mostly the internal chronology of these texts, which he reconstructs on screen. He treats the reconstruction as his own reading and presents Qumran's expectation of a "teacher of righteousness" as central to that chronology.

The talk ends with what Tabor calls three strategies for surviving failed prophecy: re-dating the scheme, reinterpreting the fulfillment (as Luke does with Mark), or abandoning timetables altogether, which he says the rabbis discuss in Talmud Sanhedrin 98 and which he says the later church eventually adopted. He closes with a brief note on modern dispensationalist and spiritualizing readings. The Q&A is not in this transcript.

## Key Teachings

### The "ambiguous oracle" in Josephus, Tacitus and Suetonius
Tabor reads Josephus (*Jewish War*) as naming a scriptural oracle that a ruler from Judea would rule the world, which he says Josephus treats as the main spur to revolt. He cites Tacitus (Roman historian, writing into the second century) saying the oracle was in "the ancient records of their priests" and was read by Romans as pointing to Vespasian and Titus. He cites Suetonius for the same rumor spreading across the Orient. Tabor's point is that the Romans heard the oracle as predicting Roman rule, while the Judeans read it as predicting their own messiah. He says he was initially unable to identify the text until he worked through Josephus's *Antiquities* verse by verse.

### Daniel 9 and the 70 weeks as the clock
Tabor identifies the oracle as the 70 weeks prophecy of Daniel 9, in which Jeremiah's 70 years are reread as 70 weeks of years, or 490 years. He divides it into 7 weeks (49 years), 62 weeks, and a final week, during which "the desolating sacrilege" appears. He notes that Mark 13:14 ("let the reader understand") alludes to this. His hypothesis is that Josephus took the "prince who is to come" as the Roman emperor, not the Jewish messiah, while the Judeans expected the fulfillment of their own hopes at that time.

### The Qumran teacher of righteousness and the 390/20/40-year chronology
Tabor works from the Damascus Document (CD), which, on his reading, places the planting of a root from Israel and Aaron about 390 years after the exile, followed by about 20 years of groping "like blind men" before the teacher appeared. He reconstructs the teacher's career at roughly 40 years, which leaves about 80 years of Daniel's 490 by his arithmetic, and then about 40 years left after the teacher's death. He notes that the Qumran texts describe a "liar" who split from the community and betrayed the new covenant, and that the teacher is expected alongside "the messiahs of Aaron and Israel." He also identifies three figures awaited: a prophet like Moses (citing Deuteronomy 18:15), a priestly messiah, and a Davidic messiah. He says the group's texts treat the teacher as a unique, mysterious figure to whom "all the mysteries" were revealed (1QpHab, cited through the Habakkuk commentary).

### Jubilee cycles and the final countdown
Tabor describes the Qumran group as counting 49-year jubilees, so that 490 years equals ten jubilees, with the tenth ending on Yom Kippur. Citing an 11Q Melchizedek fragment (which he calls "11q milk" in the captions), he argues the text places the "year of grace" for the sons of light and the men of the lot of Melchizedek at the end of the 490 years. He calculates that the first week after the night (441 years, from 9 × 49 + 7) would be followed by 42 years remaining, which he says fits the forty-year figure. He presents this as the group's own arithmetic, not a dated record.

### Psalm 37, Habakkuk, and the strategy of delay
Tabor cites a 4Q pesher on Psalm 37:10 ("a little while and the wicked will be no more") that applies the verse to the teacher's community and reads the forty-year countdown into it. When the expected end did not come, he says, the Habakkuk commentary (1QpHab) reinterprets the delay: the final age "shall be prolonged and shall exceed all that the prophets have said," and though it "tarries," it will "surely come." He argues this is one of the first strategies for dealing with failed prophecy: the date stays, but the group says God may extend the time.

### Jesus, Paul and the same forty-year frame
Tabor argues that the early Jesus movement counted down a similar period. He cites Mark 1:15 ("the time is fulfilled"), Galatians 4:4 ("the time had fully come"), and 1 Corinthians 7:29-31 (the "appointed time has grown very short," and advice not to marry). He reads Jesus's Olivet discourse in Mark 13 (including "this generation will not pass away") as evidence that the early community expected the end within about forty years of the death of Jesus, placing the death around 30 CE and the expected end around 70. He links Mark 13's "desolating sacrilege" to Daniel 9 and says Luke later reinterprets this as referring to the destruction of Jerusalem, placing the final end after "the times of the gentiles," which Tabor calls a historical reinterpretation that moves Daniel 9 away from the end of the age.

### Three strategies when prophecy fails
Tabor gives three responses. (1) Recalculate the scheme: Qumran, or the early church in Luke, moves the starting point or the period. (2) Claim fulfillment in another form: Luke says the destruction of Jerusalem and the statue of Zeus in the temple count as the desolating sacrilege, so the expected end is "not the end." (3) Abandon timetables. He says the rabbis in Talmud Sanhedrin 98 list many possible schemes and conclude it could come any time, and that this is "the safest" option and what the later church eventually adopted. He briefly notes that dispensationalist and spiritualizing readings later revive or redirect the same prophecies.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- The 70 weeks prophecy of Daniel 9 was simply a prediction of Jesus. Tabor argues that Josephus and his Roman audience read it as pointing to Vespasian and Titus, and that the Qumran group and the Jesus movement each applied it to their own timetables.
- Prophecies that fail lead a movement to disappear. Tabor argues the opposite in the cases he traces: Qumran recalculated and extended its timeline, and Luke reinterpreted Mark's expected end as the destruction of Jerusalem.
- The early Christian expectation of the end was unique to Christianity. Tabor presents it as one case of a broader pattern shared with Qumran and with a range of second-temple apocalyptic groups.

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor places Paul's advice in 1 Corinthians 7 (the "appointed time has grown very short") in the same forty-year frame he finds in Mark 13, and treats Paul's Romans 3-4 quotation of Habakkuk as a point of contrast with the Qumran reading.

- **Kingdom on earth and the end of the age:** The talk is about apocalyptic timetables, the "end of the age," and how groups handled their failure, with the expected overthrow of evil and oppression as the central question.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor compares the Qumran teacher and the anointed figure of Isaiah 61 with the Jesus of Luke 4, but he does not reach a conclusion about Jesus's self-understanding in this talk.
- **How the gospels were formed:** Tabor's example of Luke reinterpreting Mark's Olivet discourse, moving the end of the age after the "times of the gentiles," is a concrete case of gospel editing responding to unfulfilled expectation.
