## Summary

Tabor asks whether the Shapira scrolls, sixteen strips of inscribed leather that Moses Shapira offered to European scholars in 1883 and that were then declared forgeries, were dismissed too quickly. He presents this as a lecture for the Biblical Archaeology Society's "Bible and Archaeology Fest," summarizing two 2021 books, Ross Nichols's *The Moses Scroll* and Idan Dershowitz's *The Valediction of Moses*, and their joint article with Tabor in *Biblical Archaeology Review*. Tabor is a co-author of that article, and he says at points that the case is "getting close to a kind of a slam dunk," so his account is advocacy for authenticity, not neutral reporting.

His evidence is mainly historical and documentary. He traces Shapira's life and trade, the 1878 Bedouin provenance, the 1878 letter to Schlottmann, the 1883 Leipzig reactions (Guthe, Meyer, Ebers, Erman), and the London and Paris judgments (Ginsburg, Clermont-Ganneau). He argues that Guthe's report is more nuanced than its popular summary, and he points to Dershowitz's discovery of three handwritten "purple pages" in Berlin as evidence that Shapira was working through readings and not forging. He also cites a physical objection to the forgery theory (the strips are curved, so they could not have been cut from a Torah scroll) and a line of scholars who reopened the question (Allegro, Mansoor, Cyrus Gordon, Gil, Edmund Wilson).

The talk ends without a verdict on the text itself. Tabor describes the scrolls' later history (Dr. Philip Brooks Mason, a showing in Burton on Trent in 1889, and a trail that "goes cold in 1903") and says the scrolls may still be recoverable. He deliberately withholds the scroll's contents and sends listeners to the books, Dershowitz's Hebrew University/Harvard lecture, and a forthcoming BAR counterpoint article.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Shapira's provenance and the 1878–1883 sequence
Tabor says the strips were found by Bedouin in a cave in a wadi on the east side of the Dead Sea (Wadi Mujib, the Arnon). Shapira bought them for a small sum in 1878. The strips were wrapped in linen and stuck together, and one Bedouin reportedly kept them in his tent and prospered. Shapira sent a letter about them in September 1878 to Friedrich Schlottmann at Halle, who was "just livid" and called them cheap forgery, objecting to a Ten Commandments reading that resembled the Nash papyrus. Shapira then waited five years, read Bleek's *Introduction to the Old Testament*, and sought other opinions, including from paleographer Paul Schroeder in Jerusalem. He wrote to Strack in Berlin, then took the scrolls to Hermann Guthe at Leipzig in June 1883. Tabor says Guthe, Eduard Meyer, Georg Ebers and Adolf Erman were initially excited, and he quotes Meyer's July 8 letter calling the writing "wonderful." Tabor also says Ebers warned that the forgery-spotters were "scarecrows" keeping "clever sparrows" away from fruit, which Tabor reads as a caution against dismissing the scrolls.

### 2. The "purple pages" and Dershowitz's new evidence
Tabor calls Dershowitz's discovery in the Staatsbibliothek in Berlin of three handwritten pages by Shapira, probably written around 1878–1883, the key new evidence. Tabor argues they show a scholar reassessing readings ("does this word mean this and does this column mean that"), including visible mistakes, which Tabor says a forger would not leave. He adds that the letter to Bond in Amsterdam, the Schlottmann letter (recently found in the Israeli archives, per Tabor), and the Schlottmann correspondence together show Shapira as an investigator and not a forger.

### 3. Paleography and the comparison with other inscriptions
Tabor says the earliest objections relied on paleography, but that the critics were working from facsimile drawings and not the real letters, and that only Guthe's account gives real paleographic detail. He argues Guthe found the script combined features that "the most skilled paleographer could not invent so beautifully," and he links the script to the Mesha Stele (found 1868) and the Siloam inscription (found 1880). Tabor says the Siloam inscription's parallels show the Shapira script is not obviously a later imitation. He also points to the Dead Sea Scrolls copies of Deuteronomy in paleo-Hebrew (4Q45 and 4Q46) as a comparison, and says Dershowitz (a Hebrew Bible scholar) is leading that analysis while Tabor is "more of the early christian scholar."

### 4. The physical objection: curvature and the "cut from a Torah scroll" theory
Clermont-Ganneau's view that the strips were cut from the bottom of a Torah scroll is a central target. Tabor says the strips are curved, so "I don't think anybody would cut a torah scroll." He says the early critics even straightened the edge in their drawings. He attributes the rebuttal to Dershowitz and to Gil.

### 5. Shapira's character and death
Tabor argues the popular story of a broken, suicidal forger is not what Shapira's letters show. He says Shapira was "disappointed" rather than depressed, that he stayed in correspondence, and that he planned for Amsterdam's world's fair and perhaps the United States. He reports that a medical account mentions two shots to the head, which "raises a question," and that the reports disagree about the body's position and whether a gun was found. He does not claim murder, but says robbery is a possible motive. Tabor also notes that Shapira's trade included forgeries (the Moabite pottery, or "Moabitica"), which he says makes Shapira's name a shorthand for forgery, and that he has heard "shapiro the forger" since his graduate school days.

### 6. The reconsideration tradition
Tabor traces a line of scholars who questioned the dismissal: John Marco Allegro (1965), Menahem Mansoor (1956, which he calls a strong article "very dense"), Cyrus Gordon ("there's no longer any doubt that shapira's parchments were genuine"), Gil's academia.edu article, and Edmund Wilson's 1995 *New Yorker* piece on early skepticism toward the Dead Sea Scrolls. He argues the Dead Sea Scrolls' own early reception shows how readily scholars assumed texts could not survive, and that the "it couldn't survive" objection is ironic now.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Nobody accepted the Shapira scroll; it was always thought to be a forgery."** Tabor says Nichols's book shows the story is "much more complicated," with Guthe, Meyer, Ebers and Erman initially excited, and that Guthe's report is more nuanced than its popular summary.
- **"Scrolls like this could not survive two thousand years in a cave."** Tabor argues this objection is ironic, because the Qumran scrolls, which are widely accepted, were found in caves, and the early objections to them relied on the same reasoning.
- **"The strips were cut from the bottom of a Torah scroll."** Tabor says the strips are curved, so this is unlikely, and that the early critics straightened the edges in their drawings.
- **"The paleography shows forgery."** Tabor says the critics were reading facsimile drawings, not the actual letters, so the paleographic judgments were unreliable.
- **"Shapira was a depressed, suicidal forger who killed himself in Rotterdam."** Tabor says Shapira was disappointed, not depressed, and that his letters show he was still active. He notes conflicting medical and press reports (including two shots to the head) and says the death is not certain to have been suicide.
