## Summary

Tabor tells the story of how he and his team found the "Tomb of the Shroud" in the Hinnom Valley south of Jerusalem. On June 14, 2000, after a day of digging at the cave he calls the John the Baptist cave at Suba, he and five students went sightseeing with Shimon Gibson, an Israeli archaeologist and his co-director. They found a freshly opened tomb that had clearly been robbed, with broken ossuaries outside it. Gibson went inside with the Israel Antiquities Authority on the way, and a student then reported that a skeleton wrapped in a burial shroud lay in a loculus in the lowest of the tomb's three chambers. Tabor's central claim is that this was an intact first-century Jewish burial of a kind rarely found, since the family had not moved the bones into an ossuary as was usual. He sets out the evidence: a sample of the cloth sent to the University of Arizona in Tucson dated to the first half of the first century AD, and ossuary inscriptions and DNA work pointing to family links. The man was an adult male, probably of aristocratic birth, who had leprosy and most likely died of tuberculosis. Tabor also draws a parallel with the New Testament account of Jesus's shrouded burial, while saying he had "no reason to speculate" that this tomb was connected to Jesus's own tomb.

## Key Teachings

### The discovery and the robbed tomb
Tabor frames archaeology as often accidental: "what we most hope to discover we seldom find and what we least expect can suddenly appear." He gives two examples, the Dead Sea Scrolls (1947) and a crucified man's skeleton found by a road crew in Jerusalem (1968). The Hinnom tomb was found by chance while the team was sightseeing. Tabor explains that tomb robberies in the area were relatively rare, and that robbers usually take only the inscribed or finest ossuary fragments and break the rest, which is why the fragments outside the tomb suggested it had been robbed the night before.

### Jewish burial practice in the time of Jesus
Tabor, citing Gibson, describes two stages of burial. First the body was washed, anointed with oils and spices, wrapped in a shroud, and placed on a stone shelf or in a loculus cut into the rock. After up to a year, the bones were gathered into a limestone ossuary, often inscribed with the deceased's name. He says ossuaries were used in and around Jerusalem from about 30 BC to AD 70. The Tomb of the Shroud was unusual because the skeleton was still in its loculus in its shroud, so the family had apparently not returned for the secondary burial. Tabor says cloth normally could not survive outside a desert, so finding one in damp Jerusalem "seemed unbelievable." He notes that Gibson allowed that the burial might be from a later period, such as the Crusades.

### Dating the shroud at Tucson
The team hastily obtained permission for scientific export of a cloth sample and sent it to the University of Arizona in Tucson for carbon-14 dating. Tabor says the same lab had dated the Shroud of Turin to AD 1300 in 1988. He contacted Douglas Donahue, who had supervised those Turin tests, without telling him where the sample came from. Donahue reported that the cloth dated to the first half of the first century AD. Tabor quotes Donahue's cover letter, which said that "our friends from shroud of turin days would certainly have appreciated a result like this."

### Inscriptions and DNA: a family tomb
Of 20 ossuaries restored, three bore inscriptions the thieves had missed. The clearest reads "Maria" or "Mary," written in Aramaic, and a second possibly reads "Salome." Tabor reports that DNA testing on bone samples, which he says succeeded despite the 2,000 years, established a network of sibling and maternal links among those buried. He says families often reused a tomb over several generations. For the shrouded man, the tests showed an adult male, probably of aristocratic birth, with leprosy (Hansen's disease) who most likely died of tuberculosis.

### The link to Jesus's burial
Tabor says that the New Testament accounts of Jesus's shrouded burial are among the most valuable evidence for Jewish burial in Judea and Galilee in this period. Jesus's body was washed, wrapped in a two-piece linen shroud, laid with spices on a stone slab in a rock-hewn family tomb outside Jerusalem's walls. He says the shrouded man "must have been similarly prepared for burial." He then adds that "we had no reason to speculate that our tomb was in any way connected to the one in which jesus was initially taken," but suggests that their man "lived and died in jerusalem in the time of jesus" and may have seen the events of that Passover weekend.

### The illegal market and the missing fragments
Tabor says the team traced the missing inscribed fragments to the illegal antiquities market in the Old City, and that they explored a legal way to recover them. The intifada made that plan too dangerous. On a later visit, people who had spoken with him earlier began to act as though they had never met him, and he links this to an October 2002 announcement about an ossuary that alarmed people who deal in antiquities in the Old City. ## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That organic cloth cannot survive in Jerusalem's damp climate.** Tabor notes that the cloth was thought impossible to preserve there, which is why the find seemed "unbelievable," but the preserved shroud is evidence that it can survive in sealed tombs.
- **That archaeological discoveries are the product of careful planning alone.** Tabor argues that "time and chance are equal partners" with method, and that some of the most important finds come unexpectedly.
- **That tomb robbers take everything of value.** Tabor explains that robbers often remove only a few inscribed fragments and break the rest, which is why inscriptions can survive in a robbed tomb.
