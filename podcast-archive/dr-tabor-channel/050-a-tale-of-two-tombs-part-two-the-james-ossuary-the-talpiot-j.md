## Summary

Tabor tells the story of the "James ossuary," a limestone bone box inscribed "James son of Joseph brother of Jesus," and asks whether it, or the nearby Talpiot tomb with its "Jesus son of Joseph" ossuary, could point to the family of Jesus of Nazareth. He presents the ossuary's public history from its October 2002 announcement through its Toronto exhibit and the Israel Antiquities Authority (IAA) investigation. He argues that the inscription is "likely authentic," even though a June 2003 IAA committee declared the ossuary genuine but the inscription partly forged and Oded Golan was indicted. Tabor's main evidence is the expert paleographic and patina testing, Andre Lemaire's support for the inscription, and a circumstantial link: Golan's shifting account of where he got the ossuary, which Tabor and Shimon Gibson connect to the Hinnom valley "Tomb of the Shroud," where a matching ossuary border style was found.

The talk then moves to the 1996 Talpiot tomb story, which Tabor recounts through the BBC film crew led by Ray Bruce and Chris Mann. He lists the names on the Talpiot ossuaries and explains why the discovery was so sensational, while also reporting that the names were common and that an IAA spokesperson called the chances of a holy-family burial "almost nil." Tabor ends by proposing mitochondrial DNA testing of the James ossuary remains, which he says the IAA refused in November 2003, and by saying he hopes to pursue such tests after Golan's trial. He notes that Israeli law requires human remains to go for reburial, which limits further scientific work.

## Key Teachings

### The ossuary's early authentication
Tabor recounts that Golan showed a photo of the ossuary to Andre Lemaire in April 2002, and Lemaire, who was "immediately intrigued," later became convinced of its authenticity after studying the actual object. Herschel Shanks, editor of *Biblical Archaeology Review*, arranged for geologists at the Geological Survey of Israel and several expert paleographers to examine it. Tabor says the ossuary "passed all the authenticity tests with flying colors." His reasoning: modern cuts into ancient limestone lack the natural patina that coats the stone over time. Later, one crack through part of the inscription let the Royal Ontario Museum team confirm that the patina was "firmly adhering to the stone and consistent with the rest of the ossuary."

### The forgery verdict and Tabor's reply
Some critics argued that "brother of Jesus" was in a different hand and was added later. Others argued that even a genuine inscription could not prove the James son of Joseph was Jesus's brother, since all three names were common. Tabor reports that the IAA confiscated the ossuary in February 2003 and that a 15-member committee in June 2003 declared the ossuary genuine but the inscription partly forged. Golan was indicted for adding the phrase and for faking the patina and lying about his acquisition. Tabor argues the indictment left the public with the wrong impression. He says Lemaire still defends the inscription and that IAA geologists had to back away from their proposed theories of how the patina was produced.

### Golan's shifting account and the Tomb of the Shroud link
Tabor identifies the main inconsistency in Golan's story as its timing: Golan said in October 2001 he had owned the ossuary about 15 years, later said the mid-1970s, and at one point said 1967. Tabor notes the rest of the account stayed consistent: an Arab antiquities dealer in the Old City said it came from the Silwan area. In December 2002 Golan placed the find in the Hinnom valley, which Tabor identifies as the location of the Tomb of the Shroud. Citing Shimon Gibson, Tabor says only two tombs in that area were looted in the 1990s, and that one ossuary from the Tomb of the Shroud has a "simple and sized border" matching the James ossuary's style. Gibson and Tabor reportedly saw no other matching examples in the IAA warehouse. Tabor suggests a single family might have bought two ossuaries from the same artisan.

### The proposed DNA test
Because the James ossuary still held bone fragments when Shanks first saw it, and Golan later cleaned them out, Tabor proposes mitochondrial DNA tests comparing those remains with the Tomb of the Shroud remains, to see whether the James ossuary held a sibling or perhaps a mother from that tomb. Tabor says he and Gibson requested the test on November 17, 2003, and the IAA "promptly denied" it, reasoning that the bones and ossuary had been added by Golan to camouflage the forgery. Tabor hopes the test may become possible after Golan's trial.

### The Talpiot tomb and its names
Tabor recounts the 1996 story of a tomb in East Talpiot, found in 1980 after a construction crew detonated TNT, that held ossuaries with names associated with the Jesus family: a Mary, a Joseph, a second Mary, a Jude son of Jesus, a Matthew, and a Jesus son of Joseph. He notes the BBC crew found, among the roughly thousand or more ossuaries in the IAA warehouse, six bearing the name Jesus, two of them "Jesus son of Joseph." The crew was told the ossuaries were empty, indicating a likely earlier robbery, but Tabor says the official 1996 report states that they definitely held bones.

### Why the names were both intriguing and disputed
Tabor lays out the possibilities the producers raised: that the tomb held Jesus's remains with his parents, that the other Mary was a sister or Mary Magdalene, and that Jude might be a biological son. He then records the counterpoints: Mary was the most common female name of the period and Joseph the second most common male name after Simon. He quotes an IAA spokesperson who said the chances of these being the actual burials of the holy family were "almost nil." Father Jerome Murphy-O'Connor of the École biblique is reported to have said that proof would be disastrous for the faith. Tabor notes that Jesus of Nazareth also had a brother named Joseph, so a Joseph ossuary need not refer to his father.

### Israeli law, residues and the limits of testing
Tabor argues that Israeli law requires human remains to go to orthodox Jewish authorities for reburial, which seemed to rule out DNA or other scientific tests. He says, though, that most ossuaries, even those in state collections, still contain slight residues of bone, and that modern DNA tests "can yield evidence from the tiniest sample." He presents this as a reason tests may still be possible.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That the 2003 verdict proved the whole ossuary a forgery.** Tabor says the committee declared the ossuary genuine and only the inscription partly forged, and that the indictment gave the public the wrong impression. He argues the inscription is "likely authentic."
- **That the name "James son of Joseph" must refer to Jesus's brother.** Tabor acknowledges the critics' point that the names were common, and he treats the identification as an open, circumstantially supported possibility rather than a settled fact.
- **That the Talpiot ossuaries were empty.** The crew was told they were empty, suggesting an earlier robbery, but Tabor reports that the official 1996 report says they definitely held bones.
- **That Jesus could not have had a brother named James, or that Mary's virginity implies no siblings.** Tabor reports Golan's own admission that he, as a Jew, was familiar with the Christian teaching of Mary's virginity but had never imagined Jesus had a brother. Tabor presents this as an assumption shared by many people.
