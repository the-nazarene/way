## Summary

Tabor, speaking at a 1992 lecture at Reunion Institute (per the video description, recovered from a VHS tape), asks two questions. First, why it took more than forty years for the Dead Sea Scrolls to be fully released. Second, what the newly available texts show about the group that produced them and about its relation to Jesus and John the Baptist. He says the delay was mainly the fault of the Jordanian-era international team of scholars, not the Israelis, whom he credits with publishing their own material quickly. He describes the scrolls' community as a "wilderness apocalyptic new covenant movement" and rejects "Essene" as the only label.

His main evidence is a text he calls the "messianic apocalypse," which he says Michael Weiss identified in the photographs. Tabor argues it contains the first "absolute literary parallel" to a New Testament text: a list of messianic signs (healing the wounded, raising the dead, preaching good news to the poor) that matches Jesus' reply to John the Baptist in Luke 7. He presents this as evidence that Luke drew on a shared, technical list of messianic criteria. He also discusses an Isaiah 53 reading ("from the travail of his soul he will see light") that he thinks Paul may have used in 1 Corinthians 15. He is candid that a second text, which may describe a slain or slaying messiah, depends on a disputed vowel point and cannot yet be settled.

The talk ends with Tabor's overall view: the scrolls' group and the Jesus movement share a great deal of vocabulary and expectation, but Jesus probably broke with the group on Gentiles, women, outcasts, lepers and some Sabbath rulings. The Q&A that follows covers the Qumran settlement's identity, dating, the "Teacher of Righteousness," the Septuagint, and the Roman suppression of messianic groups. Tabor repeatedly says that several questions are unresolved.

## Key Teachings

### The delay in publication
Tabor traces the release history: the cave 1 scrolls were bought and published by the early 1950s, and Yadin published the Temple Scroll after 1967 "within two years." The bulk of the material, from Cave 4, was held by an international team (under Jordanian jurisdiction until 1967, then left in place at the Rockefeller Museum). The team was small and, in the 1940s to 1960s, included no Jews, and Tabor attributes the delay to that group and to scholarly rivalry. Robert Eisenman's campaign and Herschel Shanks's coverage in *Biblical Archaeology Review* (BAR) pushed for release, and the Huntington and Claremont libraries then opened their photograph sets. Tabor estimates "maybe twenty five hundred maybe two thousand" unpublished photographs at that point, and says the team was widened in the 1980s to include Israeli scholars.

### Who the group was: a wilderness movement
Tabor reads Isaiah 40:3 as the founding text of the group, and notes that John the Baptist quotes the same verse. He argues the "way" in the wilderness meant strict Torah observance, purity and separation from a corrupt Jerusalem priesthood. He proposes the label "wilderness apocalyptic new covenant movement" in place of "Essene," because the group believed itself the elect, true community of God and expected the end. On the settlement at Qumran, he says the proximity of the caves, the water system and the ritual baths (mikvaot) points toward residence, though he acknowledges other hypotheses (a Roman fortress, a villa) and that "nothing's agreed upon." He cites a Roman historian who located Essenes north of Engedi, though he does not name the historian in the talk.

### The messianic apocalypse: a literary parallel to Luke
This is the talk's central claim. The text says "the heavens and the earth will obey his messiah," then describes the messiah's spirit hovering over the "poor" (*anavim*), releasing captives, making the blind see and raising the downtrodden, phrases Tabor says echo Psalm 146. In line 12 Tabor reads the messiah as healing the wounded, raising the dead and announcing good news to the poor. He argues these phrases are also in the amidah, the daily Jewish prayer, which suggests a liturgical setting. Tabor's key point is that Isaiah 61 mentions good news to the poor but not the raising of the dead, while the scroll and Luke 7:18-23 both combine them into the same list. He says that in Luke, Jesus replies to John's disciples with that list, and that Luke 7 is part of the Q source, which he calls the earliest written collection of Jesus' sayings (per "the dominant theory"). He says this shows a technical list of messianic criteria was circulating early.

He also claims that the text is "high christology" in function, since the messiah rules heaven and earth and raises the dead, things Tabor says only God does. Tabor adds that this is the clearest scroll evidence for belief in resurrection of the dead, which he had assumed but could not previously prove.

### One Qumran rule or two messiahs?
Tabor argues that the common claim that the Qumran community expected "two messiahs" rests on very little. He says only one text (a version of 1QS) uses the plural "messiahs" (of Aaron and Israel). A newly available version of that same passage lacks the lines, so it may be a later addition or an editorial stage. He concludes that scholars should not repeat the two-messiah claim without re-examining the material. He also notes that "messiah" means "anointed one" and can refer to priests and kings generally.

### The "slain messiah" text and the vowel dispute
Tabor describes a fragment quoting Isaiah 11:1 (the scepter from the root of Jesse) with a line that, read one way, says the leader of the community, "the branch of David," was "put to death," with a high priest mentioned, and read another way, says the messiah will put an enemy to death. He explains that the Hebrew is unvocalized, so the verb "put to death" (*hamitu*) versus "they will put to death" (*hamitu* with different points) depends on how the vowels are supplied. He notes that BAR's cover had argued for the "slaying" reading, and that he had written a counter-article. He says the fragment is too damaged to settle, and that its line length is unknown. He does not claim it refers to Jesus. He says the people who could have been killed in that period include John the Baptist, Jesus and James the brother of Jesus, all descendants of David in some sense (he says James was a descendant of David, though the caption is unclear), and suggests the community may have been mourning a leader.

### Isaiah 53, "light," and Paul
Tabor compares the Qumran Isaiah scroll to the Masoretic text. In Isaiah 53:11 the scroll reads "from the travail of his soul he will see light," which the Masoretic text lacks and which the King James translation renders awkwardly. He argues that "light" here may be a metaphor for resurrection, that the reading was probably in the text used by early Christians, and that Paul's phrase in 1 Corinthians 15, "died ... according to the scriptures ... raised ... according to the scriptures," follows the same sequence: led to slaughter, buried in a rich man's grave, then seeing light. He says Paul is probably doing a "pesher" interpretation of this text. He notes that some modern translations (the new RSV, and he thinks the New Jerusalem Bible and NIV) now place the scroll reading in the main text or a footnote.

### Dating and the later history of the movement
Tabor says dating is very hard: the community's presence is archaeologically attested from about 160 BCE to the Jewish revolt of 68 CE, a span of more than two hundred years. He uses the image of a modern apocalyptic group reinterpreting world events as a model, and argues that some texts (e.g., a lament about a destroyed temple) could refer to 586 BCE, 70 CE, or the events of Antiochus. He thinks the group probably survived the revolt in part, with branches going to Pella, but was effectively crushed as a political force, since the Romans suppressed messianic movements. He cites Pliny's letter to Trajan (as "Plenty's letter") about Christians being executed for being "messianists," and Trajan's reply.

### Jesus in relation to the group
Tabor's own answer: Jesus "had a lot to do with this group," knew it and used its language, but "would have broken with such a group" over Gentiles, women, outcasts, lepers and "sinners," and was "a little looser" on Torah interpretation. His example is a scroll rule forbidding help to an animal that falls into a pit on the Sabbath, which he contrasts with Luke and Matthew (no verses given), where Jesus and the Pharisees allow rescuing an animal on the Sabbath. He says the scrolls and the Jesus movement are the same group only "if you take a broad view of apocalyptic messianic judaism."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Israelis were holding back the scrolls.** Tabor says this is "absolutely untrue"; he credits the Israelis with quick publication and blames the Jordanian-era international team and scholarly rivalry for the delay.
- **The Qumran community believed in two messiahs.** Tabor says only one text uses the plural, a newer version of that text lacks the lines, and the claim should be re-examined.
- **The scrolls are all pre-Christian Essene texts, with no bearing on Christianity.** Tabor argues the community spanned over two hundred years, some texts may reflect later events, and "just to say they're all before christ" is "increasingly difficult."
- **The missing books of the Bible were suppressed in a conspiracy.** Tabor says books like Enoch were "judged ... not up to standard" by various communities, not hidden, and remain available.
- **Ancient means more accurate.** Tabor notes that an old text "can be very corrupt," so the Dead Sea reading is only preferred where the weight of evidence supports it.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor's Isaiah 53 "light" argument presents Paul's 1 Corinthians 15 as drawing on a scriptural sequence (death, burial, seeing light) that may have been known to the early community.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor lists James the brother of Jesus among figures who were killed, and says he was stoned, a descendant of David; he treats this as possible context for the mourning in the slain messiah text, but does not link James to the scrolls.
- **The Nazarenes/Ebionites/Essenes:** Tabor says the scroll group called itself "the poor" (*anavim*) and that the term is used as a technical self-designation, which he links to the Ebionite idea and Jesus' "blessed are the poor." He rejects "Essene" as the only label.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet and nonviolence:** Tabor discusses ritual purity, mikvaot and the scroll Sabbath rule on animals, and says Jesus was "a little looser" on Torah interpretation. He does not discuss sacrifice or diet in depth.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** Tabor argues the messianic apocalypse is the clearest scroll evidence for belief in the resurrection of the dead, and that Isaiah 53's "light" may be a metaphor for it.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor says Luke 4 presents Jesus reading Isaiah 61 and claiming its fulfillment, and that the messianic apocalypse describes a messiah with functions of God ("high christology"). He does not claim the scrolls mention Jesus.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor argues Luke 7 belongs to Q, the earliest written source, and says the Ethiopic church's canon includes Enoch, while other traditions do not.
