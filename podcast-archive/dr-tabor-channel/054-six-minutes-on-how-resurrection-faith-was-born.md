## Summary

Tabor asks what we can know historically about how followers of Jesus came to believe he had been raised from the dead, and he sets aside the empty tomb and the gospel accounts as the starting point. He argues that the earliest evidence is Paul's letter to the Corinthians, which he calls "our only eyewitness source." In 1 Corinthians he reports sightings of Jesus by Peter, James, the Twelve, a group of 500, and finally Paul himself. Tabor reads Paul as describing a visionary experience, and says Paul cannot tell what kind of body he saw. He notes that Paul expects the resurrected to have a glorious, immortal body that is "not flesh and blood."

Tabor then turns to the gospels. He says they are not eyewitness accounts and that their authorship is uncertain. He argues that Mark 16 places the first post-death encounters in Galilee, not Jerusalem, and that Matthew 28 places the Eleven on a Galilean mountain, where "some doubted." He also points to the Gospel of Peter, whose ending (as he reads it) has the disciples mourning for eight days after Passover before going back to Galilee to fish. In his view, these accounts are later, and the visionary Galilean beginnings are older. He says the "flesh and blood" picture of Jesus walking around Jerusalem is not the origin of resurrection faith.

The talk ends with Tabor's summary: resurrection faith should be traced to "some sort of visionary experience" in Galilee, and the sightings were reported without anyone being able to describe the body involved. He presents this as his own reading ("that would be my take on it") and does not claim it is settled. The talk is short (about six minutes) and is a solo presentation by Tabor. He does not engage with other scholars or with objections in the transcript.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Paul is the earliest and only first-person witness
Tabor's starting point is 1 Corinthians. He says Paul writes in the first person ("I have seen the Lord," which he locates in chapter 9) and gives a list of sightings in chapter 15: Peter, then James, then the Twelve, then 500, then "all the disciples," and finally Paul ("last of all he appeared to me"). Tabor takes Paul as the earliest source and defines an eyewitness account as one in which a named author tells what he himself saw or experienced. On this definition, he says, the gospels are "secondary."

### 2. Paul's risen body is not described physically
Tabor says Paul "can't tell you what kind of a body he saw Jesus in." Paul does say that those raised at the parousia (Tabor's word for the second coming) will be transformed into a body like Jesus's, which is glorious, immortal, powerful, and "not flesh and blood." Tabor reads Paul's experience as a vision or apparition that Paul compares to what the disciples perceived. He does not spell out the comparison in detail.

### 3. Mark 16 and the Galilee tradition
Tabor reads Mark 16 as the earliest gospel account of the empty tomb. He notes that the young man at the tomb does not say Jesus "has been resurrected from the dead," but that "he's been lifted up," a term he links to apotheosis (exaltation to heaven). The messenger then says the disciples will see Jesus in Galilee. Tabor says he also recalls an earlier passage in which Jesus says he will meet them in Galilee after the crucifixion, but he states that whether Jesus said it "is irrelevant." What matters, in his view, is the memory that they saw Jesus in Galilee. He also says that Paul "wasn't in Galilee but he wasn't the first witness he was the last," so Peter, not Paul, is the first witness in the sequence.

### 4. Matthew 28 and the doubters
Tabor reads Matthew 28 as placing the Eleven on a mountain in Galilee, where they have the experience. He highlights the detail that "some doubted," which he treats as evidence that the sightings were not straightforward. He says the appearance was "glorious... powerful... amazing," probably like the Transfiguration in Mark 9. He adds that the later claim that "they all saw him" and the report of an appearance to 500 are things "we don't know anything about."

### 5. The Gospel of Peter's ending
Tabor calls the Gospel of Peter "a very important text." He says it "breaks off" with Peter announcing he is going fishing, and that the text places the disciples mourning and weeping eight days after Passover. For Tabor, this shows that the disciples had not been "hanging out with Jesus for a week," and that the Galilean fishing return is an early tradition. He takes the accounts of Jesus's post-death presence in the Gospel of Peter as later.

### 6. Visionary origins, not a revived corpse
Tabor's conclusion is that the birth of resurrection faith should be understood as a visionary experience in Galilee. He thinks Peter "saw something," and suggests it was probably like Paul's experience, "presumably in the sky or something of that sort." He says the sightings were reported as people having visions, not as "the Flesh and Blood running around Jerusalem looking at wounds." He describes the figure as perhaps "a bright light and a voice or something of that sort."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Belief:** The gospels are eyewitness accounts of the resurrection. **Correction:** Tabor says none of the synoptic or Johannine gospels are eyewitness accounts, and that only Paul gives a first-person account.
- **Belief:** The risen Jesus was a flesh-and-blood body that walked around Jerusalem. **Correction:** Tabor argues that the earliest reports are visionary and Galilean, and that the flesh-and-blood picture is not the birth of resurrection faith.
- **Belief:** Paul describes the physical appearance of the risen Jesus. **Correction:** Tabor says Paul cannot describe the body he saw and describes the resurrected body as "not flesh and blood."
- **Belief:** The first disciples immediately rejoiced in the resurrection. **Correction:** Tabor reads the Gospel of Peter as having the disciples mourning for eight days and returning to fishing, and he notes that "some doubted" in Matthew 28.
