## Summary

Tabor opens with Sepphoris, the Roman-era Jewish city four miles from Nazareth that he has excavated since 1996, and asks what Mary's life looked like in that setting. He argues that the early 4 BC revolt after Herod's death, and the Roman suppression that burned Sepphoris and crucified about two thousand men, shaped the family into which Jesus was born. He then turns to the gospels as sources. He dates Mark to around 70 AD, Matthew to around 80, Luke to around 90, and John to the close of the first century, and describes the Synoptic literary relationship and the hypothetical Q source. He says the Gospel of Thomas, a Hebrew Matthew, and second- and third-century apocryphal gospels exist, but that the New Testament gospels are the most reliable base for reconstructing Jesus, when read critically.

The main body concerns Mary's pregnancy and the birth narratives. Tabor works through Matthew's account (Joseph's dream, the Holy Spirit, the Isaiah quotation) and Luke's (Gabriel's annunciation, the Bethlehem census, the stable, and the implication that Joseph became legal adoptive father). He argues that Matthew's Isaiah citation was not originally about Jesus, that the "holy spirit" language stops short of saying God fathered Jesus in the mythological sense, and that the marriage likely occurred after the birth. He distinguishes the Immaculate Conception (Mary's own conception, per Catholic teaching) from the virgin birth (the source of Jesus's conception), and closes by saying that later Christian tradition idealized Mary so far that her ordinary humanity and Jewish married life were lost.

The talk ends with the description's "To Be Continued" framing. Tabor points to his longer lecture and book preview on "The Lost Mary" (linked in the description) for more. He presents his positions as a historian's reading, not as settled conclusions, and most of his claims rest on reading the gospel texts themselves.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Sepphoris and the Herodian-Roman setting
Tabor describes Sepphoris as a dominant city on a hill, visible from Nazareth, which he estimates had about 200 people. After Herod the Great died in 4 BC, Judas son of Ezekias broke into the royal palace at Sepphoris and armed his followers. Varus led three legions from Syria, burned the city, enslaved its inhabitants, and crucified two thousand men. Herod Antipas later rebuilt Sepphoris as his capital, with a theater of about four thousand seats, a water system and baths. Tabor cites Josephus as an eyewitness to the city's splendor and as the source for Herod's destruction of genealogical records and his fortress at Masada. His argument is that Mary's family moved to Nazareth in this aftermath, and that the Christmas story should be read against crucifixions along the roads and a burning city.

### 2. The gospels as sources: dating and relationships
Tabor's working framework is: Mark is earliest (around 70 AD) and supplies the narrative skeleton; Matthew (around 80 AD) uses Mark and a sayings collection he calls Q; Luke (around 90 AD) uses Mark and Q plus its own material; John (close of the first century) is independent of the Synoptics and more theologically focused, but still useful for geography and chronology. He notes that Matthew and Luke are the only gospels with birth narratives, while Mark and John begin with Jesus as an adult. He says the gospels are anonymous, and the traditional names are just that: traditional.

### 3. Mary's betrothal, pregnancy and the marriage question
Tabor reasons that Mary, around 14 or 15, was betrothed to Joseph, and that she became pregnant before the marriage. He follows Matthew's account that Joseph intended to break the engagement quietly. Mary went to Ein Karem to stay with Elizabeth and Zechariah, and Tabor infers the families were close enough that Jesus and John the Baptist were related. He reads Luke's phrase "son of Joseph" as evidence that Luke did not think Joseph was the biological father, and that Luke implies Joseph became Jesus's legal adoptive father through marriage after the birth. Matthew's note that the couple had no sexual relations until after the birth, he argues, fits this reading.

### 4. The angelic announcements and the Isaiah citation
Tabor contrasts the two accounts. In Matthew, Joseph receives a dream in which an angel says the pregnancy is "by a Holy Spirit" and tells him to name the child Jesus. In Luke, Gabriel speaks to Mary, who asks "how will this be since I don't know a man," and is told the Holy Spirit will come upon her. Tabor argues the "by a Holy Spirit" wording falls short of saying God was literally the father, unlike the Greco-Roman myths of Zeus and Hercules. He claims that Matthew's citation of Isaiah's "a young woman shall conceive" is applied to Jesus, but that Isaiah was speaking of a child in his own day in the eighth century BC. He presents this as a case where the Matthean reading departs from the original text.

### 5. Immaculate Conception vs. virgin birth, and the later idealization of Mary
Tabor separates two doctrines that are often confused. The Immaculate Conception, as Catholic teaching holds it, concerns Mary's own conception by Anna and her freedom from original sin. The virgin birth concerns the source of Jesus's pregnancy and, as Tabor puts it, "refers more to the source of the pregnancy than to the birth itself." He notes Catholic belief in Mary's perpetual virginity and says Luther, Calvin, Zwingli and Wesley shared that view. His broader claim is that Mary was idealized over time into a figure so far from her culture that her married Jewish life, her later children and her forefathers were lost from view.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Sepphoris is unimportant and absent from the Bible.** Tabor says the city is largely forgotten and not mentioned in the New Testament, and that it was the dominant regional city in Jesus's youth, four miles from Nazareth.
- **Immaculate Conception and virgin birth are the same thing.** Tabor says the Immaculate Conception concerns Mary's own conception by Anna, while the virgin birth concerns the source of Jesus's pregnancy.
- **Matthew's Isaiah citation was originally about Jesus.** Tabor says Isaiah's child was a figure of the eighth century BC, and Matthew applied the text to Jesus.
- **Matthew and Luke's birth stories are like the Greco-Roman myths of divine fathers.** Tabor argues the wording "by a Holy Spirit" does not say God fathered Jesus in the mythological sense.
- **Mary was always a figure of divine holiness with no ordinary married life.** Tabor argues that later tradition removed her Jewish humanity and her ordinary married life, including later children.
