## Summary

Tabor asks how Jesus could be a son of King David when Matthew and Luke give him no human father, and why their two genealogies differ even though both trace Jesus to David through Solomon and Nathan respectively. He frames the stakes early: the Hebrew prophets tied the Messiah's legitimacy to sitting on David's throne, so a claim of Davidic descent needed a credible pedigree (00:00).

His reading of Matthew's genealogy starts with its irregularity. Matthew lists four women in a male-only list (Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and "uriah's wife" Bathsheba), each with a scandalous story, and then a fifth, Mary, who is named without a father-son link. Tabor reads this as Matthew preparing readers for Jesus's unusual birth (01:32 to 04:39). He adds that Joseph's branch of the line was placed under Jeremiah's curse on Jeconiah, so Joseph's ancestry could not on its own secure the throne. Joseph is Jesus's legal but not biological father, and Tabor argues Jesus needed another route (04:39 to 06:14).

Tabor then argues that Luke's genealogy supplies that route. Luke runs backward from Jesus to Adam, and Tabor reads "Eli" as Mary's father, perhaps a short form of Eliakim or Joachim. ## Key Teachings

### Matthew's genealogy prepares the reader for Jesus's birth
Tabor says Matthew's list of 40 male names, from Abraham through David to Joseph, is unusual because it includes four foreign women with scandalous reputations: Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and Bathsheba (unnamed in Matthew, as "uriah's wife"). Mary then appears as a fifth woman in the final line. For Tabor, this signals that Matthew is preparing readers for the account of Mary's pregnancy before marriage (01:32 to 04:39).

### Joseph's line was under the Jeremiah curse
Tabor says Joseph's branch of David's family had supplied the ancient kings of Judah, but that Jeremiah pronounced a curse on Jeconiah, declaring that none of his seed would sit on David's throne. Tabor reads this as "as if Jeremiah were declaring the Covenant that God had made with David null and void." Because Joseph was Jesus's legal rather than biological father, Tabor argues Joseph's ancestry alone could not secure a claim to the throne, so Jesus needed another lineage (04:39 to 06:14).

### Luke's genealogy traces Mary's line
Tabor says Luke starts with Jesus and runs back to Adam, with 76 names. He reads Luke 3:23-38's phrase "of Eli" as Mary's father, arguing "two grandfathers means two separate family trees." He suggests that "Eli" may be short for Eliakim, a form of the traditional name Joachim, and says early Christian tradition gives Mary's parents as Joachim and Anna. Since Luke includes only males, Mary is not named in the list (06:14 to 07:50).

### Mary's line runs through Nathan, not Solomon
Tabor argues that Mary's connection to David ran through Nathan, Solomon's brother, who was also a son of Bathsheba. Nathan never reigned, so his line became obscure. In Tabor's reading, this gave Jesus a davidic claim through his mother that avoided the Jeconiah curse (07:50 to 09:25).

### Nazareth as "Branch town" and the Branch of David
Tabor translates Nazareth as "Branch town," deriving it from the Hebrew *netzer*. He cites the Dead Sea Scrolls, written before Jesus's lifetime, which he says often describe the future king as the "branch of David," drawing on Isaiah 11. He suggests the village may have been known as a settlement of royal-family members, and that the later followers of Jesus were called "nazarenes or branchites" (09:25 to 10:59).

### Mixed Judah and Levi ancestry, and the Talpiot ossuaries
Tabor notes six instances of the name Matthew in Luke's list, two of them sons of fathers named Levi. He argues that the Davidic (tribe of Judah) and priestly (tribe of Levi) lines may have been combined in one family, pointing to the pattern of Aaron, a Levite, marrying a princess of Judah. He says the Talpiot tomb's ossuaries, which hold names including two Marys, a Joseph, a Jesus, a Jude, and a Matya (Matthew), support this as a plausible family pattern. He recounts seeing the ossuaries in the Israel Antiquities Warehouse at Beth Shemesh (10:59 to 12:34).

- **Implied belief that the genealogies are straightforward male lists.** Tabor argues Matthew's list includes four named women and a fifth woman in the final line, and that Luke's list covers Mary's line by implication, so neither list is a simple male pedigree.
