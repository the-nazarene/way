## Summary

Tabor asks why the Jesus movement survived the execution of its leader when other first-century messianic movements the Romans crushed did not, and why the first-century leadership of James, the brother of Jesus, has been so thoroughly forgotten in the Christianity that followed. He argues that after Jesus's death the Jerusalem community was led by James, and that Jesus's mother, brothers and wider family gave it an anchor of continuity. He says the familiar story, in which Peter took over the Twelve and Paul became his partner in building a Gentile church, is not how things happened.

His main evidence comes from three directions. First, he reads the book of Acts as a text written by Luke with a theological agenda: Tabor says Luke promoted Paul's mission, shifted the Galilee and cross scenes away from Jesus's family, and stopped his narrative in Rome around 60 AD, leaving out James's death in 62 AD and the succession of Simon. Second, he uses Paul's letter to the Galatians, which he treats as the earliest evidence that James was a leading figure and named before Peter and John. Third, he cites the Gospel of Thomas, saying 12 of Jesus's sayings directs the disciples to James, and then lists later witnesses (Clement of Alexandria, Hegesippus as preserved by Eusebius, and the Syriac *Ascents of James*) that he says agree on James's leadership.

The talk ends by framing Paul as the chief obstacle to recovering this Jerusalem legacy. Tabor says the next step is to look at Paul's dominant influence in the New Testament, which he calls the greatest challenge to any attempt to recover what he calls the Jesus Dynasty. The talk is a single-speaker reading with no guest or host.

## Key Teachings

### Luke rewrote the record to fit Paul's mission
Tabor argues that Acts "might better be named the mission and career of Paul," and that Luke's central purpose was to promote Paul's mission and message. He says Luke was the only non-Jewish writer in the New Testament, wrote around the 90s AD after the Jewish-Roman revolt had marginalized the movement's Jewish origins, and presented Paul, a Roman citizen, favorably to Gentile Roman readers. Tabor adds that Luke also softened Pilate and changed Mark's cross scene: where Mark names Mary, the mother of James and Joseph, and Salome, Luke speaks of the women who had followed Jesus from Galilee. Luke also drops Jesus's cry of dereliction and gives him a prayer for the soldiers. Tabor's conclusion is that "Luke was not writing history he was writing theology."

### Acts deliberately suppressed the Jerusalem family
In Acts 1, Tabor says, Luke lists the eleven apostles and then places Mary and Jesus's brothers in a single qualifying clause about prayer, which he reads as making them appear present but insignificant. Tabor says Luke also has all post-resurrection appearances in Jerusalem and ignores Galilee, although Matthew and John place a renewal of faith in Galilee. Tabor reasons that Luke wrote about 30 years after James's death in 62 AD, and knew Simon, another member of Jesus's family, had succeeded James. By ending in Rome around 60 AD, Luke "had no obligation" to record either event.

### Paul's own letter shows James in charge
Tabor treats Galatians as the earliest source for James's leadership, written around 50 AD. In Galatians 1, Paul says that three years after his conversion he visited Jerusalem, saw Peter for fifteen days, and "did not see any other Apostle except James the Lord's brother." Tabor says Paul calls James an apostle and identifies him as Jesus's brother, and that Paul's choice to meet James shows James was the one in charge. In Galatians 2, Paul describes a trip about fourteen years after his conversion to gain authorization for his Gentile mission from "the three pillars," James, Peter and John. Tabor stresses that James is named first, which he reads as an established order of authority, with James at the head of the Twelve and the three exercising primary leadership.

### The Jerusalem Council confirms James's authority
Tabor says that when Luke reports the Jerusalem Council in Acts 15, he is forced to show James in full charge, even though Acts has not described him as one of the Apostles or as Jesus's successor. The issue was whether Gentiles had to live as Jews, including circumcision and the full Torah. Paul and Peter took the more open position. Tabor says the decision mattered less than the "unambiguous Authority James wielded over the Nazarene movement." He also notes that Gentile god-fearers in synagogues were not expected to be circumcised, so James's ruling fit general Jewish practice.

### The Gospel of Thomas, logion 12
Tabor reads Gospel of Thomas saying 12 as the disciples asking who will lead after Jesus leaves, and Jesus answering that wherever they go they are to "go to James the just, for whose sake Heaven and Earth came into being." He describes Thomas as discovered at Nag Hammadi in 1945 and as preserving an Aramaic document from the early Jerusalem Church. He calls this "an outright statement from Jesus himself" handing leadership to James. The talk gives no further argument for the Aramaic origin or the date.

### Later witnesses speak with one voice
Tabor lists several later sources. Clement of Alexandria, writing in the late second century, says Peter, James and John did not seek glory but chose James the Just as overseer of Jerusalem. Eusebius, an early fourth-century historian, preserves the testimony of Hegesippus, a Jewish Christian of the early second century whom Tabor describes as from the generation after the apostles, that the succession passed to James the brother of the Lord. Tabor also cites the recently discovered Syriac *Ascents of James*, which describes Jerusalem seven years after Jesus's death with James "made overseer over it by our Lord." He says the sources come from different authors and periods yet "speak with a single voice."

### The Qumran pattern of twelve and three
Tabor notes that Paul's listing of three pillars matches a pattern from the Dead Sea Scrolls. He quotes the Community Rule, which says the council of the community is to have twelve men and three priests versed in all that is revealed of the Torah. He suggests the Jerusalem group's structure, a council of twelve with three leaders, follows a similar model, with James in the central position, Peter on one side and John on the other.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Peter took over the Twelve after Jesus and became the first pope.** Tabor says this "is not how things happened"; James succeeded Jesus and was the undisputed leader, and Peter's role is largely absent from his account of succession.
- **Peter and Paul were the twin pillars of the emerging Christian faith.** Tabor argues that Paul's own letters name James first among the pillars, and that Acts minimizes James's role.
- **Acts gives a complete history of early Christianity.** Tabor says Acts was written with a theological agenda and deliberately stops before James's death and the succession of Simon.
- **Mary's role was that of a divine mother only.** Tabor argues her role as the human mother of a family of six sons and two daughters has been lost, and that the family anchored Jesus's movement.
- **Jesus's brothers were insignificant.** Tabor says the New Testament marginalizes them, but Galatians and the later sources show James in a position of real authority.
