## Summary

Tabor asks what the original Jerusalem movement led by James, Jesus's brother, actually taught, and how that teaching compared with Paul's gospel. He also asks how and why James was killed. He argues that the Letter of James, the Q source, and the Didache preserve a non-Pauline Christianity: an ethic centered on Jesus's teaching, with no divinity of Jesus, no atonement through his blood, and no resurrection doctrine in the Didache. He says the Letter of James contains about 30 echoes of Jesus's teaching from Q and "draws nothing at all from the tradition of Mark." Tabor also reads James's own message in the light of his brother's execution, presenting James as following Jesus's model of suffering for righteousness.

For the death of James, Tabor relies mainly on Josephus, with additional detail from Hegesippus and Epiphanius. He places the killing in 62 CE, at Passover, under the high priest Annas (the younger), who had James arrested and stoned after the Roman governor Festus died and before his successor Albinus arrived. He reports that Hegesippus describes James being thrown from the southeast wall of the Temple and then stoned, and that Epiphanius names Simon son of Clopas as present. Tabor ends by noting that Jesus and James both died at Passover at the hands of the Annas family, and says, "if I am correct," that Jesus was crucified outside Jerusalem's eastern wall, placing both deaths close together in place and season.

Most of the talk is Tabor's own argument, and he frames several points as interpretations ("I believe James had his brother Jesus in mind," "if I am correct"). No guest or host speaks.

## Key Teachings

### The Letter of James as a witness to original Christianity

Tabor says the Letter of James was nearly excluded from the New Testament when the canon was formed in the fourth century, for two reasons. First, James mentions Jesus only twice, in passing. Second, the letter lacks any reference to Paul's view of Jesus as Divine Son of God, and it disputes Paul's teaching of salvation by faith apart from the works of the law. He cites James 2:14 and 2:17 ("what does it profit my brothers if a man says he has Faith but has not works can his faith save him") and James 2:10 on keeping the whole Torah. Tabor reads the letter as strongly upholding the Torah's positive, enduring validity.

### Ethical parallels with Q and the Didache

Tabor argues that the ethics of James are directly parallel to the sayings of Jesus preserved in Q, the sayings source behind Matthew's Sermon on the Mount and Luke's Sermon on the Plain. He counts "no fewer than 30 direct references Echoes and allusions" to Jesus's teaching in the short letter. He then turns to the Didache (which he calls the "dedicay"), a handbook for converts dating, in his view, to the beginning of the second century or earlier. Its first six chapters present a "way of life" and "way of death" built on the great commandments and a form of the Golden Rule. He says that nothing in it corresponds to Paul's gospel: no divinity of Jesus, no atonement through his body and blood, and no resurrection.

### The apocalyptic outlook and the title "Lord"

Tabor says James and Jude shared the apocalyptic outlook of Jesus and John the Baptist. James wrote that "the coming of the Lord is at hand" and that "the judge is standing at the doors," and Tabor argues that "the Lord" there refers to God, not to a second coming of Jesus. He says James and Jude call their brother Jesus "Lord" in the sense of a respected master, the Greek *kyrios* as a term of respect like "Sir" or "Mister," not as a divine title. Tabor contrasts this with Paul, who he says equates Jesus with the Lord of the Hebrew Bible and treats "Christ" as a name rather than a title.

### Jesus as davidic Messiah, not Divine Son

Tabor argues that for James, Jude, and the Q community, Jesus was the revered master and anointed davidic Messiah, heralded as king of Israel and executed for that claim before he could assume the throne. In Tabor's reading, Paul "downplays" Jesus's davidic lineage in order to assert his heavenly divine sonship, which he says "few Jews could accept." He states that there is "no evidence that James worshiped his brother or considered him Divine," and that James's emphasis was "not upon the person of Jesus but upon what Jesus taught."

### The suffering of the just and James's death as imitation

Tabor reads James's letter, which says "you have condemned you have killed the just one and he does not resist you," as referring to Jesus and also to John the Baptist. He connects this with Jesus's call to the twelve to "take up a cross," and argues that James saw Jesus as a model to follow in faith, ethics, and courage, and that James died "in testimony to the same idea." Tabor also reads Acts 15:16-17 as James's statement that the restored "tents of David" were meant so that "all other people may seek Yahweh," which Tabor calls "the lasting Legacy of the Jesus Dynasty."

### The death of James in 62 CE

Tabor gives the historical setting: after Jesus's death in about 30 CE, James led the movement from Jerusalem for three decades. Its main opponents were the Sadducean and high-priestly families who controlled the Temple. Tabor takes Josephus as the best source, since Josephus was a contemporary who knew James was Jesus's brother. Per Josephus, the younger Annas took advantage of the gap between governors (Festus had died, and Albinus was coming from Rome) to have James and others, "presumably nazarenes," brought before the Sanhedrin and stoned for breaking the law. Tabor reports that leading citizens complained to Agrippa II, that Albinus was furious and wrote to Annas, and that Agrippa removed Annas from the priesthood after three months.

### Hegesippus, Epiphanius, and the place of burial

Tabor reports that Hegesippus says James was pushed over the southeast wall of the Temple complex into the Kidron Valley, was still barely alive, and was then stoned and beaten to death with a club. Epiphanius names Simon son of Clopas, James's brother and Jesus's half-brother, as present and trying to intervene. Both sources, as Tabor reads them, place James's burial near the Temple, and Hegesippus says the grave was known in his own day. Tabor notes that the Herodian stones of the southeast corner are still in place and that the Mount of Olives lies just east, across the Kidron.
