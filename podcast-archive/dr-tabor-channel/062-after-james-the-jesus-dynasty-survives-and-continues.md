## Summary

Tabor asks what became of the family of Jesus, the "Jesus Dynasty," after James the Just was killed in 62 AD, and whether that line of leadership survived the Jewish Revolt and the destruction of Jerusalem. He argues that it did. Citing Eusebius and Epiphanius, he says the surviving apostles and relatives of the Lord chose Simon, son of Clopas (rendered "clothus" in the captions), as James's successor. He says Peter was probably still alive then and was allied with James, though he admits Peter's history is poorly documented. Tabor reads Peter's "Keys of the Kingdom" as a position of service under the Davidic leadership, not a papal succession. He presents a later list in which a third leader, Jude or Judas, may also have been a brother of Jesus, and he traces the family into the second century, when Hegesippus (preserved by Eusebius) reports relatives of Jesus being questioned under Domitian and Simon being executed under Trajan. Tabor's evidence is a mix of church-historian lists, Josephus, Daniel, and archaeology, including the James ossuary, a Peter-related ossuary, and the Masada skeletons. He hedges repeatedly ("we simply cannot know," "possible even likely").

The talk ends on a broader note. Tabor says the Ebionite tradition, which was marginalized by Eusebius, preserved teachings grounded in Jesus and his brothers. He closes by calling Jesus "the most influential figure in human history" and arguing that understanding his family matters to secular, Jewish, Christian and Muslim audiences alike. The talk is a single presenter's lecture with no guest.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Simon son of Clopas as James's successor
Tabor relies on Eusebius, who wrote that the remaining apostles and "the family of the Lord" took counsel and unanimously chose Simon, son of Clopas, who was of Davidic lineage. Tabor notes that Eusebius identified this Clopas (mentioned in the Gospel of John) as the brother of Joseph, Mary's husband. He argues the choice shows how central the Jesus family remained in the apostolic group's thinking. Eusebius and Epiphanius both list Simon second and a man named Jude or Judas third. Tabor says the Apostolic Constitutions, which he dates to the 4th century, call that third leader a brother of Jesus. If so, the line would run through four brothers: Jesus, James, Simon and Judas. Tabor says "we simply cannot know."

### 2. Peter and the Keys of the Kingdom
Tabor says Peter's role is poorly documented after Jesus's death. He takes Paul's account at face value to say Peter was allied with James and shared the Jesus Dynasty's message. He rejects the Roman Catholic reading of Matthew's "Keys of the Kingdom" as a sign that Peter led the movement. Using the image of the Key of David from Isaiah, he says the keys denote a chief-of-staff role in a royal household: "what Jesus was promising Peter was not succession but that he would occupy the right-hand position of responsibility." That role, he says, Peter fulfilled in service to James. On Peter's death, Tabor says the tradition of martyrdom in Rome is "difficult to know how much weight to give" and may be more theological than historical. He points to the Aramaic ossuary on the Mount of Olives inscribed "Simeon bar yonas," which he says is otherwise unknown in Jewish records, and suggests Jerusalem is a more likely resting place for Peter. He does not claim the ossuary is Peter's.

### 3. Daniel, messianic fervor and the Roman War
Tabor describes the 60s AD as a time of intense messianic expectation. He says Josephus, after the war, blamed his countrymen for misreading Daniel's prophecy of the "anointed one shall be cut off" and the "troops of the prince who is to come." He argues the chief cause of the war was religious. He says the Jerusalem followers read Daniel differently even before the war, spurred by James's murder in 62 AD. Tabor calculates that James's death came seven years before the siege in summer 69 AD, short of the 490-year period in Daniel. He notes the Romans' siege, the famine, and the reported crucifixions of up to 500 people a day, and the destruction of the Temple. Josephus's question of who the "anointed one who is cut off" was goes unanswered in Josephus, Tabor says, but the followers of Jesus "had read Daniel's prophecy in a similar way."

### 4. The flight to Pella and the Masada skeletons
Eusebius and Epiphanius preserve a tradition that the Jerusalem community under Simon fled before the siege, following a prophetic oracle, to Pella in the Decapolis near Wadi Cherith, where Elijah and Jesus are said to have hidden. Tabor suggests Simon might have been returning to a region Jesus knew. He says Qumran was abandoned during the war and that 960 refugees died at Masada in 73 AD. He reports skeletons found in a cave at the south end of the fortress during the 1963 excavations (24 people, apparently separate from the main Zealot group). He says these may have been Essenes or Nazarenes but calls the identification speculative.

### 5. Persecution, Hegesippus and the Ebionites
Tabor reports that Eusebius records a search under Vespasian for anyone of the royal House of David. He cites Hegesippus, also preserved by Eusebius, on two grandsons of Jude arrested and released under Domitian, who were leaders of churches because of their witness to the movement's origins. Hegesippus also linked Simon's crucifixion under Trajan (around 106 AD) to a sweep of the Jewish royal house. After the Bar Kokhba revolt (132-135 AD), Tabor says hopes for a kingdom on earth faded, and the surviving believers, known as Ebionites ("poor ones"), lived east of Palestine into the 4th century. Eusebius considered them heretics. Tabor says they held that Jesus was a plain man born of Mary and Joseph, kept the Torah, believed salvation came through works as well as faith (as in the Letter of James), rejected Paul as an apostate, and used a Hebrew Gospel of Matthew now lost except in fragments. Tabor argues these views are "grounded in the teachings of Jesus himself and that tradition passed on by his brothers."

- **Peter as the first Pope or head of the Jesus movement.** Tabor says the Keys of the Kingdom are a chief-of-staff role serving the Davidic leadership, and that Peter's Roman bishopric is a later claim that may have shaped the martyrdom legend.
- **Peter's crucifixion upside down in Rome as established history.** Tabor says the tradition is "difficult to know how much weight to give" and may be more theological than historical.
- **The Ebionites as simply heretical.** Tabor says Eusebius's label reflects his alliance with Constantine, and that the Ebionite beliefs (Jesus as a plain man, Torah observance, works with faith) are rooted in Jesus and his brothers.
- **The Jewish Revolt as purely political.** Tabor argues Josephus himself saw the war's chief cause as religious, tied to messianic expectation.
