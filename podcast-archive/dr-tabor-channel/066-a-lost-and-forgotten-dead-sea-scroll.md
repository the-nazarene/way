## Summary

The talk asks whether the "Shapira Scroll," a set of leather strips bought in 1878 by Moses Shapira, a Jerusalem antiquities dealer, from Bedouin near the Dead Sea, was a genuine ancient manuscript or a modern forgery. Tabor, who presents the lecture at the Flint Institute of Arts as part of the Sheppy Dog lecture series, says he is working with a team (Idan Dershowitz, Ross Nichols, David and Patty Tyler, and Matthew Hamilton) to argue for authenticity. The lost scroll is described as a text in paleo-Hebrew purporting to contain Moses' final speech, with a Ten Commandments version differing from the standard Bible.

Tabor's main evidence is threefold. First, he reviews the 1880s German and British scholarly reception: the scroll was rejected in part because Shapira had earlier sold "Moabite" figurines later judged fake, and because critics thought leather could not survive for about 1800 years in the desert. Several German scholars who examined the strips were enthusiastic, and Shapira's own purple-ink notes show him working to decipher the text. Second, he cites Dershowitz's paleographic and textual arguments, and Tabor's view that the curved shape of the strips is consistent with cutting from a Torah scroll edge rather than a forgery pattern. Third, Matthew Hamilton's research traced the strips to the collector Mason, whose 1903 obituary mentions owning "the famous Shapiro Scrolls," and an 1889 society meeting where they were shown.

The talk ends with Tabor's plan to recover the strips, which are now lost, and to test them scientifically for date and provenance. He says he hopes to vindicate Shapira's reputation and notes that, if authentic, the text would be the oldest biblical manuscript known. He presents these as his and his team's conclusions, and the authenticity question remains open in the talk itself.

## Key Teachings

### The forgery charge and the "Moabitica" precedent
Tabor says Shapira's reputation was damaged by earlier sales of clay and stone "Moabite" figurines, which the Berlin and British museums later found to be fakes. He notes that Schlottmann, who rejected the scroll at Halle, "never agreed that they were all fake" and suggests that Craftsmen in Jerusalem may have made some of the figurines. Tabor argues that the label "forger" attached to Shapira regardless of whether he forged the scroll, and that this affected how the scroll was judged.

### The text and the Ten Commandments variant
Tabor says the strips contained a Ten Commandments version different from the Bible, and that the Deuteronomy text presents Moses in first person, as in other passages. He points to the two standard biblical versions (Deuteronomy 5 and Exodus 20) as proof that the Bible itself contains variants. He says Ross's English translation includes a Shema-type command to love God "with all your heart," blessings and cursings typical of the entry into the land, and the claim to be Moses' final speech. Tabor connects Shapira's reading to Friedrich Bleek's 19th-century source-critical argument that earlier forms of Deuteronomy may have differed from the later edited text.

### Physical and paleographic evidence
Tabor argues that the strips' curve is consistent with cutting from the bottom edge of a Torah scroll, since a Dead Sea scroll copy of Leviticus is written on a similarly curved piece. He cites an 1979 silver amulet from Jerusalem's Hinnom valley with the priestly blessing as evidence that paleo-Hebrew examples were more numerous than in the 1880s, which he says requires re-examining the scroll. He also cites the 1883 Berlin and London committee reports, which doubted survival for 1800 years, and argues that Dead Sea Scroll preservation makes survival in dry caves plausible.

### Shapira's notes and the provenance trail
Dershowitz's article, which Tabor says is freely available, describes three pages of Shapira's own handwriting in purple ink, which Tabor reads as working notes from someone deciphering the text, including visible errors and corrections. Tabor argues a forger would have produced cleaner work. Hamilton identified the later owner as Mason, whose obituary (Mason died in 1903) lists the Shapiro Scrolls among his distinctions, and a Burton on Trent society meeting on 8 March 1889 where Mason showed them publicly.

### Ginsburg, Clermont-Ganneau and the 1883 rejection
Tabor describes Christian David Ginsburg, appointed by the British government to examine the scroll, who at first believed it was genuine but later declared it probably forged. Tabor says Clermont-Ganneau said the strips were cut from the bottom of a Torah scroll and then forged, and claims that evidence suggests Ginsburg wondered until the end of his life whether they were genuine. Tabor also notes a Punch cartoon that caricatured Shapira in an antisemitic style.

### The recovery plan
Tabor describes his team's search, including trips to England and Germany and the pursuit of wills and other records, with the aim of locating the strips and submitting them to scientific dating and provenance testing. He says the cave near Wadi Mujib (called Arnon in the talk, and "the Wadi Mujib" today) should be re-explored, since other manuscripts may remain there. He states his hope that Shapira's life would be vindicated and that, if authentic, the scroll "belongs to the world."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The strips could not survive about 1800 years in the desert.** Tabor says this objection was central to the rejection, and argues that the Dead Sea Scrolls show dry-cave preservation is plausible.
- **Shapira was a master forger.** Tabor says the "forger" label stuck after the Moabitica sales, even though Shapira was not accused of making the figurines and the scroll's authenticity is a separate question.
- **Biblical texts are uniform.** Tabor argues that the two biblical Ten Commandments versions (Deuteronomy 5 and Exodus 20) differ, so a variant text is not automatically a forgery.
