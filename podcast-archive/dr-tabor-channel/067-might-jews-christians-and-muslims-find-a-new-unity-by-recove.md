## Summary

This five-minute reflection by James Tabor asks whether Jews, Christians, and Muslims could find a new unity by recovering the historical Jesus, meaning what he was in his own time. Tabor, speaking alone, opens by saying Jews have historically struggled to think about Jesus apart from the conduct of people who acted in his name. In his account, what Jews have rejected is "not so much Jesus as the systems of Christian theology" that equated him with God, set aside the Torah, and displaced the Jewish people from their covenant.

His central proposal is that the circle of James and Jesus's other early followers, who kept hoping for messianic redemption and held to biblical ethics grounded in the Hebrew prophets, offers common ground. ## Key Teachings

### Jewish rejection targets theology, and Jesus may still be a messiah by historical measures
Tabor argues that Jewish objections have been directed at Christian doctrines that made Jesus God and nullified Torah, not necessarily at Jesus himself. He then says that if Jesus cannot be the Messiah for Jews as a Davidic descendant who began an unfinished messianic program, he is "surely by historical measures a messiah." Tabor does not develop this claim further in the talk.

### James and the early Jerusalem circle as a point of unity
Tabor presents James and Jesus's other original followers as people who "continued to Hope and strive for Messianic Redemption" and who held a biblical ethic based on the Hebrew prophets after Jesus's death. He says this has been "neglected" as common ground between Jews and Christians. He calls this the "Jesus Dynasty" and says understanding it opens a way to recovering Jesus's Jewish roots, which he notes many Christians have been exploring through Jewish customs and holidays.

### James's version as a rival claim to authenticity against Paul
Tabor argues that if Christians recognized James as Jesus's successor, they would see that his form of the faith has "claims to authenticity that override those of Paul even more." The talk does not spell out the textual comparison behind this claim. It is presented as the speaker's position.

### The Muslim parallel: Jesus as messenger of Abrahamic faith
Tabor says Muslims do not worship Jesus, whom he calls "Esau" in the transcript (the caption garbles the Arabic name, which is Isa), and do not consider him divine, but do regard him as a prophet or messenger. He says this emphasis on Jesus as "Messianic prophet and teacher" is "quite parallel" to the Q Source and the Letter of James. He reports that Islam holds that neither Jesus nor Muhammad brought a new religion, and that both called people back to what he calls "abrahamic faith," which he connects to the emphasis in James. He notes that the gulf between Islam and Christianity over Jesus is still wide, but says the Dynasty's view "conflicts" little with Islam's basic perception.

### Interpretation and humility in the quest
Tabor says modern historians know that sources and their own readings are not transparent, and that "it is impossible to gaze upon facts without interpretation." He says prejudice is a particular risk in the quest for Jesus because "no other figure in history elicits such passionate responses." He describes 40 years of work and expects that colleagues may reach different conclusions.
