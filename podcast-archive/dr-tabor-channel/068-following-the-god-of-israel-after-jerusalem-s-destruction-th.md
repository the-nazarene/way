## Summary

Tabor asks what Jewish response to the destruction of Jerusalem and the Temple in summer 70 CE can be found in the New Testament, and how a Jew could "follow the God of Israel" without the Temple. His answer is that the Gospel of Mark, read apart from its title and its place in the canon, is the earliest surviving Jewish text written after 70. He dates it shortly after 70 and calls it "a direct response to the Roman destruction of Jerusalem." He places it earlier than Josephus's *Jewish War* (dated to 73), which he describes as history rather than a guide to post-70 practice. He credits his former graduate student Jenny Beaumont's M.A. thesis, which he says he worked on with her.

His main evidence is textual. He lists seven features of Mark that he reads as a post-Temple Jewish proposal: Sabbath observance, ritual purity, the Shema, the scribe who ranks love of God and neighbor above burnt offerings, the widow's gift, the destruction of the Temple followed by preaching to the nations, and the coming of the Son of Man. He reads Mark 13 as a reinterpretation of Daniel and treats the resurrection as Jesus's exaltation rather than a revived corpse. He also argues that Matthew and Luke later "christianized" Mark. Throughout, Tabor frames these as his own interpretive case, invites the audience to test the dating, and answers two "what if" questions from Glenn, one about the Temple's survival and one about how Paul might have read its fall. The talk ends with an open discussion, and Tabor closes by urging listeners not to caricature Judaism as legalistic.

## Key Teachings

### Dating and method: read Mark as a historical Jewish document
Tabor asks listeners to strip the New Testament title and frame, and to read the work as "a historical less theological kind of document." He accepts Markan priority and the standard dating shortly after 70 CE. He says the book's first line, "the beginning of the good news of Jesus the Messiah," would serve as its title if it had one. He notes that Mark's ending is a live textual question and that he regards the shorter ending as authentic, pointing listeners to a BAS article on it. [00:00], [01:28], [18:42]

### Mark's seven features as a post-Temple Jewish proposal [18:42–21:49]
Tabor lists seven items he says Mark proposes for life without the Temple: how to keep the Sabbath, what to do about ritual purity, affirming the Shema, commending a wise scribe, the widow's gift, the Temple's destruction followed by preaching to all nations, and the coming of the Son of Man. He presents the list as one heuristic among several he could have chosen ("I could give 12. I could give 15.").

### Sabbath, purity and ethics (Mark 2, Mark 7, Mark 12)
- **Sabbath (Mark 2):** Tabor reads the grain-picking episode as "situational" Sabbath interpretation, with the "David" precedent showing that human need can override the law. He says the Pharisees "are doing it with this interpretive lens" too. [23:22]
- **Purity (Mark 7:1-23):** He argues that the saying about what defiles a person is about the heart, not food laws. He says the English "thus he declared all foods clean" is a poor translation of a phrase about "cleansing all food," and that the saying is "giving you a biology lesson" in a world without a Temple. He notes Pharisees had moved ritual purity "to the table." [24:56], [21:49]
- **The scribe (Mark 12):** Tabor calls the scribe's answer on the Shema and love of neighbor, which he says is "more than all the burnt offerings and sacrifices," the central point of the lecture. He argues that Matthew and Luke omit the scribe, and that Mark's "you're not far from the kingdom" is a judgment on the Temple's replacement. [21:49], [37:29]

### Mark 13 and the reinterpretation of Daniel
Tabor reads Mark 13 as "a reinterpretation of Daniel 7 and Daniel 9 and Daniel 11," with the "desolating sacrilege" already fulfilled in the Roman destruction. He reads the Son of Man coming "in the clouds of heaven" (Daniel 7:14) as the vindication of the people of God, not a literal return at that moment. He describes Mark's tone as sorrowful ("the beginning of Sorrows") with a call to endure to the end. [34:20], [35:55], [40:37]

### Resurrection as exaltation, not resuscitation
Tabor argues that Mark has no resurrection appearances in the earliest copies and that "go to the Galilee" is the young man's only instruction. He reads the Transfiguration (Mark 9, which he places on Mount Hermon) as a vision of Jesus glorified. He cites Norman Perrin for the view that Mark's resurrection is ascent and glorification, and links this to Paul's view that believers will be transformed. He also discusses the Gospel of Peter's ending, which he says suggests a Galilean vision. [49:?] See the resurrection paragraph beginning "so can you speculate how Mark saw Jesus after his death" [48:28] and the following paragraph [50:04]–[51:38].

### Mark among Jewish groups and Paul's delay
Tabor compares the post-70 groups to Pharisaic and rabbinic developments around Yohanan ben Zakkai, and says Mark's community was dealing with "the delay of the parasia." He contrasts Mark's collective reading of the Son of Man with Paul's expectation of imminent return in his early letters. In answer to Glenn, he says Paul's seven undisputed letters show an expectation of the parousia in his own lifetime and no clear sign that Paul read the Temple's destruction as a theme, while 2 Thessalonians 2 contains a later-sounding passage about an evil ruler in the Temple. [43:46], [45:19]

### Gentile inclusion within Judaism
Tabor says Mark was written for both Jewish and Gentile groups and that Gentile converts were expected to turn from idols and live morally (summarized in the Ten Commandments), while ritual distinctions were not required of them. He calls Mark's position "a form of Jewish faith" closer to ethical monotheism than to Christianity as later developed, and says he does not want Judaism caricatured as legalistic, citing the value of *kavanah* (intention) in Jewish prayer. [53:11], [54:46], [56:20], [57:53]

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mark is a Christian book.** Tabor argues it is a Jewish text, "Christians were Jews," and that the Traditions are about Jews, which is why he removes the title and reads it historically.
- **Jesus is God in Mark.** Tabor points to the "why do you call me good" passage as presenting the one God as the only good, and says the Messiah in Mark "is going to point you to God."
- **The Son of Man's coming is Jesus's literal return in the clouds.** Tabor reads Daniel 7 in Mark 13 as the people of God taking rule and the vindication of the community, not a single literal return.
- **Resurrection means a corpse revived and walking around Jerusalem.** Tabor says Mark's resurrection is Jesus's exaltation and glorification, and that Mark "doesn't care about revive corpses."
- **Judaism is legalistic.** Tabor says 19th-century Christian readings of Judaism were wrong and that *kavanah* (intentionality) is central to Jewish practice.
- **Mark's Gentile converts must take on Torah.** Tabor says Mark does not think Gentiles should convert to Torah, and that the Gentile requirement is turning to God and living morally.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor uses the seven undisputed Pauline letters as his baseline and contrasts Paul's imminent expectation with Mark's struggle with the delay of the parousia. He also says Mark may share Paul's view that being Jewish means turning to the one God and living ethically, not having a pedigree.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** The talk mentions James as dead by the time of Mark and as the leader of the Jerusalem church whose authority disappeared, but does not develop James's role in depth.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet and nonviolence:** The Sabbath, purity, the scribe's ranking of love over sacrifice, and the Temple-less practice of prayer and table fellowship are central. Tabor says Mark would not have pushed Gentiles toward Torah observance.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Tabor reads "you're not far from the kingdom" as a statement about God's rule and what God wants, not about the apocalypse.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** The resurrection-as-ascent argument and Paul's transformation of believers are the main points. The talk does not treat hell.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor says Mark's Jesus is the Messiah but not God, and that Mark's Messiah points to God.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor argues that Matthew and Luke christianize Mark, that Mark was largely ignored by the church fathers, and that Matthew was placed first in the canon. He also raises the question of the shorter ending.
