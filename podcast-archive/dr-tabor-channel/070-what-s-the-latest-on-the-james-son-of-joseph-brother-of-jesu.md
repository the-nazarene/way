## Summary

The talk, about twelve minutes long, takes up a question an unnamed interviewer puts to Tabor at the outset: is the "James ossuary," inscribed "James son of Joseph, brother of Jesus," authentic, and is it really connected to Jesus's family? Tabor carries the whole answer. He says the ossuary belongs to the antiquities collector Oded Golan, who told him he bought it from a shop in the Silwan area and did not know Jesus had a brother. Tabor describes the controversy that followed its revelation around 2000 to 2003, including a Toronto viewing with Hershel Shanks and Hebrew Bible scholars, and the long trial in which Golan was acquitted on all counts. Tabor is clear that the acquittal establishes no proof of forgery, and that absence of proof does not establish authenticity.

Tabor's main positive evidence is a peer-reviewed article, which he says is from last year, reporting soil tests by the Israel Antiquities Authority and the Israel Geological Society. He presents these results as showing the ossuary most likely came from the Talpiot tomb, a tomb with other names including Jesus son of Joseph. He reports the lead researcher, Aryeh Shimron, as fully convinced of that origin, while noting this does not settle whether "brother of Jesus" was added later. Tabor says he would "lean heavily toward" authenticity, but that whether the ossuary belongs to Jesus's brother requires further inferences.

The talk then shifts to future work: DNA analysis of ossuaries (Tabor cites a tomb he and Shimon Gibson studied, the "tomb of the shroud," with a PLoS publication), and a possible DNA prosopography across Jerusalem tombs. Tabor says that project has stalled because of COVID. The talk ends with a brief reflection on how fast DNA technology is advancing, with no firm conclusion on the James inscription beyond his lean toward authenticity.

## Key Teachings

### The ossuary's provenance and the acquittal
Tabor explains that the ossuary has no secure provenance. Golan claims he bought it in Silwan and kept it for years without recognizing the name. Tabor argues Golan is a collector rather than a dealer, that his collection is legal, and that he has no obvious financial motive. The trial acquitted Golan on the charges, and Tabor stresses that this shows no proof of forgery but does not establish authenticity. He adds that the trial was "not conclusive" on whether the inscription itself was forged.

### Soil analysis linking the ossuary to Talpiot
Tabor's central new point is the soil study. He explains that each tomb has an environmental "fingerprint" in trace elements such as iron, copper and aluminum, which can differ even between tombs about 30 feet apart. He reports that the study concluded the ossuary "most likely came from" the Talpiot Jesus Family Tomb, which would place it among tombs bearing names such as Jesus son of Joseph. Tabor notes that the result has not been widely discussed and that he has not seen it refuted.

### Authenticity as a lean, not a conclusion
Tabor says he would "lean heavily toward" the ossuary being authentic, and that "the case favors authenticity." He separates that question from two others: whether the ossuary belongs to Jesus's brother, and whether "brother of Jesus" was added to a genuine ossuary. He says it is possible that a later addition was made, and that the soil result does not rule this out. He also says that most scholars now consider an unprovenanced object best left alone, so the topic is not actively discussed in academia.

### DNA prosopography as future method
Tabor describes a planned DNA prosopography, using scrapings from ossuaries to map family relationships across Jerusalem tombs. He cites the tomb of the shroud, which he and Shimon Gibson studied in 2000, where DNA on skeletal remains suggested some maternal relationships and found one individual unrelated to the others, with results published in PLoS. He names tombs on the Mount of Olives with names such as Simon Bar-Yona, Lazarus, Mary, Miriam, and a tomb holding Mary and Martha in one ossuary. He says about 600 names and roughly 3,000 ossuaries are in the corpus, many without known tomb origin, and that the project paused during COVID.
