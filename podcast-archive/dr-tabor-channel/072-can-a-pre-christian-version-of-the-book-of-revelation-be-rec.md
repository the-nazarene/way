## Summary

Tabor, interviewed by host Derek Lambert, argues that the Book of Revelation in our Greek New Testament is a reworked version of an earlier Jewish apocalypse, which he dates before 70 CE. He says the Christian material is an "overlay" that can be removed, leaving a text that is more coherent and "about God" rather than Jesus. He traces the idea to J. Massyngberde Ford's Anchor Bible commentary on Revelation, which he says suggested a pre-Christian apocalypse possibly connected with John the Baptist and his followers. Tabor says he took the idea further himself.

His main method is literary: he points to phrases such as "the testimony of Jesus" and "Jesus Christ" in Revelation 1 and 2 that he thinks are interpolations. He compares this to the Testimonium Flavianum in Josephus, where he says the Arabic version does not contain the Christian additions. He also cites the repetition of the "testimony of Jesus" phrase, the twelve tribes and apostles as foundations and gates, and a monotheistic emphasis. He reports that in a graduate seminar students tried to reconstruct the original text. The full analysis and the recovered text are on his blog, which the interview does not reproduce, so the evidence presented here is mostly his verbal assertion.

The conversation ends with Tabor encouraging viewers to read his blog posts and judge the thesis for themselves. Lambert does not offer substantial counterarguments in the transcript.

## Key Teachings

### Revelation as a pre-Christian Jewish apocalypse with a Christian overlay
Tabor's core thesis is that an earlier Jewish apocalyptic work underlies the Greek text, reworked to add Jesus as Christ. He says the original "reads perfectly" once the Christian "tacons" are removed. He suggests that this would show that central elements of Christian eschatology do not depend on faith in Jesus. The transcript gives no full text, so the claim rests on the blog material he points to.

### Interpolation as a detectable method
Tabor argues that ancient interpolators "always go too far," and offers the Josephus Testimonium as an example of an obvious addition. He says that the Arabic version of Josephus, which he claims "didn't pass through Christian hands," reads as expected once the Christian phrases are removed. He applies the same logic to Revelation 1:1, 1:2, 1:4, and 1:9, where phrases such as "Jesus Christ" and "the testimony of Jesus" recur as apparent add-ons. He argues that the phrase "testimony of Jesus," which he says appears about a dozen times, is "always stacked on." He also says that in composite works such as Galatians, Jesus cannot be removed without the text falling apart, which he presents as a contrast with Revelation.

### Monotheism and the role of Jesus as angel
Tabor emphasises the text's strong monotheism, citing the description of God as "who is and was and is to come" and the image of God on the throne. He says the attempt to make Jesus an angel is "strained," with the figure telling the narrator not to worship him, and he contrasts this with the Gospels' worship of Jesus. He also cites the line "the kingdoms of this world have become the kingdoms of our Lord and of his Christ" as an example of material he regards as later.

### The twelve tribes and the twelve apostles
Tabor notes that the New Jerusalem has twelve foundations bearing the names of the apostles and twelve gates bearing the names of the tribes of Israel. He says the apostle names are "added," and he compares this with the Gospels' sayings about the disciples judging the twelve tribes. He says the twelve-apostles motif may already appear in Q.

### Dating and the "Jesus-sizing" of the text
Tabor says the final edition of Revelation probably dates to the reigns of Domitian and Nero, and that by then it was being "christianized or jesus-sized." He describes the theology as "not real Pauline" but with a "primitive Christian feeling."

### Paul, James, and the Nicolaitans
Tabor reports that Elaine Pagels (in her book on Revelation) and others link the passage in Revelation 2 about meat sacrificed to idols and sexual immorality with a Pauline school or church. He says this is "very possible." He then links the theme to Acts, where Paul is told by James to show observance by paying for four men's vows, as a sign that Paul does not teach Jews to abandon the law. He says that interpreters have often read the Nicolaitans as proto-Gnostics in the early second century, but he considers that date "a little late."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Revelation is a Christian book from start to finish.** Tabor argues that much of the text is a Jewish apocalypse with Jesus added later, and that its core "is really a Jewish book" about God.
- **The Nicolaitans are best read as early second-century proto-Gnostics.** Tabor says that date is "a little late," and suggests a link to a Pauline school or church instead.
- **Revelation's Jesus is simply the object of worship.** Tabor says the text pushes against this, with Jesus refusing worship in the angel passage, which he sees as strain from later editing.
