## Summary

The talk asks who James, "the brother of the Lord," was and why the New Testament and later church tradition give him so little prominence, even though Paul and the early sources place him in charge of the Jerusalem community. The host frames the discussion around the mythicist and historicist debate over whether James existed as a biological brother of Jesus, and Tabor says he wants to go further than that argument.

Tabor argues that James was a biological brother of Jesus, born to Mary. His main reasons are that Mark 6:3 and Matthew name James, Joseph, Simon and Judas as Jesus's brothers, and that Paul presents James as a leader in Jerusalem ("I saw James the Lord's brother"). He says the "biological" reading is the most obvious one and that most scholars hold it. He contrasts this with the Western view (cousins) and the Eastern view (Joseph's children from a prior marriage), which he attributes to the doctrine of Mary's perpetual virginity. He also cites the Gospel of Thomas, a reference to James as the one "for whom Heaven and Earth came to be," the Jewish idea of the 36 righteous, and later writers (Clement of Alexandria, Clement of Rome, Irenaeus) who describe leadership passing to James. He says Acts plays down the family and Mary, and that Acts 15 shows James deciding the matter. He suggests that James's earthly, ethical outlook survives in the Epistle of James and in material close to Q, and says he cannot prove that the historical James wrote the letter.

The talk ends with Tabor's remarks on the twelve tribes, Isaiah 11 and the regathering of Israel, and on Old Testament foreign marriages. Most of the content is Tabor's answers. The host supplies the questions and framing, and the closing segment has no clear speaker label.

### Key Teachings

### James as a biological brother of Jesus
Tabor says the most natural reading of Mark 6:3 and Matthew is that James, Joseph, Simon and Judas were Mary's children. He ties this to Paul, who first names James in his list of people he met. He contrasts this with the Western view (cousins) and the Eastern view (sons of Joseph from an earlier marriage). He attributes these alternatives to the doctrine of Mary's perpetual virginity and argues that the idea of a childless marriage would have clashed with the Torah command to "be fruitful and multiply."

### James as leader and "pillar"
Tabor says Acts 15 shows James making the decisive ruling, and that Paul went to see him to gain clearance. Galatians names James, Peter and John as the pillars. Tabor says the Gospel of Thomas, which has the disciples send people to James, and the phrase "for whom Heaven and Earth came to be," point to James as a central figure, linked to the Jewish idea of the 36 righteous who keep the world from destruction. He also cites a Malachi 4 curse as part of this background.

### Later sources and the Pseudo-Clementines
Tabor says Clement of Alexandria, Clement of Rome and Irenaeus describe leadership passing to James, and that the picture is "pretty uniform." He mentions Eisenman's thesis that the Stephen stoning in Acts is a cover story for James, and says he thinks the Pseudo-Clementines, especially the Syriac version, contain a reference to James being thrown down steps about seven years after the crucifixion and dying in 63. He says he does not want to take a firm position on Eisenman's argument but finds the parallels real.

### The Epistle of James and the "piety of James"
Tabor says the letter's Greek is too polished for a semi-literate Galilean, but he thinks it is still close to James's outlook. He notes that Jesus is named only twice, that the letter lacks Pauline themes (the blood and cross of Jesus), and that its ethics parallel the Sermon on the Mount and the Q source. He describes its piety as turning to God in prayer and repentance, citing Psalm 51, and says the letter's apocalyptic judgment is aimed at economic injustice such as withholding wages. He says he loves the letter but "can't prove that James the historical James wrote it."

### The twelve tribes and the restoration of Israel
Tabor reads the letter's address to the twelve tribes against Isaiah 11, arguing that the restoration of Israel comes first and that the nations are included. He says James probably held a view like Paul's about the regathering, though not necessarily in the same terms. He adds that the Hebrew Bible shows foreign marriages (Joseph, Moses) and that the restrictions in Ezra were specific to one political situation.

### The Dead Sea Scrolls and an earthly redemption
Tabor says much of James's outlook is reflected in the Dead Sea Scrolls: salvation and the future are conceived with the Earth as central ("on Earth as it is in heaven"), not as a dualistic cosmic drama. He describes this as what has been lost when the church story set James aside.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **James was only a "pillar," not a biological brother.** Tabor says the biological reading is the most obvious one and that most scholars hold it.
- **Mary was a perpetual virgin, so the brothers were cousins or step-brothers.** Tabor says these readings come from later doctrine and clash with the Torah command to have children.
- **Peter holds the keys and leads the church, while James is subordinate.** Tabor argues that the early sources place James in charge of the Jerusalem church, and that the Vatican's focus on Peter and Paul leaves James out.
- **James the son of Zebedee is the Beloved Disciple.** Tabor argues Zebedee is badly portrayed in the Gospels and suggests James the brother of Jesus may be the Beloved Disciple.
- **The Epistle of James is Hellenistic and so cannot reflect James's outlook.** Tabor says the Greek is too polished for James but the letter's themes are still close to his.
