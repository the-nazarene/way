## Summary

Derek Lambert asks Tabor whether Jesus, Paul, and the early movement were wrong about the "soon" return of the kingdom and the transformation of the world. Tabor answers as a historian of apocalyptic movements. He opens with a claim he says he teaches in his course: that messianic apocalyptic expectation has a "100 failure rate." He sets Jesus and early Christianity within a longer line that starts with the Dead Sea Scrolls community, which he calls the Qumran group, and their reading of Habakkuk. He argues that the Qumran group did not reinterpret its failed deadline as fulfilled. It extended the timetable and said "the mysteries of God are inexhaustible." Tabor then turns to Paul's expectations in 1 Thessalonians 4, 1 Corinthians 7 and 15, and Romans 8, which he reads as expecting a literal, cosmic transformation within Paul's own lifetime. He also argues that the Synoptic apocalyptic material and Daniel 9 may reflect an expectation of Jerusalem's destruction that did in fact happen.

Tabor's main position is that the "preterist" reading, which holds that the prophecies were fulfilled in some other sense, is the most sophisticated way to remain Christian. But he says it works only by making words "mean things they may not mean." He concludes that prophecy failed in the plain sense, and he lists the strategies believers use to cope with failure: resetting the date, dropping the subject, spiritualizing the claim, or accepting that apocalyptic movements have a poor record. He ends by suggesting that the world will probably end the way science describes, not in a cataclysm from the sky. Along the way he draws on Albert Schweitzer's idea of "interim ethics" as a way to keep Jesus's radical ethic without the expectation of cosmic collapse.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Failed apocalyptic expectation as a repeated pattern
Tabor frames the topic as "when prophecy fails" and says his course starts about 100 years before Jesus with the Dead Sea Scrolls, which "record in detail the expectations disappointment and failure of an apocalyptic Messianic eschatological group." He treats the Qumran group as the earliest real apocalyptic movement he knows of, and argues that all such movements in the West have failed. His underlying point is that Jesus's movement belongs to this series. The question is not only about timing. As the description puts it, what was expected never came and "history... marched on."

### 2. The Qumran response: prolonging time instead of reinterpreting
Tabor's main example is Habakkuk. He quotes "if it tarries wait for it it will surely come" and says the Qumran group read this as a promise tied to a deadline. He claims they did not become "preterists," meaning they did not say the event had already happened in an unexpected way. He reports that they said "all the times have been prolonged" and that "the mysteries of God are inexhaustible," so the fault lay in the timetable or in God's timing, not in the prophecy. He notes that the Qumran text refers to Daniel's 490-year, seventy-week period and to a Teacher who died, with a promise that "in 40 years" the men of wickedness would be destroyed. He compares this to the Jehovah's Witnesses and to Charles Taze Russell, whose 1914 date was later reinterpreted as a spiritual, hidden coming. He says the Qumran group did not make that move in its text.

### 3. Paul's change of Habakkuk's meaning
Tabor says Paul's main verse, "the just shall live by faith," comes from Habakkuk, but Paul renders it as "he who through faith is righteous shall live." In Habakkuk the sense is that the righteous person keeps faith while waiting for the vision that "will surely come." Tabor says Paul removes the apocalyptic setting and turns the verse into an argument about justification, citing Romans 3 and the need for "the blood of Christ" and grace. He calls this "the most interesting move in the New Testament." He says he will leave justification for another discussion.

### 4. Paul's expected transformation and the failure of his timeline
Tabor argues that Paul expected to be alive at the parousia. He cites 1 Corinthians 7 ("the appointed time has grown very short," "the form of this world is passing away," and the advice about marriage) and 1 Thessalonians 4 (the dead in Christ rising, the living transformed, and the "Lord in the air"). He says the living would be "transformed into these glorious Spirit beings" who "will judge Angels." He reads 1 Corinthians 15 and Romans 8 as describing a literal new creation in which "the creation itself will be set free from its bondage to Decay," which Tabor takes to mean the abolition of biological death. He says this did not happen. He also rejects the idea that these passages are symbolic, arguing that the descriptions are concrete and that the resurrection of Christ, which Paul treats as a real transformation, is the model for what follows.

Tabor cites Norman Perrin's line, as Tabor recalls it from class at Chicago: "what they most expected to happen never happened" (the parousia), while "what they least expected did happen" (the destruction of Jerusalem and the scattering of God's people).

### 5. Jerusalem's destruction and the Daniel 9 scenario
Tabor says he does not fully agree with Perrin. He thinks Jesus and his followers may have anticipated Jerusalem's destruction, based on Daniel 9's statement that "the prince who is to come will destroy the city and the sanctuary." He suggests that Mark 13, Matthew 24, and Luke 21 may preserve something Jesus expected, and that Jesus, like Jeremiah and the Qumran group, saw the Temple as a "den of Thieves." He thinks the Gospels make the event more literal later, especially Luke's description of armies surrounding the city. He suggests Jesus's followers may have expected this destruction to be followed by a kingdom of God on earth, a political and social kingdom, while Paul's expectation was more cosmic. He says he agrees with Paula Fredriksen on the political reading, while also saying Paul may have pictured the world dissolving as in 2 Peter.

### 6. The preterist position and its cost
Tabor describes preterism as the most sophisticated Christian attempt to keep the expectation of Jesus's near return while accepting that it did not happen literally. He says it still "make[s] words mean things they may not mean" (quoted in Quotable Moments). In his view Daniel 11 describes Antiochus's desecration and Daniel 12 describes the resurrection, and the end described in Daniel 11 "never happened," so the resurrection has not either. He contrasts this with the Restoration Movement, citing Alexander Campbell and Barton Stone, who he says argued that the Millennium is the church. He suggests that even such readings still have to hold open the final events of 1 Thessalonians 4, 1 Corinthians 15, and Romans 8.

### 7. Interim ethics and the wheel of history
Tabor gives Albert Schweitzer's concept of "interim ethics" as an alternative to abandoning Jesus's teaching after the failed expectation. Schweitzer's view, as Tabor states it, is that Jesus's radical demands were for the time between wickedness and the coming righteousness, and that following them does not depend on the end of the cosmos. Tabor says Schweitzer wrote that "Jesus threw himself on the wheel of history and it lunged back and crushed him," and asks whether one is willing to take up those ethics. Tabor links this to the actions of Daniel Berrigan and John Dominic Crossan's account of Jesus's message as a challenge to empire. He acknowledges that love of enemies and turning the other cheek are hard to apply in every situation.

### 8. Four strategies for coping with failed prophecy
Tabor lists the moves he teaches. (1) Reset the date, as William Miller did, though he notes this can only stretch about 20 to 40 years. (2) Drop the subject and marginalize texts like Daniel and Revelation. (3) Spiritualize the claim and assert that it happened. (4) Recognize that apocalyptic movements have a poor track record and that the world will likely end the way science describes, with the sun and solar system, and for each person at death. He ends by thanking the host.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That the expected return was really fulfilled in some hidden or symbolic way.** Tabor argues the texts describe concrete, literal events (the dead rising, the living transformed, the end of death), and that the prophecy in its plain sense failed. He calls preterism the most sophisticated attempt to avoid this conclusion, but says it requires words to mean something other than what they say.
- **That Paul's expectation was merely symbolic.** Tabor says 1 Thessalonians 4 does not read as symbolic, and that Paul expected to be alive at the parousia, so the timing and the expectation both failed.
- **That Paul's reading of Habakkuk preserves its original sense.** Tabor says Paul moves "the just shall live by faith" out of its apocalyptic setting and into an argument about justification.
- **That the failure of the expectation requires abandoning Jesus's ethics.** Tabor, following Schweitzer, argues that the radical ethic can be understood as "interim ethics," lived in the time before God's justice is complete, without the expectation of a cosmic end.
