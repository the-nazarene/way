## Summary

Tabor asks whether Jesus had a biological father, and whether the early tradition that his father was "Pantera" is a real name or a slur. He starts from his own presupposition that Jesus was a historical human with a human father, and that bodies do not rise from the dead. He says that if he were filling out Jesus's birth certificate he would write "father unknown," but he argues that the evidence for a named father is more substantial than most scholars allow.

His main evidence runs through several strands. Mark 6:3 calls Jesus "son of Mary," which Tabor reads as a signal that the father was not named. He cites Celsus (early third century, as reported in the transcript) and the Talmud, where Jesus is called "Jesus Son of Pantera." He puts weight on Tosefta traditions in which Sepphoris rabbis call Jesus "Yeshua bar Pantera" in neutral or positive terms, and on Epiphanius, who says the name was a family name of Joseph's or Mary's family. He argues that the pun on *parthenos* ("virgin") was first proposed by a German scholar in the 19th century and is not found in antiquity. He also discusses a tombstone of a Roman soldier named Tiberius Julius Abdes Pantera, from Sidon, found at Bingerbrück on the Rhine, which he connects to the chronology and geography of Jesus's family. Tabor labels this connection speculative, and he says he has not proven it.

The talk ends with Tabor's reconstruction: Mary may have been in a relationship with a young man named Pantera, a relative of Joseph or Mary, who was later impressed into the Roman army. He repeats that the father is still formally "unknown," but concludes that the name should not be thrown out. The conversation then moves to Samaritans and the "son of a Samaritan" insult in John 8, to Matthew's twelve-tribes framing, and to Morton Smith's comment on the Pantera tombstone. Tabor closes by asking for further work on the question.

## Key Teachings

### The virgin conception is a theological position, and the father question is historical

Tabor separates the "virgin birth," which he says is really a claim about conception, from the historical question. He calls the virgin conception "more mythological," comparing it to Greco-Roman heroes begotten by gods. He says the Apostles' Creed presents the theological position that Jesus had no biological father, and that most of his colleagues would answer the historical question by saying Joseph was the father. He says he does not take that answer as settled.

### Mark 6:3 and the name "son of Mary"

Tabor argues that calling Jesus "son of Mary" in Mark 6:3 is "a red flag," because a man would normally be identified by his father's name, as Matthew and Luke do ("son of Joseph"). He cites Tal Ilan, whom he describes as an Israeli name expert who teaches in Berlin, for the argument that a son takes his mother's name when she is prominent. Tabor says he would prefer Ilan's explanation, that the phrase means Mary was prominent, but he thinks the context may point to something more. He also notes that Matthew appears to have changed Mark's wording by inserting Joseph, which he reads as a sign that Matthew was uneasy with the phrase.

### Sexual violence as a possible explanation, and why Tabor rejects it

Tabor reports Steve Mason's suggestion that, in wartime, women in destitute situations could be raped and their children would be identified through the mother, since the father could not be named. He mentions Jane Schaberg's book *The Illegitimacy of Jesus* and says he evaluates it. He says, "I personally don't think it's rape." He points instead to John 8, where Jesus's opponents say "we weren't born of fornication," which he takes as evidence that a charge of illegitimacy was already circulating by the time John was written. He also cites John 6:42 ("Jesus Son of Joseph whose father and mother we know") as a possibly ambiguous line, and Paul's phrase "born of a woman" in Galatians, which he notes could mean only that Jesus was human.

### Celsus, the Talmud, and the name "Pantera" as a real name

Tabor argues that the name is early and was not coined as a slur. He says Celsus, a Roman writer of the early third century as reported by Origen, claims Jesus was the son of a Roman soldier, but that Celsus was "quite a bit later." He reads the Talmud's "Jesus Son of Pandira/Pantera" as quotation of a name rather than mockery, and he treats the Tosefta (described as early, contemporary with the Mishnah) as the strongest evidence. In these Tosefta traditions, rabbis in Sepphoris, four miles north of Nazareth, call him Yeshua bar Pantera. Tabor says the traditions are not negative: a rabbi named Eliezer is healed in the name of "Yeshua bar Pantera," and another rabbi quotes Yeshua's saying about wages from prostitution, that they should be used to build toilets. Tabor treats this as evidence that the name was neutral, and he identifies Jesus's sayings as "one of Jesus's funny jokes."

### The "parthenos" pun is a modern invention

Tabor argues that no ancient writer connects "Pantera" to *parthenos*. He says the pun was first proposed in 1850 by a German scholar, and that his colleagues sometimes assume he must know the pun. He points to the spelling difference and says that "nobody in Antiquity" ever made the pun. Ancient writers, Tabor says, took the name as a real one.

### Epiphanius and the family name

Tabor says that Epiphanius, in the fourth and fifth centuries, reports that the name was a family name, either from Joseph's family or Mary's family, or both, possibly related and both of Davidic descent. He reads this as evidence that the early Christian writers themselves treated the name as genuine. Tabor says he is not Epiphanius, because Epiphanius believed in the Virgin birth, and Tabor does not.

### The Roman soldier tombstone from Bingerbrück

Tabor reports that Adolf Deissmann's *Light from the East* (early 20th century) includes a picture of a Roman soldier's tombstone found at a Roman camp on the Rhine, at Bingerbrück. He says the inscription names Tiberius Julius Abdes Pantera, a soldier from Sidon who died aged 62 after serving about 40 years, first in Vienna and then on the Rhine. Tabor says he has visited the site and the museum and has traced the chronology, which he says fits the lives of Mary and Jesus. He explicitly says he is not claiming this is Jesus's father. He links Sidon to Mark 7 (Jesus "entered sidon") and to the Gospel passages about Sidon and Tyre. He also points out that "Roman" did not mean ethnically Roman, since the army was a mixture of peoples.

### Tabor's speculative reconstruction

Tabor offers a theory he treats as speculation. Mary, perhaps around 14, falls in love with a young man named Pantera, perhaps about 18, who is a relative of Joseph or Mary. She becomes pregnant. Matthew's account of Joseph learning of the pregnancy, and of the marriage arranged by the families, is read as a cover. Later, in 4 BC, the Romans sent three legions into Galilee and exiled people, and Tabor speculates that the young man was impressed into the Roman army. He says the only firm points are that the name is real, is associated with the family, and is found in a soldier from the Sidon region. He sums up: "I'm still going to put father unknown," but "there's a name Pantera and it's early and it's not negative."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"The Pantera name is a pun on *parthenos*, 'virgin.'"** Tabor says the pun was proposed in the 19th century, and that no ancient writer makes it. The Greek-speaking writers of antiquity took "Pantera" as a real name.
- **"Pantera was a slur, meaning Mary was raped by a Roman soldier."** Tabor says the Tosefta and Epiphanius treat the name as neutral or as a family name, and he says, "I personally don't think it's rape."
- **"Virgin birth" is a historical claim about Mary's pregnancy.** Tabor says the phrase is really about conception and is a theological position, not a historical one.
- **"Roman soldiers were ethnically Roman."** Tabor says the Roman army recruited from all over, so the soldier's origin cannot be read from the title.
