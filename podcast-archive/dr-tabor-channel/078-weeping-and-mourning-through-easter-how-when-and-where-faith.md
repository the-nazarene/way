## Summary

The talk asks where and how faith in Jesus' resurrection began, and whether the Gospels' familiar Easter-morning story (empty tomb, then appearances of a physically risen Jesus in Jerusalem) is the earliest form of that belief. Tabor argues it is not. He treats John 20:1-10 as the earliest surviving account of the empty tomb, read as a plain burial-search story with no angel, no message, and no resurrection claim. He then argues that the decisive faith experiences happened in Galilee in the weeks after the crucifixion, as visionary experiences, and that Paul's own report of his experience is the only first-person testimony to what resurrection faith meant to Jesus' followers. Tabor supports this with a fragment of the Gospel of Peter, which has the disciples weeping for seven or eight days before Peter goes fishing. He also argues that Mark, a Paul-influenced Gospel, presents the apostles as failing and makes suffering come before glory.

Most of the talk is Tabor speaking, with Lambert asking questions. The Steve Mason point near the end is introduced by Lambert, and Tabor answers it. Tabor frames much of this with "I think" and "I would think," so these are his interpretations rather than settled findings. The talk ends with Tabor's summary that "the gospel is that greatness comes from suffering exaltation comes from service."

## Key Teachings

### Kingdom come with power is not a resuscitated corpse
Tabor starts from Mark 9, where Jesus says some of his hearers will not taste death until they see the kingdom of God come with power, and then takes three disciples up a mountain "seven days later." He calls this a "foretaste." He pairs it with 1 Corinthians 15, where Paul says flesh and blood will not inherit the kingdom of God. Tabor reads this as implying that a Jesus who walked out of the tomb and ate fish or bread would not be "the coming of the kingdom of God," which he describes as a transformation, comparing it to a caterpillar becoming a monarch butterfly.

### John 20:1-10 as the earliest empty-tomb account
Tabor says John 20:1-10 is a separate story from the other Gospels' empty-tomb accounts. Mary Magdalene comes alone before sunrise and finds the stone moved. She reacts as someone whose anointing plans have been frustrated: "where have they taken him." Tabor's reasoning is that Joseph of Arimathea and his associates were in charge of the burial, that John places the burial near the cross because of the approaching Passover, and that Jewish law and the festival delayed a proper burial until after the Sabbaths. On this reading the body had been moved during Saturday night, and Mary's visit comes early Sunday. Tabor notes that the passage has no angel, no messenger, and no statement that Jesus had been raised, and he says the account is "just what happened" from a practical standpoint. He flags the narrative sequence (Peter and the Beloved Apostle, then the appearances) as a point for later discussion.

### Three strands in John and the harmonization problem
Tabor sees John as containing three different things: an empty tomb, appearances in Jerusalem (including Thomas and the "don't touch me" to Mary, which he reads as John trying to bring the appearances in line with a return to heaven), and a separate Galilee scene in John 21 with fishing and a meal on the lake. He says the Gospels can be harmonized "if you work hard enough," but he prefers to treat them as literary accounts written decades later, each shaped by its author's concerns. He describes Jesus' appearances as partly physical, comparable to angelic appearances that eat and then vanish, which he calls "epiphany," but says Paul would call it "metamorphosis."

### The Gospel of Peter fragment and Galilean visionary faith
Tabor relies on the broken ending of the Gospel of Peter, where the disciples say they wept and mourned for seven or eight days and then Peter says he is going fishing. He calls this "a little kernel of what really happened": the disciples grieved through the seven days of Unleavened Bread, then went up to Galilee and had some visionary experience there. He admits the fragment is late and legendary in other respects, but argues that this one detail fits the historical situation. His reasoning is that if the appearances had happened during that first week, the disciples would not have been grieving or returning to fishing. His own view, he says, is that faith was recovered through extraordinary visionary experiences in Galilee, that some believed and others did not, and that the legend grew from there. He also mentions the Sea of Galilee as the likely setting.

### Paul's visions as the only first-person testimony
Tabor says Paul's account is the only first-person testimony we have to what resurrection faith first meant. He draws the parallel with the Galilean visions, arguing that Paul's experience was visionary and transformational rather than a report of a body walking around. He cites 2 Corinthians for the phrase "we no longer know Jesus according to the flesh," which he uses to argue that Paul had moved beyond the physical Jesus.

### Mark as Paul-influenced: suffering before glory
Tabor argues that Mark is very close to Paul theologically and that Mark does not present physical appearances because Mark did not believe in that kind of resurrection. In response to Lambert's question about Steve Mason's view that Mark presents the apostles as failing as a polemic against rival claims, Tabor says he is sympathetic to it but is not sure whether Mark invented the failure or reflects a real one. He draws on his teacher Norman Perrin, who argued that Mark's gospel is about the crucifixion first and glory afterward. Tabor then points to Paul's list of sufferings and to Romans 8 on suffering with Christ before being glorified, and he says Paul "is a kind of a second Christ, so to speak," citing Robert Price's view that Paul saw himself as anointed for a mission.

### James and the Jerusalem community (brief and speculative)
Tabor says he thinks the "Beloved Apostle" in John is James, but adds "we'll get to that maybe" and does not develop the argument in this talk. He says that James died a horrible death at the end, and he suggests that the Jerusalem leaders may have been living a somewhat different life from Paul, citing Acts 15 as evidence that they were meeting together. He does not argue this at length.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Belief:** The empty tomb and physical appearances in Jerusalem on Easter are the earliest form of resurrection faith. **Tabor's correction:** John 20:1-10 is an early burial-search story with no resurrection claim, and the decisive faith experiences were visionary, in Galilee, in the weeks afterward.
- **Belief:** The Gospels present the resurrection as a physical event that the apostles immediately understood. **Tabor's correction:** Mark presents the apostles as failing to understand, and the resurrection's meaning is tied to suffering and transformation.
- **Belief:** Resurrection means Jesus walking out of the tomb and eating. **Tabor's correction:** Following 1 Corinthians 15 and Mark 9, the kingdom and resurrection are a transformation, and a resuscitated body would not fulfil the kingdom.
