## Summary

Tabor asks whether Jesus began as a follower of John the Baptizer and whether the late Second Temple idea of two Messiahs, one priestly and one kingly, was later suppressed. He says the question was prompted by Martin Bashir, who was surprised by the book's chapter on two Messiahs, and that this idea is the most novel part of *The Jesus Dynasty* for many readers. Tabor argues that "Messiah" means "anointed one," that priests and kings were both anointed, and that a two-Messiah expectation appears in Zechariah 6, in Malachi 3, and in the Dead Sea Scrolls (Qumran), where a Messiah of Aaron and a Messiah of Israel are described. He places John the Baptist as the first Messianic figure of the movement, reads the Gospels' baptism scenes as showing Jesus joining John and then splitting the work with him, and cites Q-type sayings in which Jesus praises John.

Tabor then argues that Jesus saw himself as the Davidic Messiah, citing Paul's statement in Romans 1 that Jesus was descended from David "according to the flesh," and the Isaiah 11 themes in Jesus' teaching. He argues that Jesus anticipated his own death, drawing on Qumran parallels to the Teacher of Righteousness and on Isaiah 53. He ends by saying James took over the movement after Jesus, and that this is why he calls the movement a "dynasty." The talk is mostly Tabor speaking; Lambert mainly asks questions and occasionally comments.

## Key Teachings

### Messiah as anointed office, and the two-Messiah expectation

Tabor says "Christ" is not a surname but means "anointed one" or "chosen one." Because priests from Aaron onward and kings from Saul onward were anointed, he argues the category covered both offices. He cites Zechariah 6 ("the son of David is going to sit on his throne and there will be a priest by his side") and says this was "a pretty common view in second temple Judaism." He points to the Dead Sea Scrolls, which he says "clearly have two Messiahs": a Messiah of Aaron (the priest, the spiritual leader and teacher of Torah) and a Messiah of Israel (the king, "more like the president the executive"). He says the scrolls picture them seated at the heavenly banquet, with a "council of peace" between them. He adds that the priest's original role was teaching Torah, not slaughtering animals, which he calls a surprise to many readers.

### John the Baptist as the first Messiah of the movement

Tabor describes John as the starting point, saying the movement is "really... the John the baptizer movement" before it was a Jesus movement. He notes Luke's account that Mary visited Elizabeth and that the two boys were relatives, and he assumes Jesus and John met at festivals. He says Jesus "joins the movement" and "I think he looks at to John as his teacher." He reads the baptism accounts as showing a progression: in Mark there is "no objection at all," in Matthew John objects, Luke omits the exchange, and John's Gospel has John's "Behold the Lamb of God" statement. He says that after this the two "split the work": John preached in the north and Jesus in the south, with Jesus also baptizing disciples. He cites a sayings tradition, which he calls "Q," in which Jesus says "among those born of women there's none greater than John." He treats the clause about "the least in the kingdom" as a later interpolation.

### Jesus' teaching on the kingdom and on nonviolence

Tabor says Jesus proclaimed the kingdom as at hand and believed in inaugurating it by defeating Satan, citing "by the Finger of God cast out demons." He also cites "the kingdom is right within you" and "love your enemies, turn the other cheek." He argues against describing Jesus as militant in the sense of arming for guerrilla war, which he attributes to readings by Brandon and, in part, Robert Eisenman. He says "pacifist" is "probably the wrong word," since Jesus expected God to intervene "and cause lots of fireworks" while leaving the violence to God. He reasons that Rome crucified or beheaded would-be Messiahs who gathered arms, and that Jesus knew this.

### Jesus as the Davidic Messiah

Tabor says he "absolutely" thinks Jesus believed he was the Davidic Messiah. His earliest evidence is Paul's letters, which predate the Gospels. He cites Paul in Romans 1 saying Jesus was descended from David "according to the flesh," and argues that Paul would not have invented a claim that others could easily have contradicted. He points to Isaiah 11 as the source of Jesus' agenda: meekness, "blessed are the poor in spirit," and the reunion of the twelve tribes. He says many colleagues today hold that Jesus did not think of himself as Messiah, which he disputes.

### Jesus anticipating his death

Tabor argues that Jesus anticipated his suffering and death. He cites recent books by Michael Wise and Israel Knohl (which he says were published months apart and independently) arguing that Isaiah 53 and the Suffering Servant passages were applied to the Qumran Teacher of Righteousness. He reads the Habakkuk pesher and the middle of the Thanksgiving Hymns, where he hears autobiographical suffering, and he notes parallels to a betrayer who "lifted up his heel" against the Teacher. He thinks the death of John shattered Jesus, who then quoted "strike the shepherd and the sheep will be scattered." He says Jesus did not, in Tabor's view, speak of being raised, which he regards as later. He compares this to Bonhoeffer's view that the call to follow means taking up a cross, not claiming to be the suffering servant alone. Tabor says Paul in Romans 8 expects others to share the suffering.

### James and the succession

Tabor says the church's teaching that Joseph and Mary had no other children obscures the family, including James, the brother who "really takes over the movement." He says James is "very important and nobody talks about him," and that this is why he calls the movement a "dynasty." He says he would otherwise stand by his book with few changes.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That there was only one Messiah expected in Judaism.** Tabor argues late Second Temple Judaism expected two, a priestly and a kingly figure, citing Zechariah 6 and the Dead Sea Scrolls.
- **That "Christ" is Jesus' last name.** Tabor says it means "anointed one," a title that applied to priests and kings.
- **That the priest's role was chiefly cutting animals' throats.** Tabor says the original role was teaching Torah.
- **That Jesus was militant, planning armed revolt.** Tabor disputes this reading (associated with Brandon and, in part, Eisenman), citing the "turn the other cheek" and "love your enemies" sayings.
- **That Jesus was a pacifist.** Tabor says "pacifist" is "probably the wrong word," since he expected God to bring the kingdom.
- **That Jesus did not think of himself as Messiah.** Tabor says he thinks Jesus did, citing Romans 1 and Isaiah 11.
- **That Joseph and Mary had no other children.** Tabor says this is the church's official teaching, which hides Jesus' brothers, including James.
