## Summary

Tabor asks what Paul meant by the "thorn in the flesh" (2 Corinthians 12:7), which he says was sent to keep him from being exalted after his heavenly ascent, and which he calls a "messenger of Satan." Tabor treats the ascent passage (2 Corinthians 12:2-4) as Paul speaking about himself in the third person, a rhetorical device he says is well attested in Greek literature. He argues that the revelation Paul received was new and concerned ordinary human beings being destined to sit at God's right hand, as the exalted Christ does. The "thorn" was a counterweight to that exaltation.

Tabor says the usual reading, a physical disability, is only a guess, and he lists the candidates scholars have offered. He says he has "leaned towards" blindness or poor eyesight, pointing to Paul's remark about his "bodily presence" and to the large letters in Galatians 6:11. He then sets out a speculative alternative: sexual temptation. His reasons are Paul's advice on divorce (quoting "the Lord said" in 1 Corinthians 7), the "flesh" passages in Romans 7, the typical struggles of celibate ascetics, and Augustine's *Confessions*. He presents this as "just an idea" and says the fuller argument is in his book *Paul's Ascent to Paradise*. The talk ends with a plug for his self-published books. The segment is a short interview with Derek Lambert, and Tabor carries most of it.

## Key Teachings

### The ascent and the "unutterable" things
Tabor reads 2 Corinthians 12 as Paul describing his own experience of being "caught up into the third heaven" and entering paradise. He links the "third heaven" to Hellenistic cosmology, in which the highest heaven is where God and paradise are found. He says the Greek word for "unutterable" can mean either "too amazing to tell" or "not permitted to tell," and he connects the secrecy to initiation motifs, citing Lucian's *Metamorphoses* on Isis initiation as a parallel.

### The content of the revelation
Tabor speculates that Paul was shown Christ seated beside God and was told he too would one day sit at God's right hand. He suggests this underlies Paul's claim in his letters to a "mystery," and that it concerns "lowly" human flesh and blood being destined for exaltation, "not as Angels Above angels." He cites 1 Corinthians 6:3 ("do you not know we will judge Angels") in support. He also says the idea may draw on "Enoch material," without naming a text in the transcript.

### The thorn as a physical condition
Tabor says most readers take the thorn as a physical disability. He mentions that he once listed about twenty proposals in a footnote, including nearsightedness and epilepsy. He notes the superstitious reading that Paul was demon-possessed. He leans toward eyesight, reasoning that Paul's dictation may have been a scribal process, and he links this to Galatians 6:11 and to the "bodily presence" remark (see Scripture Citations on the attribution).

### The thorn as sexual temptation (his speculation)
Tabor's alternative is that the thorn was sexual temptation. He reads 1 Corinthians 7:10-15 (no divorce between Christian spouses, but the unbelieving partner may depart) as a sign that Paul's marital situation may have been disrupted, perhaps by a wife who left after his visions. He reasons that a rabbi of Paul's day would normally be married, and he rejects the claim that Paul was gay as a way to dismiss the question. He draws on later monastic diaries and medieval female saints' records to argue that temptation for celibates often takes the form of sexual desire. He suggests Paul's "dreams" might have involved a woman he was teaching. Tabor notes that Paul's "messenger of Satan" language fits a goad that drives him.

### Flesh and covetousness in Romans 7
Tabor reads Romans 7:15-19 ("the good that I would I do not") as Paul describing an ongoing inner conflict in his Christian life, in which "the flesh" is responsible for wrongdoing. He says "flesh" in Paul often means sexual desire, though it can also mean anger or other weaknesses. He reads the coveting Paul names (Romans 7:7 in the King James wording) as desire for things Paul had renounced, and he says he cannot picture Paul coveting a house or property. He concludes that sexual desire is the likeliest candidate.

### Augustine as a parallel
Tabor compares Paul to Augustine, who, in the *Confessions*, describes a lifelong struggle with desire and a child by a partner he could not marry. Tabor uses this to suggest that a sincere, committed celibate could still be overwhelmed by sexual temptation. He presents the parallel as illustrative only.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The thorn was definitely a physical disability.** Tabor says "most people take it as some kind of physical disability," but argues the phrase is "not used anywhere" in a way that settles the question, so the physical reading is a guess.
- **Paul's condition was demonic possession or epilepsy-style fits.** Tabor mentions the superstitious reading, in which Paul suddenly "goes into some crazy fit," and presents it as an ancient assumption rather than evidence.
- **Paul was gay, so celibacy posed no sexual problem.** Tabor says that claim "still wouldn't get you over sexual Temptation," and that he doubts Paul was gay.
- **A first-century rabbi would have been single.** Tabor says rabbis of the period were typically married, which is why he thinks Paul probably had a wife.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor's reading of 1 Corinthians 7:10-15 shows Paul quoting "the Lord" on divorce and then adding his own qualification for mixed marriages. Tabor's discussion of Paul's marriage history is speculative and rests on that passage.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor's reconstruction has Christ seated beside God and Paul's revelation pointing to human beings sharing that seat. This bears on the exaltation theme, though Tabor does not develop a christological argument in this clip.
- **Torah:** Tabor's appeal to Genesis 2:24 and to the rabbinic marriage norm touches on Jewish practice, but he does not discuss Torah observance as such.
