## Summary

The talk asks where Jesus of Nazareth fits among the ancient Jewish groups, and what kind of Jew he was. Tabor starts from Josephus's description of the schools of Judaism (Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes, and the Zealots, whom Tabor says Josephus presents as close to the Pharisees but more militant). Tabor argues that Jesus cannot be fitted neatly into any one of these, and that the New Testament's picture of Pharisees as Jesus's main opponents reflects later writing rather than a hatred of the Pharisees as such.

His central move is a game in which he lists seven characteristics of a group: preparing the way in the wilderness, separatism, prophetic and Messianic expectation, a New Covenant, baptismal purification, opposition to the Jerusalem Temple, and communal living. He says the listeners would identify this as the Essenes, and then argues that all seven also describe the Jesus movement. He says the Pharisees and Sadducees do not fit, and he treats the Dead Sea Scrolls and Josephus as a partly reconstructed composite. Tabor puts Jesus closer to Hillel's teaching and to the prophetic books (Micah, Amos, Hosea, Isaiah, Jeremiah), and describes him as an eschatological figure who expected the end to be near.

The talk ends with a discussion of John the Baptist, whom Tabor presents as less rigid than popular films suggest, drawing on the Q sayings and on a family tie through Mary and Elizabeth. It closes with Tabor's view that Jesus was of Davidic descent, which he attributes to Paul, while noting that many scholarly colleagues doubt it. The conversation breaks off there in the captions. ## Key Teachings

### Josephus's schools and the Zealots
Tabor says Josephus describes three or sometimes four schools of Judaism, which he suggests translating as "schools" or "sects." The schools are the Pharisees, Sadducees and Essenes, plus the Zealots. Tabor says Josephus presents the Zealots as "not really a separate school," close to the Pharisees in outlook but more militant, and willing to fight Roman occupation through armed resistance. Tabor says Josephus was born around 37 CE, a date he gives in the talk without citing a source.

### Jesus and the Pharisees: disputes over Torah, not enmity
Tabor says the Gospels (he cites Mark chapter 2 for Sabbath criticism) present the Pharisees as Jesus's main opponents, but he argues that such disputes are ordinary Torah arguments about how to keep the Sabbath, not "a dispute where you just want to despise the person." He says the hostility comes later, when Jesus goes to Jerusalem and faces the priestly and Herodian forces. He compares the Pharisees to Baptist churches in a town, which look very different up close. He says some Pharisees, including Hillel, probably would have liked Jesus. In the talk, Tabor compares Jesus's teaching to Hillel's summary of the Torah ("love God with all your heart, love your neighbor as yourself, the rest is commentary") and to Hillel's story about the Gentile who asked to be taught the Torah while standing on one foot, which Tabor says was answered by Hillel but rejected by Shammai. Tabor says he is not sure Jesus is a "card-carrying member" of any of the schools.

### The seven-characteristic test: Essenes, and then the Jesus movement
Tabor sets out seven marks he says point to the Essenes: (1) preparing the way in the wilderness, citing Isaiah 40:3; (2) separation from the ungodly to form a holy people within Israel; (3) prophetic and Messianic expectation, including Elijah and a Davidic king, which he connects to the questions put to John the Baptist and to Jesus in the Gospels; (4) a New Covenant, based on Jeremiah 31:31, which he says is "a renewed covenant" rather than supersessionism; (5) a purifying immersion (Mikvah), which the group understands as cleansing by repentance, not by water itself; (6) hostility toward the Jerusalem Temple, which the group calls a "den of robbers," an image Tabor attributes to Jeremiah; (7) communal living with the surrender of property. Tabor says the Pharisees fit none of these, and that Sadducees fit only in the sense that they believed in resurrection and in an end of time. He then says all seven apply to the Jesus movement.

### How strict the Essenes were, and how Jesus differs
Tabor argues that the core of the Essene group was not these outward marks but a very strict reading of the Torah. He cites the Community Rule's Sabbath rule, which forbids helping an animal out of a well until after the Sabbath, and he says Jesus raises the same case with a Pharisaic audience and assumes that nobody would leave the animal in the well. Tabor also cites the Temple Scroll for its many purity rules, and says the Qumran group would not touch a leper or accept a Samaritan. By contrast, Jesus touches a leper, tells him to follow the law of Moses, and does not send him to the Temple. Tabor also says Jesus eats with tax collectors and sinners and does not accept the ablution of hands as binding. He ties this to the oral tradition ("minhog"), which he says both the Pharisees and the Qumran group had, and to Jesus's saying that what defiles is what comes out of the heart. He cites Luke chapter 8 for the women who supported Jesus's movement, including Mary Magdalene, Joanna and Susanna, one of whom he says was the wife of Herod's Chief of Staff.

### Jesus, the Sadducees, and afterlife
Tabor discusses two books of the Apocrypha, Ben Sira and the Wisdom of Solomon, as examples of two ancient views. He says Ben Sira reflects an older sapiential view, in which God controls everything, justice will eventually be seen, and there is no afterlife. He says the Wisdom of Solomon sounds Platonic, with immortal souls and punishment for the wicked. Tabor says the Sadducees seem "more epicurean-like" and not worried about death, and that Jesus does not fit that picture. He says Jesus is "very much I think an eschatological apocalyptic guy," and says he agrees with Schweitzer on this, while noting that Crossan, Allison, Fredriksen and others differ on details.

### Jesus as prophetic and eschatological
Tabor says Jesus is most closely identified with the prophetic vision of Micah, Amos, Hosea, Second Isaiah and Jeremiah. He gives examples of deep teachings rather than fulfillment-of-prophecy claims: quoting Jeremiah to say God never wanted sacrifice, calling the Temple a den of robbers, and stressing that God looks at the inner person rather than the outer, along with mercy, justice and judgment. He also says Jesus thought the end was near, and that he suggested some people might not marry or have children in this time. Tabor says this expectation produced a kind of revival of the Essene-style expectation in the next generation, with Jesus and John the Baptist at its head.

### John the Baptist and the sources
Tabor argues that John the Baptist is usually portrayed too harshly, through films that show him with wild hair. He cites the Q source, which he says opens with John's sayings ("let him who has two coats share with him who has none") and his talk of blessings, which Tabor says resemble Jesus's teaching. He says there is no good evidence for a rigid conflict between the two men. He draws on Luke's tradition that Mary and Elizabeth were related and says Jesus and John were probably close relatives who may have grown up together. He puts John's traditional home at Ein Karem, near Jerusalem. He also says that teachings in the Gospels must be sifted, because many reflect the post-70 period, and he names Michael Wise as a dating authority for the Teacher of Righteousness movement, which he places around 75 BCE.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Pharisees were Jesus's implacable enemies.** Tabor says the Gospels' conflicts over the Sabbath were ordinary disputes about how to interpret the Torah, and that some Pharisees, such as Hillel, would probably have agreed with Jesus.
- **The Essenes and Jesus were close in practice.** Tabor argues that, although the Essenes shared many outward marks with the Jesus movement, the Essenes were far stricter on the Sabbath, purity and contact with outsiders, and Jesus differs sharply from them.
- **John the Baptist was a fierce, rigid ascetic who clashed with Jesus.** Tabor says the Q sayings present John as teaching sharing and blessing, and that there is no good evidence for a rigid conflict between the two.
- **Jesus was a card-carrying member of one of the Jewish schools.** Tabor says Jesus cannot be pigeonholed into any single school and that his teachings must be placed in their time and place.
