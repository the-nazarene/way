## Summary

The talk asks whether Jesus and his earliest followers were vegetarians who did not eat animals. Tabor says yes, and he calls the case a "blue thread" running through Jewish scripture and into the first century. He begins with the Genesis 1 diet of plants and seeds, then moves to Genesis 9, where meat-eating is granted as an accommodation. From there he traces an ideal of the wilderness and Eden through Israel's complaints for meat, Daniel's refusal of the king's food, Jeremiah's critique of sacrifice, and Solomon's prayer. He reads Isaiah 56 and Isaiah 11 as pointing to a future with no slaughter on God's holy mountain. He then argues that John the Baptist ate no bread or meat, that James likely shared that diet, and that the Ebionites, whom he describes as Jewish followers of Jesus, were vegetarian and opposed sacrifice. He also argues that Jesus's own diet was more moderate: the phrase "eating and drinking" does not necessarily mean meat, and Jesus drank wine and ate bread.

Tabor takes up the apparent counter-evidence and offers alternative readings. He argues that the Passover and fish-eating passages are late or apologetic, and he notes that Chilton reads the Last Supper as a replacement for the Passover lamb. He also argues that Paul's Romans 14 shows a community that was vegetarian and that Paul himself was not. He closes by saying that a plant-based diet is healthier and that if John and James were vegetarian, Jesus probably was too. Most of the claims rest on Tabor's own readings of Hebrew wording and on sources he treats as late or uncertain, such as the Slavonic Josephus and the Gospel of the Hebrews. ## Key Teachings

### The Genesis ideal and the accommodation of meat-eating
Tabor begins with Genesis 1:29, where humans are given "every fruit of a tree every seed and so forth." He reads this as the ideal diet that Jesus's culture knew, whether taken literally or mythologically. He notes that Jesus quotes Genesis on marriage ("from the beginning it has not been so"). He then points to Genesis 9, where God gives Noah permission to eat meat "for the first time." He says this is "given as an accommodation" and that scholars do not agree on why the change was made, but he does not claim a settled answer.

### Wilderness, Daniel and the "Genesis diet"
Tabor reads the wilderness narrative as a time when Israel was returned to a plant diet and then complained for meat. He says the text presents the craving as a disappointment, and he recounts Moses's report that God would give meat "not for a day not for a week but a month until it comes out their noses." He then tells an unrelated anecdote about David Koresh and the Branch Davidians, which he uses as an analogy. In Daniel, he says the exiles in Babylon refused the king's food and asked for "seeds and water," and he claims they thrived. He presents this as Jews in exile trying to live by the Genesis diet.

### Jeremiah, Solomon and Isaiah: critiques of sacrifice
Tabor reads Jeremiah 7 as God saying that in the day he led Israel out of Egypt, "I did not command you to offer sacrifices I only said obey my voice and follow me." He says the phrase "den of thieves" should be read with the Hebrew verb *peraz*, meaning a lair or den of animals that rip and tear. He takes this as a reference to sacrifice rather than to money-changing. He contrasts Solomon's prayer at the temple dedication, which he says contains no mention of sacrifice and speaks of a foreigner coming to pray, with Isaiah 56, which he says has been expanded. He argues that the sacrifice lines were added between verses that otherwise read smoothly, and he compares this to interpolation in the Josephus testimonium. Isaiah 11 and 65 describe a future in which no one will "hurt or destroy" on God's holy mountain. Tabor reads "destroy" in Hebrew as slaughter. He also cites Hosea 6:6, "I desire mercy not sacrifice," and argues that the prophets value mercy over sacrifice. He says the Shapira scroll, which he discusses in a Bar magazine article, may show a Torah without the sacrifice laws, though he presents this as a possibility only.

### John the Baptist, James and the Ebionites
Tabor says that John the Baptist "doesn't eat meat or drink wine or even eat bread," and that the Slavonic Josephus describes a similar diet. He addresses the "locusts" in the Gospels and suggests that the Greek word for locust differs by a single letter from a word for honey cake, with a version of the Gospel of the Hebrews as his evidence. He describes the Ebionites as Jewish followers of Jesus who rejected Paul, did not accept the virgin birth, saw Jesus as human, and were vegetarian. He says they were opposed to sacrifice and believed God destroyed the temple because God never wanted it. He presents them as a movement "coming from James and his followers" and says James was succeeded by Simon, who he thinks was a cousin or brother.

### Jesus's diet: "eating and drinking" and wine
Tabor argues that the King James "meat" is an old English term for food. He reads the sayings in which the Son of Man "came eating and drinking" as meaning ordinary food. He notes that the gospels say Jesus ate bread and drank wine, while John neither ate bread nor drank wine. He suggests that Jesus was less strict than John and that James may have been a modified version of John's practice, since James did not drink wine.

### Paul, Romans 14 and the Eucharist
Tabor reads Romans 14 as Paul describing a Roman church divided over diet. Some members ate only vegetables and avoided wine, following "the Genesis diet," and others ate meat and drank wine. He says Paul did not take sides strongly and wanted to avoid division. He suggests that the vegetarian group at Rome was influenced by John or James. On the Last Supper, he cites Bruce Chilton's view that the bread and wine replaced the Passover lamb, though he says he is not sure Chilton is right. He notes that Jesus in Mark says he "will no longer eat Passover," and he asks whether this was an earlier view or a later one.

### Fish and the resurrection appearances
Tabor argues that the fish-eating scenes in Luke and John are apologetic, meant to show that Jesus was not a ghost. He describes a development from no appearances in Mark to one appearance in Matthew to many in Luke and John. He notes that Paul describes the risen body as "spiritual" and that Paul did not hold the view that a physical body walked and ate. He says he cannot see reason to think Paul's experience differed from that of James and Peter.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus ate fish and the Passover lamb.** Tabor argues that the fish scenes in Luke and John are apologetic additions and that the Passover reference in the Last Supper may be a replacement for the lamb.
- **"Meat" in the King James "son of man came eating and drinking."** Tabor says the word "meat" in older English meant food generally, so the passage does not say Jesus ate meat.
- **The temple traders were money-changers exchanging pagan money.** Tabor says the money changers were exchanging coins for the pure Tyrian shekel that the temple required. He says the traders' main business was selling animals, and he presents this as the reason Jesus objected.
- **Paul held a physical resurrection body.** Tabor argues that Paul described a spiritual body, and that the physical details in Luke and John reflect later apologetics.
- **Jeremiah's "den of thieves" concerns money.** Tabor reads the Hebrew as a lair of animals that rip and tear, and he links it to sacrifice.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught.** Tabor contrasts Romans 14, where Paul is "on the meat-eating side," with the vegetarian practice he attributes to Jesus and John. He also contrasts Paul's account of the spiritual body with the physical body in Luke and John.
- **James and the Jerusalem community.** Tabor argues that James was likely vegetarian and that a movement of James's followers, later called the Ebionites, rejected sacrifice and Paul, and that Simon succeeded James.
- **Nazarenes and Ebionites.** The talk's central claim is that the Ebionites were vegetarian, opposed sacrifice, and held that Jesus was human and born of Joseph. These claims rest mainly on quotations from the Gospel of the Hebrews and on the Slavonic Josephus.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet and nonviolence.** Tabor reads the Hebrew prophets and the Genesis narrative as an ideal of no slaughter and no sacrifice, which he links to Jesus's temple protest and to nonviolence.
- **Resurrection and afterlife.** Tabor discusses the post-resurrection fish scenes and Paul's spiritual body, but he says the resurrection is "another subject" that he has covered elsewhere.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed.** Tabor argues that the fish scenes and some Eucharist details reflect later apologetic development, and that interpolation shaped passages such as Isaiah 56 and the Josephus testimonium.
