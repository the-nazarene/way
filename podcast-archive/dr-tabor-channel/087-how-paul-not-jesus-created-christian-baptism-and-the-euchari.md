## Summary

Tabor asks whether the baptism and the Eucharist (the "Lord's Supper") practiced by later Christians go back to Jesus, John the Baptist and the Jerusalem community around James, or whether Paul introduced them. He begins with the synoptic Gospels and Josephus, treating John's baptism as a Jewish movement rite of repentance and forgiveness, distinct from the routine Mikvah immersion for ritual purity. He then traces how the Gospels progressively reshape Jesus's own baptism (Mark, Matthew, Luke, John). He argues that Paul's baptism, which he links to "mystical union" with the body of Christ, is a later development. Acts shows disciples who knew only John's baptism being rebaptized "in the name of Jesus," but Tabor reads this as a movement continuous with John rather than the Pauline sacrament.

On the Eucharist, Tabor states that a blessing of bread and wine at shared meals was ordinary Jewish practice and that the Qumran texts describe a messianic meal. He contends that Paul's account in 1 Corinthians 11, which he says Paul claims to have received "from the Lord," introduced a Eucharist of body and blood. He argues that James and the Jerusalem community would not have drunk ritual blood, which the Torah forbids. He says he cannot identify Paul's source. The interview is mostly Tabor speaking, with Lambert asking questions. Tabor frames his conclusions as a historian's thesis (his phrase: "I think Paul originated it"), and he treats the Didache and Q as evidence that is open to dispute.

## Key Teachings

### John's baptism as a Jewish reform rite, not Mikvah purity
Tabor distinguishes John's baptism ("the baptism of repentance for the remission of sins") from the routine immersion for ritual purity. Purity immersion, in his account, follows contact with a corpse, sexual intercourse, menstruation and similar states, and it restores access to the Temple or ordinary life. The immersion takes place in flowing water, and he cites the Qumran mikveh as an example. John's baptism, by contrast, was a one-time moral and covenantal act. Tabor adds that Josephus describes John as teaching moral reform, and that the Dead Sea Scrolls' Community Rule describes initiation by water as "the renewal of the heart." He compares the baptism to a wedding ring: a sign of an inner commitment, not the cause of it.

### Jesus's baptism as progressively reshaped by the Gospels
Tabor presents the baptism of Jesus as the clearest example of Gospel development. In Mark, Jesus simply joins the movement, and the Holy Spirit speaks to him directly. Matthew adds John's objection that Jesus needs no baptism. In Luke, "all the people" are baptized and the Spirit descends during Jesus's prayer, which pushes the water rite into the background. In John, the Baptist names Jesus as the Lamb of God and the baptism is not narrated. He reads this as the progressive removal of John from the story of Jesus's initiation.

### Paul's "mystical union" baptism
Tabor's thesis is that Paul's baptism is a different thing. He cites Romans 6, and Ephesians and Colossians, which he treats as deutero-Pauline, for the idea that believers are "baptized with Christ, buried with Christ, raised with Christ," and that baptism initiates them into a "cosmic" body of Christ. He cites 1 Corinthians 12:13 in the form "we're all baptized into one body" (the reference he gives is loose). He says he does not think Paul meant it magically, but he does treat it as a new initiatory union that the earlier movement did not have.

### Acts and the rebaptism question
Tabor notes that Acts describes disciples who knew only John's baptism and then receive baptism "in the name of Jesus," including Aquila and Priscilla meeting Paul's followers. He reads this as the sign of a later, Pauline form of baptism, and he doubts that the Twelve, including James, were rebaptized in the same way. He argues that Acts 2:38 ("repent and be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ for remission of sins") is more likely a later formulation, since in his view the early Jerusalem movement was "joining a movement that includes John in Jesus." He says he is "not convinced" that the Jerusalem disciples had a Pauline understanding in the 30s to 50s.

### John and Jesus as one movement: the Q evidence
Tabor cites the Q sayings material in Matthew and Luke as earlier than the Gospel frameworks. He points to Jesus's statement that "among those born of women there's none greater than John," and to the call to share coats (Luke only). He argues from these that John was Jesus's teacher, and that the two shared a message. He also says Q portrays John as the Malachi figure who prepares the way and, in his reading, as a priestly messianic figure. He says the idea that John was "more than a prophet" must be early, because it comes before Paul.

### The Eucharist as a Jewish messianic meal
Tabor argues that Jewish groups routinely blessed bread and wine at meals, especially on the Sabbath, so that breaking bread "in a spiritual way" was ordinary practice. He cites the Qumran texts, which describe the Messiah seated at the head of the table with the priest, blessing bread and wine for the messianic community, and he calls this "the original Eucharist." He cites Didache 14 (the transcript garbles it as "dedicate") as a non-Pauline text in which the loaf stands for the unity of God's people and the cup for "the vine of... David," with no reference to blood. He argues that James and the Jerusalem community would not have drunk a ritual meal that represented blood, because the Torah forbids consuming blood and Acts 15 (he cites it as a list of prohibitions that includes blood) says so.

### Paul's claim to revelation and the Eucharist he "invented"
Tabor says that Paul's account in 1 Corinthians 11 is "very new" and that Paul says he "received" it "from the Lord," not from the community. He contrasts this with 1 Corinthians 15:3, where Paul says he received the gospel and passed it on (he reads the Greek verb as paradidomai, "pass on"), a passage he says scholars generally read as received tradition. He concludes: "I argue that Paul invented the Eucharist the way we knew it." He says he cannot identify Paul's source and that he will not judge the experience behind the claim. He reads Paul's line "examine yourself" as turning the meal into a "moral inventory." He also notes that Luke has two cups, and that the one linked to the body and blood looks like Mark's and Paul's influence on a source that had a different cup.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Ritual impurity meant dirt.** Tabor says the word "unclean" in English translations misleads readers. The Torah's categories concern ritual status, not dirt, so scrubbing with soap does not cure them.
- **Jesus's baptism was a cosmic or Spirit-filled initiation like the later Pauline sacrament.** Tabor argues that the Gospels progressively reshape a plain movement baptism into a more significant event.
- **The Eucharist was Jesus's own institution.** Tabor argues that the Eucharist of body and blood is Paul's innovation, and that the earlier Jewish practice was a blessing of bread and wine.
- **The Jerusalem community practiced a symbolic drinking of blood.** Tabor argues that James and the Jerusalem believers, bound by Torah prohibitions on blood, would not have done this.
- **Paul's "received from the Lord" simply means tradition.** Tabor reads 1 Corinthians 11 as a claim to direct revelation, not a passing-on of an earlier tradition, though he acknowledges this reading is contested.
