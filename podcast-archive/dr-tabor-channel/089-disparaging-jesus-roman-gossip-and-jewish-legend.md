## Summary

Tabor asks who Jesus' father was, and why the early sources give such different answers. He frames the question around the Creeds, which say nothing about Jesus' teaching but affirm a virgin birth, and contrasts them with groups such as the Ebionites and Nazarenes, who (as he understands them) held that Jesus had a human father and emphasized Torah as "teaching." He argues that the Gospels move from silence to elaboration: Mark, which he treats as the earliest Gospel and as following a sayings source (Q), has no birth narrative and no Joseph, while Matthew and Luke add birth stories and Matthew edits Mark's "Is this not the carpenter's son?" into "son of Mary" and then removes "Mary." Tabor weighs two explanations for the birth stories: a Hellenistic "divine man" legend, or a response to a rumor of irregular conception. He leans toward the second, reading the Matthew 1 phrase about Mary being "found with child" as a possible "historical kernel."

The second half turns to the Pantera tradition. Tabor cites Tosefta passages that name "Yeshua ben Pantera," Celsus's second-century version of a rumor that Mary conceived by a soldier named Pantera, and Origen's reply, which he says accepts Pantera as a family name. He presents Deissmann's 1906 argument against a "son of a virgin" pun, a Jerusalem ossuary inscription naming "Joseph Pantera," and the tombstone of the Sidon-born archer Tiberius Julius Abdes Pantera, which he visited in Germany. He says he never claims this man was Jesus' father, and he defends Mary's dignity against assumptions about a Roman soldier. The talk ends with his "imagine the best" point: if the story is true, it could be a love story, not a scandal. The talk is a single lecturer's presentation, with no guest or host.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The Creeds and the missing teaching of Jesus
Tabor argues that the Apostles' and Nicene Creeds define Jesus by his entrance and exit (pre-existence, virgin birth, death, resurrection, ascension) and "don't say a single thing about what he taught or his message." He reads the letter of James as a branch that stresses Jesus' ethical teaching, noting its Sermon-on-the-Mount-like exhortations ("swear not") and comparing Josephus's account that the Essenes swear no oaths. He observes that James mentions Jesus only at the opening and in 2:1, and says that removing those references would not be possible with Paul's letters. He suggests "Lord" might be better rendered as "Rabbi" or "master," and that "Torah" is better rendered "teaching" than "law" to avoid Pauline associations.

### 2. Ebionites, Nazarenes and Arians
Tabor says he uses "Ebionites" and "Nazarenes" largely interchangeably, though "later they get distinguished," and admits that "we don't have good sources for this." On "one crucial point," he says, they held that Jesus "was an ordinary man with a human father," and that Joseph was probably the father "from what we know." He contrasts this with Arianism, which he describes as denying Jesus' pre-existence (while still allowing a virgin birth), and with the Ebionite view that Jesus received a calling at baptism without pre-existence. He notes that Jehovah's Witnesses are the main modern exponents of Arian views.

### 3. Mark's silence and the editing of Matthew and Luke
Tabor says he is "utterly convinced" of the standard theory that Mark was first and that Matthew and Luke used Mark and a sayings source (Q). The earliest Gospel, Mark, "does not have the birth." He notes that Mark 1:1 begins with John the Baptist, not a birth, and that Mark 6:3 reads "is this not the carpenter the son of Mary?" He calls "son of Mary" "shocking" and says Matthew 13:55 changes it to "is not his mother called Mary." He argues that Joseph appears in Mark only in such a silence, and that Matthew's "Joseph" appears only at the birth. He argues Luke, which he calls a "sourceman," gives a different story focused on Mary, with Joseph mentioned but not described as troubled or dreaming. He builds on the single age reference in Luke 3:23 (about thirty) and a correlation with John's priestly age.

### 4. The competing explanations for the birth stories
Tabor sets out two explanations. The first is the Hellenistic "divine man" motif, with Greek and Roman parallels such as Heracles, whose father was Zeus, shown on a Greek vase. He says critical scholars "commonly would say" that the birth stories embellish Jesus' divinity. The second, which he leans toward, is that the Matthew 1 phrase "she was found to be with child" preserves "a historical kernel" of an irregular conception, and that the Holy Spirit explanation was given to answer a scandal. He mentions Jane Schaberg's work on illegitimacy and John Meier's objections, and says he is "more conservative with the text" than many colleagues. He asks scholars who reject a kernel to explain why a birth story would have arisen at all.

### 5. The Pantera traditions in Tosefta and Celsus
Tabor says the Tosefta (which he calls "the tocepta," additions to the Mishnah) has two passages naming "Yeshua ben Pantera," and he argues they are not polemical: one about Rabbi Eleazar ben Dama, bitten by a snake and cured in the name of Yeshua ben Pantera, the other about Jacob of Kefar Sama. He calls them "identification," and argues that the Pantera name was known in Sepphoris, four miles from Nazareth, as a Jewish, intra-Jewish reference. He argues Celsus's version, transmitted through Origen's reply, is a rumor "garbled" from Jewish sources: that Mary's husband drove her out after she was pregnant "by a Roman soldier named Pantera." Origen and Epiphanius, he says, accepted Pantera as a family name, claiming Joseph's father was named Pantera.

### 6. Deissmann, the Jerusalem ossuary and the Germany tombstone
Tabor says Adolf Deissmann (rendered "Dyson Adolf diceman" in the captions) argued in 1906 against a pun on parthenos, citing tomb inscriptions in which "Pantera" appears, favored by Roman soldiers. Tabor notes a Jerusalem ossuary, found in the 1890s by a French archaeologist, reading "Joseph Pantera of Drusus" (broken off), which he calls more significant than Deissmann's evidence. He describes the tombstone of "Tiberius Julius Abdes Pantera" of Sidon, age 62, of 40 years' service in the first cohort of archers, buried near Bingen, Germany, and reports a 1859 painting of the discovery. He stresses that he never claims this soldier was Jesus' father, and that the connection is a hypothesis, not a conclusion.

### 7. Mary's dignity and "imagining the best"
In the closing minutes Tabor says that the assumption that a Roman soldier's fatherhood would be "a horrible thing" is unwarranted, since "you know nothing about it." He says the soldier might not have been a soldier when the relationship happened, and might have been young, and that the story could be "a love story of the ages." He suggests Mary, if the story is true, would have had a right to privacy, and he imagines her telling the young Jesus that his origins were holy and "don't listen to the gossip." He flags this as a scene for a film, and says he is "dreaming."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mark has a virgin birth.** Tabor says the earliest Gospel does not include a birth narrative or Joseph; the birth stories come from Matthew and Luke.
- **Pantera means "son of a panther" or is a pun on "virgin."** Tabor reports Deissmann's 1906 rejection of the pun and cites inscriptions and an ossuary showing Pantera as a name.
- **Jesus' father was a Roman soldier, according to Tabor.** He says press reports misstate his view: "I never say" that, and he presents the soldier as a hypothesis for one name in the sources.
- **Being the son of a Roman soldier is necessarily shameful.** Tabor argues that nothing is known about the circumstances, and that the assumption of horror is unwarranted.
- **Jesus is a carpenter in every Gospel.** Tabor says the word appears in only one place, Mark 6:3 (and its Matthean parallel).
- **Jesus' age is known.** Tabor says Luke 3:23 is the only age given, and that everything "hang[s] on a thread like that."
