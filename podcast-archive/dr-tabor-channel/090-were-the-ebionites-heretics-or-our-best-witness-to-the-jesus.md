## Summary

Tabor asks whether the groups the church fathers called Ebionites (and the related Nazarenes) were heretics or, as he argues, one of our best witnesses to the original Jesus movement, the circle around John the Baptist, Jesus, and his brother James in Jerusalem. He begins by noting that the word "Ebionite" (from the Hebrew for "poor ones") never appears in the New Testament, and that nearly everything we know about them comes from hostile writers such as Epiphanius, Jerome, and Augustine. Tabor's method is to "read the text backward": take the enemies' accusations (human-only Jesus, no birth narrative, rejection of Paul, rejection of the Temple and sacrifice, vegetarianism) and treat them as clues to positive beliefs, supported by quotations from Jewish-Christian gospel fragments, the Pseudo-Clementine writings, and the letter of James.

He argues that the labels "Jewish Christian" or "Judaeo-Christian" are misleading for the early period, and that the Nazarene/Ebionite split is probably too neat, since readers of Paul could read him in more "Ebionite" ways. He is cautious about calling Paul an Ebionite himself, pointing to Paul's expectation that the end was near. The talk ends with Tabor saying he wants to "redeem" the Ebionites and that he believes they would have been "much closer than we know" to Jesus and James. Lambert mostly asks questions and prompts, and Tabor carries the substance throughout.

## Key Teachings

### The name and the enemies' portrait
Tabor says the term connects to Isaiah's "poor" and to the Beatitudes ("Blessed are... poor ones"), but he doubts it was a formal name taken from Jesus' words. The main evidence for the group comes from Epiphanius's heresiology (third to fifth century CE), with Jerome and others. Tabor's view is that the enemies' descriptions must be reread to recover what the group actually held. He also distinguishes the Nazarenes, who in his account accepted Paul and held a higher, non-Trinitarian Christology, from the stricter Ebionites, while noting that "some recent studies" argue the dichotomy is not valid and that readers of Paul could vary widely.

### Christology: a human Jesus adopted at baptism
Per the enemies' reports, the Ebionites used a Gospel of Matthew without a birth narrative, with Joseph as father, and held that Jesus became Christ only at his baptism. Tabor reads from a quotation Jerome gives from the "Gospel of the Hebrews," in which the voice at the Jordan says, "You are my first begotten son who rules forever," and the Spirit rests on him. Tabor calls this "adoptionism" (a later term) and compares it to Paul's language of believers being adopted by the Spirit. He says Paul likely thought Jesus was born of a human father and did not pre-exist, though he admits this is his own view and not settled.

### Torah: the "least in the kingdom" saying
Tabor cites Matthew's Greek gospel: Jesus says he came not to destroy the Torah and the prophets, and whoever "looses" one commandment "will be least in the kingdom." He says the Ebionites would take this as a rebuke to Gentile Christianity. Acts, he says, preserves the "laws of Noah" as a compromise for Gentiles, but the Ebionites' concern was with Jewish followers being told they need not keep the law. Tabor then softens the picture: keeping the law "with the intent of the law" may be what is meant, and Paul himself, on divorce, is "pretty liberal" and rabbinic in reasoning.

### The Temple, sacrifice, and scripture
Tabor says the Ebionites rejected the Temple and animal sacrifice, quoting Jeremiah that God did not command sacrifices on the day he brought Israel out of Egypt. He cites a saying from the "Gospel of the Ebionites": "Unless you cease sacrificing, the wrath of God will not depart from you." He also reports their view that the priestly writers corrupted the Torah (he compares this to the "P" source of modern scholarship), using Psalm 51 as an example of a sacrifice verse that looks added. He notes the Ebionites quoted Jeremiah's "false pen of the scribes."

### Diet and the Passover
Tabor reports that the Ebionites were vegetarian and held that John the Baptist ate not locusts but a honey cake, since Greek words for locust and a honey-cake plant sound alike. He connects this to Luke 22, where Jesus says he has desired to eat the Passover, and suggests that in an alternate version Jesus says "Do you think I desire to eat meat with you on this Passover?", presenting the bread and wine as replacing a sacrificial lamb. He presents these as Ebionite readings and says they show the group's distinct traditions.

### Paul versus Peter, and the authority of James
In the Pseudo-Clementine literature, Tabor says, Peter and Paul debate, and the material claims to come from James the brother of Jesus. Peter says he knew Jesus "in the flesh," while Paul appeals to heavenly visions; Tabor reads this against Paul's line in 2 Corinthians about no longer knowing Christ "according to the flesh." The Pseudo-Clementines tell readers to trust only what comes from James "the just." Tabor adds that the Gospel of Thomas says something similar, and he links this to the Jewish idea of hidden righteous ones (the "sadikim").

### James, the letter, and the Gospel of the Hebrews
Tabor argues that the letter of James contains nothing Pauline. He contrasts James's ethical Torah piety with the cross and blood of Romans 1-5, and calls the letter addressed to the "twelve tribes" a sign of James as leader of a restored Israel. He also reads the beloved disciple of John as James rather than John son of Zebedee, arguing that John's Gospel is strangely vague about John. A Gospel of the Hebrews passage, quoted by Jerome, has James swearing not to eat bread until he sees the risen Lord, and Jesus then telling him, "My brother, eat your bread." Tabor says Paul's list of appearances (1 Corinthians 15) fits this tradition and that the gospels suppressed James's importance.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Ebionites were simply heretics.** Tabor says the heresy label comes from hostile writers (Epiphanius, Jerome) and that the groups should be read on their own terms through the surviving sources.
- **Jewish Christianity, Judaeo-Christianity, and Nazarene/Ebionite are clean categories.** He argues these hyphenated labels are misleading for the late Second Temple period, and that the Nazarene/Ebionite dichotomy may not hold, since readings of Paul vary.
- **Christianity was a new religion from the start.** He says the Jesus movement was a Jewish movement within late Second Temple Judaism, and that calling it Christianity early on is misleading.
- **Ebionites were rigid legalists.** Tabor suggests that "keeping the Torah" may mean keeping its intent, and that the Ebionite view of the law could be more humane than the stereotype.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor reads Paul's divorce teaching and his expectation of an imminent end as partly liberal and partly apocalyptic, and questions whether Paul held a pre-existence Christology. He does not settle the question.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** The central claim is that the Ebionites trace back to James's Jerusalem church. Tabor supports this with the Pseudo-Clementines, the Gospel of Thomas, the letter of James, the Gospel of the Hebrews appearance, and a reading of 1 Corinthians 15.
- **Nazarenes/Ebionites/Essenes:** Tabor distinguishes Nazarenes (who accept Paul) from Ebionites (who reject him), but says the distinction is likely overdrawn. He mentions the Essenes in passing on Sabbath law.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet, nonviolence:** He covers Torah (the "least in the kingdom" saying and the laws of Noah), sacrifice (rejection of the Temple and Jeremiah's claim), and diet (vegetarianism and the locust reading). Nonviolence is not discussed.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor's view is that the Ebionites held Jesus as Messiah and anointed Son by adoption at baptism, not as pre-existent, and that the Spirit's indwelling was extended to followers. He presents this as a reconstruction.
- **Resurrection and afterlife:** He says the Ebionites believed Jesus was raised and was "the firstborn of those who sleep," and links this to the Gospel of the Hebrews appearance to James.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor argues the canonical gospels suppressed James's role (e.g., Peter as "pillar"), and that John's Gospel is vague about John son of Zebedee.
