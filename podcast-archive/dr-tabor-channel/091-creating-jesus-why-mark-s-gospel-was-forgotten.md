## Summary

Tabor asks why the Gospel of Mark, which he calls the first and original Jesus story, is so little read by ordinary Christians, even though Matthew and Luke build on it. He says the reason is partly visible in the text itself: Mark has no birth story, an opening line that works more like a title, and an ending in which the women flee the empty tomb in fear and "said nothing to anyone." He also points to features of Mark's Jesus that he thinks do not fit later Christian portraits: parables used so that the crowds will not understand, a rebuke of the man who calls him "good," and a sharp rebuke of Peter after Peter confesses him as the Christ.

His central claim is that Matthew and Luke do more than use Mark. In his words, they "override it," covering over parts of Mark they found inadequate, such as the disciples' failure, which he says Luke removes. Tabor says that his earlier view was that they "rewrite" Mark, and that he now uses the stronger term. He presents Mark as a literary creation whose influence was absorbed and marginalized.

The talk is a promotional preview for a seven-lecture course, "Creating Jesus," with a study guide, bibliography and maps. Tabor argues his positions from the text as he reads it, and the talk does not offer detailed evidence for dating, sources or the argument as a whole. It ends with Tabor's remark that he likes to "dig back down" into what is buried and forgotten, and with Derek thanking him and inviting viewers to the course.

## Key Teachings

### Mark is the original Jesus story, and Paul gives none
Tabor says that Mark "creates Jesus" and "gives us the only Jesus story that we have really," while Paul's letters give no story of Jesus. He contends that Matthew and Luke use Mark as their base, and that Mark's version "Remains the story really" even as later tradition developed it. This is the main thesis he sets up, and he does not defend the priority of Mark with arguments in this clip.

### Matthew and Luke override Mark rather than simply copy it
Tabor says Matthew and Luke "don't just use it they override it," and that they "want to cover things that are in the first story because they're not adequate." His example is Luke's removal of the failure of the disciples. He presents this as an editorial motive, which he reads from the text's differences, and he does not cite specific passages of Matthew or Luke beyond this.

### The opening and ending of Mark seem unfitting to later readers
Tabor notes that Mark has no birth story and begins with a line he says "is not part of the gospel story but it's kind of a title." The ending in the empty tomb, where a "young man" tells the women that Jesus "was taken up" and they flee, seems to him an odd way to end a gospel. He says that three later endings were added by scribes who found the original ending embarrassing. Derek refers to a prior conversation with Tabor about these endings, and Tabor's account of them is brief here.

### Jesus in Mark is hard to fit with later portraits
Tabor cites three examples from Mark: Jesus teaching in parables to the crowds so that they will not understand or be converted; a man calling him "good Rabbi" and Jesus replying, "why are you calling me good there's none good but God"; and Peter's confession of Jesus as the Christ. Tabor says that in his course he teaches students that Peter's confession is "a false confession inspired by Satan." He treats this as Mark's own view and says the reader must work it out in the text.

### Reading Mark on its own terms
Tabor recommends that readers take their own text of Mark, "don't assume anything," and read it without the harmonizing of the Gospels. He presents Mark as a literary work whose meaning "is left" to the reader. He says that this approach has been the most popular part of his teaching, and he describes Mark's reception as a kind of forgetting, which he wants to recover.
