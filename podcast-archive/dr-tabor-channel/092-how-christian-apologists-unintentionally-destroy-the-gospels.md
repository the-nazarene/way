## Summary

Tabor asks what happens to the four Gospels when they are harmonized into one continuous narrative, using Kermit Zarley's book as his example. He argues that a harmony "destroys the gospels" because it removes the separate voices of Matthew, Mark, Luke and John. For historical study, he says, the differences between the texts are the evidence. The talk is an hour-plus slide-and-comparison walk-through, and Tabor carries nearly all of it. Lambert appears mainly to introduce the topic and relay questions from the chat.

Tabor's method is to lay out the Two-Source hypothesis (Mark first, with Matthew and Luke each drawing on Mark and on a shared sayings collection he calls Q) and then compare parallel passages. His central claim is that Matthew and Luke usually shorten, soften, theologize or "correct" Mark, and that Mark is the earliest and most "original" of the three. He cites the Malachi/Isaiah composite quotation, the baptism, the Beatitudes and Woes, the Lord's Prayer, the "hate your father and mother" saying, the Sabbath controversy, Peter's confession, the Gethsemane account, and the endings. He also argues that Mark is "very Pauline" and that Mark's version is less triumphant than Matthew's.

The talk ends with Tabor stating his preference for Mark's "original version" over the later Gospels. He says he does not prefer Mark theologically, only as the earlier story. ## Key Teachings

### 1. Harmonizing the Gospels erases their distinct witnesses
Tabor uses Zarley's harmony as a foil. Everything in the four Gospels appears in it, but "no Matthew no Mark no Luke no John," so the result is "the Kermit Zarley gospel." He recommends synopses instead (Throckmorton's *Gospel Parallels*, Huck's numbering, and the American Bible Society RSV, which he says includes John), where readers can compare the texts side by side. He says harmonies are not a historical-critical tool.

### 2. The Two-Source hypothesis and Mark's priority
Tabor describes Mark as the first narrative, with Matthew and Luke each rewriting Mark and adding a shared sayings source, Q ("just means source in German"), plus their own material. He says Q is "mainly the teachings of Jesus," resembles the Gospel of Thomas, and has about 60 pericopes. He notes that the dating (70–95) is arbitrary but that all three are placed around or after the destruction of Jerusalem. He says about 95% of scholars accept this model, names Dale Allison, Bart Ehrman, Paula Fredriksen and John Dominic Crossan as holding it, and says he agrees with Streeter and the nineteenth-century scholars who proposed Markan priority. He places Mark's origin in the land of Israel, very Pauline, and "kind of an anti-gospel." Lambert notes Mark Goodacre as the main voice for the Farrer position, and Tabor says Goodacre does not deny the shared material. Tabor's point is that the question is whether Q was a separate document, which he says he does not need to settle.

### 3. Matthew "corrects" Mark: the Malachi/Isaiah quotation and the baptism
In Mark 1:2-3 the composite quotation combines Malachi and Isaiah, and Matthew drops Malachi and keeps only Isaiah. Tabor argues that a later writer would not invent a combined quotation, so Matthew is the corrector. At the baptism, Tabor says Mark has a private voice to Jesus ("you are my beloved Son"), Matthew adds John's objection and a public voice, and Luke separates Jesus's baptism from John's by saying he was praying after being baptized. He says John has no baptism scene. Tabor's pattern is that the expanded version "drowns" the original.

### 4. The Beatitudes, the Woes and the Lord's Prayer
Tabor argues that Luke's "blessed are you poor" is closer to the original sayings source than Matthew's "poor in spirit." Luke has four Woes that mirror the four Blessings, which Matthew lacks. He says Luke's version has a "drum beat" and is hard to say in church, while Matthew's is liturgical. For the Lord's Prayer, he argues Luke's shorter, staccato version is original and Matthew's expanded version was used by the King James translators' tradition of Luke. He suggests John the Baptist may have taught this prayer, citing Luke 11:1.

### 5. "Hate" and the rhetoric of the sayings source
On the saying about hating one's father and mother (Luke 14:26), Tabor argues Luke preserves the harsh, oral, rhetorical version and Matthew's "loves more than me" version explains it away. He discusses the "let the dead bury their dead" exchange and claims Luke has a third example that Matthew omits, noting Matthew usually shortens. He reads the "not peace but a sword" saying (Luke 12:51-53) to show Luke's repetitive, rhetorical style. He also argues that Luke's "fear Him who can destroy both soul and body in hell" (Luke 12:4-5) is more forceful than Matthew's version.

### 6. The Sabbath controversy and situational law
Tabor argues that in Mark 2:23-28 the grain-picking story rests on the principle "the Sabbath was made for humans" and "laws are for people." He says Matthew (Matthew 12:1-8) adds the temple priests' Sabbath work and an argument from Jesus's greatness ("something greater than the temple"), changing the basis from a general principle to Jesus's authority. He notes a slip in Mark's David reference (Mark says Abiathar was high priest; Tabor says it was Abimelech) and treats it as an example of an error that Matthew and others did not copy. He compares Mark to Paul's counsel to the Corinthians about food sacrificed to idols.

### 7. Peter's confession, the disciples' failures and the Gethsemane account
In Mark 8:27-33 Peter confesses Jesus but then misunderstands and is rebuked ("get behind me Satan"). Matthew 16 adds a blessing on Peter before the rebuke. Tabor says Mark's disciples "never understand," and that the sons of Zebedee's request (Mark 10:35-45) is turned into their mother's request in Matthew 20. He says Luke removes most disciple failures. In Mark 14:32-42 the disciples fall asleep three times; Luke has one prayer and mentions sleeping only "for sorrow" if that line is Matthew's. He notes Luke omits "they all forsook him and fled" and "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me" (Mark 15:34), and adds "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34).

### 8. Mark's ending and the empty tomb
Tabor argues that Mark (16:1-8) ends with the women fleeing and saying nothing, the young man in white telling them Jesus is risen and will meet them in Galilee, and the "curtain down." He describes Matthew 28 as a "Glory Hallelujah" ending with an earthquake, a lightning-like angel and appearances on a mountain in Galilee. He presents Mark as a theatrical ending with no visible resurrection, and he frames the rest of the Gospel as a call to take up the cross. He says Paul's letters to Corinth fight the same problem of power, glory and status that Mark's disciples display. He also says Mark's centurion confession ("truly this man was the son of God") is key.

### 9. Mark 12 and the temple
Tabor treats the scribe in Mark 12:28-34 as the one person in Mark who understands the Kingdom, and says Matthew and Luke soften or drop him. He argues that for Mark, once one sees the temple is not needed ("a den of thieves" and the segregated courts), "you don't need the temple anymore to serve the one God of Israel." He defines the Kingdom as the rule of God on earth "as it is in heaven," for all humanity and not a Jewish or Israelite kingdom. He says Mark's scribe is told "you are not far from the kingdom of God."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **A harmony of the four Gospels gives you a fuller picture of Jesus.** Tabor argues the reverse: a harmony erases each Gospel's distinct voice and story, so you lose the evidence historians need.
- **Matthew and Luke are simply copies of Mark with extra details.** Tabor says Matthew and Luke also have a shared sayings source, and that the Gospels' differences often show Matthew shortening, softening or theologizing Mark.
- **The "hate" saying means hating your family.** Tabor argues that Luke's rhetorical version is the original and that "hate" is hyperbole, which Matthew's "love less" version then explains; he says he agrees Matthew's reading is probably what it meant.
- **The Gospels all end in triumph.** Tabor says Mark ends with fear, flight and silence, and that Matthew's ending, with its earthquake and mountain appearances, is a later glorified version.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor argues Mark is "very Pauline," and compares Mark's Sabbath principle and the disciples' pursuit of status to Paul's letters to Corinth. He sees Paul's message of the crucified Messiah as the same message Mark's ending carries.
- **Torah:** The Sabbath controversy (Mark 2:23-28) is central. Tabor argues Mark's "laws are for people" principle becomes, in Matthew, a justification from Jesus's greatness.
- **Sacrifice and the temple:** Tabor reads Mark 12:28-34 as an argument that the temple is no longer needed, with "whole burnt offerings and sacrifices" subordinate to love of God and neighbour.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Tabor defines the Kingdom as "the rule of God on Earth... as it is in heaven," for all humanity, and says the scribe in Mark 12 is "not far from the kingdom of God."
- **Resurrection and afterlife:** The empty tomb ending, the Galilee appearance and the absence of visible resurrection in Mark are central. Tabor also cites Luke 12:4-5 on fearing "Him who can... cast into hell" (Gehenna).
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor notes Mark's voice to Jesus at the baptism ("you are my beloved Son") and Matthew's public version, and treats the centurion's confession as Mark's key claim.
- **How the Gospels and canon were formed:** This is the talk's main subject. Tabor argues that the Two-Source hypothesis, Markan priority and the editorial tendencies of Matthew and Luke explain how the four Gospels diverged.
