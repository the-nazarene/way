## Summary

Tabor's question is whether the historical Jesus was a Torah-observant Jew, and whether his teaching was a new religion or a renewal within Judaism. He answers that "there's quite good evidence now" for the first, and he marks it as "just my opinion." He argues that Jesus stressed interior and ethical dimensions of Torah, citing the Sermon on the Mount and Matthew 23:23, and that Jesus was also a messianic and eschatological prophet. He reads Paul as moving from that Jesus-centered Judaism toward a Heavenly Christ, while still working within Jewish frameworks and expecting an imminent end. His main supports are Jeremiah 31:31 (which he says keeps the Torah operative), Paul's letters (especially Galatians 2 and 1 Corinthians), the Dead Sea Scrolls text MMT, and the contrast between Ignatius of Antioch and Matthew. He also offers a textual point about Mark 7.

The exchange is conversational. Williams sets up several of the framing claims (the "new species" image, the "miscarried tradition" idea), and Tabor responds at length, so many positions in the transcript are Tabor's replies to Williams rather than Tabor's opening claims. The talk ends with Tabor's account of the Jerusalem's fall as a watershed. He says it left Christianity and Judaism as separate religions, and that rabbinic Judaism then took shape around Mishnah and Talmud study in place of Temple worship.

## Key Teachings

### Jesus as an interiorizing renewer of Torah, not a founder of a new religion
Tabor says Jesus's "religion... is basically Judaism but understood in the particular way according to the teaching of Jesus." He points to the Sermon on the Mount, to Matthew 23:23, and to Jesus's critique of "mere externality." He frames this emphasis as "a renewal" and a "restoration" of the spirituality that Moses already placed in the Torah, and he accepts Jesus as messiah-like and eschatological in some sense. He also says the Synoptic Jesus, especially in Matthew, is more authentically historical than the Jesus of John.

### Jeremiah 31:31 and the "New Covenant" as Israelite renewal
Tabor argues that Jeremiah 31:31 is about "a new covenant as Israel comes back to the land." He says that when the law is written on hearts, "the Torah is still operational" and "is not being abolished or abrogated." Evangelicals who read the passage as replacing the law, he suggests, have misread the context. He adds that Moses also spoke of putting Torah on the heart, so the idea is not new.

### Halakhah, practice and the limits of an "ethics only" Judaism
Tabor says that at Qumran, among Sadducees and Pharisees alike, Torah was "a religion with practices with festivals with holidays with the temple," and that halakhic interpretation was central. In his view Jesus "is absolutely tied to all of that." He illustrates how Paul, a former Pharisee, would be concerned about details such as the Sabbath's 39 categories of work. He describes MMT from the Dead Sea Scrolls as a strict text on purity regulations, and says it would make the Pharisees look liberal.

### Gentiles, God-fearers and the "universal Torah of faith"
Tabor says that in Judaism "the righteous of all nations have a place in the world to come" (he attributes this to the Mishnah). He points to the God-fearers in the New Testament (the Centurion, Cornelius) as people "fully accepted" without conversion. He reads Paul's concern in Galatians as a problem of adding observances to faith, not an invitation to a new faith. He calls the result "a kind of a universal Torah of faith" or an "abrahamic faith."

### Paul's "interim" and the Mark 7 question
Tabor argues that Paul believed the present order was "passing away," so believers should "work in the interim" (he cites Schweitzer's "interim ethics"). He reads Paul's remarks on women's head coverings, marriage and slavery as pragmatic measures within that short window. On Mark 7, he says the parenthetical "thus he declared all foods clean" is "actually not in the Greek," and that the passage's point is about what comes out of a person, which he calls "good Judaism." He doubts that the verse is about pork or shellfish.

### Ignatius of Antioch and the separation of the two religions
Tabor cites the letters of Ignatius of Antioch (martyred, he says, "A.D 107 or thereabouts") as rejecting Judaism outright. He sets this against Matthew's Jesus, who answers the rich young man's question about eternal life by telling him to obey the commandments. He says this shows the gap between the Gentile church and its Jewish origins. He also suggests that the Epistle of Barnabas and Ignatius were articulating "a completely distinct and separate religion."

### The fall of Jerusalem as a watershed
Tabor says that with the deaths of the leaders (he names Peter and James) and the destruction of the Temple, "the child had divorced its parents." He draws on Brandon, whose title he gives as "The Fall of Jerusalem and the Christian Church," for the claim that this moment is under-appreciated. He then describes the shift to rabbinic Judaism, with Mishnah codification, Talmud study and a literary rather than sacrificial center.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus founded Christianity as a new religion.** Tabor says Jesus was "basically" a Torah-observant Jew whose teaching was a renewal within Judaism; the new religion came later, from Paul and then the Gentile church.
- **Jeremiah 31 announces the New Covenant that ends the Torah.** Tabor argues that Jeremiah describes the law being written on hearts within an Israel still bound to the Torah.
- **Paul's gospel was a Gentile-only religion with no Torah.** Tabor says Paul expected Jews to keep the Torah and that Gentile converts were not asked to convert to Judaism, but Paul's own practice was more nuanced.
- **Mark 7 says Jesus "declared all foods clean."** Tabor says this parenthetical phrase is not in the Greek, and that the passage concerns what comes out of a person, not food laws.
- **Paul was the "original Catholic theologian."** Tabor, citing a Catholic friend's view, rejects this as "not a very Catholic thing to say," because Paul distances himself from the apostles in Galatians.
