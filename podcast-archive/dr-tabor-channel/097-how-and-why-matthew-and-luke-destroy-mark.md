## Summary

Tabor asks whether the four canonical Gospels can be "harmonized" into one account, and argues that this approach misreads the evidence. He holds that Mark was written first and that Matthew and Luke used it heavily, roughly 85 to 90 percent of Mark in Matthew and, per the video description, about 80 percent in Luke. His central claim is that the later Gospels did not simply combine Mark with other material but "overwrote" it, changing its content in ways that reveal the authors' own concerns. He presents this as a view held by "many many of us in the scholarly World," not as settled fact.

He gives several examples: Matthew's removal of "the Sabbath is made for man" from the grain-picking story in Mark 2 and its replacement with two stories about temple priests; Luke's deletion of Peter's rebuke and of the disciples' failures, and Matthew's addition of a blessing of Peter after keeping the rebuke (Mark 8, Matthew 16); and Matthew's removal of Jesus's question "why do you call me good?" in Mark 10. He recommends reading the Gospels side by side in a synopsis, such as the American Bible Society's Gospel Parallels, and says the course he is promoting goes through this material in depth.

## Key Teachings

### Mark came first, and the later Gospels should not be harmonized
Tabor says he dates the Gospels in the order Mark, Matthew, Luke, John, all in the first century, and that each writer had some connection to the Jesus movement but that they did not necessarily agree with one another. He argues that the common reader's impression of Mark as a "cut down" version of Matthew is backwards. For him, Matthew reworks Mark, and the right method is to compare the texts line by line rather than merge them, because "you're not going to get a Harmony you're going to get a conglomeration of how Matthew tried to overwrite mark."

### The Sabbath saying (Mark 2 and Matthew 12)
In Mark 2, Jesus is challenged over his disciples picking grain on the Sabbath, and the answer includes that "the Sabbath is made for man not man for the Sabbath," with "son of man" apparently meaning something like human beings. Tabor reads this as a situational ethic in which commandments serve human good and are judged by the good they promote. He says Matthew "clearly has a problem with that": Matthew removes the phrase, keeps the story, and adds two accounts of priests working on the Sabbath without guilt, because they serve the temple, and Jesus is greater than the temple. Tabor argues that Matthew "knows he's doing it" because he still has Mark in front of him.

### Peter's confession (Mark 8 and its parallels)
Tabor reads Mark's account of Peter's confession that Jesus is the Christ as undercut by what follows. Peter rebukes Jesus's talk of suffering, Jesus replies "Get Behind Me Satan," and the disciples' later questions about who will sit at the right and left hand show that they lack understanding. Tabor calls Peter's confession a "false confession" because its words are right but its understanding is not. Luke removes the whole episode of failure. Matthew keeps the rebuke but adds a blessing of Peter and a promise about building the church on him, which Tabor reads as Matthew being "very troubled by the way Mark stands as it is."

### Disciples who flee (Mark 14)
Tabor says Mark's account of Gethsemane, where the disciples forsake Jesus and flee, is a key failure motif. He says Luke removes it, and that Luke removes the disciples' failure more generally throughout his Gospel.

### "Why do you call me good?" and the Shema (Mark 10 and 12)
In Mark 10, a man asks what he must do to inherit eternal life and calls Jesus "good Rabbi." Jesus answers, "why are you calling me good," and points to there being only one good God. Tabor says Mark's emphasis on one God, the Shema, is a major motif, citing the scribe in Mark 12 who recognizes that loving God and neighbor is more than burnt offerings and sacrifices. He says Matthew removes the "good" question entirely, so the one-God emphasis is lost.

### How to read: parallels and critical comparison
Tabor's method is practical. He recommends reading the Gospels in parallel and asking what Mark says, how Matthew and Luke use Mark, and what their interests are. He says his students of all persuasions found this the most inspiring part of studying the Gospels.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Matthew is the main Gospel and Mark is a shortened version of it.** Tabor says the reverse is true: Mark came first, and Matthew (and Luke) rework it.
- **The four Gospels can be harmonized into one account.** Tabor argues that merging them produces "a conglomeration," not a harmony, because the later writers changed Mark's content.
- **Peter's confession in Mark is a valid expression of faith.** Tabor reads it as a "false confession," since Peter's words are right but his understanding is not.
