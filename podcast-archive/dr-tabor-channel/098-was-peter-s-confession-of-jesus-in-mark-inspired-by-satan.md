## Summary

Tabor asks whether Peter's confession of Jesus as "the Christ" in Mark 8 was a true confession, as Matthew and Luke seem to present it, or a false one, and whether his earlier description of it as "satanic" was hyperbole. He reads the scene in a three-column parallel (Throckmorton's *Gospel Parallels* and the American Bible Society's parallel Gospels) and argues that for Mark, Peter's confession is not a moment of insight. Mark has Jesus immediately teach that the Son of Man must suffer, be rejected and be killed, and then rebuke Peter for opposing that suffering-servant idea, calling him "Satan." Tabor's evidence is Mark's sequence, the "tell no one" charge, and his claim that Mark consistently shows the disciples failing to understand. He then argues that Matthew softens the scene by adding Peter's blessing (Matthew 16:17), and that Luke drops the rebuke and the teaching about suffering. Tabor concludes that if Peter opposed God's plan, his confession did not come with understanding, and he urges viewers to "let Mark be Mark." The talk is a single presenter's close reading and ends with a pitch for his seven-lecture Mark course.

### Key Teachings

### 1. Peter's confession is paired with rejection, not glory (Mark 8:27-33)
Tabor's central point is the order of events in Mark. Jesus asks who people say he is, Peter answers "You are the Christ," and then, in Mark 8:31, Jesus "began to teach them that the son of man must suffer many things and be rejected." Peter then rebukes Jesus, and Jesus answers "get behind me Satan." Tabor says this is why Mark's confession is "not a Glory Hallelujah confession." He reads the rebuke as Jesus opposing Peter's idea of the Christ, which he identifies with the suffering servant idea that Peter does not grasp.

### 2. The Messianic secret and the reader's knowledge (Mark 1:1, Mark 8:30)
Tabor notes that Mark 8:30 has Jesus "charged them to tell no one about him," which he links to the Messianic secret that Mark uses throughout. He says Matthew and Luke have no interest in this, because their infancy and miracle material makes Jesus's identity known early. Mark is different: "nobody knows except the evil spirits," and the reader knows from Mark 1:1 ("the beginning of the Gospel of Jesus the Messiah"). Tabor adds that some manuscripts add "Son of God" there and says he is not sure whether that belongs in the text.

### 3. Mark's disciples consistently fail
Tabor says Mark presents the disciples as failing "always without exception." He points to the dispute over who will be greatest and who will sit at Jesus's right and left (Mark 10:35-45), the poor widow who gives all she has (Mark 12:41-44), and the disciples' interest in the buildings of the Temple after Jesus speaks about it (Mark 13:1). In his reading, Peter's confession belongs to this pattern of misunderstanding.

### 4. Matthew softens the scene
Tabor argues that Matthew follows Mark but embellishes it, adding "the Son of the Living God" to Peter's answer (Matthew 16:16) and adding Jesus's blessing of Peter (Matthew 16:17: "flesh and blood has not revealed it to you but my Father in heaven"). Tabor calls this blessing an interpolation and says Matthew "wants to mitigate and soften the blow." He notes that Matthew still keeps the rebuke, "get behind me Satan" (Matthew 16:23), so the editing does not remove the tension entirely.

### 5. Luke omits the suffering teaching and the rebuke
In Luke, Peter answers "the Christ of God" (Luke 9:20). Tabor says Luke does not begin the suffering teaching at this point and does not include the rebuke. He says his own teaching copy of Luke has "omit" written beside the passage about the suffering Son of Man. In Tabor's view, this shows Luke removing the uncomfortable material.

### 6. Method: read each Gospel on its own terms
Tabor describes his method as "careful" reading, which he prefers to the label "critical." He says each evangelist edits consistently: Matthew emphasizes glory and power ("all power is given unto me," Matthew 28:18), while Luke emphasizes compassion (Luke 23:34, the forgiveness of enemies) and omits the cry of forsakenness (Mark 15:34 / Matthew 27:46). He says the Gospels can be compared, but Mark should be heard in his own voice.

### Misconceptions Addressed

- **That "get behind me Satan" is hyperbole that softens to a milder sense.** Tabor says some commenters claim the sharp language is not literal, but he argues the rebuke is genuine and expresses Jesus's opposition to Peter's view of the Christ.
- **That the Matthew and Luke versions of the confession are the true or fuller account.** Tabor says Matthew embellishes Mark and adds Peter's blessing, while Luke removes the rebuke and the suffering teaching, so neither is the original reading.
- **That he skipped the verses about suffering.** Tabor says viewers objected that he did not read the suffering passage, and he answers that he simply did not read the whole passage aloud.

- **Who Jesus thought he was (christology):** Tabor's argument turns on whether the title "Christ" in Mark's confession carries the glory sense Peter brings to it or the suffering-servant sense Jesus teaches. He also draws on the Messianic secret in Mark to show that Jesus's identity is hidden from the characters.
- **How the Gospels and canon were formed (gospels-canon):** The talk is built on the Synoptic comparison. Tabor treats Mark as the source Matthew and Luke revise, and argues the three Gospels edit this scene in consistent, different directions.
