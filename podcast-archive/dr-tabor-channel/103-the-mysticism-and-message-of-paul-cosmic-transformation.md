## Summary

This is a roughly 1 hour 47 minute interview in which host David LeBlanc walks James Tabor through *Paul's Ascent to Paradise*, a book derived from Tabor's doctoral thesis. The book's central question is how Paul's account of being "caught up" to the third heaven and into Paradise, in 2 Corinthians 12:2-4, shapes Paul's understanding of salvation, authority and the cosmos. Tabor argues that the passage describes the highest kind of mystical experience, a sight of God's glory, and that Paul's authority and his gospel grow out of it. He also argues that Paul's message is cosmic: salvation means the transformation of mortal human beings, and of creation itself, into an immortal, glorified state, modeled on Jesus as the "Heavenly Adam" (Tabor's terms). LeBlanc supplies many of the questions and reads passages from the book, and much of the conversation has him summarizing or testing Tabor's reading.

Tabor supports the ascent reading with comparisons. He points to the Talmudic and *Life of Adam and Eve* parallels, to Jewish merkava mysticism, to 1 Enoch, and above all to Apuleius's account of Lucius's initiation into the Isis mysteries. His main evidentiary point is that Paul is the only first-person witness to an ascent from the ancient world, since the other ascent texts are pseudepigraphic or written long after their supposed authors. Tabor also argues that Philippians 2:5-11 should be read through Adam in Genesis 2-3, not as the pre-existent Son giving up heavenly glory, and that Paul's resurrection language is about a cosmic transformation ("introducing the phenomenon of this Resurrection transformation into the cosmos") rather than the single event of Jesus's resurrection.

The talk ends with a series of speculations. Tabor suggests that Paul may have come to believe, near the end of his life, that he would join Christ at death, since Philippians speaks of "departing and being with Christ," and he explicitly labels this "pure speculation." The conversation then moves to a 2024-era recommendation of Tabor's Mark-focused course, the Moses Shapira scroll, and the Dead Sea Scrolls, and it closes without a firm conclusion.

## Key Teachings

### The ascent as the key to Paul's authority
Tabor reads 2 Corinthians 12:2-4 as a two-stage journey: first into the "third heaven," then into "Paradise." He argues that the passage is written in parallelism, with each affirmation repeated and explained by the next ("whether in the body I do not know, or out of the body I do not know"). In his reading, Paul is describing the highest experience, the sight of the *kavod*, God's presence, rather than a tour of the dead. The "unutterable words" are, in Tabor's words, what cannot be told because they are beyond human capacity, and the restriction on speaking is typical of mystical reports. Tabor says this experience is the source of Paul's claim to speak with authority, comparable to Moses on the mountain (Exodus 24). He cites Galatians 1 (Paul's call "from my mother's womb" and the revelation "of Jesus Christ") as possibly the same event, though he admits Pauline chronology is difficult and that Acts should not be used to fill it in.

### Paradise is a term with shifting locations
Tabor's point about the language is that "Paradise" is slippery in the late Second Temple period. In Luke 23 Jesus promises the brigand "you'll be with me in Paradise," and in Luke 16 Paradise (Abraham's bosom) is a place of comfort below. Tabor argues that Paradise can be the place of the dead, even underground, or the highest heaven, and that the term denotes God's presence. He draws on 1 Enoch (including its Similitudes), Slavonic Enoch, the *Life of Adam and Eve* (where Paradise is before the Throne of God), and Revelation's picture of God dwelling with humans. He says his book surveys every Paradise reference in an appendix-like final chapter, and he describes a cosmology of seven heavens tied to the planetary spheres.

### Paul as the only first-person ascent witness
Tabor's most emphatic claim is that Paul is the only first-person report of ascent from the ancient world. Enoch and other ascent texts speak in the first person but are pseudepigraphic, and rabbinic ascent material (Tabor names *Hekhalot* texts and the Talmudic passage pages 11b through 16a) is mostly transmitted much later. Tabor cites Morton Smith and Gershom Scholem to argue that later texts can preserve older traditions, and he uses that point to make Paul a witness to a practice already known in the first century.

### Hellenistic parallels: Lucius and the mysteries
Tabor brings in Apuleius's *Metamorphoses* (the Golden Ass), in which Lucius enters the Isis temple and describes approaching "the boundary of death" and returning "at dead of night." He reads this as a parallel to Paul's journey and argues that ascent in both is dangerous. He cites the Greek magical papyri and the Mithras Liturgy, and he insists that "magic" in antiquity was a label for practices considered dark, while the same practice could be called spirituality. He compares Paul's "buffeting" by a messenger of Satan to the demonic opposition these texts describe, and he reads Paul's self-description of the ascent as a qualified form of boasting ("I know a man"), which Colossians 2:18 may be answering.

### Philippians 2 and the Adam christology "from below"
Tabor argues against the usual reading of Philippians 2:5-11 as the pre-existent Son who gives up heavenly glory and takes on flesh. He reads "did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped" through Adam in Genesis 1:26 and Genesis 2-3: Adam was in God's image and was told not to grasp the fruit, grasped it, and became "the man of death." Jesus, a human of the seed of David (Romans 1), "did not grasp" and instead took the form of a servant, and was therefore exalted. Tabor says the believer's salvation "will parallel that of Jesus exactly," and that Jesus "becomes the pioneer" of that path. He adds that he thinks Paul believed Jesus had a human father. He lists several scholars who have argued this reading, though LeBlanc notes that the book's notes name them, and he says he is "convinced by it."

### Transformation rather than forgiveness
Tabor's main theme is that salvation for Paul is cosmic and transformative, not only a legal forgiveness. He draws on Philippians 3 (the "Commonwealth" in heaven, and the body of humility transformed "to be like his glorious Body"), 1 Corinthians 15, and Romans 8 (creation "groaning" in travail for the "Glorious Liberty of the children of God"). He argues that the "whole purpose of creation" is the transformation of the first Adam into the Heavenly Adam, with the living and the dead changing. Tabor describes three stages: Christ as first fruits; those who belong to him at the parousia, which Paul expected soon; and then the "Telos," when all rule, authority and power are subdued and God is "all things in all." He reads these as a universalist scheme, though he is careful to say Paul never talks about hell or eternal punishment. He says the dead who have not yet been transformed "perished," meaning they are dead, not that they are in hell. He also argues that Paul's sufferings serve a redemptive role for the church, and that Paul identified himself with the servant of Isaiah 49.

### Syncretism and the myth of pure beginnings
Near the end, Tabor argues against the idea of syncretism as a pollution of a pure original. He says Jesus was born into the religious world of late Second Temple Judaism, with its ideas of resurrection and afterlife, and that there is never a fixed starting point ("there's never a place to start and say okay this is the beginning"). He credits Jonathan Z. Smith, Robert Wilken's phrase "the myth of Christian Beginnings," and his own study of Samuel (with Baruch Halpern) for the view that traditions are always already mixed, including the Torah. LeBlanc connects this to the "faith once delivered" language, and Tabor adds, "you trade one fundamentalism for another."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Paul downplays his own mystical experience.** LeBlanc describes the common Evangelical view that Paul is reluctant, and Tabor agrees that Paul is "absolutely bragging on it in scare quotes." Tabor reads the self-qualifying third-person style as boasting that deflects ego ("I'm not gonna say it was me").
- **Paul's salvation is just forgiveness of sins.** Tabor (following E. P. Sanders) argues that for Paul salvation is cosmic and transformative, with a "pietistic Journey of transformation" that "is not the Paul … that gospel."
- **Ascent to heaven texts are late, medieval inventions.** Tabor argues that Paul is a first-century first-person witness, and that later rabbinic ascent texts may preserve older traditions.
- **Philippians 2 describes a pre-existent divine being who gives up glory and returns.** Tabor says this "standard" reading misses Adam christology: Jesus did not grasp equality with God and was exalted as a human.
- **Paul's resurrection is only the event of Jesus's rising from the dead.** Tabor reads Romans 1:4 as Jesus's exaltation being the introduction of a resurrection transformation "into the cosmos."
- **The dead who do not rise go to hell.** Tabor says Paul "never really talks about hell or Eternal punishment"; "perished" means they are dead until God raises them.
- **Religious traditions began pure and were later polluted by outside ideas (syncretism).** Tabor argues there is no fixed starting point, and that all traditions, including Torah, are already the result of mixing.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught.** Tabor's Jesus-to-Paul connection is through the figure of Jesus as exalted human pioneer and the Markan call to take up the cross, but the talk is almost entirely about Paul. Tabor says Jesus "does it for you" and Paul "says watch me do it."
- **Who Jesus thought he was.** Tabor's reading of Philippians 2 and Romans 1 makes Jesus a human of the seed of David who was exalted, and he suggests Paul believed Jesus had a human father. Tabor also calls Paul's own role a "second Christ with a small C" (Isaiah 49).
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell.** This is the talk's richest thread: the transformation of the living and the dead, the three stages (first fruits, parousia, Telos), the absence of hell in Paul, and the speculation that Paul expected to "depart and be with Christ" at death.
- **Torah, sacrifice and the body.** Tabor reads Genesis 2-3 and the Adam story as the framework for Paul's cosmology. He also says Paul's "suffering" has a redemptive role for the church. The talk does not discuss diet, nonviolence or sacrifice in any depth.
- **The Kingdom on earth.** Tabor says God will be "all things in all," with the Kingdom handed over to God "on Earth in heaven," and the cosmic subjection of "rule Authority and power." This is the closest the talk comes to a Kingdom-on-earth theme.
- **Nazarenes, Ebionites and Essenes.** Tabor refers to "emergent forms of the Nazarene movement" as part of the Hellenistic phenomenon of ascent and transformation. He does not discuss Ebionites or the Dead Sea community directly.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed.** Tabor notes that Paul writes before the gospel narratives, and that Acts should not be used to reconstruct Paul's chronology.
