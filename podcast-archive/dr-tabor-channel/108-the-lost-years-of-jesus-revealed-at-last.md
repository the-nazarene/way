## Summary

Tabor asks what can responsibly be said about Jesus's "Lost Years," the gap between his childhood and his public ministry, which the canonical Gospels largely skip. He sets aside the legendary material (the infancy Gospel of Thomas, and traditions of travel to Egypt, India, and Britain) as unhistorical, and argues that the Lost Years can be partly reconstructed by combining archaeology at Sepphoris with the Gospels' own hints. His main claims are that the family was very poor, that the Greek word *tekton* means a builder, probably a stone worker, rather than a woodworker, and that Jesus may have worked as a day laborer in the rebuilding of Sepphoris under Herod Antipas.

His evidence includes the sacrifice Luke describes at the Temple, where Mary and Joseph offer a pair of doves rather than a lamb, which Tabor reads as a sign of poverty. He also compares Matthew's birth narrative, with its Magi, gifts, and flight to Egypt, with Luke's account, and argues that Matthew's version is theological. On the occupation, he notes that Mark's townsfolk call Jesus "the tekton" while Matthew softens this, and he cites the second-century writer Celsus for a tradition that Mary was a spinner. He also draws on his own excavation at Sepphoris and a walk from the site to Nazareth.

The talk does not reach a conclusion. It ends, around 15:43, with Tabor posing questions about Jesus's attitude toward Rome and whether he saw his claim to kingship as a threat to Roman power. Those questions are left open.

## Key Teachings

### The Gospels are not full biographies, and the legendary material is not history
Tabor opens by saying the Gospels do not offer four complete biographies. He argues that Mark begins with Jesus at about age 30 and that Matthew, Luke, and John spend much of their space on the final days in Jerusalem. He treats the infancy traditions (Jesus shaping sparrows from mud, killing and raising children) as entertainment. He describes the travel legends (Egypt, India, and Britain, with Joseph of Arimathea and Glastonbury) and says historians give them little credibility. He also notes that the Egypt story appears in "later Jewish polemics against the Christians."

### Poverty, shown through the temple offering
Tabor's central historical argument is that the family was poor. He cites the Book of Numbers on the firstborn, the five silver shekels paid for the redemption ceremony, and the rule that a mother who cannot afford a sheep may bring two doves or pigeons. He takes Luke's report of a pair of doves and "no mention of a sheep" as evidence that the family could not afford a lamb. He describes the Bethlehem stable as a likely dwelling during the 40 days of purification and says the family had no friends or resources.

### Matthew's birth story versus Luke's
Tabor contrasts the two accounts. Matthew has a house in Bethlehem, Magi bringing gifts, Egypt as a refuge, and a massacre under Herod the Great. Tabor argues that the massacre would have been recorded by Josephus or other Roman historians, so Matthew's account is "clearly theological." He finds Luke's account more credible: a teenage mother, an unmarried couple, and a birth in a cave-like stable. He also notes that Matthew does not seem to know the family came from Nazareth.

### *Tekton* as builder or stone worker, not carpenter
Tabor's most specific linguistic claim is that the traditional English "carpenter" is misleading. He argues that *tekton* is a general term for a builder, and that in first-century Galilee it more likely meant a stone worker, because houses were built of stone and wood was scarce. He says Jesus's images of building on bedrock reflect this, and he describes the work as close to day labor, the lowest class of worker in Roman culture. He points to Mark's townsfolk calling Jesus "the tekton" and to Matthew's edit ("the son of the tekton"), which Tabor reads as Matthew softening a more original tradition.

### Sepphoris and the possibility of Jesus's labor there
Tabor describes Herod Antipas rebuilding Sepphoris after its destruction in 4 BC, with peasant labor and a booming construction trade. He argues that Joseph, and possibly Jesus in his late teens and twenties, could have worked there, since Nazareth lies only a few miles away. He describes walking from the dig site to Nazareth in about an hour and a half, and a wall and floor from the early first century that his students uncovered. He admits, with some humor, that the stones could not be linked to Jesus specifically.

### The fatherless family and the economics of day labor
Tabor argues that Joseph disappears from the sources, so Jesus probably became the family's main breadwinner, supporting his mother and six younger siblings. He cites Celsus's tradition that Mary earned money as a spinner, while admitting "we have no way of knowing Mary's livelihood." He reads the Matthew 20 vineyard parable (a denarius a day, workers hired at dawn, the "burden of the day") as reflecting Jesus's familiarity with working poor.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus was a carpenter.** Tabor argues the English "carpenter" misrepresents *tekton*, which more likely means a builder, probably a stone worker, and that the family's work was closer to day labor.
- **The Gospels give four full biographies of Jesus.** Tabor says Mark starts at about age 30 and that much of each Gospel is devoted to the final week in Jerusalem.
- **Jesus traveled to Egypt, India, or Britain as a young man.** Tabor treats these as legends, with the Egypt story as a theme of later anti-Christian polemic.
- **Matthew's birth story is historically reliable.** Tabor argues that its massacre would have been recorded by Josephus and that the story serves later theology, while Luke's account is more credible.
