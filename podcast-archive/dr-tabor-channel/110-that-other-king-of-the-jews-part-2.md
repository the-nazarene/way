## Key Teachings

### Herod the Great's building and the economy behind it
Tabor says Herod is remembered for his "cruel lust for power" and his building program. The temple remodeling, begun in 20 BC, and the expansion of its courts, which he credits to Josephus's report of 10,000 workers, were meant to make Herod "a second King Solomon." Tabor argues the money came from land and agriculture, which he calls "the only significant basis of the Palestinian economy," with wealth "essentially drawn from peasant labor extracted by taxes." He adds a shift from family farms to larger estates and a market tax on buying and selling, which he describes as a pattern across the empire in which urban elites grew rich while the countryside grew poor.

### Judas the Galilean, the Zealots and his sons
After Herod's death in March 4 BC and Roman suppression of uprisings, including the destruction of Sepphoris, Tabor describes Judas the Galilean leading a revolt when Judea was annexed in 6 AD. Tabor says Judas urged refusal of Roman taxes and preached that God was the only master. He calls Judas the inaugurator of the Zealot movement, whose agenda was Jewish independence. Tabor also says Judas's sons James and Simon were tried and crucified by a Roman procurator about a decade after Jesus's death, which he presents as a dynastic pattern.

### The names of Mary's sons
Tabor argues that the names Judas, James and Simon came from the Maccabean family that threw out the Greeks in the second century BC, and that it was "no random choice" for Mary to give three of her five sons these names. He states that Simon received the nickname "the zealot," and that Jesus was about 10 years old at the time of Judas's revolt. He suggests Mary chose the names to show solidarity with Judas's cause, "for which three of her five Sons were to die brutal deaths," naming Jesus and Simon by crucifixion and James by stoning. This is Tabor's inference and rests on name-choice reasoning rather than a cited text.

### Antipas, Sepphoris and Tiberius
Tabor says Herod Antipas became ruler of Galilee at 16 and wanted to be king of the Jews, finding Galilee a disappointment compared with Judea and Jerusalem. His first project was Sepphoris, which Tabor says yielded 200 talents of gold annually, "nine tons," according to Josephus. Around 19 AD, after Tiberius succeeded Augustus, Antipas began building Tiberius on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee. Tabor links this to the economy of the lake, noting Magdala's salted fish trade and Capernaum as Jesus's later base. He suggests this explains why the gospels so often mention tax collectors.

### The silence of the gospels about the two cities
Tabor stresses that Jesus grew up near Sepphoris and set up his headquarters a few miles from Tiberius, yet "neither city is mentioned in our new testament gospels." He asks "what are we to make of this silence" but does not settle the question in this part.

### Jesus's contempt for Herod
Tabor says Jesus "held Herod Antipas and all he stood for in utter contempt," that Jesus once called Herod "that fox," and that when Herod questioned him on the morning of his condemnation, Jesus refused to answer. He ties this to Herod's execution of John the Baptizer, which he says Jesus witnessed the consequences of. Tabor says Jesus saw how Herod's pursuit of wealth and power had oppressed his countrymen, and that he likely walked the streets of both cities many times.

### The kingdom of God against "the kingdoms of this world"
Tabor concludes that by about age 30 Jesus had formed a plan to overthrow Rome, its Jewish sympathizers and the corrupt temple establishment. He says Jesus found this vision in the Hebrew prophets, and that "the time had arrived" for "the kingdoms of this world" to "become the kingdom of God and of his Messiahs." Tabor presents this as Jesus's own expectation, drawn from scripture, and the talk does not cite particular prophetic passages.
