## Summary

This short video, mostly delivered by James Tabor with a brief host introduction and closing from Derek, asks whether Jesus in the Gospel of Mark wants people to repent, be forgiven, and be saved. Tabor's answer is that Mark's Jesus deliberately hides his teaching in parables from outsiders, and that the key line is Mark 4:10-12: "so that they may indeed see but not perceive... unless they should turn again and be forgiven." He says Matthew and Luke remove or ignore this line, so Mark must be read on its own terms. He treats the "secret of the Kingdom" as a riddle the reader must work out, and the disciples as repeatedly failing to understand it.

Tabor's main evidence is a close reading of Mark's structure: the private teaching to the Twelve (and sometimes Peter, James, John and Andrew), the explanation of the sower parable given only in private, the prohibitions against telling who Jesus is, and the shift to teaching about suffering in chapters 8 through 10. He also says Matthew incorporates about 90 percent of Mark and Luke about 85 percent, so Mark's distinctive material is lost unless Mark is read separately. He argues that the disciples are never praised in Mark, while some unnamed figures are commended.

Most of the talk is promotional. Tabor ends by inviting viewers to a seven-lesson Mark course and a live Zoom question-and-answer session on March 5 at noon Eastern, open only to people who have bought the course. ## Key Teachings

### The secrecy motif and the "secret of the Kingdom"

Tabor argues that Mark 4 is the key to the whole Gospel. After Jesus teaches in parables, the disciples ask why, and he answers that "to you has been given the secret of the Kingdom," while "those outside" receive everything in parables. Tabor reads the purpose clause as indicating that outsiders are meant not to understand "unless they should turn again and be forgiven." He says he once read the parables as an easy teaching device as a Sunday school child, but that Mark reverses this. He also notes that Jesus keeps leaving the crowds and teaching privately, and that Mark repeatedly includes prohibitions against telling who he is or what he has done.

### Matthew and Luke remove the difficult line

Tabor's methodological claim is that Mark must be read as Mark, because Matthew (about 90 percent of Mark, by his figure) and Luke (about 85 percent) embed Mark's material in larger works. He says that in those works the verse about outsiders not being forgiven is dropped or ignored, and that Luke's ending, which he paraphrases as preaching "forgiveness of sins to the whole world," would conflict with Mark's version. He presents this as an editorial tendency, a reason not to assume the two later Gospels preserve Mark's intent. He does not cite the parallel passages in Matthew or Luke by chapter and verse in this video.

### Insiders, outsiders, and the reader as tested

Tabor says the reader is placed in the position of either an insider or an outsider. He describes the Gospel as a kind of initiation in which the reader is "invited to become one of the Insiders," and suggests that the text tests whether the reader "gets it." He presents the questions he poses as a challenge for viewers taking the course. He also says a confession that "Jesus is the Christ" does not by itself guarantee a true confession, pointing to Peter as an example (he says the course will return to this).

### The disciples misunderstand and are denounced; unnamed figures understand

Tabor claims that the disciples never understand the secret, that Jesus rebukes them for this at four points, and that they are never praised in Mark. He points to Mark's ending of the disciples' story as their forsaking Jesus and fleeing at the arrest, and says Jesus is then "completely alone." He contrasts them with a few unnamed people in Mark who do understand and are commended, whom the course identifies "not by name" but by the praise given. Tabor frames this pattern as one of the Gospel's "Mysteries."

### Suffering teaching in chapters 8 through 10

Tabor says that up to chapter 8 Mark says nothing about the suffering and death of Jesus, and that teaching on this begins in chapter 8 and continues through 9 and 10. He describes this as "the key to how Mark understands Jesus as this suffering figure," and says this understanding is "very much like Paul in some ways," though he does not develop the comparison here. He notes that this teaching is given privately, and that Jesus tells the disciples not to tell anyone that he is the Christ.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Parables were meant to make things clear.** Tabor says he once believed this from Sunday school, and argues that in Mark 4 the parables are a device that hides meaning from outsiders, with the explanation given only in private.
- **Jesus wants everyone to listen and be saved.** Tabor frames the question as whether Jesus wants people to be forgiven, and points to Mark 4:10-12 as a passage that seems to say outsiders are not meant to understand unless they turn and are forgiven. He presents this as a challenge to the common view.
- **Matthew and Luke give the fuller picture of Jesus's teaching.** Tabor argues that Mark's distinctive material is lost in Matthew and Luke, and that Mark must be read on its own to see the secrecy motif.
- **Confessing that Jesus is the Christ equals true faith.** Tabor says a confession of this kind does not necessarily mean a true confession, citing Peter as an example, and says he will develop this in the course.
