## Summary

Tabor asks why Matthew and Luke "hate" the Gospel of Mark, a question he says he came to only recently. He moves from the older view that they "edited" or "rewrote" Mark toward a stronger claim: that they "overwrote" it and, in his words, "just don't like it." He uses "hate" in the sense of "really really not wanting it to uh stand." He opens with the obvious gaps: Mark has no birth narrative and no resurrection appearances.

His main evidence is a short comparison of passages. He argues that Matthew reworks the Sabbath-grain controversy (Mark 2 / Matthew 12) to present Jesus as an authority above the temple and the law. He says Matthew changes the "good teacher" exchange so that Jesus does not disclaim the title "good" in the way Mark's version does. Matthew also drops the commended scribe (Mark 12) and the widow's offering (Mark 12). Luke, Tabor says, cuts the long section of Mark 6:45-8:26 altogether, including Jesus's harsh rebuke of Peter and the disciples, and omits the cry "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me." Tabor links these changes to the "earliest view of Jesus" and to Mark's expectation that the end is near and the kingdom of God has arrived.

He closes by saying the point of his Mark course, "Creating Jesus," is that Mark's account "got lost and forgotten because it was subsumed and overwritten" by Matthew and Luke. He does not argue the claims in detail here; the talk is a nine-minute overview that points to the course for the full case, and it ends without further argument.

## Key Teachings

### Matthew and Luke treat Mark as a source they reshape, not preserve
Tabor's core thesis is that both gospels used Mark as a main source but altered its content to suit later theological ideas. He begins with the most visible gaps: Mark has no Christmas material (shepherds, wise men), and its ending lacks the glorious resurrection scene with an earthquake and angels. He argues that a reader who knows Matthew first will "tend to forget" Mark because they think they have already read it.

### Sabbath and law: Mark's Jesus relativizes the rule, Matthew's Jesus is an authority
In the grain-plucking story (Mark 2 / Matthew 12), Tabor says Mark's Jesus justifies the act by citing David and says the Sabbath was made for people, not people for the Sabbath. He does not present this as a defense "as you would in Judaism." Tabor says Matthew "hates" this because it could lead to "chaos" if people decide how to keep the law. In his reading, Matthew's Jesus is "greater than the temple" and the authority figure, which is why Matthew keeps Jesus's authority at the center of the story.

### Christology: "Why do you call me good?"
Tabor describes the rich man's question about inheriting eternal life, in which Mark's Jesus rebukes the title "good" and points to the one God of the Shema (he cites "Hear, O Israel... the Lord our God is one," which corresponds to Deuteronomy 6:4). Tabor says a Jewish author like Matthew would not object to the monotheism, but Matthew's "very high view" of Jesus's divinity makes Mark's wording a problem. Mark's version can be read, Tabor says, as Jesus disclaiming the honor himself.

### Omissions that remove positive or uncomfortable material
Tabor lists several passages he says Matthew or Luke remove:
- **The commended scribe (Mark 12):** the only figure in Mark commended for understanding the kingdom of God, a Pharisee and scribe. Matthew omits him while keeping Jesus teaching in the temple.
- **The widow's mites (Mark 12):** Tabor reads the widow as giving more than all the temple's practices, and says Matthew does not include the story.
- **Mark 6:45-8:26:** Luke removes these two chapters entirely, Tabor argues, to cut the sustained rebukes of the disciples, including "are you without understanding?"
- **Rebuke of Peter ("Get behind me, Satan"):** Tabor says Luke removes it because Luke "doesn't want any rebuking of Peter."
- **The cry of dereliction:** Tabor says Luke does not include "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me."

### Mark's eschatological frame: the kingdom is near and Eden is restored
Tabor says Mark believes "the end is near," and that "in this time of the end we go back to Eden so to speak." For him, the "good news" (gospel) is that the kingdom of God has arrived, which is the central theme of Mark. He says the question is "what is the kingdom of God," and that Mark's answer is what Matthew and Luke later reshaped. Tabor does not give textual support for the Eden phrasing in this talk.

### The priority of Mark as the basis of the argument
Tabor states that "Mark wrote first" and calls the priority of Mark "a pillar of New Testament scholarship," which makes Mark "our earliest Source." His argument depends on that consensus: if Mark is earliest, changes in Matthew and Luke can be read as deliberate revisions.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Matthew and Luke simply "edited" Mark in a neutral way.** Tabor says that "edited" or "rewrote" is too neutral; he argues they overwrote Mark because they did not want some of its ideas to stand.
- **Mark is the shorter, less interesting version that readers can skip after Matthew.** Tabor says readers who know Matthew first tend to think they have already read Mark and so "don't pay attention" to it.
- **Mark's rebukes and omissions are incidental.** Tabor argues that the changes Matthew and Luke made in these passages are systematic and deliberate.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Jesus's teaching vs. later theology:** Tabor claims Mark's Jesus has a less exalted christology and a looser view of Sabbath law than Matthew's, and that the later gospels recast these ideas. This bears on how the early teaching is recovered from the texts.
- **Torah and the Sabbath:** The grain-plucking story is presented as Mark's relativizing of the Sabbath ("made for people") against Matthew's authority-centered reshaping.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** The "why do you call me good" exchange is Tabor's main example of a christology that Matthew changes.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Tabor reads Mark's "gospel" as the announcement that the kingdom of God is near and that the present time recalls a return to Eden. He says the question of what the kingdom is remains central to Mark.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor's thesis is that Mark was "subsumed and overwritten" by Matthew and Luke, and that its original meaning was lost. This is the talk's central claim about gospel formation.
- **Resurrection:** Tabor notes that Mark lacks the resurrection appearances and the glorious scene of Matthew and Luke.
