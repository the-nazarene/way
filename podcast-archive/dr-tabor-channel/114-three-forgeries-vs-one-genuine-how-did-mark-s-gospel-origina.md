## Summary

Tabor asks how Mark's Gospel originally ended, given that manuscripts offer several endings. He argues that the ending most readers know, Mark 16:9-20, is a later forgery, and that the original Gospel ends at Mark 16:8, where the women flee the tomb "and they said nothing to anyone." He describes this as a "curtain going down in a theater," meant to leave the reader astonished. He says the longer ending is "pretty late," probably from the sixth to eighth century ("hard to know"), and that its compiler "took part of the ending of Matthew part of the ending of Luke and part of the ending of John." He also rejects two other endings he says appear in some manuscripts, one reporting briefly to Peter and another with an apocalyptic speech from Jesus, which he calls "bogus."

On the resurrection, Tabor argues that Mark does affirm that Jesus was raised, but that Mark reports no appearances. The young man at the tomb tells the women that the disciples "will see him in Galilee as he said," which Tabor reads as a hint of a visionary experience in the north, comparable to the Transfiguration. He supports this with the Greek passive verb for "lifted up" (egerthē), which he says Mark also uses for Jesus lifting a demon-possessed boy in Mark 9:27. The talk is promotional. Its closing sections advertise Tabor's Mark course and a live Q&A. ## Key Teachings

### The authentic ending is Mark 16:8
Tabor states that "many many scholars," including evangelical and critical academic scholars, agree that Mark ends at 16:8. He reads the verse aloud: the women "fled from the tomb for trembling and astonishment" and "said nothing to anyone." He presents this as the intended effect of the Gospel, a deliberate open ending. He says the longer ending "actually destroys the gospel of Mark" because the final view of Jesus should be the crucified figure, not a glorious risen one. He points to the Roman centurion's words at the cross as the last impression Mark leaves.

### The longer ending (16:9-20) is a cobbled compilation
Tabor says the traditional ending draws on other Gospels: the two travelers on the road to Emmaus (from Luke), Mary Magdalene encountering Jesus (from Matthew), the idea of meeting in Galilee (from John), and the Great Commission (from Matthew). He calls it a composition by someone who "didn't understand Mark," dated in his view to a late period. He notes that the King James Version and many conservative translations include it, and that the Revised Standard Version (around 1950) printed it in small print at the bottom of the page, which he says provoked protests and "book burnings." He notes that the more recent RSV printing still includes it, now with a note that it is not in the early manuscript witnesses.

### The two alternative endings are also spurious
Tabor describes a shorter ending that has the women report "briefly to Peter and those with him," after which "Jesus himself sent them out" to proclaim "the sacred and imperishable proclamation of Eternal salvation." He says this language does not sound like Mark and reflects a "Glory Hallelujah" impulse. He calls the Codex Washingtonianus ending his favorite: an apocalyptic speech in which Jesus says "the term of the years of Satan's power has been fulfilled." He describes it as bogus and says that no listener "would want either one of those two endings."

### The young man at the tomb and the Galilee appearance
Tabor says Mark does believe Jesus was raised but does not report appearances. The young man in white tells the women to go to Peter and the disciples, who "will see him in Galilee as he said." Tabor reads this as a promise of a glorified appearance in Galilee, possibly near Caesarea Philippi and Mount Hermon, which he says he has visited with a tour group. He suggests the disciples may have had a visionary experience there that Mark knows about, similar to the Transfiguration. He asks why Mark lacks the appearance to the Twelve, the meal, and Luke's forty days, and answers that he thinks "those Traditions have developed yet" or that Mark did not know them. He claims Mark knows only one idea of the resurrected Jesus.

### "Lifted up" versus "raised"
Tabor argues that the angel says Jesus "has been lifted up," a passive form of the Greek verb egeirō. He compares Mark 9:27, where Jesus takes a possessed boy "by the hand and lifted him up and he arose." He argues this is a simple act of helping someone stand, not a supernatural resurrection. He says Mark "doesn't use the word Resurrection" and presents a heavenly, glorified Jesus "very similar to Paul." This is a brief comparison, and he defers the full argument to the course.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The longer ending (16:9-20) is original to Mark.** Tabor says it is a late compilation, drawn from Matthew, Luke and John, and that the earliest manuscripts do not contain it.
- **Mark reports the risen Jesus appearing to people.** Tabor says Mark reports no appearances and ends at 16:8. He reads the Galilee promise as a visionary experience.
- **Mark's "resurrection" is a supernatural raising from the dead.** Tabor says the Greek "lifted up" can mean simply standing someone up, as in Mark 9:27.
- **The Gospels can be read as one blended story.** Tabor says reading each Gospel carefully reveals that Mark's ending differs from those of Matthew, Luke and John.
