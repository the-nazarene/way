## Summary

Tabor asks why readers assume Jesus's miracles are meant to prove he is the Messiah, and argues that in Mark, the earliest gospel, they are meant to do the opposite. He calls this "the secret" of Mark and says the miracles are "actually to disprove he's the Messiah," while adding that he is "being purposely provocative." His core claim is that a Messiah identified by miracles is a false concept, and that Mark's Jesus repeatedly tells people not to spread who he is.

His evidence is drawn from Mark's own sequence. He counts 22 miracles (exorcisms, healings, nature wonders, and one raising of the dead). He points to the disciples' response to the storm-stilling ("who then is this"), to Peter's confession of Jesus as Christ in Mark 8 followed by Peter's rebuke when Jesus describes suffering, and to the healings of two blind men and a deaf man that frame the disciples' failure to "get it." He compares Jesus with Hellenistic and Jewish miracle-workers (Apollonius of Tyana, a rabbinic circle-drawer, Elijah) and with Mark's refusal of a "sign," which he contrasts with Matthew's "sign of Jonah."

The talk ends as a promotion. Tabor says the ideas are covered thoroughly in his online Mark course, "Creating Jesus," and invites viewers to a private Zoom Q&A on March 5. He presents Mark as the original story that Matthew and Luke later reworked, and says he plans later courses on those gospels.

### Key Teachings

### 1. Miracles do not establish Messiahship in Mark
Tabor's opening thesis is that "you'd think Miracles are to prove Jesus is the Christ," but in Mark they "disprove he's the Messiah." He argues that Mark does affirm Jesus as Messiah in its first line ("the beginning of the Gospel of Jesus Christ or Jesus the Messiah"), but that the concept of Messiah derived from the miracles is "a false concept." He presents this as the secret he is teaching, and contrasts Mark with Matthew and Luke, which he says remove or soften Mark's prohibitions about telling who Jesus is.

### 2. The disciples' question after the storm
Tabor cites the storm-stilling, where the disciples ask "who then is this that even the wind and the Sea obeys him." He says that a reader who takes this as proof of a powerful Messiah has misunderstood "what it means to be the anointed one or the chosen one." The disciples' amazement is, for him, a sign of incomprehension rather than insight.

### 3. Peter's confession and rebuke (Mark 8)
Tabor reads Peter's confession of Jesus as Christ in Mark 8 as the point where Jesus says "okay you said it don't tell anybody." Peter then misses the meaning, and when Jesus explains what the title really means, Peter rebukes him. Tabor says "most readers are like Peter": they hear the confession as the goal and skip the explanation of suffering that follows. He describes this as halfway through the book.

### 4. The Hellenistic and Jewish background of divine-man figures
Tabor places Jesus's miracles beside the wider ancient expectation that a wonder-worker should be followed. He names Apollonius of Tyana, whom he calls "later than Jesus" with "tons of Miracles," and a rabbinic figure (captions read "circle jar," which the talk appears to mean as the circle-drawer) who could command weather. He also notes that Elijah in the Hebrew Bible works similar miracles. His point is that Mark knew these models and has Jesus reject them as the basis of his authority, saying he acts "by the authority of God" but that this is not "what a messiah is."

### 5. The healings of blind and deaf people as a parable of perception
Tabor says the last two miracles in the sequence are healings of two blind people and one deaf person, and that he does not take them as merely symbolic ("they're narrated as if they happened"). He notes that the first blind man is healed in stages (touched twice, first sees imperfectly, then clearly) and that this comes just before three predictions and the disciples' failure to understand (Mark 8 through Mark 10). The inner meaning, he argues, is that Jesus can heal those who cannot see or hear but cannot heal those who cannot "understand the secret of the Kingdom."

### 6. "No sign" in Mark versus the sign of Jonah in Matthew
Tabor reports that when enemies ask for a sign, Mark's Jesus says "there will be no sign given to this generation," even though he has been giving miracles throughout. For Tabor, "those were not the signs of the Messiah." He says Matthew, by contrast, has Jesus promise "the sign of Jonah of the resurrection of Jesus," which he reads as Matthew contradicting Mark and therefore as evidence that Matthew "doesn't understand mark."

### 7. Mark as the earliest gospel, with Matthew and Luke as later stages
Tabor frames his method as careful reading of each gospel, criticizing preachers who choose from Matthew, Mark, Luke or John "like a cafeteria." He says Matthew and Luke "represent other stages and understandings as Christianity began to shape and form itself," but that he wants to go "to the earliest," which he identifies as Mark. He says the teaching has been forgotten because he never heard it growing up.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Popular belief:** Jesus's miracles are meant to convince people that he is the Messiah. **Correction:** Tabor argues that in Mark the miracles do not establish messiahship and the disciples and crowds misread them; the true meaning concerns the suffering and "secret of the Kingdom."
- **Popular belief:** Readers can freely choose verses from any gospel to build a picture of Jesus. **Correction:** Tabor calls this "like a cafeteria" and "abusing the gospels," and says careful reading of each gospel in its own context is needed.
- **Popular belief (implied):** Matthew and Mark agree about signs. **Correction:** Tabor says Matthew's "sign of Jonah" contradicts Mark's "no sign given," so Matthew does not understand Mark.
