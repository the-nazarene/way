## Summary

Tabor asks why the gospels present "King of the Jews" as a mocking title for Jesus, when it was an official Roman-granted title for Herod the Great. He starts with Pompey's 63 BC conquest of Jerusalem, which he says included entering the Holy of Holies on Yom Kippur, a violation he calls a religious humiliation as much as a military one. He then traces how Rome set up client rule in Judea, how Herod was made king by Antony and Octavian, and how Herod consolidated power through force. His evidence is mainly Josephus, whom he cites for the Yom Kippur detail, plus the general sequence of Roman events.

Tabor argues that Herod was a rule-bound outsider who needed legitimacy. He describes Herod's executions, his fortresses (including Masada), his building in Samaria, Jerusalem and Caesarea, and his Roman-facing culture. ### Key Teachings

### Pompey's conquest and the Temple
Tabor says Pompey took Jerusalem in 63 BC after a three-month siege, killing about 12,000 Jews, and attacked on the Sabbath when observant Jews would be less ready to fight. He says Pompey and his staff entered the Holy of Holies, which the Torah reserves for the high priest once a year on the Day of Atonement. He attributes the Yom Kippur timing to Josephus. Tabor frames this as a theological crisis: the God Israel worshipped as "Master of the world" seemed unable to protect his Sanctuary.

### Roman settlement and the Messianic expectation
Tabor describes Syria being annexed as a province and Palestine being split into self-governing districts with local rulers under Roman military control. He says the Jewish hope for a Messiah-King who would rule Israel and all nations "never looked more hopeless" at this point. He then follows the Roman civil wars (Caesar, Pompey, Brutus and Cassius, Antony, Octavian/Augustus) to explain why Augustus needed a reliable eastern client.

### Herod's appointment and legitimacy problems
Tabor says Herod travelled to Rome and, with Antony and Octavian still allied, was declared king of Judea. He describes a coronation with a sacrifice to Jupiter on the Capitoline Hill. He emphasises Herod's outsider status: an Arab mother, an Idumean father who had converted to Judaism, and a native population that regarded them as illegitimate. Herod married Mariamne, a Hasmonean, but Tabor notes that her family could not claim Davidic descent. He says Herod wanted to borrow Hasmonean prestige. He also says that after the 31 BC earthquake (about 30,000 dead), critics saw it as divine judgment on Jews who accommodated Roman rule, and that Augustus confirmed Herod's title at Rhodes.

### Herod's rule by force
Tabor lists Herod's early executions (45 of the 70 Sanhedrin members, in one account), his fortification of Hasmonean desert fortresses including Masada, and his fear of a native revolt led by someone claiming Davidic legitimacy. He says Herod "ruthlessly slaughtered all who opposed him" and that he founded Sebaste as a defensive citadel with a temple to Augustus.

### Roman-facing building and his sons
Tabor says Herod built theatres and an amphitheatre in Jerusalem and a palace in the upper city. He describes Caesarea, begun in 22 BC and taking about twelve years, with an artificial harbour, a theatre, a hippodrome and a temple to Roma and Augustus. He presents this as a display of Roman culture and a point of contact with visitors from Rome. He says Herod's sons Antipas and Archelaus were educated in Rome under the emperor's direction.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The title "King of the Jews" was a mocking invention.** Tabor's point is that it was an official title granted by Augustus and held by Herod the Great, and that his sons coveted it after his death (as the description says). Its use in the gospels makes sense against that background.
- **Herod was a native Jewish king.** Tabor argues that Herod was an outsider, with an Arab mother, an Idumean father, and a native population that regarded him as illegitimate. He emphasises that Herod was "Roman at heart."
