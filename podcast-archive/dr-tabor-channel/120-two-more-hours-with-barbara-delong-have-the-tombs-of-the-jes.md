## Summary

Host Barbara DeLong interviews James Tabor, a professor of Christian Origins at the University of North Carolina at Charlotte, about his book *The Jesus Discovery*. The central question is whether a tomb found in 1980 in the Talpiot neighborhood of Jerusalem (the "Jesus family tomb") could be the tomb of Jesus of Nazareth and his family, and whether an ossuary inscribed "James son of Joseph, brother of Jesus" belongs with it. Tabor explains Jerusalem burial practice (cave tombs, family use, bones gathered into limestone ossuaries), then walks through the Talpiot inscriptions and name statistics.

Tabor argues that the names on the Talpiot ossuaries are individually common but form a cluster that, in his view, no other known Jerusalem tomb reproduces. He compares name frequencies in ossuary inscriptions with names in literary sources such as Josephus, and says the James ossuary's origin is supported by a geochemical study (Aryeh Shimron's team) matching its soil residue to the Talpiot tomb. He also points to a Jonah image and a Greek-Hebrew inscription on a second Talpiot tomb (1981) as possible early Christian symbols. On resurrection, he argues that the empty tomb and the presence of bones are compatible with Paul's view of a transformed "spiritual" body. He gives his own probability estimate, moving from "50 50" to "in the 90s like a slam dunk" once James is added, while repeatedly noting he is a scholar and that others remain unconvinced.

The interview ends with DeLong and Tabor agreeing on further study, including more DNA work, and with Tabor saying a Christian visitor can accept the tomb's findings while still holding that Jesus was raised in a transformed form. Tabor's conclusions are his own positions, and the talk does not settle the identification.

## Key Teachings

### Jerusalem burial practice and why ossuaries matter

Tabor describes the first-century practice of placing the dead in family cave tombs, letting the body decompose, and later gathering the bones into limestone ossuaries. He says roughly 900 cave tombs and about 3,000 ossuaries are known from this period, of which about 650 are inscribed, and that inscriptions are often scratched names rather than formal epitaphs. He also argues that the practice was not limited to the wealthy, citing ordinary families, and links the rise of individually named ossuaries to a growing belief in resurrection, since names "individualize the death."

### Name statistics as the core argument

Tabor's main method is onomastic: he compares how often names appear on ossuaries with how often they appear in literary sources such as Josephus. He gives figures such as about 2.3% of male names in literary sources being Matthew against 2.5% on ossuaries, and says Yose and James are rare names (about 0.4% and 1.7% in his figures, with the ossuary rates given at similar levels). Using a stadium analogy, he argues that a family with a mother named Mary, a brother named Yose and a brother named James would be a near-unique cluster. He acknowledges that the names are common individually and says the argument depends on the cluster, not on any one name.

### The Talpiot tomb (1980) and the cluster of names

Tabor recounts that in April 1980 bulldozers exposed the tomb during construction at Talpiot. Archaeologist Amos Kloner excavated it and the bones were reburied, with the ossuaries removed and stored. He reports six ossuaries with inscriptions reading (in the form Tabor gives) Jesus son of Joseph, Maria, Yose, Miriam (also called Mara or Mariamne), Matia, and Yehuda bar Yeshua (Jude son of Jesus). The tomb was not published until a 1996 report, after a film crew sought Jesus-related ossuaries for an Easter documentary. Tabor says the names "Jesus" and "Mary" were common but the cluster was not.

### The James ossuary and the geochemical link

Tabor says an ossuary reading "James son of Joseph, brother of Jesus" surfaced on the antiquities market around 2002 and was tried for possible forgery; the owner was acquitted, and Tabor says the patina is an authentic natural accretion. He ties James's death to about 63 CE (stoning, Josephus and church historians). Tabor reports a peer-reviewed study (Archaeological Discovery, 2020) by a team led by Aryeh Shimron that sampled soil residue from ossuaries and found the James ossuary's signature closely matched the Talpiot tomb, while other Jerusalem tombs did not. Tabor calls this strong evidence but says it is not a "fingerprint" match.

### The second tomb (1981): Jonah, a Greek-Hebrew inscription and symbols

Tabor describes a second tomb about 200 feet away, found in 1981 and partly cleared by Kloner. He highlights an ossuary with a carved fish, which he says Princeton scholar James Charlesworth read as bearing the letters "y o n h" (Yonah, Jonah). He argues Jonah is the earliest Christian image, linking it to Jesus's "sign of Jonah" saying and to Jonah imagery in the Roman catacombs. He also reports a four-line inscription on another ossuary (Greek "Theos," Hebrew "Yahweh," Greek, Hebrew, "raise up"), which he reads as something like "God Yahweh raised him up" or a request to be raised. He notes that the chevron-and-dot symbol on the Talpiot tomb is unexplained.

### Resurrection as spiritual transformation, not reanimation

Tabor argues that the empty tomb does not imply the absence of bones, because early Christian resurrection belief was transformation. He cites Paul's 2 Corinthians 5 imagery of clothing and tent, the Greek term "metamorphosis," and Paul's phrase "a life-giving spirit." He notes that Mark originally ends with the empty tomb and that Matthew speaks of "sightings." He argues that John's account of a temporary tomb near the crucifixion site fits a burial later moved, possibly to a family estate, and he suggests Joseph of Arimathea as a possible owner, while stating that he cannot prove it.

### Mariamne DNA and the status of the identification

Tabor says preliminary DNA testing around 2005 on the Mariamne ossuary found no mitochondrial match with the Jesus ossuary, which would rule out a sibling relationship but not a marital one. He says the cluster of three ossuaries in the first niche suggests the core family, while admitting that the original arrangement is unrecorded. He presents his own estimate as a probability (first "50 50," later "in the 90s" with James added) and notes the statistics are his interpretation.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Common names rule it out.** Tabor argues the names are individually common, but the cluster is rare and the statistics matter more than any one name.
- **Ossuaries were only for the wealthy.** Tabor says many ordinary families used them, with only some (like the high priest Caiaphas's) being elaborate.
- **An empty tomb means no bones.** Tabor argues that a transformed, spiritual body in Paul's sense is compatible with leftover bones.
- **The Talpiot tomb was a secret.** Tabor says it was excavated in 1980 but not published until 1996, and the documentary drew attention to it.
