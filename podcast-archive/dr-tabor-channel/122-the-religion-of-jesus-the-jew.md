## Summary

Tabor asks what kind of Jew Jesus of Nazareth was, given the varieties of Judaism in first-century Roman-ruled Palestine, and what his "religion" as a Jew consisted of. He opens by arguing that Jesus was a Jew and not a Christian, and that 2,000 years of separation between Judaism and Christianity have hidden how thoroughly Jesus belonged to his ancestral faith. He rejects the older scholarly picture of Jesus moving beyond a "fossilized" Judaism toward universal ethics, calling that view without historical basis and a form of Christian anti-Semitism.

His method is largely contextual. He describes Galilean village life (houses of fieldstone and mud, thatched roofs, a diet of olives, bread and lentils, and high child and early adult mortality), the Sabbath synagogue gatherings where contested questions were debated, and the Passover pilgrimages to Jerusalem, which he links to Josephus's figure of about 2.5 million Jews gathered there. He draws on Josephus's account of the Pharisees, Sadducees and Essenes, on the Dead Sea Scrolls for Essene expectations, and on the Nazareth Village project for the physical setting. He argues that Jesus kept the core markers of Jewish practice (circumcision, Sabbath, forbidden foods, pilgrim festivals, purity, tassels) while differing from strict Essene practice on Gentiles, women, Sabbath and purity. He places Jesus within a Messianic and apocalyptic movement that began about 200 years before Jesus and took its "definitive shape" under Jesus, John the Baptizer and James.

## Key Teachings

### Jesus as a practicing Jew, not a Christian
Tabor's opening claim is that Jesus "was a Jew not a Christian." He lists observances the Gospels' Jewish setting implies: circumcision, Passover, reading the Bible in Hebrew, and keeping the Sabbath. He says Jesus "saw himself as doing nothing other than fulfilling the words of Moses and the prophets." He also says Jesus was not "liberal with regard to Jewish observances in any modern sense of the term." The only rejection he attributes to Jesus is of "certain oral traditions and interpretations that some rabbinic teachers had added to the biblical Commandments." No text is cited for this beyond the general reference to Torah.

### Judaism as nation, land and election, not only belief
Tabor says that for many Jews in Roman Palestine "it was impossible to separate" military occupation and economic oppression from piety. He summarizes Judaism under four rubrics: God, Torah, land, and chosen people. He describes the expectation that Israel would be a model nation drawing the nations to Jerusalem in "the last days." He presents Jesus, John and James as men who spread this vision in a way that "threatened the highest levels of the political and religious establishment."

### Sects, population and the limits of Josephus's categories
Tabor summarizes Josephus's three main "sects or philosophies" (Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes) and the "fourth philosophy" of Judas the Galilean. The Sadducees, drawn from the priestly classes, controlled the Temple and the Sanhedrin and were skeptical of resurrection, angels and end-time events. The Pharisees speculated freely on such matters and were more flexible in law, with Rabbi Hillel the most influential. Tabor cautions against reading these groups as modern denominations. He cites population estimates of 1 to 3 million Jews in Palestine and Josephus's figures of 6,000 Pharisees and 3,000 Essenes, and concludes they were "broad categories of religious thought" that "only a handful of the elite or learned might take on as formal labels."

### Jesus compared with the Essenes
Tabor says Josephus describes the Essenes "with whom he was evidently sympathetic," and that the Dead Sea Scrolls' community expected "two Messiahs a Priestly figure and a davidic king." Jesus "shared some important beliefs and practices" with them, but Tabor argues that the Essene leadership would have condemned him for his "open attitude toward Gentiles and women" and his looser stance on Sabbath and ritual purity. He presents this as a point of contrast rather than a reconstruction of Jesus's own teachings.

### Jesus within the Messianic movement
Tabor places Jesus in "the Messianic movement of first century Palestine," which he calls "intense apocalyptic" and broader in appeal than the Essenes. He says the movement "began to take shape 200 years before he was even born," and that Jesus, his "Kinsman John the baptizer and his brother James" gave it its definitive form. He says Simon, identified as Jesus's younger brother, "became part of the inner Council of 12 Apostles," and that Rome crucified Jesus for sedition as "the rightful King of the Jews." He links this to later zealot-type claimants including Judas the Galilean and Bar Kokhba, whom Tabor says the Romans crushed in 135 AD.

- **Jesus as a prophet honored by his hometown.** Tabor says Nazareth's townsfolk "on the whole seemed to have scoffed at the idea that Jesus had some special prophetic role."
