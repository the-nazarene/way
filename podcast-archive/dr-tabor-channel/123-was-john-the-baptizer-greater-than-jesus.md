## Summary

Tabor asks whether the New Testament's portrait of John the Baptizer as a forerunner who fades before Jesus is accurate. He argues that the earliest sources show something different: John was a priest-prophet who launched a baptizing movement, Jesus joined it as a disciple, and the two worked as partners, each seeing himself as a Messiah, one of priestly and one of royal lineage (as the video description puts it). Tabor's main evidence is the Q source reconstructed from Matthew and Luke, a Hebrew version of Matthew he says preserves unedited wording, the Suba cave excavation he co-directed with Shimon Gibson, and Josephus's account of John's popularity. He claims that the Gospels, written around 70 to 100 A.D. by his dating, were shaped to downplay John and to remove any rival Messiah, including James. He ends by saying that Jesus's "deference to John is unmistakable" and that the year 27 A.D. is "largely blank in our records," which he reads as the lost joint work of the two Messiahs. The talk is a single presenter's lecture with no guest or host.

## Key Teachings

### John as a priestly prophet of the Judean desert
Tabor describes John as a descendant of Aaron who turned away from the priesthood at about age 30 and went into the Judean desert near the Dead Sea. He says John drew on Isaiah's "a voice Cries Out prepare the way of Yahweh in the desert" and on Malachi's "I am sending my Messenger." Tabor argues that John understood his task as preparing Israel for God's apocalyptic judgment through repentance and baptism, not as preparing the way for a messiah. He ties the Arava to this role and presents the Essenes as earlier Jews who had read the same Isaiah verse and moved into the same desert, also calling themselves "people of the way."

### The Suba cave and the archaeological case
Tabor reports that in December 1999 Shimon Gibson found a cave at Suba, a few miles west of Ein Karem, with primitive drawings that Tabor says depict John: a figure with right hand raised and a staff, wearing a skin garment, along with a bodyless head, a platter with a sword, and three crosses. He describes a large plastered water reservoir, roughly 78 by 13 by 16 feet, with 12 steps. Excavation from March 2000 uncovered thousands of broken handheld water vessels in early Roman layers and a rock-cut footrest with a trough for anointing. Tabor says these point to purification rites and that the Essenes may have used the site. He concedes that John's presence there "can never be proven" without an inscription, but he calls Suba "our earliest archaeological evidence related to John the baptizer and very possibly to Jesus himself."

### John's ascetic identity and kinship with Jesus
Tabor says John was a Nazarite (not a "Nazarene" from Nazareth), citing a Torah-based vow of separation. He argues that the Greek word "locusts" in Matthew's account of John's diet is a mistake, and that an ancient Hebrew Matthew has a term for a desert-plant cake similar to manna. He says John and Jesus were related through their mothers and only about six months apart in age, so they likely grew up knowing each other. Tabor presents the two as formulating a joint plan to bring down Roman rule.

### The Q source and the saying "among those born of women none is greater than John"
Tabor argues that Q, reconstructed from material shared by Matthew and Luke but absent from Mark, preserves Jesus declaring John greater than anyone born of woman (Luke 7:28). He says the qualifying clause "yet the least in the kingdom is greater than he" is a later interpolation that "protests too much." He treats Mark as the earliest of the Gospels, used by Matthew and Luke as their narrative base.

### The Hebrew Matthew
Tabor says a published Hebrew version of Matthew confirms the Q reading. In it, Jesus's praise of John stands unedited, and John "has been sent to save the world," whereas Greek Matthew has John sent "to restore all things." Tabor says later Christians would have been troubled by any text that made John the savior rather than Jesus.

### Shared teaching: the two coats and the Lord's Prayer
Tabor credits John with the saying "he who has two coats let him share with him who has none," which he says has become associated with Jesus. He argues that the shorter Lord's Prayer in Luke's Q-derived material, "father let your name be holy let your kingdom come," reflects the prayer Jesus learned from John when his disciples asked to be taught to pray "as John taught his disciples." He also suggests that parts of the Sermon on the Mount reflect a message the two preached together.

### Suppression, James, and the two-Messiah thesis
Tabor claims that by the time the four Gospels were written (70 to 100 A.D., by his dating), there was "an overt attempt" to downplay John and that "there was no room for two Messiahs." He says James, who succeeded Jesus, was "largely written out of the history." He describes the Gospels' treatment of John as a "progressive tendency" to minimize his role. He closes by saying that the year 27 A.D. is largely missing from the record and was the year of "the joint work of the two Messiahs now lost to Christian history and memory."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Christians read Isaiah's voice as preparing the way for Christ.** Tabor says the texts "say nothing about a messiah"; John understood the call as preparing Israel for repentance and God's judgment.
- **John was only a forerunner who faded away.** Tabor argues John was a rival Messiah and Jesus's teacher, and that the Gospels downplay him.
- **"Locusts" in John's diet is accurate.** Tabor claims the Greek word is a mistranslation of a Hebrew term for a desert-plant cake, as in the Hebrew Matthew.
- **The "least in the kingdom" clause is original.** Tabor calls it an interpolation added by later Christians uncomfortable with Jesus praising John.
- **John and Jesus were unrelated.** Tabor says they were related through their mothers and grew up near each other.
- **The Lord's Prayer and the two-coats saying originated with Jesus.** Tabor credits both to John, passed on to Jesus.
