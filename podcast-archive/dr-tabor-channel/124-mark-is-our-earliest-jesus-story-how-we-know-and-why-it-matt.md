## Summary

Tabor sets out what he calls the data behind the Synoptic Gospels (Matthew, Mark and Luke) and why he thinks Mark is the earliest narrative account of Jesus. The talk is an interview with host Jacob Berman, but Tabor carries nearly all of it, walking through slides (available as a PDF linked in the description) that compare parallel passages. He frames the talk as an introduction to his online course "Creating Jesus," which is about why Mark's Gospel was forgotten. He says he prefers to show the texts rather than argue a theory first.

Tabor's central claim is that Mark is "the earliest story of Jesus that we have." He describes Mark as "certainly a mixture of myth and history" that already has a developed theology, which he thinks is heavily influenced by Paul. He dates Paul to the 50s CE and Mark to probably the 70s. He allows that a collection of Jesus' sayings may have circulated before Mark, but says the story has to be read in Mark. His main evidence is side-by-side comparison: Matthew and Luke often reproduce Mark's order and wording while also changing it. Tabor argues Matthew expands and "spiritualizes" material and Luke tends to keep material closer to its source. He reads these edits as deliberate theological choices. He concludes that harmonizing the Gospels loses the distinct voice of each, and he presents his own reconstruction of the sayings source (Q) as possibly the earliest written gospel. He says he is still persuaded by Markan priority but remains open to other views.

## Key Teachings

### Mark as the first story, and Matthew and Luke as rewrites

Tabor argues that Matthew and Luke both use Mark as their main narrative source, in its order and in its main events. He says they "overwrite" Mark rather than merely copying or polishing it, and that the rivalries between the accounts are real. He reads Mark's missing birth narrative as meaningful: if Mark knew the virgin birth or genealogies, he argues, "nobody would leave out" those elements had they been important, and the same goes for the resurrection appearances narrated in Matthew and Luke. He notes that Mark ends with the women fleeing the tomb and "they said nothing to anyone" (Mark 16). He also says Mark's Jesus being "lifted up" is a point to discuss, and that whether "Resurrection" is the right English rendering is itself a question.

### Method: parallels, not harmony

Tabor recommends reading the Gospels in parallel columns (he names Throckmorton's *Gospel Parallels*) rather than in a harmony. He criticizes the harmonizing approach of Kermit Zarley's book, which he says combines material from all three accounts. "If you harmonize you destroy the texts," in his argument, because "if you put them all together you no longer have Mark." He uses "critical" to mean careful comparison rather than tearing down. He compares the task to hearing several people tell the same story in turn, each with different details.

### Q as a derived source, and Luke closer to it

Tabor describes the usual two-source model: Q is the material Matthew and Luke share that is not from Mark. He notes that Q is a definition by subtraction, mostly sayings, and that scholars usually date it around 50. He gives examples where he thinks Luke keeps the earlier form and Matthew expands:

- The Beatitudes: Luke has four blessings followed by four woes; Matthew has expanded blessings ("poor in spirit," "hunger and thirst after righteousness") and no woes. Tabor calls Matthew's version more spiritual and "liturgical."
- The Lord's Prayer: Luke's is a shorter, "staccato" prayer. Tabor says Matthew's is the one the churches memorized.
- The cost of discipleship (the "hate your father and mother" saying): he argues that Luke's version is "hyperbole" and not "interested in making it easy," while Matthew "immediately interprets it" in a Sunday-school sense.
- Luke's Sermon-like "division" saying is, in his reading, rhetorical and "shouting," while Matthew summarizes it.

He concludes Matthew is "probably not the original version of Q" because it is "constantly expanding and embellishing" the material.

### Matthew and Luke revising Mark

Tabor gives several cases where he thinks the evangelists edit Mark:

- Matthew moves a quotation: Mark cites "as it is written in the prophet Isaiah" but gives a Malachi-type line, and Tabor thinks Matthew corrected the attribution by placing it later under Isaiah.
- At the baptism, Matthew adds John's objection ("I need to be baptized by you") and Luke says Jesus was "praying" when the Spirit came down.
- Matthew's paralytic story is "cut down" from Mark's, and Tabor reads this as a Matthean interest in Jesus' authority to forgive sins.
- Matthew takes Mark's "Sabbath made for man" line out, and Luke removes Mark's rebuke of Peter and several disciple-misunderstanding passages.
- In the Gethsemane scene, Luke softens the disciples' sleeping into "sorrow" and "tired."
- Matthew moves the request of James and John into their mother's mouth, which Tabor reads as mitigating the brothers' guilt.
- Matthew omits the poor widow, which Tabor links to Mark's scribe "not far from the kingdom of God" passage, arguing the scribe is probably an original element that Mark later "completely rewrites."

### Sabbath, David, and situational ethics in Mark

On the grain-on-the-Sabbath story, Tabor says Mark's Jesus uses David's eating of the holy bread as precedent, and that "the Sabbath is made for people" opens a risk of situational ethics. He translates the principle as "God's laws are for people for human good," and gives a hypothetical about Anne Frank's rescuers to show the problem. He ties this to Israel's Yom Kippur War as a case where observance and survival conflicted. He says Mark's line "is a little bit too tough" for Luke, who he thinks removed it.

### Dating and possible earliest gospel

Tabor says the traditional dates (Matthew 80s, Luke 90s, John 100) are "frankly pretty arbitrary," and that the internal evidence he points to for Matthew being later includes the Ecclesia and the primacy of Peter, which he says Mark lacks. He describes his own published reconstruction of Q (on his website, from a graduate seminar) as possibly earlier than Mark. "If so it wouldn't just be a collection of the things of Jesus it would actually be the earliest gospel account." He also suggests Mark may have known Q and composed a narrative of rejection to add to its sayings.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Harmonizing the Gospels is a helpful way to read them.** Tabor says harmonizing destroys the distinct texts and loses Mark as Mark.
- **Matthew and Luke are independent accounts that each improved on Mark.** Tabor argues they overwrite Mark and are in rivalry with each other, not merely adding detail.
- **The traditional authorship and dates (Matthew, Mark, Luke and John by their traditional authors) are reliable.** Tabor says scholars today do not generally accept Eusebius's attributions and that the usual dates are arbitrary.
- **"Hate your father and mother" means "love God more."** Tabor says the Sunday-school reading is Matthew's interpretation and that the original saying is hyperbole.
- **The Lord's Prayer and Beatitudes as commonly memorized are the original forms.** Tabor argues Matthew's versions are liturgical expansions and that Luke's shorter forms are likely earlier.
