## Summary

Tabor asks where Jesus was and what he was doing in the period between his baptism and the start of his public work, a stretch that Mark's Gospel covers in a few lines. He argues that Mark's account, in which Jesus withdraws for 40 days and then begins preaching in Galilee after John is arrested, leaves a gap. He says the Gospel of John, though theologically elevated, contains enough narrative detail to reconstruct this period. In his reading, Jesus worked alongside John the Baptizer as a "full partner" in a joint baptizing campaign: John in the north, Jesus in Judea. The campaign, he says, was timed to a sabbatical year (26-27 CE) and to apocalyptic calculations based on Daniel's 70 weeks, and it rested on the expectation of two Messiahs, one priestly and one Davidic.

His main evidence is a mix of John's narrative (the disciples who first followed John, the Cana wedding, the move to Capernaum, the baptizing "in the country of Judea"), the Hebrew prophet Zechariah, the Dead Sea Scrolls' Community Rule, and archaeology. The archaeological strand is the Suba cave in Judea, where Tabor and Shimon Gibson recovered first-century pottery that Gibson had linked to ritual water pouring. Tabor says he now thinks the cave may be linked to Jesus rather than John. He presents this as a working reconstruction, and he offers it as the basis of his course on Mark's Gospel.

The talk ends with news from Galilee that Herod Antipas has arrested John, and with Jesus, alarmed by Pharisaic opposition in Jerusalem, going "underground." The video closes on that note, with no further argument. It works as a preview of a longer case that Tabor develops in his book and course.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Mark's gap and John's narrative clues
Tabor argues that Mark compresses the period after Jesus's baptism. Mark moves from the 40 days in the desert to "now after John was arrested Jesus came to Galilee," which leaves the impression, in Tabor's words, "that hardly any time had passed." He says Matthew and Luke follow Mark's lead and that the Gospel of John "provides us with Clues" to what Mark omits. John's theological view of Jesus is one Tabor sets aside, concentrating on "narrative details." He treats the Johannine material as historically useful even though the Gospel is "even more exalted" in its portrait of Jesus.

### 2. A joint baptizing campaign in Judea and the north
Using John 3, Tabor says Jesus and his disciples went "into the country of Judea" and baptized there while John was baptizing at a site the transcript renders as "non-ners Saleem" (the transcript's garbled spelling; the place is the one John names near Salim). He calls this "nothing less than a joint baptizing campaign," with John in the north and Jesus in the south. He argues that the Gospel of John's note that Jesus baptized more disciples than John is meant to imply Jesus's superiority, but he holds that "there is no reason to think that either their message or their mode of operation differed." He also says that none of the apostles received a Christian baptism in the Trinitarian sense: they "were baptized by John and that was it in the lifetime of Jesus."

### 3. The first disciples and the Galilean family
Tabor says that Peter, Andrew, Philip and Nathanael were first disciples of John, as John 1 reports. He then places Jesus at a wedding at Cana (John 2) and says Jesus took his mother and brothers from Nazareth to Capernaum in the spring of 27 CE. He suggests that James, "the next eldest," may have been the one getting married, and that by spring 27 Jesus had gathered at least ten male disciples, including some of his brothers. These details are inferred from John's narrative and from Tabor's reasoning about who arranged the wedding; the talk does not cite any source beyond John for them.

### 4. Sabbatical year, Daniel's 70 weeks and the "time of the end"
Tabor argues that the campaign was timed. He says 26-27 CE was a sabbatical year "according to the Torah," when agricultural work stopped and rural people were free to join a movement. He also says that Pious Jews of the period were calculating "the time of the end," and that the favored scheme was Daniel's 70 weeks. Starting from Ezra's return in 457 BCE and counting 69 weeks (483 years) gives 26-27 CE, with one week left. He presents this as a plausible reconstruction of how John and Jesus might have thought about timing, not as established fact. He reads Jesus's "the time is fulfilled" as a specific reference to this timetable.

### 5. Two Messiahs: the priest and the king
This is the talk's central claim. Tabor says that "in text after text we read not about one but two Messiahs," one Davidic and one priestly, and that the two were to stand together. He cites Zechariah, who speaks of "the branch" who bears royal honor on his throne, with a priest beside him and "peaceful understanding between the two of them." He also cites the Community Rule from the Dead Sea Scrolls, which expects "the Messiahs of Aaron and Israel," and the "teacher of righteousness" whose death, in Tabor's account, convinced the community that "The Final Countdown had begun." He then argues that John (a Levite) and Jesus (a Davidide) together would have stirred hopes of the two Messiahs.

### 6. The Suba cave and a new reading
Tabor describes his work with Shimon Gibson on first-century pottery from the Suba cave in Judea, which they began removing in 2001. Gibson had proposed that large crowds came to the cave for an immersion-like ceremony in which water was poured over the head from small jugs, and the jugs were then broken so they could not be used for ordinary purposes. Tabor says he had earlier linked this to John's early activity. While writing his book, he came to think the evidence might point to Jesus, because "we have reliable tradition that Jesus himself preached and baptized in the Judean Countryside." He imagines the scene at sunset, with the apostles and perhaps Jesus's mother and brothers at the cave. He offers this as an imaginative reconstruction and does not claim proof.

### 7. Jesus's message and the Christian picture of him
Tabor argues against the image of a "meek and lowly Jesus who seldom raised his voice." He says that "like John the baptizer Jesus's radical message divided households and Villages and shook the religious and political establishment." He also says that by the end of 27 CE, in the movement's view, Palestinian Jews were divided into two groups, those who had responded and been baptized and those who had not: "the wheat had been separated from the chaff."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mark's account is the whole story of Jesus's early career.** Tabor argues Mark omits a substantial period, and that John's Gospel contains historical clues about it.
- **Jesus was a follower or rival of John, or began his work only after John's arrest.** Tabor argues Jesus was a full partner with John in a joint campaign that ran until John's arrest.
- **Jesus was a meek figure who seldom raised his voice.** Tabor says Jesus's message was radical, divisive, and confrontational, like John's.
- **Early disciples received a Christian (Trinitarian) baptism.** Tabor says they received only John's baptism during Jesus's lifetime.
- **The Jews of Jesus's time expected a single Messiah.** Tabor argues that texts of the period, including Zechariah and the Community Rule, expect two Messiahs, a priest and a king.
