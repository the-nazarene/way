## Summary

Tabor asks where Jesus went after the winter of 29 CE and how he came to make his final, public move on Jerusalem at Passover in 30 CE. He narrates the sequence: a winter retreat east of the Jordan, a secret visit to Jerusalem at Hanukkah, the trip south with a mixed band of followers, the healing at Jericho, the entry on a donkey, and two days of confrontation in the Temple (Monday and Tuesday of Passover week). He presents the retreat site as the Wadi Cherith, which he says he identified in 1991 through "textual sleuthing" and a map, and which he links to Elijah's hiding place and to the later flight of Jesus's followers to Pella in 68 CE. Tabor argues that the Temple money changing was not illegitimate in Jewish eyes, and that Jesus's Temple action was a symbolic prophetic sign, not a plan to start a revolution. He reads Jesus's teaching on the Shema and the "kingdom of God" as combining political upheaval with spiritual insight.

The talk ends with the priests resolving to act quickly because Passover is two days away. The transcript is a narrative given by Tabor alone, with no guest or host, and his claims rest largely on the Gospels of Mark and John, with some reference to Josephus and to archaeological finds he says he saw.

## Key Teachings

### The winter retreat and the Wadi Cherith
Tabor says Jesus withdrew to the Decapolis, outside the borders of Galilee and Judea, after word reached him that his enemies in Jerusalem sought to arrest him. He reasons from John 3:23 that John the Baptist had baptized at "Anon near Salim," and identifies Tel Salim as the site. Across the Jordan from it he locates the Wadi Cherith, where Elijah had hidden. He says the Wadi lies a few miles south of the Decapolis city of Pella. He reads Pella as the destination of the Jerusalem community's flight in 68 CE, when, in his account, James was already dead and Simon led the Nazarenes. On this reading, Simon's choice of the Pella region may reflect time he had spent there with his brother Jesus in the winter of 29 CE. Tabor describes caves in the wadi and pottery fragments he says date to the first-century Roman period. He acknowledges that the group's size that winter is unknown, "whether he took the larger group of over 100 or just the inner group."

### The secret Hanukkah visit to Jerusalem
Tabor dates a clandestine trip to mid-December 29 CE from John's reference to Hanukkah and the Portico of Solomon (John 10:22-39). Jesus was cornered by Jewish authorities who asked him plainly whether he was the Messiah. Tabor argues that a messianic claim was, for Romans, a claim to kingship and therefore sedition, since "the Romans had zero tolerance for Messiahs." Jesus's answer, "you do not belong to my sheep," provoked a stoning attempt, and he escaped back across the Jordan. Tabor frames this as the reason Jesus had "so far never publicly said he was Israel's rightful King," and as the basis for his waiting for the right time.

### The journey south and the Jericho healing
Tabor reconstructs a three-day trip down the Jordan Valley in late March, with pilgrims from Galilee on the road to Passover. He describes the group as mixed in age and sex, drawn largely from Galilee, with the Twelve, Jesus's brothers, his mother and sisters, Mary Magdalene, Salome, Joanna, and Susanna at its core, and women of means funding the movement (Luke 8:3). At Jericho, a blind man called Jesus "Son of David," which Tabor calls "revolutionary words." Jesus healed him, and the crowd grew excited. Tabor places the arrival in Jerusalem on 10 Nisan, the Sunday before Passover, and says the sequence began "a final countdown."

### The triumphal entry as prophetic sign
Tabor says Jesus, on reaching the Mount of Olives, sent disciples for a donkey's colt and rode it down the western slope. He ties the action to Zechariah 9:9 ("Rejoice greatly O daughter of Zion... behold your king comes to you... riding upon an ass"). He argues the procession was deliberate and fulfilled prophecy rather than leading a revolt: Jesus "melted into the crowd" after entering the city, and the group stayed in Bethany with Mary and Martha.

### The Temple action and the defense of money changing
This is the talk's most developed argument. Tabor says the popular idea that Jesus objected to money changing is "incorrect." He argues that Jews from across the world needed coinage converted, that sacrificial animals had to be available at the Temple especially at Passover, and that the problem for Jesus was not pagan images on coins. He says the Tyrian shekel was the only coin accepted, because of its consistent silver content, and that the priests "conveniently argued" that the purity of the offering outweighed any defilement from images. He describes the Temple commerce as highly profitable and the priests as wealthy, tied to Roman sponsors, and says "one needs only to follow the money." He reads Jesus's overturning of tables and stationing of men at the gates as a sign that the corrupt system was about to be overthrown, citing Jeremiah 7:11, Isaiah 56:7, and Zechariah 14:21. He says the action was intended to "Signal something" rather than to spark a revolution.

### The Shema, the second commandment, and the kingdom
On Tuesday, Tabor says Jesus debated the Sadducean priests, Pharisees, and Herodians. On tribute, Jesus's reply "render unto Caesar" is "famous but ambiguous." Asked which commandment is greatest, Jesus quoted the Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4-5) and paired it with love of neighbor "as oneself." Tabor reads the scribe's agreement that this outweighs burnt offerings (Mark 12:33) as Jesus's view of what God most desires. Jesus's reply to him, "you are not far from the kingdom of God," leads Tabor to conclude that Jesus's kingdom "involved not only the Revolutionary overthrow of the kingdoms of the world but also a certain spiritual insight."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus objected to money changing in the Temple.** Tabor says this is "incorrect": the trade was a legitimate, necessary service for pilgrims converting coins and buying sacrificial animals.
- **Temple coinage was rejected because of pagan images.** Tabor says the accepted Tyrian shekel bore Hercules and an eagle, and that the issue was consistency of silver value, with the priests arguing that the purity of the offering outweighed any defilement from images.
- **The Temple action was meant to start a revolution.** Tabor argues it was a symbolic prophetic signal that the Temple system was about to be overthrown, fulfilling Jeremiah and Isaiah, and not a plan to lead a mob.
