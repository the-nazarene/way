## Summary

Tabor asks what happened on the last days of Jesus's life, and whether the Last Supper and crucifixion are dated and described correctly in later Christian tradition. He argues that the last meal was on Wednesday night, not the Passover meal on Thursday, and that the crucifixion took place on Thursday. His chronological case rests on the Passover itself being a Sabbath (so that two Sabbaths fell back to back in 30 AD), on a Matthew phrase about the women visiting the tomb after the Sabbath (which he says is plural in Greek), and on John's statement that the accusers would not enter Pilate's courtyard to avoid defilement before eating Passover. He also argues that Jesus had deliberately chosen Passover in Jerusalem to confront the authorities, expecting arrest and possibly crucifixion, and that the kingdom of God would then appear.

The main evidence for the second part concerns the Eucharist words. Tabor treats Paul's account (which he dates to about 54 AD and says Paul "received" from Jesus) as the earliest witness. He argues that the "body" and "blood" language is alien to Jewish meal practice and more likely reflects Paul's Greco-Roman background and visionary claim than a historical word of Jesus. He notes that John's Last Supper contains no such words and instead describes the foot washing. He then argues that Mark, written about a decade after Paul, inserted the tradition, and that Matthew and Luke followed Mark. His proposed independent check is the Didache (which he calls the "Teaching of the Twelve Apostles," dating it to the early second century and noting its discovery in a Constantinople library in 1873). He says it describes the bread and wine of the sacred meal without any reference to flesh or blood.

The talk ends with the narrative of Gethsemane: after the meal, Jesus leads eleven disciples across the Kidron Valley to the olive grove, prays that the cup might be removed, and finds his disciples asleep. ## Key Teachings

### 1. The last supper was Wednesday, the crucifixion Thursday
Tabor says that later Christian tradition placed the last meal on Thursday evening and the crucifixion on Friday, but that "we now know that this is one day off." He holds that Jesus's last meal was Wednesday night and the crucifixion was Thursday. He adds that "Jesus never ate the Passover meal," and that the confusion arose because the gospels say the body had to be taken down before sundown "because the Sabbath was near" (00:00).

### 2. Two Sabbaths in a row: the Passover argument
Tabor argues that everyone assumed the Sabbath mentioned in the gospels must be Saturday. But, he says, the Passover day itself is also a rest day "no matter what weekday it falls on." In 30 AD, Friday the 15th of Nissan was a Sabbath, so "two Sabbaths occurred back to back, Friday and Saturday." He says Matthew "seems to know this" because the women visit the tomb "after the Sabbath," and he notes that the Greek is plural. He concludes that John "preserves a more accurate chronology" (00:46).

### 3. John's chronology and the planned Passover
Tabor says that John places the Wednesday supper "before the feast of Passover" and reports that the accusers "would not enter Pilate's courtyard" because they would be defiled and unable to eat their Passover seder on Thursday evening. From this he infers that as late as Wednesday morning Jesus still intended to eat Passover on Thursday night. He describes the gathering he imagines: his mother, sisters, the women from Galilee, close supporters in Jerusalem, and "his council of 12." He says "something had gone terribly wrong, so that all his Passover plans were changed" (01:32).

### 4. Jesus's deliberate confrontation and expectation of the kingdom
Tabor argues that Jesus chose "the time and the place, Passover in Jerusalem, to confront the powers that be." He expected arrest, delivery to the Romans, and possibly crucifixion, and "firmly believed that if he and his followers offered themselves up, placing their fate in God's hands, that the kingdom of God would manifest itself." He says Jesus deliberately fulfilled two prophecies of Zechariah: entering the city as king on a donkey (the transcript garbles this) and symbolically "removing the traitors from the house of God" (02:20).

On Judas, Tabor says Jesus learned that Judas Iscariot had struck a deal to have him arrested and that this fit a scriptural pattern, citing Psalms ("even my bosom friend in whom I trusted, who ate of my bread, has lifted the heel against me"). He compares this to the Dead Sea Scroll community's "teacher of righteousness," who, he says, quoted the same psalm when one of his inner council betrayed him "over a hundred years earlier" (03:16).

### 5. Paul's account is the earliest witness, and the body-and-blood language is probably not Jesus's
Tabor calls Paul's letter to Corinth (written "around 54 AD") "our earliest account of that last meal," and quotes the words "This is my body that is broken for you" and "This cup is the new covenant in my blood" (04:07). He says these words are repeated "with only slight variations" in Mark, Matthew and Luke, and that they form "the pillar of the Christian gospel" in which humankind is saved "by the sacrificed body and blood of Jesus" (04:50).

He then argues that eating flesh and drinking blood, "even symbolically," is "completely alien to Judaism," which makes it unlikely that Jesus used such symbols. He proposes that the closest parallels are "certain Greco Roman magical writes" (the transcript garbles the term), and points out that Paul grew up in Tarsus, outside the land of Israel, and "never met or talked to Jesus," so his claimed link to Jesus is "a visionary one" (05:43).

### 6. John has no words of institution; the Didache as an independent check
Tabor notes that John recounts the Wednesday meal "but there is absolutely no reference to these words of Jesus." In John, Jesus washes the disciples' feet and then speaks of his betrayal, and Judas leaves (05:43).

He says Mark is "very close in its theological ideas to those of Paul" and that Mark, "writing a decade after Paul's account," probably inserted the body-and-blood tradition. Matthew and Luke, in his view, "base their narratives wholly upon Mark," and Luke "is an unabashed advocate of Paul as well." His conclusion is "everything seems to trace back to Paul" (06:55).

To find an independent witness, Tabor turns to the Didache (which he calls the Teaching of the Twelve Apostles), a text he dates to the early second century and says turned up in a library at Constantinople in 1873. He describes it as an instruction manual for baptismal candidates, with sections on the Eucharist "the sacred meal of bread and wine." He says it contains no mention of "the wine representing blood or the bread representing flesh," which he treats as the surprising result (06:55).

### 7. Gethsemane: the last night
Tabor narrates the walk after the meal: Jesus leads "his band of 11 disciples" across the Kidron Valley to Gethsemane, "a thick secluded grove of olive trees" at the foot of the Mount of Olives. He describes Jesus as distressed, fearful, and grieved, and says that Mark records his prayer that "if possible, the cup would be removed from him." The disciples, tired from the meal and the late hour, fall asleep (08:28).
