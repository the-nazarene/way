## Summary

Tabor's question is what actually happened from Jesus's arrest in Gethsemane to his death, and how much of the gospel account of the trial and crucifixion is historically reliable. He argues that the arrest was a Roman operation authorized by Pontius Pilate, not a purely Jewish action, and that the Roman government carried out the execution for sedition. He reads the gospels' portrayal of Pilate as sympathetic as a later Christian theological shaping. His evidence includes Philo's and Josephus's descriptions of Pilate and of crucifixion, the Jerusalem excavations said to be Annas's mansion and Caiaphas's family tomb, and the 1968 ossuary of a crucified man named Yehohanan, which he uses to explain nailing, the time to death, and the rule about burial before Sabbath.

Tabor then proposes that Jesus expected God to intervene dramatically on the third day, that the "Son of Man" coming was imminent in his view, and that Psalm 22 shaped his last words. He also identifies the "disciple whom Jesus loved" as Jesus's brother James, who in this account becomes the family's eldest after Jesus's death. The talk ends with the soldier's spear thrust and the words "the King was dead." Tabor presents most of his reconstruction as probable rather than certain, and he says several of his points rest on his own identifications (such as the beloved disciple as James).

## Key Teachings

### A Roman operation, not a Jewish mob
Tabor argues the arrest in Gethsemane, involving a Judean temple police detachment and a Roman cohort of about 600 men, was authorized by Pilate, who wanted a quiet arrest and a trial by Sanhedrin representatives before he examined Jesus himself. He cites John for the scale of the force and says Jesus's arrest was motivated by fears of the Roman response to a crowd hailing him as king. He notes that Christians were later eager to blame Jews, but that "the result was Roman through and through."

### The night hearing at Annas's and Caiaphas's house
Tabor says the night session was clearly unofficial, since the full Sanhedrin of 70 elders would meet during the day in the temple compound, not near midnight in a private house. He gives the intent as getting Jesus to make statements that could be reported to Pilate as seditious. He reads Jesus's answer "I am... you will see the son of man sitting at the right hand of power" as an admission of being Israel's king, which was enough for the authorities. He identifies the high priest's house as Annas's, based on the excavated mansion, and treats the Caiaphas tomb discovery as corroborating the trial's setting.

### Pilate's character and the political charges
He describes Pilate through Philo of Alexandria's account of a man of "vindictiveness and a furious temper" and states that Pilate had a reputation for executions without trial. Tabor argues that the charges before Pilate were purely political: threat to stability, opposition to Roman taxes, and a claim to be the Messiah/king. He says the gospel portrait of a reluctant Pilate is "simply historically inaccurate," while acknowledging that "scholars are agreed" the gospel trial accounts have been shaped by theology.

### Herod, flogging, and the charge on the placard
Tabor's sequence is: Pilate sends Jesus to Herod Antipas, who mocks him in a royal robe and sends him back; Pilate's praetorium guard then flogs him (standard practice before crucifixion) and mocks him with a crown of thorns and reed. The placard "this is Jesus the king of the Jews" was written in three languages and fixed above the head. He reads the two men crucified with Jesus as likely participants in the same recent revolt that produced Barabbas, using Josephus's term *lestes* (brigand/Zealot).

### Crucifixion mechanics and the Yehohanan ossuary
Tabor's most detailed evidence comes from the 1968 remains of a crucified man named Yehohanan, in his twenties, found in a tomb north of Jerusalem. He says the nail went through the forearms rather than the hands, since hands cannot hold a body's weight and wrists would rupture vessels, and that the feet were nailed through the heel bone, which "will not cause profuse bleeding." The nail was still in the heel bone and the wood was cut away, which shows how the body was removed. He describes death as slow, from shock, exhaustion, cramps, dehydration, blood loss, and suffocation or heart failure, sometimes hastened by breaking the legs. He ties this to the Jewish rule that those hung on a tree be buried the same day, which is why the bodies were removed before sundown. He does not give a source for this rule in the talk.

### Jesus's expectations and the cry from Psalm 22
Tabor suggests Jesus may have expected a sudden manifestation of God's kingdom: an earthquake destroying the Herodian temple, the sun darkened, the moon turned blood red, the dead raised, and heavenly armies appearing. He presents the "third day" as the prophetic day and the coming of the Son of Man as imminent. He says Jesus's cry, "my God, my God, why have you forsaken me," opens Psalm 22, and he treats the cry as a sign that Jesus may still have hoped God would intervene up to the end.

### James as the beloved disciple and the family's new head
Tabor identifies the "other disciple" in John's account, who gets Peter into the courtyard, as James, and the "disciple whom Jesus loved" who is present at the cross as also James. He says Jesus "officially put his mother in the care of this disciple," and that James is therefore now the eldest of the family. These identifications are his own readings, and the talk presents them as his interpretation rather than as established fact.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Christians have blamed the Jews for Jesus's arrest and death.** Tabor says the result was Roman through and through, and that the blame narrative was a later Christian development.
- **Pilate was reluctant and sympathetic to Jesus.** Tabor calls the gospel portrait historically inaccurate and presents Pilate as brutal and contemptuous of his Jewish subjects.
- **Jesus was nailed through the hands.** Tabor argues from the Yehohanan remains that nails went through the forearms and the heel bone.
- **Jesus was crucified for religious charges.** Tabor says the charges before Pilate were purely political: sedition, opposing taxes, and claiming to be king.
