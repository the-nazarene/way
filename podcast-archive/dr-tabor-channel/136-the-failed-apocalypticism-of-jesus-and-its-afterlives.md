## Summary

Tabor's talk is his contribution to the Westar volume *Historical Afterlives of Jesus*, a chapter he titles "Apocalypse Then and Now." He frames apocalyptic expectation as a fluid pairing of "now" and "then," and asks why the expected end-time events kept failing to arrive. His starting point is the Book of Daniel, whose "appointed time of the end" he traces through Josephus, Paul, Revelation, and later Christian and Jewish speculation about the timing of the Messiah. He argues that when the predicted end did not come, interpreters repeatedly reinterpreted the texts instead of abandoning them, first by suspending the clock (Augustine, the rabbinic "no more calculations" saying) and later by re-literalizing them (Darby, Hal Lindsey, the 1948 and 1967 events). ## Key Teachings

### 1. Apocalyptic timing: the "appointed time" and the clock
Tabor traces the phrase "the appointed time of the end" to the Book of Daniel and notes that no other Hebrew Bible text uses it in the same way. He contrasts this with Isaiah 2's "in the last days," which he says could be read as "someday" with no ticking clock. [00:01] He uses the image of a clock running down to midnight, which he links to Daniel's 70-weeks prophecy and to Paul's "the appointed time has grown very short" (he cites First Corinthians 7). [05:27]

### 2. Failed prophecy and reinterpretation
Tabor argues that Daniel 11, read against Antiochus Epiphanes (c. 165 BCE in his rough dating), is historically accurate up to about verse 40, after which "the end didn't come," so the text had to be reinterpreted. [01:33] [02:20] He cites Josephus (*Jewish War*, book six, which he says Josephus wrote c. 73 CE from the former palace of Vespasian) on an "ambiguous Oracle" that Jews took to mean a ruler from their own nation would rule the world. Tabor says that, in Josephus's own reading, the oracle pointed to Vespasian; many of the wise men "went astray." [03:53] [05:27]

### 3. The clock suspended and allegorized
In the third and fourth centuries, Tabor says, the clock stopped mattering: Augustine held that "Christ will come when Christ comes," and the Church Fathers allegorized Daniel and Revelation (e.g., the Beast as the Pope among Protestants). He notes the medieval calculations of Daniel-based periods (1290, 1260, 1235 years), which he draws from Norman Cohn's *The Pursuit of the Millennium*. [07:01] [08:33] He treats the Christian claim that "the church is the kingdom" and the spiritualization of Israel as a suspension of second-temple Messianic apocalyptic. [08:33]

### 4. The rabbinic response
Tabor cites the Babylonian Talmud, *Sanhedrin* 97–98, which says "no more calculations; the Messiah will come when he comes," and the story of planting a tree before going to check news of the Messiah. He presents this as a rabbinic retreat from calculation after repeated failed movements. [10:06]

### 5. Re-literalization in the modern era
Tabor says the 19th-century return of Jews to the land and then the establishment of the state in 1948, and the Six Day War in 1967, revived literal readings of Daniel 11, Matthew 24, and Mark 13. He points to Darby dispensationalism, Hal Lindsey, Tim LaHaye's *Left Behind*, and a World War II-era reading that cast Hitler as Antiochus and Rommel as "the King of the South." [10:06] [11:39] [13:12] [14:42]

### 6. Rowley's warning and the "cautionary tale"
Tabor closes his section by quoting H.H. Rowley's 1940s warning that "the writers of these books were mistaken in their hopes of imminent Deliverance," and that interpreters who bring "the same principles and the same hopes" to today's prophecies will prove "equally mistaken." Tabor presents the paper as a cautionary tale, and he applies the warning to present-day apocalyptic figures (he names Dom Crossan as an example of someone speaking apocalyptically about climate change, though not reading Daniel or Revelation). [11:39] [14:42] [16:11]

### 7. Apocalypticism as a dangerous afterlife
Tabor argues that apocalyptic belief has produced real-world harm, citing the Jehovah's Witnesses, the Mormons, and the dispensationalist movement, and says people can "give up all they have" under its influence. He mentions his own attention to the January 2021 rally speakers, and he refers to his involvement with Waco and its 30th anniversary. [16:11] [22:31]

### 8. The ethical strand and the "afterlives" framing
Tabor distinguishes the apocalyptic material from the prophetic ethics of justice and care for the weak, which he says Jesus exemplifies in the sayings and parables. He contrasts this with the apocalyptic material and offers Saint Francis, Mother Teresa, Acts 10, and the widow's mite as examples; he credits Crossan, Funk, and Burton Mack (as "Bert Mack") with helping him see the compelling early sayings. He also cites Schweitzer's question of what to do after thoroughgoing eschatology: whether to be crushed by history's wheel or "put your shoulder to it." [19:21] [20:57] [22:31]

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Daniel and Revelation predict modern events in literal detail.** Tabor argues that the Book of Daniel's visionary details (horns, kings of the North and South) were written about Antiochus Epiphanes c. 165 BCE, and that when the predicted end did not come, interpreters reinterpreted them rather than admitting the failure. [01:33] [02:20] [10:06]
- **Hitler and the Second World War were the Antichrist and Antiochus of Daniel.** Tabor presents this as a repeated pattern of misreading, citing the WWII-era reading in which Rommel was "the King of the South." [11:39]
- **The "Quest of the Historical Jesus" is an objective search for the real Jesus.** Tabor says the Westar volume's German title and the work it covers are better understood as an "afterlife of Jesus," in which each era finds its own image of Jesus. [17:47] [19:21]
- **Apocalyptic belief is harmless or only inspiring.** Tabor argues it can lead people to give up their lives and resources and cites movements (Jehovah's Witnesses, Mormons, dispensationalists) as examples. [16:11]
