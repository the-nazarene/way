## Summary

Tabor asks what happened to Jesus's body after the crucifixion, whether the empty tomb and the sightings of Jesus can be explained historically, and how the Jerusalem movement under James survived his death. He treats three points as indisputable: Jesus was truly dead, he was hastily buried in an unknown tomb, and the movement did not end with his death. He argues that Joseph of Arimathea's tomb was only a temporary resting place chosen because of the Passover and Sabbath, and that Jesus's family, probably Mary, Salome and the household of Mary and Martha, moved the body to a permanent tomb. He points to first-century tombs on the Mount of Olives and in the Talpiot area and to a Galilee tomb tradition recorded by Isaac Luria. He also argues that Mark's original ending (16:8) had no resurrection appearances, that the longer endings were added later, and that Paul's list in 1 Corinthians 15 is the earliest account of sightings.

The talk is a single presenter's monologue with no guest or host. Tabor presents the Galilee tradition and the family-reburial hypothesis as possibilities to weigh rather than conclusions. He states plainly that the Swoon theory and the other fringe theories have no basis in reliable historical sources. He ends with the claim that after Jesus's death James, not Peter or Paul, began to "turn things around," and that Jesus's dynasty survived him.

## Key Teachings

### Jesus's burial was temporary, and the family likely moved the body
Tabor argues that Joseph of Arimathea and Nicodemus buried Jesus in a tomb nearby only because the Passover and Sabbath were starting. The tomb did not belong to Joseph. The body was wrapped in linen and placed on a stone slab without the usual washing and anointing, and the entrance was blocked. Tabor reasons that the family would have returned after the holidays to give Jesus proper burial. He suggests Mary and Salome, possibly with Mary and Martha of Bethany, moved the body at dusk on Saturday, following Mark's note that the women bought spices after the Sabbath. The Gospel of John, he says, may preserve a memory that Mary Magdalene came early and found the tomb already empty.

### Possible sites of the crucifixion and burial
Tabor says the Church of the Holy Sepulchre and the Protestant Garden Tomb both have weak claims. The Protestant site's rock-cut tomb is dated, he says, to the Iron Age or 5th century BC, and the Catholic site rests on 4th-century traditions. He prefers the Mount of Olives. He cites one early source that describes the crucifixion as "outside the camp," which he reads as about two thousand cubits east of the temple sanctuary, and he notes that Romans often staged crucifixions on hills by main roads.

### Archaeology near the Mount of Olives as a possible family tomb
Tabor says first-century tombs on the Mount of Olives, on the Mount of Offense (captioned "Mount of a fence") and at Talpiot have "some evidence" of links to Jesus's followers. He names the Tomb of the Shroud (found in June 2000), the Talpiot tomb (found in 1980) and a James ossuary tradition tied to the Kidron Valley. He notes that a tradition says Jesus's brothers buried Mary near the crucifixion site. He says these sites "sparked such heated controversy" because a tomb there might hold Jesus's remains, and that "neither Christianity nor Judaism welcomes that proposition."

### The Gospels' accounts of the empty tomb disagree
Tabor compares John (Mary Magdalene alone, then Peter and the other disciple, who find only the linen), Mark (the women meet a young man), Matthew (an earthquake and an angel who rolls the stone away) and Luke (two men in dazzling apparel). He takes the disagreements as evidence that the stories developed over time and that the gospels were written 40 to 70 years after Jesus's death by people who were not eyewitnesses and were not living in Roman Palestine.

### Mark originally had no resurrection appearances
Tabor states that the original text of Mark ends at 16:8 with the women fleeing in trembling and astonishment and saying nothing to anyone. He says the "shockingly abrupt" ending was unacceptable, so pious scribes added an ending "sometime in the 4th Century A.D." He argues that Mark, written around 70 AD, did not regard the sightings as a necessary part of the gospel story.

### Paul's list is the earliest account of sightings
Tabor says the earliest account of sightings is in 1 Corinthians, written around 54 AD. Paul passes on a tradition that Jesus died, was buried and rose on the third day, and lists appearances to Cephas, the Twelve, 500 disciples, James, all the apostles, and finally himself. Tabor argues that Paul puts his own visionary experience on a level with the original founders, and that Paul seems willing to use "resurrection" for something like an apparition or vision, while the Gospels claim a revived body "wounds and all."

### Galilee and the Ari's Galilee tomb tradition
Tabor reads Mark 14, Matthew, and John 21 as pointing the disciples back to Galilee after the crucifixion. He argues that Matthew's mountain appearance in Galilee may recast an older tradition, and that the disciples might have been visiting a grave there. He reports that the 16th-century kabbalist Isaac Luria (the Ari) recorded a tradition that Jesus's grave lies in Galilee near the city the captions render as "svat," and that Tabor found a level, stone-piled tomb oriented toward Jerusalem that reminded him of Essene ossuaries at Qumran. He says the source is late and that its historical value is uncertain, but that the tradition deserves consideration.

### Historical conclusion: the body was moved
Tabor's conclusion is that dead bodies don't rise, and that since Jesus was clinically dead after the crucifixion, an empty tomb is best explained by the body being moved and reburied. He mentions Tertullian's account of a gardener who supposedly moved the body to protect his vegetables, and a 6th-century Coptic text that names the gardener Phylogenes and describes angels and a resurrection. He treats these as embellishments of John's gardener scene.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Joseph of Arimathea's tomb was the tomb of Jesus.** Tabor says the tomb was not Joseph's and was a temporary burial chosen because of the holidays.
- **The Holy Sepulchre and the Garden Tomb are the verified sites.** Tabor says both have problems, and he prefers a Mount of Olives location.
- **Jesus survived the cross (Swoon theory and related ideas).** Tabor says these theories have no basis in reliable historical sources, and that a clinically dead body would not rise.
- **Mark's Gospel ends with resurrection appearances.** Tabor says the original ended at 16:8 and the appearances were added in the 4th century.
- **The resurrection stories were the earliest Christian proclamation.** Tabor says the sightings developed over time, with Paul's list as the earliest written evidence.
- **The disciples immediately built the church after the crucifixion.** Tabor says the early days after the death were a crisis, with a possible retreat to Galilee.
