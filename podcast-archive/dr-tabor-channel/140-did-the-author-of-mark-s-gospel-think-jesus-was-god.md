## Summary

The talk asks whether the author of the Gospel of Mark, the earliest narrative Gospel, thought Jesus was God. Tabor frames the question narrowly: he wants to hear only what the earliest sources say, not the later Trinitarian creeds, and he treats Mark as the first of those sources. The first part of the video is Tabor's own introduction, with announcements about tours, his book on Mary, a Waco anniversary series and his Mark course. The main part is a long interview in which Paul Williams asks questions and Tabor walks through Mark passage by passage.

Tabor's answer is that Mark presents Jesus as the Messiah and Son of God but not as the one God. He supports this with the opening verse (where he says the phrase "Son of God" is bracketed in the Greek text), the baptism voice echoing Isaiah 42, the forgiveness of sins in Mark 2, the transfiguration, the "good teacher" exchange in Mark 10, the Son of Man sayings tied to Daniel 7, and the Shema in Mark 12. He argues that Matthew and Luke revise Mark, changing or removing passages that could be read as exalting Jesus, and that Mark's abrupt ending (16:1-8) has no resurrection appearances. He presents the appearances tradition in Paul (1 Corinthians 15) as later and more visionary in character. Tabor treats the scholarly consensus on dating and authorship as provisional, and he stresses that his method is historical rather than systematic-theological.

The talk ends with Williams and Tabor agreeing that Mark is a corrective to a "salvation machine" gospel of glory, and with Tabor saying that the Q source will be taken up in a later video. The closing minutes are mostly book and project announcements.

## Key Teachings

### The opening verse, the missing birth narrative, and the secret
Tabor reads Mark 1 on its own terms, without importing Matthew or Luke. He notes that Mark has no virgin birth and that the narrator does not tell the reader who Jesus is at the outset: Jesus "came from Nazareth of Galilee and was baptized by John." On the phrase "Son of God" in Mark 1:1, Tabor cites Bruce Metzger's Greek New Testament, which gives the phrase a C rating and brackets it. He says he personally thinks it was not in the original, "because that would give away the secret." He reads Mark's messianic secret (the demons and disciples are told to keep quiet) as a device that makes the reader work out who Jesus is.

### The baptism voice and Isaiah 42
Tabor argues that the voice at the baptism ("You are my beloved son, in whom I am well pleased") is best heard through Isaiah 42, where the servant is the one in whom God delights and on whom God puts the spirit. He says "son of God" is a metaphorical, not exclusive, title in the Hebrew Bible: Adam, the kings of Israel, Psalm 2's royal figure, and the "peacemakers" who "shall be called sons of God" (which he attributes to Q). On this reading the title becomes an exclusive designation for one man only later, in what he calls "an innovation" after Matthew.

### Forgiveness of sins (Mark 2:7) and Matthew's reading
In Mark 2, the onlookers say that only God can forgive sins. Tabor argues that Jesus' claim, "the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins," is a delegated authority of the kind given to human figures such as Abraham as a prophet who intercedes, not a divine prerogative. He points out that Matthew 9:8 tells the same story and ends with the crowd glorifying God "who had given such authority to human beings." For Tabor, Matthew is the earliest commentary on Mark and reads it as a human commission.

### The transfiguration and the shared resurrection glory
In Mark 9 Jesus is seen in glory with Moses and Elijah. Tabor reads this as a vision and a foretaste of "the kingdom of God come with power," not a revelation of Jesus' divinity. He says the voice then simply repeats the Isaiah 42 language and tells the disciples to listen. He also argues that Mark, like Paul, expects resurrected followers to share a glorified state, citing Mark 12:25 (being "like angels") and the God-of-the-living argument. On Tabor's reading, Mark's Jesus is not uniquely glorified; the disciples are the ones who want glory now, and Mark rebukes them (Mark 8:33: "Get behind me, Satan").

### "Why do you call me good?" (Mark 10:17-18)
Tabor takes the exchange with the rich man as a denial that Jesus is above human status: "No one is good, but God alone." He notes that Matthew changes the wording so that it no longer reads as a rebuke. He cites James Dunn's view, as Williams and Tabor summarize it, that Matthew was embarrassed by Jesus' denial of divine goodness. Tabor uses this to argue that Matthew treats his sources freely when he disagrees with them.

### Son of Man, Sabbath, and Daniel 7
Tabor argues that "the Son of Man is lord of the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27-28) is a general principle that human need governs the interpretation of the law, not a claim that the Son of Man is divine. He gives the example of hiding Anne Frank as a case where keeping a law would cause harm. He says Matthew changes the passage to say Jesus is "greater than the temple," so the Son of Man reading becomes Christological.

For the "Son of Man" sayings, Tabor connects Mark 14:62 to Daniel 7:13, where one "like a human being" comes to the Ancient of Days and receives dominion. In Daniel the figure is interpreted as the "people of the most high God," so Tabor reads Jesus' claim as to be the Davidic Messiah and agent of that end-time process, not as God. He reads the high priest's charge of blasphemy (Mark 14:63-64) as a response to a presumptuous claim by a figure the Sadducean high priest would not accept, and he notes that the high priest's reaction is ambiguous among scholars. He compares the Messiah's cosmic role with 4Q521 from Qumran, which he describes as a "divine agent," but not God.

### The empty tomb and the Galilee tradition
Tabor argues that Mark 16:1-8 has no resurrection appearances and no sightings of Jesus. He says "egeiro" in the passive is the wording used for being raised up or carried away, not the noun "anastasis," and he contrasts Mark 9's "raised him up" for a healed man. He reads the young man's message (Mark 16:7, "you will see him in Galilee") as pointing to a Galilean tradition. He says the Gospel of Peter's seven days of weeping (after the tomb is found empty) indicate a period of despair before a recovery of faith in Galilee. He connects that recovery to the appearances listed by Paul in 1 Corinthians 15:5-8, which he calls "apparitions," and he compares them with visionary experiences in other traditions (Medjugorje, Fatima).

On the endings, Tabor says the longer ending (Mark 16:9-20) was added in the second century and cobbled together from Matthew, Luke and John. He cites Codex Sinaiticus as lacking those endings, and says the longer ending "takes the two men on the road to Emmaus" from Luke. He notes that the RSV's 1952 removal of the longer ending led to Bible burnings in America. Tabor also says that Mark "does believe" in resurrection, probably in a glorified heavenly life, but that Mark's Gospel does not show it.

### The Shema (Mark 12:28-34) and monotheism
Tabor argues that Mark's Jesus, asked for the greatest commandment, quotes the Shema ("Hear O Israel, the Lord our God is one") and affirms the one God "very unambiguously." He says the Christian answer, to love God and love neighbor, is actually the second commandment in Mark. He argues that Matthew omits the Shema and that Jesus' answer does not include the Trinitarian formula. He links Sura 112 of the Quran to the same monotheistic affirmation and calls Christianity "the odd one out" on this point. He reads the scribe's agreement and Jesus' reply ("You are not far from the kingdom of God") as Mark's climax.

### Mark as a corrective, and the Gospels' relationship to each other
Tabor argues that Mark is a corrective to a "glory hallelujah" gospel: a message of heavenly security and a Jesus who will save you from hell, rather than one of suffering and prophetic witness. He cites Mark 8:34 ("take up a cross") and Mark 10:45 (the ransom saying), noting that Luke deletes the latter. He argues that Matthew and Luke were written to replace Mark, which is why Mark is not placed first in the canon, and he quotes John Barton's view (in a Blogging Theology interview, as Tabor reports it) that Mark was "obsolete" in the eyes of Matthew and Luke. Tabor says that the Gospel dating conventions (Mark around 70, Matthew around 80, Luke around 90, John around 100) are guesses, and he says the more defensible claim is that Matthew, Luke and John are later than that.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mark has a virgin birth and resurrection appearances.** Tabor says Mark has neither: no birth narrative, no sightings of Jesus, and an ending at 16:8 with the women fleeing in fear. The longer ending with appearances was added later.
- **Mark's "Son of God" makes Jesus God.** Tabor argues that Son of God is a metaphorical royal and prophetic title in the Hebrew Bible, applied to Adam, kings and peacemakers, and that Mark's use is a commission from God, not a claim of divinity.
- **Jesus claimed to forgive sins as God.** Tabor says the Mark 2 claim is a delegated human authority and that Matthew 9:8 reads it that way.
- **Jesus is God because he is glorified in the transfiguration.** Tabor reads the transfiguration as a foretaste of the kingdom, with the voice repeating Isaiah 42, not a revelation of divinity.
- **The Son of Man is divine.** Tabor reads Daniel 7 as referring to the "people of the most high God," and reads Mark 14 as a claim to be the Davidic Messiah and agent of that end-time process.
- **The greatest commandment is about the Trinity or about belief in Jesus.** Tabor says Jesus' answer in Mark 12 is the Shema, and that the church's Trinitarian doctrine is not present in it.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor says Mark and Paul both expect a glorified resurrection shared by followers (Mark 12:25, Romans 8 and 1 Corinthians 15), and that both reject the idea of a glorified individual over followers in the sense of "glory now."
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** Tabor reads Mark 12:26-27 (God of the living) as hope of a kingdom beyond death, not annihilation, and reads Mark 16:1-8 as a Galilee tradition that Paul's appearances later echo.
- **Torah:** Tabor's reading of the Sabbath passage (laws are for people) and of the Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4 quoted in Mark 12) are direct engagements with Torah.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Tabor reads the transfiguration as the kingdom "come with power," and Mark 12:34 ("not far from the kingdom") as Mark's climax.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor argues that Mark's Jesus is the Messiah and an agent of the Daniel 7 process, not God, and that the high priest's reaction reflects a presumed claim.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor argues that Matthew and Luke revise and in some sense replace Mark, that the longer ending is a later addition, and that the four-Gospel canon places Mark awkwardly in relation to its successors.
- **Nazarenes, Ebionites and Essenes:** Not addressed, apart from the Qumran 4Q521 reference.
