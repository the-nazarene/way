## Summary

Sam, the host, interviews James Tabor about Paul's view of salvation. Tabor's 43-year work on Paul began with his University of Chicago dissertation, which became *Paul's Ascent to Paradise*. He opens with Paul's cosmology: a layered universe in which the lower, material world is corrupt and temporary, the heavens above are more lasting and divine, and angelic and demonic "powers" occupy the levels between. Tabor argues that Paul shares this Hellenistic frame but reads it through Genesis 1, so that creation is good in origin yet made subject to decay by God's own will, as a plan to produce a new, glorified race of children of God.

The second half turns to Christ and the afterlife. Tabor argues that in Paul's seven undisputed letters Jesus is fully human and not a pre-existent being, that Philippians 2 is best read as an Adam-pattern of grasping versus obedience, and that Jesus was exalted and "deified" after the resurrection, which Paul understood as a transformation rather than a resuscitation of the corpse. Tabor says Paul expected the end of the age in his own lifetime, that the expected delay is the main problem for reading him, and that Paul may have held a modified universalism. The talk ends with Sam thanking Tabor and both proposing future conversations, including on Paul's universalism and on David Capes.

## Key Teachings

### The layered cosmos and the escape from the lower world
Tabor says the central concept for Paul is escape from a lower material world, with Hades at the bottom, the living earth above it, and the air filled with powers as one rises toward the moon and the seven planetary spheres. He ties this to Plato, Cicero, Philo, and the *Ascension of Isaiah*. He reads Romans 8's "height nor depth" as a list of cosmic entities (angels, principalities, powers) rather than as a generic reassurance. He also says Satan, "the God of this world" in Galatians, is associated with the lower air, and that Paul's references to being hindered by Satan (1 Thessalonians-era travel, 1 Corinthians 5's handing over to Satan) reflect that frame.

### Paul's ascent and the third heaven
Tabor argues that 2 Corinthians 12 is the only first-person ascent account from antiquity by an identifiable author, in contrast to texts such as *1 Enoch*, which are collections written by many hands and attributed to figures such as Enoch. He discusses the "third heaven" and "Paradise" as a two-stage journey, with Paradise as the highest, unbounded heaven where God's throne is. He uses the "messenger of Satan" and the "thorn in the flesh" to show that ascent was dangerous, with guardians to pass. He suggests, as a guess, that the thorn may have been a sexual temptation, citing Romans 7 and ascetic parallels such as Anthony. He also notes that 2 Corinthians 12 and Galatians 1 imply Paul's gospel came directly from Jesus. Tabor says he is open to a 14-year chronology from Galatians 1 but thinks the chronology is hard to make work.

### Creation in bondage and the "new creation" of glorified children
Tabor argues that Paul differs from Gnostic and Platonic dualism because the lower world was not a fall or accident. Paul holds, in Tabor's reading of Romans 8, that the cosmos was subjected to decay "by the will of God," in hope of freeing it. The plan is to bring forth a "second race" or "genus" of spiritual beings, with new bodies and immortality, even above angels. Tabor calls this "Paul's greatest idea," describes Adam's descendants as first atoms of flesh, and says the children of God are the second atoms, a "new creation" that Paul himself names. He also discusses Genesis 1:26's "male and female" and whether Paul's glorified humanity is androgynous, saying Paul does not follow the Gnostic myth of split, reunified light beings.

### Crucifixion, suffering, and the path to glory
Tabor says Paul's teaching on glorification depends on suffering with Christ (Romans 8), reading "I die daily" and "crucify the flesh" (Galatians) as ethical struggle. He cites 1 Corinthians 2 for the claim that the powers did not know what they were doing when they crucified the Lord, and 1 Corinthians 4 for Paul's sarcasm toward the Corinthians who thought they were already reigning. Tabor compares this with Norman Perrin's anecdote about a historical Jesus who cursed his enemies on the cross, which he says would make Paul's salvation scheme fail.

### Christology: fully human, exalted, and "high human christology"
Tabor argues that in the seven undisputed letters (1 and 2 Corinthians, 1 Thessalonians, Galatians, Philemon, Philippians, Romans), Jesus is "fully human," "of the seed of David" (Romans 1:4), with no pre-existence. He treats the pre-existence passages (2 Corinthians 8, 1 Corinthians 10:4, Philippians 2) and rejects them as clear evidence, though he allows that Colossians, Ephesians, and the Pastorals move toward pre-existence. He says Philippians 2 is an "Adam christology": Adam grasped at eternal life and died, while Jesus did not grasp but took the form of a slave and obeyed. He discusses 1 Corinthians 8:6 ("one Lord") and argues that *kyrios* there is a title of mastership and that Paul does not identify Jesus with the one God. He describes Paul's view as "a very high christology only because he believes a lowly flesh and blood human being can be deified."

### Resurrection as transformation, not resuscitation
Tabor argues that for Paul resurrection is a transformation or metamorphosis, not the resuscitation of a dead body, and that 1 Corinthians 15 is about transformation of the living and dead alike. He draws on Philippians 3:20-21 (salvation as the transformation of the lowly body), 2 Corinthians 5 (shedding the "old clothes" for re-clothing), Daniel 12 (the sleeping dead wake), and the imagery of seed and plant or caterpillar and butterfly to argue that the bodily form is different in kind. He says Paul's pattern would not need the corpse of Jesus to rise, and he disagrees with Tom Wright and Bart Ehrman on bodily continuity, arguing for continuity only in "the essential self." He also points to Revelation's language of the sea and death giving up the dead as showing that no physical remains are needed.

### Parousia, timing, and possible universalism
Tabor says Paul expected the end of the age in his own generation, citing 1 Corinthians 7's advice to live as though the world's form is passing, and calls the non-arrival of the end "the big problem of Paul." He describes Paul's staged scheme (Jesus as first-born, the many at the parousia, then the *telos* in 1 Corinthians 15) and suggests Paul may have been a modified universalist, though he says he is unsure what Paul thought and that the question deserves a future conversation.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Popular belief:** "Heights and depths" in Romans 8 means God protects you from every danger (a comforting "nothing can separate us" reading). **Tabor:** Paul is listing cosmic entities and powers that exist between the levels of the universe.
- **Popular belief:** Paul's cosmos is like the modern scientific cosmos, or the ancient world was flat-earth. **Tabor:** Ancient cosmology was spiritually animated and layered, with the heavens more divine than the earth, and it was not flat-earth thinking.
- **Popular belief:** Paul is Gnostic. **Tabor:** Paul is not Gnostic because he affirms creation as good, and he does not hold that God was surprised by the fall.
- **Popular belief:** Resurrection means resuscitation of the corpse. **Tabor:** Paul means transformation or metamorphosis into a new kind of body.
- **Popular belief:** Believers go straight to heaven at death. **Tabor:** Paul expects the transformation at the parousia, not an immediate immortal soul in heaven.
- **Popular belief:** Jesus pre-existed as a divine being who came down to earth (incarnation). **Tabor:** In the seven undisputed letters, Jesus is fully human and was exalted and deified after resurrection.
- **Popular belief:** Paul called Jesus "God" in the Yahweh sense. **Tabor:** Paul distinguishes "one God the Father" from "one Lord" (*kyrios*), and his use of the term is one of mastership.
- **Popular belief:** Worship of Jesus is a problem for Paul's monotheism. **Tabor:** Bowing or giving homage to an exalted human is consistent with Paul's view, which is a human being deified rather than God in the Trinitarian sense.
