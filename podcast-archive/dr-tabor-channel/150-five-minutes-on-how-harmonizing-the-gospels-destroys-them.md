## Summary

Tabor asks whether harmonizing the four Gospels into one continuous narrative helps or harms historical study. He answers that harmonization "destroys the Gospels," and he uses Kermit Zarley's *The Gospels Interwoven*, which merges Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John into one text, as his example. His point is that everything from all four appears in Zarley's book, yet none of the individual Gospels survives as a distinct document: "No Matthew, no Mark, no Luke, no John." He presents this as a loss of the texts that historians need to compare. He recommends a synopsis instead, a parallel-column edition that sets the Gospels side by side so readers can see where they agree and differ. He shows the American Bible Society edition, which uses the Revised Standard Version, and mentions a Greek synopsis (Huck) that shows the editorial work behind the English. Tabor says Zarley's approach has some use, but "it's not a historical critical use." The talk ends with a short promotional pitch for Tabor's Mark course, which the video description links to. The video is brief, and its argument rests mostly on the comparison of formats rather than on detailed textual evidence.

## Key Teachings

### Harmony versus synopsis
Tabor distinguishes a harmony, which merges the Gospels into one story, from a synopsis, which prints the texts in parallel columns. In his view, a harmony removes the separate authorial voices. A synopsis keeps each Gospel intact and lets the reader compare them directly. He says that "getting all three or four across the page" allows "your own comparisons," and he presents this as the proper tool for historical work.

### Zarley's book as a case study
Tabor describes Zarley as a sincere Christian believer and former professional golfer, and he calls him a friend. He says Zarley's book includes all the material from the four Gospels but in a single merged narrative, so the separate Gospels are no longer visible. Tabor says he wrote to Zarley to tell him so, half-jokingly: "You have destroyed the gospels." He adds that Zarley's book may still have a use for readers who want a single story, but not for historical critical study.

### Synoptic and Greek resources
Tabor shows the tools he uses in his classes. He names Throckmorton's *Gospel Parallels* as the synopsis he has used, and he says the American Bible Society edition, in the Revised Standard Version, is his current preference because it is "a little more literal" and includes John. For readers with Greek, he points to Huck, which he says is the Greek text behind Throckmorton's parallels, and says the pericope numbers match between the two. He notes that the Greek sometimes shows editorial changes that the English hides.

### Relations among the Gospels
In passing, Tabor says he thinks John knew Mark and also knew the other Gospels. He offers this as a personal opinion ("I think"), and he does not argue for it in this video.

### Audience and approach
Tabor says his students at Notre Dame, William and Mary, and UNC Charlotte come from a range of religious backgrounds, including conservative Christians. He says he teaches a method for reading the text that people of any belief can use, and that the talk is meant to be useful whether the viewer sees the Gospels as mythology, as historical sources, or as the word of God.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Merging the Gospels into one story gives a fuller, more reliable picture.** Tabor argues that harmonizing removes the separate texts, so historians can no longer compare how each Gospel tells the story. A parallel synopsis keeps the texts distinct and is the better tool for historical work.
- **A harmony is just another way of reading the same material.** Tabor says that a harmony and a synopsis are different tools. He says a harmony has its uses for devotional or narrative reading, but it is not a historical critical method.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor's point is about reading format. He argues that keeping the four Gospels separate, as a synopsis does, is necessary for seeing their individual shapes and differences. He does not discuss how the canon itself was formed.
- **Relations among the Gospels:** His brief remark that John knew Mark and the other Gospels touches on literary dependence, but he offers it as an opinion and does not support it here.
