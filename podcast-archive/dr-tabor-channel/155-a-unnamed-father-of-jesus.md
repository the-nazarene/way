## Summary

Tabor asks who Jesus's biological father was, given that only Matthew and Luke describe a virginal conception and the idea of a divine conception became a central dogma of later Christianity. He starts from the historian's assumption that Jesus had a human father and treats the question as open. He argues that the Virgin birth stories were probably written to answer a real scandal about Mary's pregnancy before her marriage to Joseph, not to present Jesus as a Greco-Roman style divine hero. His evidence includes Mark's wording "the son of Mary" and its absence of Joseph, Matthew's rewording of the same Nazareth scene, a hostile remark in John's gospel about being "not born of fornication," the saying of Jesus about knowing one's father and mother in the Gospel of Thomas, and the early name "Jesus son of Pantera" in Celsus and rabbinic sources.

The central case is that Pantera was a Roman soldier, and Tabor describes his own visit to a gravestone in Bingerbrück, Germany, for a soldier named Tiberius Julius Abdis Pantera, who came from Sidon and died in the middle of the first century. Tabor is explicit that this cannot be proven as Jesus's father's tomb without DNA from identifiable remains. He concludes that Joseph was probably not the biological father, that Jesus's father "remains unknown but possibly was named Pantera," and that the question should be left open.

The talk ends by pointing out that if Joseph was not Jesus's father but later fathered other children with Mary, the family situation would be complicated, and Tabor says "things are never quite so simple" in a family as royal as this one. He does not resolve the question.

## Key Teachings

### The Virgin birth as a response to a scandal

Tabor argues that Matthew and Luke's birth narratives were "a way of affirming the divine nature of Jesus" and that they may have been developed to address a real problem. He notes that the Virgin birth appears "only" in Matthew and Luke and "is found nowhere else in the New Testament." He argues that Matthew's genealogy names four women who had sex outside marriage, at least two of whom became pregnant, and that by naming them Matthew "seems to be implicitly addressing Mary's situation."

### Mark and John preserve traces of the illegitimacy charge

Tabor's first textual argument is that Mark, "our earliest gospel written around 70 A.D.," has the townspeople of Nazareth say "the son of Mary." He reads this as evidence of an unnamed father, since "in Judaism children are invariably referred to as Sons or Daughters of the father not the mother." Mark never mentions Joseph. Tabor says Matthew reworks the same scene to remove the implication, and that some later Greek manuscripts of Mark change the text to "the son of Mary and Joseph," which he reads as a move to "mute or play down the Scandal."

For John, Tabor points to a Jerusalem dispute in which Jesus's critics say "we were not born of fornication." He takes this as an attack on Jesus by reference to a rumor about his birth.

### The Gospel of Thomas, saying 105

Tabor describes the Gospel of Thomas, found at Nag Hammadi in sealed jars with other Coptic papyri, as containing 114 sayings and possibly dating to the second century. He cites saying 105, "one who knows his father and his mother will be called the son of a [harlot]," as an echo of the illegitimacy label. On his reading, the saying implies that the charge was unjust and that Jesus knew the circumstances of his birth and the identity of his absent father. The saying is cryptic, and Tabor frames this as a reading "many scholars have found," not his own certainty.

### "Jesus son of Pantera" as paternal identification

Tabor traces the name Pantera through Celsus, whose anti-Christian work *On the True Doctrine* he dates to around 178 A.D., and through an early rabbinic dispute about healing a snake bite "in the name of Jesus Son of ponteur" (Pantera). He argues that these early sources say nothing about a Roman soldier and that "in Judaism when you want to identify a person you attach the name of the father." On this view, the phrase identifies rather than insults. He adds that the fourth-century Christian writer Epiphanius accepted some authenticity in the tradition and explained it by claiming Joseph's father was named Jacob Pantera. Tabor notes that Pantera was a Greek name found in Latin inscriptions, especially as a surname of Roman soldiers.

### The Bingerbrück gravestone

Tabor cites Adolf Dieckmann's 1906 article on inscriptions with the name Pantera, which described a tombstone of Tiberius Julius Abdis Pantera found in 1859 at Bingerbrück, near the Nahe and Rhine rivers. The inscription, as Tabor reads it, names a man "of sidon age 62 a soldier of 40 years service of the first cohort of archers." Tabor visited the museum in Bad Kreuznach in 2005 and examined the stone and the archive. He argues from the names that Pantera was probably a former slave who became a freedman with Roman citizenship, and that "abdis" is a Latinized form of the Aramaic *ebed*, "servant of God," suggesting Semitic or Jewish background. He says the cohort had moved from Palestine to Dalmatia in 6 A.D. and to the Rhine frontier in 9 A.D.

Tabor is clear about the limits: "there's no way to prove a connection with this type of evidence short of DNA test of identifiable remains."

### Possible scenarios and the question left open

Tabor lists several possibilities: a Roman soldier raping Mary, a prior relationship Mary chose before her engagement to Joseph, or a Pantera who left the area without knowing of the pregnancy. He suggests the Pantera buried in Germany "would have been a younger man closer to Mary's age." He argues that Matthew and Luke's chronology, which places the pregnancy after the engagement, should not be "the final word," and that "from a historian's point of view this question in particular should be left open."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Matthew and Luke's Virgin birth stories are the only plausible explanation of Jesus's origin.** Tabor argues these stories may have been written to answer a real scandal about Mary's pregnancy.
- **The "son of Mary" wording is a neutral description.** Tabor reads it as evidence of an unnamed father, since Jewish usage normally names the father.
- **The Pantera tradition is pure slander invented by enemies.** Tabor argues the early references "are intended to identify not to malign," and that early Christians took the tradition seriously enough to respond to it.
- **The Bingerbrück gravestone is Jesus's father's tomb.** Tabor explicitly says this "cannot be proven" without DNA from identifiable remains.
- **Joseph was Jesus's father.** Tabor argues that the best evidence points away from this.
