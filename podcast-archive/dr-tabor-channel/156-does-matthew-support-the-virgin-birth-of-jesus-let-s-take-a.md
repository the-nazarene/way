## Summary

The talk asks whether the Gospel of Matthew actually teaches the virgin birth of Jesus, a question Tabor says seems obvious because Matthew quotes Isaiah 7:14. He argues that a close reading of Matthew's Greek does not support that reading. In his account, the core of Matthew's birth narrative is that Joseph, a "just man," learns in a dream that the already pregnant Mary should become his wife, so that the child is legally his and his name is Jesus son of Joseph. Tabor supports this with three main lines of evidence: the genealogy's inclusion of four women with irregular sexual reputations (Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and Bathsheba), his claim that Isaiah 7:14 concerns a young woman in Isaiah's own time and not a prediction about Jesus, and the statement that Joseph "knew her not until" the birth, which he reads as implying later marital intercourse. He also cites Mark 6:3 for Mary's other children and contrasts Matthew with the later Proto-evangelium of James, which he treats as the source of the idea of Mary's perpetual virginity.

Tabor closes by saying that "virgin conception" may be a more accurate label than "virgin birth" for Matthew, and that the passage is meant to make readers cautious before judging Mary. He says he does not know what Matthew himself thought about the pregnancy's origin. He frames the talk as the first in a series of short responses to viewer questions and promises a later video on the birth account in Luke, along with a link to his earlier video on the unnamed father of Jesus.

## Key Teachings

### The genealogy's four women as a signal to the reader
Tabor reads the genealogy in Matthew 1:1-17 as more than a royal list. Its only women named are Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and Bathsheba, and he says that in a Jewish genealogy women are not normally mentioned. He describes each as having a questionable sexual history: Tamar, who disguises herself and sleeps with Judah; Rahab, a prostitute in Joshua who helps the Israelites; Ruth, whom he says "basically... seduces Boaz"; and Bathsheba, whose adultery with David is followed by murder. He reads this as Matthew preparing readers to hesitate before judging the circumstances of Mary's pregnancy.

### Joseph as legal father, not biological father
Tabor argues that Matthew's story is "actually a story about Joseph the legal father of Jesus," who marries a pregnant Mary and so takes the child as his own. He says Matthew is "pretty open" that Jesus is known as the son of Joseph, citing Matthew 13:55 ("not the son of Joseph the tecton"), and he notes that Joseph's own dream instructs him to "take Mary your woman." Tabor stresses that this solves the question of shame and public condemnation under Torah but not the biological question of Jesus's lineage. He also draws a parallel with Genesis, where Isaac is Abraham's biological son yet the birth is attributed to divine agency.

### The Greek phrase "of the Holy Spirit" and the Abraham-Sarah parallel
Tabor says the Greek construction in Matthew 1:18 and 1:20 can mean "through the agency of the Holy Spirit" without implying that the Spirit fathered the child. He thinks Luke more clearly points to a virginal conception, but he says even Luke's wording could be read more loosely. He uses the Genesis story of Abraham and Sarah, where a messenger promises a son, to show how the same kind of phrasing can work without literal divine paternity.

### Isaiah 7:14 concerns a young woman, not the Virgin birth
Tabor says Matthew 1:23 introduces its quotation of Isaiah 7:14 with "all this took place to fulfill," and that this phrasing means "this is like that," not a prediction fulfilled. He argues that the Hebrew *almah* means a young woman presumed to be a virgin, and that the Greek *parthenos* carries the same implication in context. He says that in Isaiah 7 the child is a sign for Isaiah's own time, and that in Matthew the child is "Mary's Child," so the verse is about "a young virtuous woman having a child," not a prediction of the virgin birth.

### Mary's other children and the Gospel of Mark
Tabor contends that Matthew's "he knew her not until she had brought forth her firstborn son" implies that Joseph later had marital relations with Mary. He cites Mark 6:3, which names Jesus's brothers as sons of Mary, and estimates she had "at least seven children, probably eight." He says the idea of perpetual virginity, along with Joseph's celibacy and the claim that the "brothers and sisters" were Joseph's children from a prior marriage, develops later in the second through fourth centuries and is not an early biblical idea.

### The Proto-evangelium of James and later divinizing of Mary
Tabor treats the Proto-evangelium of James as a later text, from roughly the second century by his account, in which Joseph is an elderly widower with children, Mary is born to Joachim and Anna after a barren marriage, and the birth occurs in a cave with a light and a midwife named Salome. Tabor describes Salome's examination of Mary and her burned and then healed hand as a bizarre story, and he calls it an example of "divinizing these human beings." He says later texts go further, claiming Mary did not defecate or urinate, and he links these developments to the later doctrine of Mary's Assumption. He explicitly separates the Immaculate Conception, which he says concerns original sin, from the virgin birth.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Matthew clearly teaches the virgin birth.** Tabor argues that Matthew's focus is Joseph taking a pregnant Mary as his wife so the child is legally his, and that the phrase "through the agency of the Holy Spirit" does not clearly state a virginal conception.
- **Isaiah 7:14 predicts the virgin birth of Jesus.** Tabor says the verse concerns a young woman and a sign in Isaiah's own time, and that Matthew's "this is like that" framing does not mean a prediction fulfilled.
- **Mary remained a virgin after Jesus's birth.** Tabor argues that Matthew implies later relations with Joseph, and that Mark 6:3 names Mary's other children; he calls perpetual virginity a later development.
- **The virgin birth and the Immaculate Conception are the same idea.** Tabor says the Immaculate Conception concerns original sin and is a different doctrine from the virgin birth.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Jesus family:** Tabor's central point is that Joseph is Jesus's legal father and that Mary bore other children, which bears on the question of Jesus's family and Mary's later life.
- **Gospels and canon:** Tabor argues that Matthew, Mark, and Luke should be read separately, "let each one say what it wants to say," and that the Proto-evangelium of James is a later text whose ideas developed after the canonical Gospels.
- **Who Jesus was (Messianic lineage):** The genealogy's Davidic emphasis, along with Isaiah 11 and Jeremiah 23, is Tabor's account of why Matthew presents Jesus as qualified to be called the Christ.
