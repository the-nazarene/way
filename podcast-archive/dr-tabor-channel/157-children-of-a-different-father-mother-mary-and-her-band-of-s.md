## Summary

Tabor asks who Jesus's brothers and sisters were, whose children they were, and why later Christian tradition made them hard to see. He argues that the New Testament and early sources present them as real siblings, and that the later doctrine of Mary's perpetual virginity came after the early period. He reads Mark's list of brothers and women at the cross, John 19:25's "Mary wife of Clopas," and Joseph's disappearance from the narratives as evidence that Mary had children by more than one man. His main proposal is that Joseph died childless and that Mary was then married to Joseph's brother, Clopas, under the levirate custom. Tabor presents this as a reconstruction from scanty records, not a proven account, and says so in the video's own description.

The talk ends with Tabor's summary: Joseph was not Jesus's father, the four brothers and two sisters were all Mary's children by a different father (Joseph or Clopas), and Jesus by about age 30 was functioning as head of the household. He then says he will turn to Jesus's own life, but the final caption lines are only music and applause.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The perpetual virginity of Mary is a later doctrine
Tabor says the teaching that Mary never had children other than Jesus and never had sexual relations "is simply not found in the New Testament and is not part of the earliest Christian creeds." He dates the first explicit mention to 374 A.D., in the writings of Epiphanius, and says most earlier Christian writers "took for granted that the brothers and sisters of Jesus were the Natural Born children of Joseph and Mary." He links the doctrine to a view he attributes to Paul, that the earthly and sexual were denigrated in favor of the heavenly, with celibacy as the higher way. He describes this as a cultural shift that Mary was "totally removed from her first century Jewish culture and context" to accommodate.

### 2. The church's two explanations of "brothers"
By the late fourth century, Tabor says, the church gave two alternatives. In the West, "brothers" was read as a general term for cousins, which he says became the standard explanation, advocated by Roman Catholics. In the East, the brothers were said to be Joseph's sons from a previous marriage. Tabor says these explanations were theologically motivated and that restoring Mary's Jewish name (Miriam or Maria) and her first-century village setting frees the historical question.

### 3. Joseph's disappearance and the levirate marriage
Tabor argues Joseph likely died early: he appears in the birth stories and is called "son of Joseph" a few times, but "never appears in any narratives" afterward, not at the crucifixion, and not among the followers in Jerusalem. If Joseph died childless, Tabor says, the Torah or law of Moses required his oldest unmarried brother to marry the widow and raise a child in the dead man's name, a practice he calls a leverage marriage, "yaboom in Hebrew." He argues that Mary bearing children through more than one man would have been "normal" and "honorable" in that setting, and cites Tamar and Ruth in Matthew's genealogy as widows in such marriages. He adds that the gospel writers' theological aims mean they may not spell this out.

### 4. Identifying "the other Mary" in Mark and John
Tabor sets up a list of women at the cross and tomb. Mark names Mary Magdalene, "Mary the mother of James the younger and of Joseph," and Salome. Tabor thinks Salome may have been Jesus's sister. He asks whether Mark's "Mary the mother of James and Joseph" is Jesus's mother. John's list gives "Jesus's mother Mary, his mother's Sister Mary the wife of clofus and Mary Magdalene," with Salome dropped. Tabor says three women all named Mary, with two of them sisters, is improbable, and that the same name for sons "born in the same Order James Joseph and Simon" would be strange.

### 5. Clopas as Joseph's brother, and a proposed decrypting of John
Tabor says the church's settled solution is that Clopas's wife was a sister-in-law of Mary. He finds this implausible and proposes that Mark's Mary was Jesus's mother, and that John "or its later editors" created a third Mary to hide the fact that Mary married Joseph's brother. He offers a rewritten John reading: "standing by the cross of Jesus were his mother Mary wife of clothus and Mary Magdalene." He notes that the name Clopas "comes from the Hebrew and means to change or to replace," so it may be a nickname for the brother who replaced the childless Joseph. Clopas is mentioned only once in the New Testament, in John 19:25. Tabor says that if Joseph and Clopas were much older than Mary, neither was likely alive when Jesus was an adult.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mary was a perpetual virgin from the start, and the early church always believed it.** Tabor says the idea is absent from the New Testament and earliest creeds, first appears explicitly in 374 A.D. (Epiphanius), and that earlier writers assumed Jesus's brothers were Mary's natural children.
- **"Brothers" must mean cousins (the Western church's view) or half-brothers from Joseph's earlier marriage (the Eastern view).** Tabor treats both as theologically driven explanations that a restored first-century reading makes unnecessary.
- **Mary bearing children by different men would be scandalous and unthinkable.** Tabor argues that levirate marriage was a required and honorable custom in that setting, and that the "outrageous" reaction comes from modern and later theological standards.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor ties later attitudes to sexuality and the body to Paul's dualism and advocacy of celibacy, and suggests Luke was an advocate of Paul who downplayed Jesus's family. He does not cite Paul's texts in this talk.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor says Mary and Jesus's brothers were part of the Jerusalem group after the death of Jesus, and suggests that the "beloved disciple" in John 19 is "most likely James," though he admits John does not name him. This is his speculation.
- **Torah:** The levirate custom is the central legal premise of his reconstruction.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor argues the gospels are "primarily theological accounts" written a generation or more after Jesus, that Luke de-emphasized the family, and that John or its editors may have added a third Mary. He presents these as possibilities.
