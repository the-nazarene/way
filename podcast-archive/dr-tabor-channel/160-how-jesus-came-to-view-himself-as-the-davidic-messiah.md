## Summary

Tabor asks how Jesus of Nazareth came to see himself as the Davidic messiah, and when and how he developed that self-understanding. He says the question is unanswerable with certainty ("we cannot be sure"), but argues Jesus "surely knew growing up that he and his brothers were male Heirs of the royal line of King David." ## Key Teachings

### Jesus as reader of Scripture who applied prophetic texts to himself
Tabor argues that Jesus "most likely began to read certain passages of the Hebrew scriptures and apply them directly to himself," and calls this "absolutely vital" for his developing messianic identity. Isaiah 49 is his main example: a chosen figure says "Yahweh called me before I was born," and later, when discouraged, is corrected by God, who says it is "too light a thing" to restore only Jacob's tribes, and promises to make him "a light to the Nations." Tabor says "dozens of the Psalms function in the same way," especially those that appear messianic. He does not give verse-level detail for these passages in the talk.

### The Dead Sea Scrolls' Teacher of Righteousness as precedent
Tabor cites the Thanksgiving Hymns, parts of which he says scholars believe were written by the Teacher of Righteousness. He describes the text as "in places" taking "the tone of an autobiography" and as showing the teacher forming his own messianic self-identity as prophet to his community. Tabor suggests that Jesus, given his Davidic lineage, "undergoing a very similar process" is easy to imagine. He concedes Jesus "was not the first" to do this kind of scriptural self-application.

### Scripture-driven versus scripture-imposed
Tabor frames the scholarly debate as a "protracted chicken or egg debate": was Jesus driven by texts, or were texts retrofitted to show he fulfilled scripture "in all but a few cases"? He sides with the first view, arguing the scriptures "gave him inner assurance and strength of conviction" and "provided a type of road map." He does not engage specific scholars by name in this passage.

### The Psalms of Solomon and the expected Davidic king
Tabor says the "complete portrait" of the expected king is captured in "a popular first century BC text known as the Psalms of Solomon," which he summarizes as a six-point program: ruling Jerusalem from David's throne; purging Jerusalem and the land of foreign rulers; establishing a rule of righteousness; separating sinners from Israel; extending rule over the godless nations; and gathering the scattered tribes. He argues that "thousands of Jews" expected a davidic king who would accomplish these things with God's supernatural power, and that "all our evidence indicates that Jesus was such a Jew."

### Kingdom of God as literal rule, not an otherworldly concept
Tabor argues the Kingdom "is not a sentiment or ethereal concept": "Kingdom" in Hebrew and Greek "means government or rule," as in "herod's Kingdom or of Roman rule." He reads the Lord's Prayer as defining the Kingdom as God's will done on earth as in heaven, "the idea of the rule of Heaven breaking into human history and manifesting itself on Earth," which he says was understood "in a literal way," meaning "nothing less than a revolution." He ties this to Jesus's message after John's arrest, "the time is fulfilled the kingdom of God is at hand."

### Jesus as political revolutionary, opposed to armed rebellion
Tabor states: "Jesus was a political revolutionary who expected nothing less than the violent overthrow of the kingdoms of the world," but "did not think it would come about by collecting arms and Gathering Rebel bands of troops" as some contemporaries attempted. He argues the first step was defeating Satan, "the behind the scenes ruler of the age," and that Jesus linked his exorcisms to binding Satan and destroying his kingdom, citing a Q-source saying about the "Finger of God." He says the campaign drew large crowds and opposition from Pharisees and agents of Herod.

### Context: John the Baptist's death and Pilate's Judea
Tabor sketches the setting from the Gospel of Mark and Josephus. John denounced Herod's marriage to Herodias; Josephus, per Tabor, does not mention this incident specifically but says Herod feared John's influence and a rebellion. John was taken to Machaerus, a remote fortress east of the Dead Sea. Tabor then describes Pilate's tenure from 26 CE (brutal rule, standards with Caesar's busts in Jerusalem, use of Temple money for an aqueduct, riots met with force) as the reason Jesus needed to retreat to Galilee. Tabor dates the crucial "making of a messiah" period to the spring of 28 CE and the preaching campaigns into early 29 CE.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Kingdom of God is a spiritual or heavenly concept.** Tabor argues the word means "government or rule," and that the prayer defines it as God's will done on earth, understood literally as a revolution in human history.
- **Prophetic language was interpreted loosely or ignored by practical Jews.** Tabor says "practically minded Jews could always interpret the language of their prophets in a less literal way," but that thousands expected the Davidic king to appear and accomplish the expected program literally.
- **Gospel writers invented Jesus's messianic identity from scripture after the fact.** Tabor argues Jesus was "much more likely" driven by the scriptures, rather than scripture being imposed on his life afterward.
