## Summary

Tabor asks what plan Jesus had for establishing his rule, and how the movement moved from preaching to crisis. He argues that Jesus set up a provisional government around an inner council of twelve men, each meant to sit on a throne over one of the twelve tribes of Israel. The video description adds that this plan rested on Jesus' reading of Daniel 2 and 7, but the captioned segment does not develop that reading.

Tabor's central evidence is the composition of the Twelve. He notes that the Synoptic lists group the apostles into three tiers of four, and that the last tier (James, Jude, Simon, Judas Iscariot) is anomalous. He argues that James, Jude and Simon are Jesus' brothers, and that Matthew/Levi may be a son of Alphaeus (Clopas) and so a fourth brother, Joseph, honored by a second name. He argues that Jesus expected imminent cosmic signs that would free John, but that he had no plan for armed action. ## Key Teachings

### The Twelve as a provisional government
Tabor says Jesus "moved to set up a provisional government made up of an inner cabinet or Council of twelve," and that the Greek word translated "apostle" means delegate or envoy. He argues the Twelve were meant to rule the twelve tribes once the government was fully operational. He sees the three-tier listing in Matthew, Mark and Luke as revealing: the first tier is Simon Peter, Andrew, James and John; the second is Philip, Bartholomew, Matthew and Thomas; the third is James, Jude, Simon and Judas Iscariot. He notes that Judas, the betrayer, would be expected at the bottom, which makes the presence of the other three in the last tier more striking.

### Three of the Twelve as brothers of Jesus
Tabor argues that James, Jude and Simon in the third tier are Jesus' brothers. He cites Luke 6:16, where Judas is called "of James," which he takes to mean "brother," and notes that Jude calls himself "brother of James" in his letter. James is called "son of Alphaeus" in Luke's list. Tabor equates Alphaeus with Clopas, the brother of Joseph and possibly Mary's second husband. Simon son of Clopas is the one who takes over the movement after James is killed. Tabor states, "I am convinced that these are the three brothers of Jesus."

### Matthew/Levi and the missing Joseph
Tabor asks why there is no Joseph among the Twelve if Jesus chose three of his brothers. He suggests Matthew is the fourth brother, identified as "Levi son of Alphaeus" in Mark 2:14, and that the name Matthew (or Levi) may have been given in honor of Joseph. He adds that this kind of paired naming was common in priestly families, and that Mary also had such a connection. He calls this "one of the best kept secrets in the entire New Testament."

### James as heir of the movement
Tabor says that when Jesus died he "turned his movement over to his brother James the eldest" and placed his mother in James's care. He identifies James with the beloved disciple of the Gospel of John. The captions give no argument for the John identification beyond this assertion.

### The mission of the six pairs
Tabor dates the mission to "sometime in the spring of 29 A.D. before Passover." Jesus divided the Twelve into six two-man groups and sent them out with only a staff, sandals and a tunic. Their message was "repent for the kingdom of God has drawn near," accompanied by healing and exorcism. Tabor calls this "a spiritual offensive" meant to usher in the kingdom, and says that "what was most expected did not come and what was least anticipated happened": Herod Antipas acted, and the movement "reeled back in shock."

### Good news to the poor and the reversal of society
Tabor quotes Luke's version of the Q source: blessings on the poor, the hungry, those who weep and those who are hated, followed by woes on the rich, the full, the laughing and those spoken well of. He reads this as a prediction of "the radical apocalyptic reversal of society from top to bottom," in which the powerful fall and the oppressed are raised. He links the casting out of demons to the messiah's task of proclaiming liberty to captives, and says John, imprisoned at Machaerus, may have expected his own release.

### Isaiah, the Dead Sea text and John's question
Tabor says Jesus declared himself "the one anointed of the spirit" who would fulfill Isaiah's prophecy: preaching good news to the oppressed, binding up the brokenhearted, proclaiming liberty to captives and "the year of yahweh's favor." He calls a Dead Sea scroll fragment, which scholars call the "Messianic apocalypse," a text that predicted the Messiah would heal the sick, raise the dead and bring good news to the poor. When John asked whether Jesus was "the one," Jesus replied with words Tabor says echo that scroll. Tabor argues Jesus expected imminent cosmic events, such as earthquakes or heavenly signs, that would lead to John's release. He says he sees "no indication that Jesus had plans to gather arms" to free John by force.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The gospels' silence about James, Jude and Simon is incidental.** Tabor argues this silence is "surely purposeful," because the brothers' roles were being written out of the record.
- **Jesus' family were outsiders to the movement.** Tabor says Jesus' brothers "were among the so-called 12 Apostles," were present at the Last Supper, and that James took over the movement.
- **Jesus' plan was purely spiritual, or else an armed uprising.** Tabor argues that Jesus expected cosmic signs and a divine overthrow, and sees no evidence he planned to use force to free John.
