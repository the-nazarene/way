## Summary

Tabor asks how Jesus responded to the execution of John the Baptist and when he came to see his own coming suffering, and that of his followers, in terms of the Suffering Servant of Isaiah 53. He tells the story from the death of John at Machaerus, which he says Herod Antipas ordered after Salome's dance (Mark's account), through Jesus's withdrawal to Bethsaida and then to Caesarea Philippi in the late fall of 29 A.D. Tabor argues that Jesus used the prophets, especially Zechariah's "strike the shepherd" passage, to make sense of John's death. He proposes that Jesus began to see his own suffering as part of God's required sequence before the kingdom of God could fully arrive.

His main evidence is textual and comparative. He cites Mark 9:13, where Jesus says Elijah has come and was treated as "it is written," and Isaiah 53's account of a righteous servant "cut off from the land of the living." He also compares the Damascus Document from the Dead Sea Scrolls, which he says describes the death of a "teacher of righteousness" and a group that turned to the same Zechariah passage to explain it. Tabor adds the 1986 discovery of a first-century Galilean fishing boat and the excavated site of Machaerus as physical context for the narrative. He presents these as reconstruction, using words such as "I am convinced" and "if I am right."

The talk ends with Tabor's statement that Jesus "set his face to go to Jerusalem," which he attributes to Luke. ## Key Teachings

### 1. John's death as a shock that reshaped Jesus's expectations

Tabor describes John's execution as "the most disappointing and shocking event in Jesus's entire life." He says that after it, "no one had been thinking about messiah's suffering and dying at that point," and that the return of the twelve from their preaching campaign "dissolved into despair." He reads Mark as placing the execution at a banquet at Machaerus, and he cites Josephus for a different motive: Herod feared John's popularity could lead to rebellion. Tabor uses both accounts to set the historical stakes of the retreat that follows.

### 2. Zechariah's "strike the shepherd" as the key to John's death

Tabor argues that Jesus turned to the latter chapters of Zechariah to understand John's fate. He quotes the line "strike the Shepherd that the Sheep may be scattered" and identifies John as the shepherd who had gathered the sheep, linking this to Jesus's mission to "the lost sheep of the House of Israel." He notes that this striking was to happen "just before the end of the age," which he says rules out a figure from the past. Tabor then points to the previous chapter, where a figure "from the House of David" is "wounded or pierced" and mourned by relatives, and he says this pierced figure is specified through the line of Nathan and the house of Levi. He suggests Jesus saw in this a link to his own mixed Davidic and Levitical ancestry, as Tabor describes his mother's lineage. His statement is: "if the shepherd was to be struck by a sword then the davidic Messiah was also to be wounded by piercing."

### 3. Caesarea Philippi as a turning point and the teaching of suffering before glory

Tabor says the group moved north to Banias (Caesarea Philippi), which was outside Herod's territory and considered a pagan cultic area, and that Jesus chose it for safety and for private teaching through the winter before Passover. At Caesarea Philippi, Tabor says, Jesus told the disciples that some would see the kingdom of God come "with power," and that followers would have to "take up a cross." He argues that Jesus "saw his sufferings ahead as a perfecting that God required of both him and his followers" so that the kingdom could fully appear. Tabor quotes the saying from the last night ("you who Stand By Me in My testing I confer on you a kingdom") and concludes that "suffering had to come before exaltation and Glory." He does not give a verse for that saying.

### 4. Isaiah 53 and the expectation of rescue

Tabor's most direct claim about Jesus's inner expectation is that "I am convinced that he expected that he would be saved from death." He supports this by noting that in "text after text that deals with the suffering of God's righteous servants they are always rescued from the gates of death at the last moment," with Isaiah 53 as the exception. He suggests Jesus may have found in Isaiah 53 an explanation for John's fate, and that if Jesus believed he had been sent to raise the dead, he might have expected to "release John from the prison of death once he completed his own trial of suffering." Tabor frames this as conjecture, saying "of course we don't know the inner thoughts and struggles of Jesus."

### 5. The Damascus Document as a parallel to Jesus's situation

Tabor describes the Damascus Document as recording a "more than a hundred years earlier" event in which the group lost "the one they called the true teacher" or "teacher of righteousness" in "some unexpected way," then turned to the prophets and found the same Zechariah passage ("strike the shepherd and the Sheep will be scattered"). He presents this as an "eerily similar course of events" to that of Jesus and John. He does not quote the Damascus Document directly in the transcript, and he does not give a column or section number.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Belief that Jesus's suffering and death were understood by everyone in advance.** Tabor argues that after John's death "no one had been thinking about messiah's suffering and dying at that point," and that Jesus's followers "they'd never dreamed he was to be killed" in John's case.
- **Belief that Matthew and Luke preserve the full tradition about John.** Tabor says both leave out Mark 9:13's statement that John's death had been foretold, which he describes as "one more example of their tendency to downplay the role of John the baptizer."
- **Belief that Jesus accepted death as inevitable without hope of rescue.** Tabor argues that Jesus expected rescue, based on the pattern of righteous sufferers in other texts, and that Isaiah 53 was the exception that led him to rethink his own fate.
