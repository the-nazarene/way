## Summary

Tabor's question is where Jesus and his followers spent the last winter of his life, the period between late 29 CE and the spring of 30 CE, and why that matters for the movement's later history. He argues that Jesus withdrew across the Jordan to a hideout in the Wadi Kareth (Cherith), a ravine opposite Tell Salim and a few miles south of the Decapolis city of Pella. His evidence is a combination of John's Gospel (the location near Aenon and Salim, the winter visit to Jerusalem at Hanukkah), Luke's account of the seventy delegates and the crowds, the Hosea "third day" passage, and his own 1991 map study and site visit, where he reports seeing first-century Roman pottery fragments on cave floors.

Tabor's central inference is that the Nazarene community's flight to Pella in 68 CE, under Jesus's brother Simon, was not random. He suggests the group chose the region because of its memory of Jesus's winter there, and because of the Elijah associations of the Wadi. He presents this as a plausible reconstruction ("it is entirely possible"), not a settled finding.

The talk ends with Tabor describing Jesus's December 29 CE visit to Jerusalem during Hanukkah, where authorities pressed him on whether he was the Messiah, and his escape back across the Jordan. Tabor closes by saying Jesus had "never publicly said he was Israel's rightful King" and that he was waiting for a time he had planned. The talk is a single narration by Tabor; no guest or host speaks.

## Key Teachings

### The seventy delegates and the coming overthrow of Satan
Tabor describes Jesus choosing seventy delegates, sending them in two-person teams ahead of him with a commission to heal, cast out demons, and proclaim that the kingdom of God had come near. He says Luke records their joy at using Jesus's name, and Jesus's reply that he saw Satan fall from heaven. Tabor reads this as a vision of the impending overthrow of Satan's kingdoms, which Jesus took as confirmation that the kingdom would soon be manifest. He says the teams probably covered Galilee and possibly Judea, though he admits "we don't know how widely these teams traveled."

### The Hosea "third day" and Israel, not Jesus
Tabor argues that Jesus's cryptic line about finishing his work "on the third day" alludes to Hosea's words that Israel, struck down, will be revived after two days and raised up on the third. He reads a prophetic day as a year, so the third day points to spring 30 CE, roughly two years after Jesus began his healing ministry in spring 28 CE. He says the later association of this phrase with Jesus's own resurrection was a confusion, since "the text of Hosea is clearly talking about the nation not the Messiah."

### Why Jesus withdrew east in late 29 CE
Tabor says that in late fall 29 CE Jesus learned his enemies in Jerusalem were seeking to arrest and kill him, and that Herod Antipas also wanted him dead. He judged the timing wrong to confront either authority and moved into the Decapolis hill country of Gilead, which Tabor describes as outside the borders of Galilee and Judea and governed by a loose federation of ten Greco-Roman city-states. He notes uncertainty about whether Jesus took the whole group of over 100 or only his inner circle.

### The hideout: John's Gospel and the Wadi Kareth
Tabor's geographic argument rests on John 3:23 (John baptizing at Aenon near Salim, "because Waters were abundant there") and John 10:40 (Jesus went away again across the Jordan and remained there). He identifies Aenon with Tell Salim, where springs supply Israeli fish ponds today. Studying a map in 1991, he noticed that directly across the Jordan from Tell Salim is a deep ravine that in biblical times was the Brook Kareth where Elijah hid. He describes the site as hard to enter from the east, with waterfalls and rocks, but opening after a short distance into a cliff-enclosed area with many caves, "wholly protected from outside excess." He reports first-century Roman-period pottery fragments on the cave floors.

### The Pella connection and Simon's leadership
Tabor's historical argument is that when Jesus's followers fled Jerusalem in 68 CE, before the Roman siege, they went to the Pella region. He says James the brother of Jesus had already been killed by then and that Simon, another brother of Jesus, led the Nazarene community. He proposes that the Wadi Kareth would have seemed a safe place to the group, partly because of Elijah and partly because Simon may have chosen Pella in light of the time he had spent there with Jesus in winter 29 CE. This is the talk's most speculative step, and Tabor frames it as a possibility ("it is entirely possible").

### The Hanukkah confrontation in Jerusalem
Tabor dates a clandestine trip to Jerusalem to mid-December 29 CE, using John's notice that it was winter at Hanukkah. He places the scene in Herod's Temple, in the area called the Portico of Solomon, where authorities demanded that Jesus state plainly whether he was the Messiah. He argues this was a trap, since to claim messiahship was to claim kingship, and that "the Romans had zero tolerance for Messiahs," who were seen as seditious rather than harmless. He cites John's account of Jesus's answer ("you do not belong to my sheep"), the attempt to stone him, and his escape back to the hideout. He concludes that Jesus had not yet publicly claimed kingship and "for Jesus timing was everything."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That Jesus's "third day" saying predicts his own resurrection.** Tabor argues the phrase comes from Hosea 6:2 and refers to the revival of Israel as a nation, with a prophetic day standing for a year. The link to Jesus's resurrection, in his view, was a later confusion.
- **That Jesus openly claimed to be king from the start.** Tabor says that before the Hanukkah confrontation, Jesus "had so far never publicly said he was Israel's rightful King," and that he repeatedly told followers "my time has not yet come."

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor states that James was already dead by 68 CE when the community fled Jerusalem, and that Simon led the Nazarenes. This is his premise for the Pella choice.
- **Nazarenes and Jesus's family:** Tabor says Jesus's mother, brothers and sisters likely lived in the Wadi during the final months, and that Simon's choice of Pella may have been shaped by that winter. He presents this as a plausible inference, not documented fact.
- **The Kingdom:** The seventy delegates' mission, and Jesus's belief that the kingdom of God was about to be manifest, are central to Tabor's account of Jesus's aims in his final year.
- **Apocalyptic expectation:** Tabor reads Jesus's vision of Satan's fall, and the reference to the Son of Man coming in the clouds, as signs of a near-term expectation that drove his final confrontation.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor's account of the Hanukkah question and Jesus's evasive answer is his main evidence that Jesus had not yet made a public messianic claim, and that Jesus's timing was deliberate.
- **Resurrection:** Tabor's reading of the Hosea third-day passage is his argument that the resurrection association came later.
