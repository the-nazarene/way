## Summary

The talk is an interview on Truth2U Radio with Jono and Jason about James D. Tabor's book *Paul and Jesus: How the Apostle Transformed Christianity*. The hosts' questions drive the conversation, but Tabor carries most of the content. The central question is how much of what later became Christianity traces back to Paul rather than to Jesus and his brother James, and whether taking James seriously would move a reader "out of" Christianity as it is now understood.

Tabor opens with the book's title sentence, "Paul never met Jesus." He says this is deliberately provocative: it corrects readers who assume Paul was part of Jesus' circle, and it points out that Paul's claimed contact with Jesus rests on visionary experience of the risen, glorified Lord. Tabor reads the Pauline evidence mainly from the seven letters he treats as undisputed (1 Corinthians, Romans, Galatians, Philippians, Philemon, and the other two generally accepted letters), and he sets Ephesians, Colossians and the Pastorals aside as a matter of method. He argues that reading the New Testament in its chronological order, with Paul first, shows that the theology of the Gospels was often shaped by Paul's framework, and that Mark probably came first. He describes the Epistle of James as containing little that is recognizably Christian, and places James's ethics alongside Jewish material such as Pirkei Avot and the teachings of Hillel. He contrasts Paul's apocalyptic, dualistic outlook with a Hebrew Bible view of a real, this-worldly creation, and he says Paul's proposals for Gentiles, and possibly for Jews, go beyond what the Torah and prophets could support. He declines to judge Paul's visions as valid or invalid, saying he records what Paul claims. The talk ends with a promise to return to Galatians and the Peter and James dispute over table fellowship, with the substantive answer deferred.

## Key Teachings

### "Paul never met Jesus" and the distinction between the earthly Jesus and the exalted Christ
Tabor says he chose the opening line to provoke two groups of readers: those who assume Paul belongs to the apostolic circle, and those who already know but have vague chronologies. He cites Acts' criterion for replacing Judas, a companion "from the baptism of John" through the resurrection, which Paul does not meet. He grounds Paul's contact with Jesus in Paul's own words: "have I not seen the Lord" (1 Corinthians 9) and the appearance of the Lord to him (1 Corinthians 15). For Paul this is a glorified, exalted figure, "a new Adam," not the Jesus who "walked the Earth." Tabor says the book deliberately separates the Jesus of the Gospels from the Christ of Paul's letters.

### Reading the New Testament backwards: Paul first, then the Gospels
Tabor says the order of the canon (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John, Acts, then Paul) obscures chronology, and he prefers reading Paul's letters first. He argues that the faith Paul formulated, "from the Lord's Supper to baptism to faith in Christ salvation in Christ", is later read back into the Gospels. He says Matthew is "a version of Jesus that's been filtered" through a Pauline lens decades after Paul, and that Gospel theology "can be traced earlier to Paul than to Jesus," while some sayings do go back to Jesus. He says Mark was probably written first, which he presents as a strong but not universal scholarly consensus. He notes that the Q source, a collection of Jesus' sayings, predates the Gospels and remains "very much coming from the rabbi Jesus."

### The Epistle of James and its Jewish character
Tabor says that in some early collections James stands before Paul's letters, and that Luther's dismissal of James as "an Epistle of straw" reflects a theological disagreement. He points out that James mentions Jesus only twice, and that its ethics about faith, the tongue and morality would fit Jewish teaching of the period. He compares it with Pirkei Avot (rendered "pure Keo vote" in the captions) and the Mishnah, and says that "you could not identify it as Christian in any way." He suggests that some precepts shared with Hillel might have passed into the Jewish tradition without Paul. He cites the conference volume *Hillel and Jesus*, edited by James Charlesworth, as a useful comparison.

### Pseudepigraphy and the seven authentic letters
Tabor explains that the pseudepigrapha are Jewish writings, mostly pre-Jesus, such as 1 Enoch, Jubilees and the Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs, collected by Charlesworth. He distinguishes this collection from the process of pseudepigraphy, writing under another's name, which he says is in effect forgery. He says the Pauline school may have extended his letters, and that the Pastorals and Acts are later. His method is to build the book on the seven undisputed letters, which he calls "the seven earliest materials," and to treat later letters as separate questions.

### Paul's apocalyptic, dualistic outlook versus the Hebrew view of creation
Tabor says Paul looks "to the things above," with a dualism he compares to Plato's cave, and expects the end soon, possibly within months or a year. He cites 1 Corinthians 7 ("the form of this world is passing away") and the instruction not to marry or start businesses. He sets against this the Hebrew view, anchored in Genesis 1's creation "good, good, very good," in which the Torah is "not in heaven" (Deuteronomy). He says Isaiah's talk of new heavens and earth (Isaiah 66) is delivered to a people facing the Assyrian threat, so it is still rooted in this world. He concludes that no futurist scheme matching Paul can be built from the Torah or Prophets without heavy allegory.

### Paul and the Torah: Gentiles, Jews and a "Torah of Christ"
Tabor says Paul clearly does not want Gentiles circumcised or converted to Judaism. The "controversial part" of his argument, he says, is whether Paul also taught that Jews need not keep the Torah to please God. He suggests Paul proposed a new covenant, a new Torah, a new people and a new land ("Jerusalem above"), transforming each major element of Judaism in ways he does not think can be traced back to Jesus. He notes that Matthew has Jesus limiting his mission to the lost sheep of Israel and then ends with a Great Commission to the nations. He draws on Jason Staples's dissertation (under Bart Ehrman, UNC Chapel Hill), which argues that Paul in Romans 9-11 has in mind the scattered Israelite tribes, not only Gentiles. He links this to James's address "to the 12 tribes" and 1 Peter's language of the dispersion. He holds that the Galatians dispute, where James is said to press Peter and Paul over shared meals, shows that Paul's answer was not settled, and he defers the full discussion.

### Methodology: describing visions without judging them
Tabor says that, as a historian of religions, he records what Paul claims about his visions and does not rule on their validity, which he says applies equally to Joseph Smith, Muhammad or Joan of Arc. He suggests that Paul's time in Arabia may have been a Sinai-type experience, which he says he argues for in the book. He reports that some readers feel the book reinforces Paul, and others feel it does not, and he presents this as evidence that the book is not "Paul bashing."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Paul was part of Jesus' original circle or an apostle in the Acts sense.** Tabor says Paul never walked with Jesus, and fails Acts' own criterion for apostleship, so his authority rests on visionary experience.
- **Paul's letters are the earliest and most reliable guide to Jesus.** Tabor argues Paul's letters are the earliest documents, but that Paul's theology then shaped the Gospels, so Gospel material should be read with that in mind.
- **James is clearly a Christian writer and the Epistle reads as Christian doctrine.** Tabor says James "would not pass any of the creedal confessional tests," and that its ethics read as Jewish teaching of the period.
- **Judaism and the Torah are inherently otherworldly in the way Paul is.** Tabor says the Torah, Prophets and Psalms are rooted in this world, and that Paul's heavenly, imminent-end framework is not found in the Hebrew Bible.
