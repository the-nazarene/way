## Key Teachings

### The Last Supper was a farewell meal, not a Passover
Tabor argues that the tradition that Jesus ate a Passover meal is not supported by the earliest accounts. He points to Paul's account ("the night he was betrayed") and to the synoptic accounts, noting there are no women present and no family gathering. He calls the meal "anticipatory": the bread and cup are taken "because you're going to do it in the Kingdom to come." He draws on a fragment from the Dead Sea Scrolls describing a Messianic banquet in which bread comes first and wine second, and on a passage in Luke about sitting on twelve thrones. He links the bread to haste ("the bread of haste," tying it to the Lot story in Genesis 19) and says he has a blog post on this topic. He also says the Didache describes a Eucharist in which bread celebrates the Kingdom and wine celebrates joy in it. He doubts that Jesus said "eat my body, drink my blood" in the words later recorded.

### Paul's resurrection body is a "new creation," not a resuscitated corpse
Tabor says Paul's view of resurrection differs from a corpse revival. He cites 1 Corinthians 15 (where Paul says "I don't know, God knows" about the kind of body), 2 Corinthians 5 (the body as a tent, the naked interim state, being "further clothed"), and Philippians 2 (the "glorious" body and Christ as a "life-giving spirit"). He uses the caterpillar and butterfly as an analogy, a transformation rather than a revived caterpillar. He says the "sightings" were visionary and that the one eyewitness account we have is Paul's, which describes a glorious, indescribable form. He also suggests that the Galilee "sightings" were probably the basis of the faith, with Jerusalem stories (Luke's Acts) later. He links group visions to phenomena such as Fatima, while stating he cannot know what happened.

### The empty tomb: an emergency burial, not Joseph's family tomb
Tabor accepts the basic narrative framework for Jesus' burial but argues that it was a temporary "tomb of convenience and emergency" near the crucifixion site on the Mount of Olives, because Passover was beginning and the body could not stay exposed. He reads John 19 ("because the Sabbath was near") as meaning the tomb was nearby and unused, not Joseph's. He says the body was likely reburied permanently later. He does not think the church of the Holy Sepulchre is the site of the burial. He discusses the possibility of tomb robbery and mentions that crucifixion nails were used as amulets, but calls robbery unlikely and reconstructs the most probable scenario as a delayed, respectful burial. He says he has a Dale Allison-inspired point: John 20:1-10 is the most authentic-sounding account, but verse 10 begins the legendary material.

### The Gospel of Peter and the "Galilee" memory
Tabor calls the ending of the Gospel of Peter his "favorite text on resurrection." He reads its details (weeping and mourning for seven days, returning to fishing) as an echo of a Galilee memory and as inconsistent with 40 days of appearances, which he says appears in Acts. He treats Mark's ending, the Gospel of Peter, and 1 Corinthians 15 as earlier and more consistent than the physical appearances in Luke and John. He notes Mark 16's reference to seeing Jesus "in the Galilee" and links the Transfiguration to the mountain setting.

### Judas and the betrayal as literary construction
Tabor thinks the Judas betrayal story was largely a later literary invention rather than history, though he does not rule out a real betrayal by someone close. He notes that the Greek word "paradidomi" means "handed over," not necessarily "betrayed," and that the Gospels' "betrayal" framing is read back from later tradition. He compares the Judas-for-silver story to Joseph sold by Judah in Genesis 37, and to the 30 pieces of silver in Zechariah. He also ties the "one who dips his bread" saying to the Dead Sea Scrolls' "man of the lie" (whom he calls the betrayer of the Teacher of Righteousness), and says this suggests a pattern of betrayal stories in Jewish sectarian literature. He also discusses the Joseph-in-prison dream-interpretation story (bread, wine, "three days," "remember me") as a possible literary source for the Eucharist and resurrection Motif, but says he has not thought it through.

### Hillel, the Pharisees, and the Torah
Tabor says the Pharisees have been caricatured in Christian tradition as rigid and hypocritical, when in fact leaders like Hillel made Torah practice possible through adjustments for human need (his example is redefining "house" for Sabbath purposes). He cites Hillel's "Golden Rule" saying and compares it to Mark 12, where a scribe is praised for understanding that loving God and neighbor outweighs burnt offerings. He reads this as Jesus suggesting the temple sacrifices are not essential, and links it to Jeremiah 6 and to a claim that Israel was not commanded sacrifice at the Exodus. He notes the "two threads" in the Hebrew Bible (priestly and anti-sacrificial), a theme from a video he and Lambert made.

### Paul, women, and 1 Timothy 2
In answer to a Super Chat about Artemis, Tabor says 1 Timothy 2:12-15 reflects a later, pseudo-Pauline (probably second-century, in his view) push against women teaching, possibly reacting to Gnostic groups that gave women leadership and praised celibacy. He argues that Genesis 2-3 provides the interpretive base for "childbearing," and that the phrase "labor" in Genesis 3 is the same word used for Adam tilling the soil, so it is not primarily about pain. He also says 1 Corinthians 11's hair passage reflects Greco-Roman display norms rather than a theology of Artemis versus Apollo. He points to earlier texts in which women have matriarchal or leadership roles (in the Torah, the Maccabees, and the New Testament) and argues that this strand existed in the Jesus movement.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Last Supper was a Passover meal.** Tabor argues the earliest accounts (Paul, the synoptics without later Passover details) describe a farewell meal, anticipatory of the Kingdom, with no family gathering.
- **Paul's resurrection body was a physical corpse coming back to life.** Tabor argues Paul describes a transformed "glorious" body, a new creation, and the interim state as a naked, unembodied existence.
- **The empty tomb proves resurrection, and Joseph of Arimathea's family tomb was used.** Tabor argues the burial was an emergency tomb near the crucifixion site, reburied later, and that an empty tomb does not by itself establish resurrection.
- **The Pharisees were rigid legalists.** Tabor says Hillel and the Pharisaic leadership adjusted Torah practice to human need, and that the Pharisee caricature comes from later Christian tradition.
- **Paul condemned modern same-sex relationships as such.** Tabor argues Romans 1 and 1 Corinthians 6 list multiple sexual sins, and that Paul's concern may have been abuse and power dynamics rather than orientation.
- **Christianity was small and insignificant in Paul's time.** Tabor disputes the view that early Jesus followers were numerically insignificant, citing Josephus, Tacitus, and the many Messianic movements of the period.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor treats Paul as the earliest account of the risen Jesus and the Last Supper, while arguing that later texts (1 Timothy, Luke's Acts, the Gospels) developed Paul's and Jesus' legacy in directions he questions.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor cites Josephus on James and John the Baptist as more prominent figures than Jesus in the period, and says Eisenman would view James as a comparable figure to the Baptist. Tabor names Luke's Jerusalem-centered Acts as a construction that conflicts with Galilee sightings.
- **Nazarenes, Ebionites, Essenes:** The Dead Sea Scrolls (Teacher of Righteousness, Messianic banquet, the "man of the lie") appear as comparisons to the Jesus movement; Tabor says the Jesus movement is not identical to Qumran but shares its piety.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet, nonviolence:** Tabor discusses Hillel's Torah adjustments, the Mark 12 scribe's praise and the critique of burnt offerings, and the "two threads" (priestly and anti-sacrificial) in the Hebrew Bible.
- **Kingdom on earth:** The Last Supper is a meal anticipating the Kingdom "to come," and the Didache's Eucharist celebrates the Kingdom.
- **Resurrection, afterlife, and hell:** A substantial share of the talk. Tabor covers Paul's body as a tent, the naked interim state, the gold-tablet instructions for the dead, and the Gospel of Peter's Galilee tradition.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor compares Jesus to other first-century Messianic claimants and says the Council of Twelve implied a "government in Exile."
- **How the Gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor notes that Acts and the Gospels developed later legendary material, that the Gospel of Peter and the Didache may preserve earlier traditions, and that 1 Enoch's use in Jude shows non-canonical texts were treated as authoritative by some early writers.
