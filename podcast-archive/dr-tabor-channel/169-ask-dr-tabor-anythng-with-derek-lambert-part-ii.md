## Summary

This is a free-ranging live question-and-answer session, the second part of a series, in which James Tabor answers questions from the chat and from host Derek Lambert's audience. Tabor's answers cluster around a few questions: when and where the resurrection appearances began, why the gospels differ, who Jesus was, who wrote the gospels, and what the Hebrew Bible and Hellenistic thought say about the afterlife. Tabor carries nearly all of the answers. Lambert moderates, reads questions, and adds short comments.

On the resurrection, Tabor argues that Mark's Galilee tradition (the young man's message that the disciples will see Jesus in Galilee) and the Jerusalem appearance traditions in Luke and John are two separate streams that do not fit together. He reads 1 Corinthians 15 as the earliest testimony and favors the Galilee beginnings, with Peter seeing Jesus first. He calls "resurrection" a misnomer and reads Paul's account as a transformation into a glorious state. On the gospels, he treats each as a response to a stage of the movement, with Mark as a kind of "anti-gospel" warning against glory before suffering, and Matthew as written later, when the delayed Parousia needed explanation. On authorship, he says the gospels are anonymous and that the traditional names were attached later.

Tabor ends with a plug for his blog, books, Patreon and the MythVision courses, and a closing remark that he is ready for a break from teaching after 43 years.

## Key Teachings

### Galilee versus Jerusalem: the timing of the resurrection appearances

Tabor says the question of why the disciples did not proclaim the resurrection right away is a matter of two traditions. He explains that Mark has the young man tell the women that the disciples will see Jesus in Galilee, and that Mark's ending leaves them silent. He notes the Gospel of Peter, which he says "kind of goes with Mark," with a week of weeping and then a return to fishing in Galilee. Luke, by contrast, has the disciples stay in Jerusalem, and John has Jerusalem appearances followed by a Galilee scene in chapter 21. Tabor says Luke realized the problem and "John comes along" to fix it. He concludes that the Awakening of faith happened in Galilee, with Peter seeing him first, followed by James, the Twelve, the 500, and finally Paul, whom he compares to himself in 1 Corinthians 9 and 15.

### The gospels as responses to stages of the Jesus movement

Asked whether each gospel addresses a specific group, Tabor says scholars disagree endlessly and that the more useful model is chronological "stages of the development of the Jesus movement." He presents Mark as a critique of people who put glory and rule before suffering, and ties this to Paul's "sufferings" language in Romans 8. He says Matthew comes from a later period, after Jerusalem's fall, and points to the three delay parables in Matthew 24 that are absent from Mark as evidence that the delay of the end had become a problem. He says Luke is "hardly Pauline at all" in the view of scholars who work on Luke, even though Paul is a hero in Luke-Acts. He argues all four gospels are heavily influenced by Paul, using the Last Supper as his example, and contrasts it with the Didache's Eucharist, which he describes as more like a Jewish blessing looking toward the kingdom.

### The "Ebionite" trajectory and early Christian streams

Tabor describes the James branch of the followers of Jesus as largely scattered by 70 CE, possibly moving to Transjordan, and later known as the Ebionites. He notes that no canonical gospel is Ebionite, but cites fragments of the Gospel of the Hebrews and the Gospel of the Ebionites. He draws on Helmut Koester's "trajectories" model (transcribed as "helmet kurster") and names a Johannine "gnosticizing" trajectory and a Markan "suffering" trajectory. He describes the second-century situation as "five six seven eight streams" of spitting cousins, not a single unified Christianity.

### Who Jesus was: a "bare bio" and a Jewish ethical teacher

Tabor says he would reconstruct the "bare bio" as: a Galilean peasant, possibly of the Davidic line, who became a disciple of John the Baptist, joined the movement, was opposed by Herod Antipas, gathered disciples, went to Jerusalem, confronted the temple authorities, and was crucified. He says the believers came to hold that he was exalted to the right hand of God, which he compares to apotheosis in Romulus and Greek heroes. On his teaching, Tabor relies on the Q "two sayings source," which he says has "a kind of thematic unity" (God looks at the inside, not the outside; accepting Gentiles and women). He ties these to Hillel, to Amos, Isaiah, Jeremiah and Micah ("what does God really want... Justice and righteousness"), and to the Mishnah tractate Pirkei Avot. He argues that this is "good spiritual Judaism," not new. Asked whether Jesus was divine, he says he personally thinks Jesus "is dead and not divine."

### Gospel authorship, genre, and the names of the evangelists

Tabor states that the gospels are anonymous and that the traditional names date to the second and third centuries, when the canon was formed. He notes that Mark never addresses the reader about the author's own witness, while John has "we know that the witness of this one is true" but still does not name the witness. He rejects the idea that John son of Zebedee wrote the gospel, and says the Beloved Disciple might be James, since Jesus entrusted his mother to a beloved disciple, which he says would fit James as the eldest surviving son in charge of the Jerusalem community. He reports a view that Mary went with John to Asia Minor, attributed to Stephen Schumacher (name as transcribed), and says this is "going too far afield." On genre, Tabor discusses Charles Talbert's book *What is a Gospel?*, ancient "Lives" (Alexander romances, Diogenes Laertius), and aretalogy. He suggests that each gospel author argues for his own presentation of the figure.

### The Hebrew Bible, Hellenism and the afterlife

Asked how Paul's theology relates to Genesis 2-4, Tabor says Paul's thought is shaped by Hellenistic and possibly Persian influence, and that "every form of Judaism" was Hellenized in the Roman period. He sets up a contrast: in Gilgamesh and in the Hebrew Bible the dead go to Sheol, and "You're a human being, it's not your place" to be in heaven. He argues that the Hebrew Bible has almost no afterlife, and says the Garden of Eden story is about humans not getting eternal life. He cites Daniel 12 and Isaiah 24:26 as exceptions and calls the late development of eternal life in the Hebrew Bible a "Hellenistic" change, possibly influenced by Persia.

### The Mark 16 ending and the RSV translation

In answer to a question about the RSV, Tabor says the translation put a note on Mark 16 but kept the longer ending. He argues that the so-called "long ending" is a composite of Matthew, Luke and John, and that the sections should not be added to Mark, comparing it to changing the ending of a film. He points readers to his blog post on the original ending of Mark. He also says Mark 16 in the King James Version is "in most manuscripts but late."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Gospel of Mark's ending includes the Great Commission and signs such as handling snakes.** Tabor says this is a late composite, not Mark's ending; the RSV's note acknowledges the question but keeps the text.
- **The gospels were written by the named disciples (Matthew, John son of Zebedee).** Tabor says the gospels are anonymous and the names were added in the second and third centuries; he rejects the John son of Zebedee attribution.
- **Jesus's teachings are unknown.** Tabor says there is a body of sayings from the Q source that are thematically unified and consistent with Second Temple Judaism.
- **Ancient parallels such as Romulus, Hercules or the "12 virgin-born gods" show Jesus was a copied myth.** Tabor says the surviving records for Jesus are much closer in time, and the claimed parallels often come from late sources; he asks for the sources for lists of such legends.
- **The Dome of the Rock is a Muslim political symbol from the beginning.** Tabor says it was a shrine or covering to protect the Rock, and that its political meaning developed in more modern times.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor says Paul's Hellenistic influence shaped the gospels and Jesus's teachings were largely Second Temple Jewish ethics; he argues the Last Supper tradition in the gospels reflects Paul's "I receive from the Lord."
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor says James took charge of the Jerusalem community, was scattered by 70, possibly moved to Transjordan, and later became known as the Ebionites; he argues James is the best candidate for the Beloved Disciple, since he received Jesus's mother.
- **The Nazarenes/Ebionites:** Tabor cites the Gospel of the Hebrews and the Gospel of the Ebionites as surviving fragments of this stream.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** Tabor's central claim is that the earliest resurrection stories are Galilean and that Paul's transformed-body language is the earliest testimony; he argues the Hebrew Bible has almost no afterlife, which he sees as a later Hellenistic development.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor says he would not claim more than the "bare bio," that Jesus was a human who died, and that the claims about his exaltation developed after his death.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor argues the gospels are anonymous, each shaped by a community, and that the names and the longer Mark ending came later.
