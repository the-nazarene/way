## Summary

This is the last session of James Tabor's university course on the Dead Sea Scrolls, recorded at the end of Spring 2022. James H. Charlesworth joins by Zoom, and most of the hour is a question-and-answer conversation. The implicit question is how far the Scrolls, especially the Community Rule, the Damascus Document and the Thanksgiving Hymns, help explain Jesus, and whether they show he was shaped by the Essenes.

Charlesworth carries the central argument. He proposes a set of criteria for testing sayings attributed to Jesus: whether the saying is probably authentic, whether it is unique to Jesus, whether the idea was already known, and whether the tradition can be traced back to Jesus and to the Essenes. He says Jesus was influenced by the Essenes "negatively and positively" but was not himself an Essene. Tabor argues more strongly that Jesus was influenced by the Essenes, and gives a parallel between Matthew 12:11 (rescuing an animal from a pit on the Sabbath) and a rule in the Damascus Document. He also argues that the Essenes fled Qumran after the Roman attack of about 67 and that some joined the Jerusalem community. Tabor makes the case that the Essenes and the Jesus movement were close neighbours in Jerusalem, and he describes the Jesus movement as "a form of Judaism." Both men also argue that the Shapira scroll deserves re-examination rather than dismissal as a forgery.

The session ends with a discussion of why the Scrolls were published so slowly, covering politics, access, damaged fragments and personal conduct. Tabor closes by thanking Charlesworth and promoting his bibliography.

## Key Teachings

### Criteria for testing Jesus sayings against the Scrolls
Charlesworth lays out a sequence of questions. First, is the saying probably from Jesus, as opposed to his followers? Second, is it unique to Jesus? If so, was the idea already well known in Judaism? If it was, the Scroll idea may have been known to Jesus. Finally, can the tradition be traced back to Jesus and back to the Essenes? He presents these as a method for assessing parallels, not as proof, and says the answer requires "probability."

### Sabbath rescue: Matthew 12:11 and the Damascus Document
Tabor's main example is Jesus's question about a sheep that falls into a pit on the Sabbath, which he cites from Matthew 12:11. Tabor reports that Jewish interlocutors in Jerusalem found the idea strange. He then reads a Damascus Document rule forbidding anyone to raise an animal's young from a pit on the Sabbath. He argues this shows a Jewish group holding exactly the position Jesus was answering, and that the parallel suggests Jesus knew such views. Tabor presents this as his own reading. He does not claim it is conclusive.

### Other parallels Tabor raises
- The Damascus Document rule about priests counting hair on a diseased head, which Tabor connects with Matthew's "even the hairs of your head are numbered."
- The phrase "sons of Light," which Tabor traces to the Community Rule's dualism of sons of light and sons of darkness.
- Celibacy in Matthew 19:12. Tabor says the Essenes were the only celibate group in the background, and notes that celibacy sits uneasily with the commandment to be fruitful and multiply in Genesis.
- The shared meal and betrayal imagery, where a Thanksgiving Hymn about one who dips his hand in the bowl is set beside the Last Supper, and a "strike the shepherd" passage is set beside Zechariah.

Tabor is candid that these are plausible readings and that the differences matter. The Essene sources he cites are 100 years or more older than the Gospels, and he says so.

### Essene fate and the Jesus movement
Tabor argues that the Romans destroyed Qumran around 67 and Jerusalem in 70, which left the Essenes at least three years to flee. He says the best explanation for some Essene terms found further east is that Essenes fled eastward, and he mentions the Silk Road. In his account, the Essenes then lived in Jerusalem in the same area as the Jesus movement, which he calls the "PJM movement" (Palestinian Jesus movement). He argues that close neighbours influence one another, and that the Essenes were "heartbroken" by the Jerusalem catastrophe. Tabor treats this as an argued historical reconstruction.

### Jesus as a Jew, and the word "Christian"
Charlesworth argues that Jesus and the first believers were Jews and that the Bible is a collection of Jewish writings. He urges students never to use "Christian" for the first century. He says the Jewish identity of the Gospels is commonly overlooked, and he tells the class that "all of the early Christians were Jews."

### Humanity of Jesus in John
In a later exchange, one speaker argues that Jesus's full humanity is essential to Christian belief and cites John. The speaker points to Jesus's thirst, his weariness at the well, and his weeping at Lazarus's tomb (John). The speaker also says the Greek of John 1:14 should be read as "the logos... became flesh" rather than as "word became flesh," which the speaker presents as a reading and not a settled rendering.

### Voice of the Teacher and first-person testimony
Violet, a student, reports her MA thesis on hearing the voice of the Teacher of Righteousness in the Hodayot. She says the strongest evidence is the suffering the text describes, especially column 11, and she is struck by the reference to cutting out a tongue and breaking an arm. Tabor argues that first-person material allows historians to "analyze what the person's like," and he contrasts it with the Gospels, which he calls "layers" of tradition. Tabor also argues that Paul's first-person letters, for instance his account of going up to Jerusalem to see James, are harder for mythicists to explain away.

### The Shapira scroll
Charlesworth and Tabor argue that the Shapira scroll should be re-examined and not simply declared a forgery, although Tabor acknowledges that Shapira had a reputation as a forger. Tabor says the Shapira scroll should be "looked at again" as proposed by Shimon Gibson and John Taylor. Charlesworth says the arguments used to dismiss it could be applied to other fragments, and that Scrolls can be preserved for thousands of years in the Qumran climate. Both note that only transcriptions, not photographs, are currently available.

### Publication delays
Charlesworth explains that the slow release was caused by poor early photography, the death of de Vaux, the wars, and disputes over ownership and access, and that the fragments were scattered. He also describes a Scroll handled carelessly by a researcher and a fragment dropped and recovered. He says he has five volumes finished for his Dead Sea Scrolls project at Princeton, begun in 1984, and that the Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs and his Hodayot edition are complete.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus was not a Jew.** Tabor says some people have claimed this, and that the claim was taken up in Nazi ideology (he names Hitler). Charlesworth and Tabor both argue that Jesus and the early believers were Jews.
- **The Dead Sea Scrolls are fakes or cannot be authentic.** Tabor says a "cartel" has claimed many Scrolls are fakes and calls that ignorance. Charlesworth says the Scrolls have survived and can be preserved in the desert climate.
- **Scrolls on leather cannot survive 2,000 years.** Charlesworth says the Scrolls prove otherwise, and that some are about 2,400 years old by the speaker's dating.
- **Jesus was an Essene.** Charlesworth says Jesus was influenced by the Essenes "negatively and positively" but was not an Essene, and that the picture is more complex than a single identity.
- **Christianity is a form of Essenism.** Charlesworth says this early view was rejected because it removed the church's dogma, while also allowing that some of it has merit.
- **Jesus was not really human.** One speaker argues that a divine-only Jesus cuts the umbilical cord to humanity, and that John presents a Jesus who is tired, thirsty and weeping.
- **Christianity spread west, not east.** Tabor warns that some Christian tradition claims the faith moved west to Europe, when Essene-related terms appear in the east.
