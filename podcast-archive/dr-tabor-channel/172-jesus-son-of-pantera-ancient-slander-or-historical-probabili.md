## Summary

The talk opens with Tabor reading from the opening of his book *Paul and Jesus*. He notes that the Apostles' Creed moves from the virgin birth straight to the crucifixion and resurrection, skipping Jesus' public life, and asks how that happened. He then turns to the interview with Neal Sendlank, which is the main subject. The question is who "Yeshua ben Pantera" (Jesus son of Pantera) was, and whether the name is a slur or a real family name. Sendlank asks the questions and Tabor carries most of the talk.

Tabor argues that the usual account is backwards. The familiar claim that "Pantera" is a pun on *parthenos* ("virgin") and that Mary was raped by a Roman soldier traces back to Celsus (late second century, preserved through Origen). He says the pun itself is not attested before the 19th century, and that the earliest and Eastern sources treat Pantera as a real name, either from Joseph's or Mary's family. He also points to rabbinic texts from Sepphoris, a 1st-century Jerusalem ossuary reading "Joseph son of Panthera," and an 1850s tombstone from Bingen of a Roman soldier named Tiberius Julius Abdes Panthera from Sidon. He says these show Panthera was a real Jewish-compatible name in the region, though he stops short of linking the soldier to Jesus.

The talk ends with Tabor's own tentative reconstruction (Mary became pregnant by a man from the Panthera family, Joseph married her, and the father then vanished). He labels this speculation and says that "we don't know the circumstances of the pregnancy." He closes with a picture of 4 BC, the destruction of Sepphoris, and crucifixions in Galilee, which he calls "the real Christmas story."

## Key Teachings

### 1. Mark is the earliest Gospel and does not name Jesus' father
Tabor says Mark 6:3 calls Jesus "the son of Mary," not of Joseph, and that Joseph is never mentioned in Mark. He adds that Paul's letters never mention Joseph either. He argues that Joseph enters the story only in Matthew and Luke, and that Matthew's account presents Joseph as learning that the pregnancy is not his. He reads the Holy Spirit language as "through the agency of the Holy Spirit," and says his own position is that Jesus had a human father, "probably not Joseph historically speaking." He calls the name "father unknown" the historian's default.

### 2. The "pun on parthenos" is a modern invention, and Celsus is late
Tabor says that a graduate student of his traced the parthenos-pun idea to the 19th century, and that the German scholar who proposed it is named in the caption as "Nietzsche" (the name is likely garbled, and Tabor's dating is 1830). He says early Christians in the second through fourth centuries treat Pantera as a real family member. He identifies Celsus's *Contra Celsum* (as preserved by Origen) as the first negative source, with its story of a Roman soldier named Panthera, a carpenter husband, and adultery. He calls it "certainly the oldest negative reference" but not the oldest reference, and he says Celsus claims to be relaying what he heard "from the Jews."

### 3. Eastern and early sources describe Pantera as a family name
Tabor cites Epiphanius, who (he says) calls Pantera a family name on Joseph's side, a later source on Joseph's side as well, and John of Damascus (sixth century), who places a Panther ancestor on Joachim's side of Mary's line. He also cites a "Teaching of Jacob" in which a teacher from Tiberias gives a genealogy of Mary through Joachim, son of Panther, and Panther as brother of Melchi, with a link to Nathan son of David. Tabor argues the Eastern sources, from Damascus and Tiberias, are more reliable than the Latin tradition.

### 4. Rabbinic texts from Sepphoris use "Jesus son of Pantera" descriptively
Drawing on Peter Schäfer's *Jesus in the Talmud*, Tabor says the Jerusalem (Palestinian) Talmud and the Tosefta contain neutral or positive references to "Jesus son of Pantera," set in the streets of Sepphoris. He says Sepphoris, about four miles from Nazareth in his account, was the urban capital of Galilee, and that Nazareth and nearby villages are its "exurbs." He argues that naming Jesus with his father's name was needed to distinguish him, because "Yeshua" was a common name, and that the rabbis do not use Pantera pejoratively in these texts. He accepts that rabbis elsewhere (in the Babylonian Talmud) are negative, and says that critical scholarship must separate these layers.

### 5. Tosefta anecdotes: healing in Jesus' name and a ruling on "filthy money"
In the Tosefta, Rabbi Eleazar ben Dama is bitten by a snake and is healed when Jacob of Sikhnin prays "in the name of Yeshua ben Pantera." Tabor says Rabbi Ishmael later attributes Eleazar's death to this, and that the texts describe Jesus as one who "broke through the fence of the Torah," not as a slur. He also recounts a ruling attributed to Jesus about money earned from prostitution (it should be used to build toilets and bathhouses), supported by Micah 1:7. He notes Rabbi Eliezer ben Hyrcanus liked the saying and was later condemned as a heretic. Tabor says he does not give these 1st-century attributions much weight historically without critical analysis.

### 6. The Sidon connection and the Bingen tombstone
Tabor notes that Mark 7 describes Jesus entering a house near Tyre and Sidon and that the Gospels describe crowds following him from Sidon. He notes that the tombstone of Tiberius Julius Abdes Panthera (from Sidon, aged 62, forty years of service, first cohort of archers) was found at a Roman camp at Bingen on the Rhine in the 1850s and studied by Adolf Deissmann (1903, *Light from the East*). Tabor says the tombstone is in the Römerhalle museum in Bad Kreuznach. He stresses that it does not prove a link to Jesus, but shows the name was real and that a Sidonian Panthera could plausibly have had Jewish connections. He adds an ossuary from Jerusalem reading "Joseph son of Panthera" as evidence the name was used by Jews in the 1st century.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Pantera is a pun on *parthenos*, making Jesus "son of a virgin" as a joke.** Tabor says no ancient source makes this pun; the proposal dates to the 19th century, and early sources treat Pantera as a real family name.
- **Jesus was fathered by a Roman soldier who raped Mary.** Tabor says this comes from the late Celsus material, not the early sources, and that the earliest and Eastern traditions do not describe a rape. He still allows that a Panthera-named man may have been involved in some way.
- **Talmudic references to Jesus are all slander.** Tabor says the Palestinian Talmud and Tosefta contain neutral or positive references set in Sepphoris, and that the negative stories come mainly from the Babylonian Talmud, which he treats with caution.
- **Sepphoris was obscure and irrelevant to Jesus.** Tabor argues Sepphoris was the Galilean capital, near Nazareth, and that Jesus would have known it.
- **The name Pantera is made up.** Tabor cites the Jerusalem ossuary and the Bingen tombstone to argue it was a real name, while cautioning that these do not prove a link to Jesus.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Jesus' family.** Mark 6:3 names Jesus' mother and brothers (James, Joseph, Simon, Jude) and no father. Tabor's argument bears directly on how the earliest sources present the family.
- **Virgin birth.** Tabor argues that Matthew and Luke use Holy Spirit language that can be read non-biologically, and that Mark and Paul do not mention Joseph. He reads the paternity question historically, not theologically.
- **Gospels and canon.** Tabor treats Mark as the earliest Gospel, dates Matthew and Luke later, and places John as latest. He uses Celsus, Origen, and rabbinic texts to show how traditions about Jesus' origins developed.
- **Jesus' teaching as preserved in rabbinic tradition.** The Tosefta ruling on prostitute money and the Eleazar ben Dama story are presented as possible traces of Jesus' legal positions. Tabor says these need critical handling and does not claim them as settled.
- **Jacob of Sikhnin.** Tabor identifies him as a follower of Jesus in the Tosefta and speculates he might be a relative, but says that is unlikely.
