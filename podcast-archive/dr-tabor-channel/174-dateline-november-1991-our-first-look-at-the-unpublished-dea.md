The talk is an archive clip from November 1991, introduced by Tabor in a recent recording. It shows him discussing newly released Dead Sea Scrolls texts with an unnamed host. The question is whether these texts show that Jewish groups before Jesus expected a Messiah who suffers, dies, or raises the dead. Tabor's intro says he discusses three texts: 4Q285, 4Q521, and the Cave 1 Isaiah scroll's reading of Isaiah 53:11.

### Key Teachings

### 1. The "slain Messiah" text (4Q285)
Tabor describes this fragment as the one that drew press attention in 1991. He reads a line in which "the prince of the congregation" (the Nasi) is put to death, with "woundings or piercings" mentioned. He reconstructs the text as quoting Isaiah 11:1 about the branch from the root of Jesse, and identifies the branch with the Davidic Messiah. He notes that Vermes read the line as a Messiah who kills his enemies with woundings and piercings, and says that reading "fits the text of Isaiah 11 much better." Tabor argues the verdict is still open. He sees two possibilities: a combined citation of Isaiah 11 and Isaiah 53, or a text written in reaction to the death of a figure the group had hoped in. He says identifying the figure with Jesus "is going beyond the evidence."

### 2. Early Jewish sources for a suffering Messiah
Tabor responds to the claim that Judaism has a suffering Messiah ben Joseph, saying that figure appears in later sources (the Talmud and midrashic material), not in early texts. He says he knows of no Hebrew text from the Mishnah period that speaks of the Messiah being pierced or slain. In his view, apart from the New Testament, 4Q285 is the earliest such text, and it shows that "other Jewish groups of the time" were discussing something similar. He cites Michael Weiss and Israel Knohl (both 2000) as independently arguing that the Qumran Teacher of Righteousness was seen as a suffering or dying messianic figure.

### 3. The Messianic Apocalypse (4Q521) and the raising of the dead
Tabor reads 4Q521 as a description of messianic deeds. Its opening line is "the heavens and the Earth will obey his Messiah." Its list includes healing and freeing the captives, and in the Scroll version, "he will make live the dead." He notes that the list draws on Psalm 145, Psalm 146, and above all Isaiah 61, but that Isaiah 61 does not mention raising the dead.

### 4. Luke 7:22 as coded messianic language
Tabor argues that Jesus' answer to John the Baptist's messengers in Luke 7:22 follows the same pattern, including "the dead are raised." He calls this "the first absolutely tight linguistic uh connection of the New Testament," tighter than phrases like "New Covenant." He reads the answer as Jesus indicating that he is the Messiah by a shared, well-understood list. He says he is not claiming that Luke quotes the Scroll or the Scroll quotes Luke, but that both draw on the same patterned language. He reads the John passage as drawing on a source he calls Q.

### 5. The suffering servant as a group, and the Mark and Paul connection
Tabor says early Christians applied Isaiah 53 to Jesus, but that Jesus himself may have meant it for a group of suffering servants who would follow him. He points to the "Son of man came not to be served" saying and the call to take up one's cross as evidence. He says this collective idea appears in Mark and probably in Paul, who speaks of "filling up the sufferings of Jesus" in his own life.

### 6. Isaiah 53:11 and the word "light"
Tabor says the Cave 1 Isaiah Scroll reads "he will see light" in Isaiah 53:11, which the Masoretic Text and the King James lack. He argues that Isaiah uses light as a metaphor for raising the dead (citing Isaiah 27), so the reading may allude to resurrection. He says a suffering, buried figure "seeing light" would fit this. He argues that Paul, in 1 Corinthians 15, saying Christ "died," "was buried," and "was raised" "according to the scriptures," may be drawing on this reading. He admits that no clear Hebrew Bible passage says the Messiah will be raised from the dead. He notes that the NIV and a recent RSV revision restored the reading.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Judaism had a clear suffering Messiah before Christianity, so the idea is unrelated to Jesus.** Tabor says the Messiah ben Joseph figure appears in later sources (the Talmud and midrash), and that he knows of no early Hebrew text from the Mishnah period describing a pierced or slain Messiah. He argues that the Scrolls are the earliest such text.
- **Christians invented the suffering-Messiah and resurrection ideas.** Tabor argues that these Scroll texts show other Jewish groups had similar ideas before the Gospels, so the ideas were not solely Christian.
- **Isaiah 61 says the Messiah raises the dead.** Tabor notes that Isaiah 61 does not mention raising the dead. The idea appears in the Scroll (4Q521) and in Luke 7:22, which he takes as a sign of a shared tradition.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor sees a possible Pauline reading of Isaiah 53:11 ("light") behind 1 Corinthians 15, and says the Mark and Paul texts carry the collective suffering-servant idea.
- **The Nazarenes/Ebionites/Essenes:** The talk centers on Qumran (the Scrolls and the Teacher of Righteousness), but does not discuss the Nazarenes or Ebionites.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor reads Luke 7:22 as Jesus identifying himself as Messiah through a coded, shared list of messianic signs.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** Tabor argues that 4Q521 and the Isaiah 53:11 "light" reading show the Scroll community expected resurrection of the dead. He notes that clear Qumran references to resurrection are debated among scholars.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor's argument that Luke 7:22 comes from the Q source, and that Luke and the Scroll share a tradition, touches on gospel sources.
