## Summary

Tabor and host Neal Sendlack take up the question "Who wrote the Dead Sea Scrolls?" on the 75th anniversary of their discovery. Tabor's answer is that the group behind the sectarian texts wrote them, but that "Essene" is an incomplete label. He says the Scrolls include copies of the Hebrew Bible (about 40 percent), books outside it such as Enoch and Jubilees (about 30 percent), and the group's own writings. He treats only the last category as the group's authorship, and he dates the group's production roughly from the Maccabean period into the first century BCE, possibly to around 75 BCE.

Tabor's main argument is that Josephus's account of the Essenes, written for a Roman audience, leaves out the group's core features: apocalyptic expectation, messianism, a "New Covenant" framed by a teacher, and a stark sons-of-light versus sons-of-darkness dualism. He reads the Community Rule's call to "prepare the way in the wilderness" (Isaiah 40:3) as a literal relocation to the Dead Sea area. He also draws parallels with John the Baptist and the Jesus movement, which he thinks arose about a century later. He is careful to note significant differences, especially in how Jesus treats Sabbath law.

The talk moves through the Teacher of Righteousness, the Liar, and the Wicked Priest, the Damascus Document's "twenty years" and "forty years" chronology, and the Habakkuk commentary. It then turns to Sabbath strictness and an archaeological hypothesis about latrines at Qumran that Tabor developed with anthropologist Joe Zias, with a tapeworm-linked theory of early death. It closes with a promise of later episodes on the Dead Sea Scrolls Bible and on Tabor's view that Jesus was a "liberal" version of this trajectory. The discussion ends without a formal conclusion, with Tabor pointing to his other videos.

## Key Teachings

### Authorship is not a single answer
Tabor argues the question depends on which Scrolls are meant. About 40 percent are copies of the Hebrew Bible, which he says the group treated as their Scripture; about 30 percent are non-biblical books such as Enoch and Jubilees, which the group valued; and the remainder are sectarian texts the group wrote itself. He says only the last group should be called the Essenes' own writings. He also notes that Jude quotes Enoch as inspired, which he uses to challenge the scholarly label "pseudepigrapha" as "false writings." He dates the Isaiah scroll from Cave 1 to perhaps 100 or 200 BCE and compares it with the Leningrad Codex (about 1000 CE). He calls this "a thousand to twelve hundred years" gap and says that many differences remain between the Scrolls and the Masoretic tradition.

### Josephus, the Essenes, and the omission of apocalyptic messianism
Tabor says Josephus (*Jewish War*, book 2, written around 75 CE from Rome) gives the Essenes far more space than the Pharisees and Sadducees. Even so, Josephus does not mention apocalyptic expectation, messianism, or the New Covenant. Tabor argues Josephus was writing for Vespasian and presenting Jewish groups as comparable to Greek philosophical schools. He contrasts this with the Scrolls' vision of a coming Messiah and says a Roman audience would have heard such ideas as a threat. Tabor also cites Pliny the Elder and Philo as sources for the Essenes and says their accounts together still miss the group's core.

### The Teacher, the Liar, and the Wicked Priest
Tabor follows the Scrolls' cast of figures. The Damascus Document says the group "wandered like blind men... for 20 years" before God raised up a Teacher of Righteousness, whom Tabor translates as "a true teacher, a prophet like Moses" who will inaugurate the New Covenant. Tabor cites Thanksgiving Hymns (1QH) as autobiographical and notes the line about a companion who lifted his heel against him, which he compares to the Last Supper betrayal. The Liar leads a breakaway group. The Wicked Priest, probably a Jerusalem high priest, persecutes the Teacher on the Day of Atonement. Tabor says the group expected the end 40 years after the Teacher's death and, when it did not come, reinterpreted the delay as God's mercy. He compares this with later movements' recalculations of dates.

### Wilderness location and the Sabbath
Tabor reads the Community Rule's call to "prepare the way in the wilderness" as a literal geographic instruction to the Arava, the Rift Valley of the Dead Sea. He compares this with John the Baptist's use of Isaiah 40:3, placing John about a century after the Scrolls group. He says the group's location across from Mount Nebo, where Moses is traditionally buried, shows they wanted to be present at the day of judgment. He describes the Qumran sect's Sabbath rules: the "dwelling" in Torah cannot be extended to a whole town, and Tabor says the group was stricter than Pharisaic practice on this point.

### The Qumran latrine and Joe Zias's theory
Tabor describes Josephus's claim that the Essenes did not defecate during the Sabbath, and the Scrolls' instruction to dig latrines northwest of the camp and cover waste. He describes a joint soil-sampling project with anthropologist Joe Zias, reporting human waste and tapeworm remains in the area northwest of the camp. He presents Zias's hypothesis, which he calls speculation, that repeated exposure to contaminated water and infection from the ritual pool could help explain early deaths among the roughly 1100 graves in the cemetery, mostly male. Tabor calls this "the high cost of being holy" and says he finds the theory worth considering.

### Jesus and the Sabbath: a different orientation
Tabor argues that Jesus shares many features with the Qumran movement (apocalyptic outlook, a messianic movement, sons of light) but differs on Sabbath law. He discusses Mark 2 (and its parallel in Matthew 12), where disciples pluck grain on the Sabbath, and Jesus replies with David and the bread of the presence. He draws the conclusion from this passage that "the Sabbath is made for people." Tabor says Jesus also disputes a Qumran law about rescuing an animal from a pit, a rule he thinks Jesus rebutted in his own teaching. He cautions that the Christian movement came much later and that the Qumran material may not be a direct source.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"The Essenes wrote the Dead Sea Scrolls."** Tabor says the answer depends on which Scrolls are meant. He argues that only the sectarian texts are the group's own writing, and that "Essene" is an inadequate label drawn from Greek and Roman writers.
- **"Pseudepigrapha means false writings."** Tabor disagrees with the term and notes Jude's use of Enoch as inspired.
- **"Josephus describes the Essenes fully."** Tabor says Josephus omits the group's apocalyptic, messianic, and New Covenant features, and that his account was shaped by his audience.
- **"Ancient people all died in their 30s or 40s."** Tabor calls this erroneous. He explains that average lifespans in the past reflect high infant and child mortality, and that survivors commonly lived to 50 or 60.
