## Summary

Tabor asks how, when, and where the idea of a bodily resurrection of the dead developed. He frames this as Part VI of a series on death and afterlife in the ancient Western world, covering roughly 200 BCE to 200 CE, and argues that the period shows "persistence and change": older views survive alongside new ones. He starts with Greco-Roman material. Cicero's dream of Scipio Africanus in *Republic* 6, Greek and Latin epitaphs, and a sailor's inscription about "astral immortality" show a Platonic picture in which the soul is immortal and the body is a prison. Tabor calls this "the idea that took over the world" and treats the Greek and Roman material as the contrast to the Jewish development.

He then moves through Jewish texts in rough chronological order. The Wisdom of Solomon gives both the "no afterlife" speech of the wicked and the hope of the righteous, whose souls are "in the hand of God." Isaiah 24-27, which he dates on the basis of the Dead Sea Scroll copy, contains a passage (Isaiah 26:19 in the RSV) where "thy dead shall live" and "their bodies shall arise," though he admits the translation is difficult. Second Maccabees 7 gives the seven brothers and their mother, whom he calls the first recorded martyrs, who expect God to raise them bodily. He then reads Dead Sea Scroll fragment 4Q521 as describing a Messiah who will "revive the dead," and sets it beside the Q material in Luke 7. He ends with Luke 20, where Jesus answers the Sadducees with a picture of resurrected people who are "equal to angels," and with the shift from urns to ossuaries in the Herodian period.

The talk ends as a survey that promises more. Tabor says he will return to Paul and to the physical-versus-spiritual body question in later episodes, and he does not reach a single settled conclusion beyond the claim that resurrection of the body is a late, distinctively Jewish development.

## Key Teachings

### Greco-Roman dualism and "astral immortality"

Tabor uses Cicero's *Republic* 6 (the dream of Scipio Africanus) to present a cosmos split between a perishable lower realm and an eternal heaven. In the dream, the father tells his son that "that is not you but your body that is Mortal" and that "the soul of each man is his real self." Tabor describes this as "pure Hellenistic dualism" and cites the Golden Plates from tombs, which say "I am a child of Earth and Starry heaven." He also relies on Lattimore's *Themes in Greek and Latin Epitaphs* to show a range of attitudes. These include an epitaph from a sailor at Marseille who "has obtained a God for a guide" and a Latin epitaph reading *non fui, fui, non sum, non curo* ("I was not, I was, I am not, I don't care"). The point is that the body is not the self and that the dead are not raised.

### The no-afterlife voice and the righteous soul in the Wisdom of Solomon

Tabor reads Wisdom of Solomon 2, where "short and sorrowful is our life" and the dead "will not return from Hades." He presents this as the view of the wicked, which he compares to Epicureanism. He then reads Wisdom 3, where "the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God" and "their hope is full of immortality." He reads this as a hint of resurrection, but notes that "it doesn't seem like there's much concern for the physical body." He says the book is useful because it shows two competing views within one text.

### Isaiah 24-27 and the partial resurrection

Tabor calls Isaiah 24-27 "the Isaiah apocalypse." He argues that it was added to the book of Isaiah, since it is not like the rest of the Hebrew Bible, and that it is "very much Cosmic." He reads Isaiah 26:13 as a statement that the other nations' dead "will not live" and "will not rise." He reads Isaiah 26:19 in the RSV as "thy dead shall live; their bodies shall arise." He admits the Hebrew shifts between singular and plural and that "this is a very very difficult passage." He links it to Daniel 12 and calls it "the earliest view that we can trace of a kind of a partial Resurrection," meaning only the righteous rise. He also connects this to Paul's claim to believe in the "resurrection of both the just and the unjust," which he describes as a later view.

### Second Maccabees 7 and the bodily martyr

Tabor describes Antiochus IV's persecution, which he dates to the Maccabean period. He reads the story of the seven brothers and their mother, who refuse to eat pork. He calls it "the first recorded case of what we could call martyrdom," and he presents the theological problem: if the faithful die for the Torah, "doesn't God have to raise you or reward you some way?" The third brother says, "I got these from Heaven... and from him I hope to get them back again," and the mother's warning is that "for you there will be no Resurrection to life." Tabor argues that this resurrection is "very bodily," unlike the Greek view, in which "the body is a prison." He uses this as the main evidence for his claim that bodily resurrection is distinctively Jewish and arose in the context of martyrdom.

### 4Q521 and the Messiah who raises the dead

Tabor presents Dead Sea Scroll fragment 4Q521, which he says he co-published with Michael Wise in the 1990s. He reads a line in which "the heavens and the Earth will obey his messiah" and in which the Lord "will heal the wounded, revive the dead and preach good news to the poor." He argues that this resembles the Q-source material in Luke 7 (John the Baptist's question and Jesus' reply about the blind, deaf, and dead). He is careful to say "I would not say that he's quoting the scroll," and he concludes only that "there was a similar Messianic expectation" when the scroll was written.

### Luke 20: resurrection as a new age

In answer to the Sadducees' levirate question, Tabor reads Jesus' saying that those "accounted worthy to attain that age and to the resurrection of the Dead" neither marry nor die, "because they are equal to angels and are sons of God." He says this "doesn't sound like we're just resuscitating a corpse" but describes a "new creation." He reads Exodus 3:6 as the argument that "God... is not the god of the dead but of the living," and he presents this kind of argument as common in Jewish literature and rabbinic texts. He also says that Jesus, in John, says "all who are in the graves will come forth," though he treats it as a symbolic promise rather than a zombie-like return.

### Urns and ossuaries

Tabor contrasts Roman funerary urns, which hold ashes and suggest the end of the body, with ossuaries (bone boxes with names written on them). He says ossuaries appear in the Herodian period and show "this individualization of the Dead," which signals that the bones "are going to come forth." This is his material-culture evidence for the shift in attitudes toward the body.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jewish belief in resurrection was uniform and ancient.** Tabor argues the idea is late and contested: the Wisdom of Solomon's wicked speaker denies any afterlife, the Sadducees hold the "old view," and the earliest resurrection texts are partial and debated.
- **Greco-Roman views and Jewish views on the body were the same.** Tabor contrasts them: the Greek view treats the body as "just a shell," while the Maccabean view insists on a literal, bodily resurrection.
- **Resurrection in the gospels means zombie-like corpses coming back.** Tabor says, "I don't think he's looking for the zombie apocalypse," and describes the resurrected as glorified, "equal to angels."
