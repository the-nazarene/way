## Key Teachings

### 1. Paul as the only first-person witness
Tabor's starting claim is that Paul is "our only first-hand witness" (transcript at 00:00). He cites 1 Corinthians 9 ("I've seen the Lord") and 1 Corinthians 15:8 ("last of all he appeared to me"), and adds Galatians 1 as a possible third reference, though he calls it less unambiguous. He argues that the gospels, which he calls derivative and later, cannot establish what the first witnesses experienced because none of their authors claims to have been there. He notes that Paul himself was not at the tomb.

### 2. The appearance list and "sightings"
Tabor reads 1 Corinthians 15 as a list of appearances: Peter, the Twelve, James, a group of 500 "brethren," and then Paul. He notes that James appears nowhere else in the New Testament list and that he discusses James's presence at the Last Supper in his book *The Jesus Dynasty*, based on the Hebrew/Ebionite gospel materials. He calls these experiences "sightings," comparing the wording to the Greek passive "was seen." He compares them to the Elvis-sighting analogy, though he qualifies that the comparison "is not comparable at all." The purpose of the comparison, in his words, is to capture the sense of "he was seen" or "he appeared" as an experience of manifestation.

### 3. Resurrection as transformation, not resuscitation
This is the center of the talk. Tabor argues from 1 Corinthians 15, Philippians 3:20-21, and 2 Corinthians 5 that Paul describes a "glorious" transformed body, "no longer of dust" but "a life-giving spirit." He reads the "first Adam / second Adam" contrast as the key. He argues that Paul's answer to "what kind of body?" is "God knows," and that Paul does not report seeing a physical body. He reasons that if the dead rose in a corruptible state, Paul's theology would make no sense, so the risen body must be incorruptible from the start. On this view, a resuscitated corpse "would not be particularly impressive" to Paul, and Tabor adds that the bones of Jesus would not bear on Paul's faith. He also claims that the Jesus of Luke 24 who says "I have Flesh and Bones" reflects a later view.

For the "clothing" analogy, Tabor cites 2 Corinthians 5 and says that Paul pictures the body as a tent and the resurrection body as a building, and that "we do not want to be found naked." He reads this as an image close to Plato, and he says this would not describe a body in a grave.

### 4. Comparative material: apotheosis and Luke 20
Tabor reads Paul's transformation alongside Hellenistic examples. He mentions Romulus disappearing in a passage he attributes to Ovid, and Josephus's account that Moses was taken up to heaven, though Tabor notes that Josephus still mentions a tomb of Moses. He argues that Peter's and Paul's visions were "apotheosis" in the Hellenistic sense. Against Celsus, he says that the claim "that could have just been a ghost" is answered by the witnesses' insistence that they saw him "in a way that was uh powerful and amazing." He also reads Luke 20 (the Sadducees' question about the woman with seven husbands) as showing that the resurrection life is "like Angels," and he links that to Paul's comment in 1 Corinthians 6 that the saints will judge angels.

### 5. The empty tomb as a later development
Tabor argues the empty tomb is a late tradition, not the earliest one. He says that Paul does mention the burial ("he died and he was buried"), which he calls "the earliest testimony you have of what happened to Jesus when he died." But he argues that Paul shows no interest in a tomb, and that Paul's view of the dead in Corinth ("they're in their tombs") would not have sent anyone to look inside them. He says Matthew's account of the tombs opening at the crucifixion (Matthew 27) shows the "resuscitating corpses" idea that Paul does not share. He argues that Matthew, too, moves the appearances to Galilee, and that Mark does not record appearances at all in its original ending.

### 6. The Gospel of Peter and the Galilee
Tabor reads the closing lines of the Gospel of Peter, which place the women's flight after the tomb is found empty, and says that the disciples "wept and cried" for the seven days of unleavened bread before going back to Galilee. He says this text "seems to be earlier," since the Gospel of Peter appears not to know the appearance stories of the first week. He then argues that the recovery of faith happened in Galilee, where Peter and Andrew return to fishing, and that the Jerusalem experience (Pentecost, Acts 2) came at the next festival. Tabor acknowledges that the Gospel of Peter also has legendary features, such as a Jesus "as high as a skyscraper."

### 7. Earthly kingdom versus heavenly transformation (brief)
Tabor contrasts Paul, who he thinks saw the gospel as a cosmic transformation, with James, Peter, and John, whom he thinks likely held a more literal hope for the kingdom in Jerusalem with the Twelve on thrones (Matthew 19 is implied, not cited). He reads Galatians 4:26 ("Jerusalem above") and Revelation 21-22 as closer to Paul's view. He says Paul expected an imminent parousia, and that the Torah, Jew and Gentile, male and female were "all in the process of passing away." He says the Antioch confrontation (Galatians 2) probably concerned Torah observance in this light, but he admits that Paul "never tells us any details about what happens after the parasia."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Resurrection meant a corpse coming out of the tomb.** Tabor argues that Paul's understanding was transformation into an immortal, glorified body, and that "what most Christians think" (a revived corpse walking around Jerusalem) "is not actually Resurrection as Paul understands it."
- **The empty tomb is the earliest evidence.** Tabor says the empty tomb is a later tradition, and that Paul shows no interest in tombs.
- **The early opponents disputed the resurrection.** Tabor argues that the opposition Paul faced concerned his apostolic authority and the Torah, and that there is no evidence of a controversy over resurrection in his lifetime.
- **The gospels are the primary witnesses.** Tabor says the gospels are "derivative" and "later," and that Paul's letters are the only first-person testimony.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor frames Paul's transformation theology as distinct from the Jerusalem community's expectations of an earthly kingdom and a continued Torah.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** He lists James among the appearance witnesses in 1 Corinthians 15, and he describes James, Peter, and John as likely holding a more literal, earthly hope. He presents this as an inference, not as established fact.
- **Torah:** Tabor argues Paul thought "the Torah with all of its interpretations" was passing away, which he links to the Antioch conflict with Peter (Galatians 2). He presents this as his reading.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Tabor reads Paul's heavenly-Jerusalem language (Galatians 4:26; Revelation 21-22) as a contrast to an earthly Jerusalem kingdom.
- **Resurrection, afterlife, and hell:** Tabor's core subject. He argues the soul is "in the keeping of God" between death and resurrection, and that Paul's resurrection is not a return to the physical body.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor argues the gospel resurrection narratives (empty tomb, physical appearances) developed "for apologetic reasons" against skeptics, and he places the Gospel of Peter as a possibly earlier witness.
