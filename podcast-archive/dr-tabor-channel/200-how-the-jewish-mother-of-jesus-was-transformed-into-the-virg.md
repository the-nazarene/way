## Summary

Tabor asks how Mary, the Jewish mother of Jesus, became the figure of Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant, and Muslim devotion that many people know today, and whether the historical Mary can still be recovered. He presents the talk as a preview of his book *The Lost Mary*. He describes Mary as "the best known least-known woman in history" and says he is "convinced that this transformation was deliberate" (01:04). He treats the change as the product of later theological agendas that wanted to remove Mary's sexuality, Jewishness, and family life, and to move her from the earthly realm to a heavenly one.

His evidence is partly textual and partly archaeological. He notes that Mary appears only a dozen or so times in the New Testament Gospels, and he reads several of those scenes in their historical context. He points to Luke 2:41-51 as the last reference to Joseph in any source, which leads him to think Mary was a widow before Jesus reached adulthood. He says several Gospel texts describe Jesus as the eldest son and head of the family, and that Mary raised eight children as a single parent under Roman occupation. He also says that James, Mary's second-born son, led the movement after Jesus's execution, with Simon succeeding him. He draws on "important Jewish sources," "newly discovered texts," and archaeological work in the Holy Land, including sites he has worked on, though he names few of these specifically in the talk.

The talk ends as a devotional preview rather than a full argument. Tabor says recovering the historical Mary "changes everything," dedicates the book to her, and closes with "Ave Maria." Most of his claims about sources, dates, and motives are offered as the basis for the book and are not argued in detail here.

## Key Teachings

### Mary as a Jewish matriarch and widowed head of household

Tabor argues that the Gospels, read in context, present Mary as a widow who led a large family. He says her husband Joseph last appears in "one text when Jesus is 12 years old," which he identifies as Luke 2:41-51, and that "several gospel texts clearly describe Jesus as the eldest son and head of the family." He describes Mary as having "raised her eight children under Roman military occupation." He presents this as the historical picture that later devotion hid, and he calls her "the creative revolutionary Jewish matriarch of the early Christian faith" (02:39).

### Dynastic succession: James, then Simon

Tabor claims that after Jesus's execution, "it was her second born son James" who led the movement, "with Mary by his side" (12:02). He says the succession was "dynastic, based on Mary's Royal and Priestly lineage." He states that James was executed in AD 62 and that his brother Simon then took over, "only to be crucified in the reign of the emperor trajan." He argues that Christian tradition later presented Peter and Paul as the co-leaders and effectively wrote James out of the story. He does not, in this talk, walk through the ancient evidence for these events.

### Theological marginalization of Mary and of the family

Tabor argues that a "theologically driven counter-narrative" began to shape Mary as "a passive non-sexual apolitical woman," "a Pious celibate," and "a retiring nun-like figure" (10:27 and 12:02 sections). He says this narrative "was already beginning to show up in our new testament writings" (13:36). He links the change to the Gospels' placement of Mary at Jesus's birth and her near absence until the crucifixion, which he attributes to later theologians who "were anxious to strip her of any kind of sexuality."

### The kingdom reframed as heavenly salvation

Tabor argues that this theological shift also changed Jesus's message. In his account, it "reshaped the revolutionary political message that centered on the arrival of the kingdom of God on Earth into one about escaping the world and finding salvation in heaven" (13:36). He presents this as part of the same agenda that de-emphasized Jesus's human family in favour of his "exalted Divinity."

### Disputed views of Jesus's siblings

Tabor describes the two main church positions on Jesus's brothers and sisters: the Roman Catholic view that they were cousins, and the Eastern Orthodox view that they were children of Joseph from a previous marriage. He says "neither view [is] found in the New Testament" (08:53 section). He also says the Gospels name "four brothers and three sisters" of Jesus, and that his students are often unaware of them. He presents this confusion as a sign of how thoroughly Mary's family was removed from common memory.

### Method: sources, texts, and fieldwork

Tabor describes his method as combining re-reading the New Testament, Jewish sources, newly discovered texts from the past hundred years, and archaeology, including "some in which I've been involved." He says he has made "more than 70 trips to the Holy Land over the past three decades." He also says that "what is not said can reveal unexpected insights," and that small details can open new understanding. The talk does not identify the specific texts or excavations he means.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mary as a nun-like, passive, pious girl.** Tabor says the popular image, reinforced by statues, paintings, and films, is of a Catholic-style pious girl. He argues she was a Jewish woman, widow, and matriarch who led a large family.
- **Jesus had no siblings, or they were cousins.** He says many students are unaware Jesus had brothers and sisters. He says the Roman Catholic view that they were cousins and the Eastern Orthodox view that they were children of Joseph from a prior marriage are both absent from the New Testament.
- **Studying Mary as a Jew detracts from her holiness.** He says some believers may think so, but he argues "the opposite is the case" and that historical study brings believers closer to her.
