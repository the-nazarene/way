## Summary

Tabor introduces a nine-page printed document, posted on his blog, that sets out the material Matthew and Luke share but Mark lacks, which scholars call the Q source. He says few people have actually read this material, and his aim is to get viewers to read it and comment on what they find. He defines Q formulaically, as the common Matthew-Luke material not derived from Mark, and reports that this yields about 50 pericopae in 15 sections. He sets aside the question of whether Q was a separate written document or material one evangelist took from the other, arguing that for the reading exercise "is irrelevant." He also says that Q may point to a Christianity before Paul, and calls it possibly "our best clue" to that earlier movement.

Tabor's stated reasons for the reading exercise are his own. He says he works on Paul, that a chapter of a book he wrote, "Christianity before Paul," addresses what the early Jesus movement was like, and that the Q material is the earliest collection of Jesus' teachings available. He uses examples to show what he thinks Luke preserved and Matthew edited: the John the Baptist teaching on sharing coats and food, the Luke form of the blessings and woes, the discipleship sayings, and the woman with ten coins. He attributes the Matthew-Luke differences to Matthew's editorial choices, such as playing down John the Baptist, and presents the Luke versions as "probably more original," giving his judgment as a reading, not a settled result.

The talk ends with a list of questions for readers (content, John the Baptist, Christology, the atoning death, apocalypticism, the future, and what following Jesus would look like) and an invitation to post impressions in the comments on the blog post. It closes without a conclusion about what the material shows.

## Key Teachings

### What Q is, and how Tabor defines it
Tabor explains that "Q" comes from the German word *Quelle*, meaning source. Operationally, he says, Q "consists of the material Matthew and Luke have in common that is not derived from Mark." He describes this as "a very formulaic definition" that a computer could apply in seconds. He credits Friedrich Schleiermacher (spoken as "slimacher") with first noting the idea in 1832 and Christian Hermann Weisse (spoken as "Visa") with developing the two-source hypothesis, published in 1838–1839. He describes the model as Mark as the narrative source plus Q as the teaching source, and says the debate has run for nearly 200 years.

### The reading exercise and the blue-text passages
Tabor's PDF marks in blue four passages that appear in Luke but not Matthew and that he thinks could still belong to Q. He presents these as Luke material that "probably was authentic and real" and "does kind of seem to hold up." The blue passages are the sections he treats as Luke's contributions. The reading guide asks readers to note what is present and absent, the main themes, how John the Baptist is presented, how Jesus is portrayed (divine, Messiah, or other titles), whether the atoning death appears, what apocalyptic outlook is present, and what following Jesus would look like on this evidence.

### John the Baptist's teaching on sharing
In the first blue passage, John the Baptist teaches that whoever has two coats should share with one who has none, and whoever has food should do likewise. Tabor says that "most people" reading it blindfolded "would think it's Jesus," and that it resembles the Sermon on the Mount. He attributes Matthew's removal of it to Matthew's wish to "play Down John the Baptist considerably over what Mark does," and says this is "my sense of this," not an established finding.

### Beatitudes and woes: Luke's form versus Matthew's
Tabor says the Luke form of the blessings ("blessed are you poor... blessed are you that hunger now...") and four matching woes sound "much more original" than Matthew's version. He reads Matthew's "poor in spirit" and "hunger and thirst for righteousness" as spiritualizing the material, and he says he does not like the term "spiritual" but notes it is the usual description. He also contends the woes are part of the earlier form.

### Discipleship sayings and the lost sheep and coin
Tabor describes three sayings about would-be followers: the one who must bury his father first, the one who must say farewell to his household, and the statement that foxes have holes and birds have nests but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head. He says Matthew "stops" before Luke's third example, the one Tabor reads as likely original. He also contrasts Luke's parable of a woman with ten coins with Matthew's man with a hundred sheep. He suggests Matthew shortened and summarized the material, as he does elsewhere in copying Mark.

### Method for readers and open questions
Tabor asks readers to compare Q with what they would get from reading Mark, Luke, Matthew, or John as a whole, and whether the Q material suggests an earlier collection of Jesus' teachings. He notes that some scholars have argued for a date as early as 50 CE, and he ends by asking, "what kind of a Jesus do we get in this material." He does not answer that question in the talk.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Q as a recovered lost gospel.** The title calls it the "so called 'Lost Gospel of Q,'" and Tabor's framing reflects that it is a hypothetical source. He describes it as a reconstruction from the overlap of Matthew and Luke, and he says whether it was an independent document "is irrelevant" to his reading exercise.
- **Matthew's beatitudes as the original.** Tabor argues the familiar Matthean beatitudes are the spiritualized, later form, and that Luke's literal blessings and woes sound "more original."
- **Q sounds like Jesus, so it must be Jesus.** Tabor notes that the John the Baptist sharing teaching sounds "like Jesus," and he treats that resemblance as a reason to read the source as a separate tradition, not as a settled attribution. He does not claim the teaching is Jesus's.
