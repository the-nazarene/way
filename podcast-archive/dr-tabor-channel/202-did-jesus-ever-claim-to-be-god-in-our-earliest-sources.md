## Summary

Paul Williams hosts this second part of a two-part conversation with James Tabor, and Tabor carries most of the talk. The question is whether Jesus of Nazareth ever claimed to be God, meaning the one God of the Hebrew Bible, and whether his earliest followers believed he was. Tabor frames the Jesus movement as a messianic movement within late Second Temple Judaism and argues that none of its strands expected a Messiah who was God or divine (02:19).

Tabor's method is to start with the earliest sources. He reconstructs "Q," the material Matthew and Luke share that is not in Mark, using the Lukan version, and he treats Mark as the earliest narrative. He then turns to Paul's letters, which he calls the earliest Christian writings. He reads Q's temptation scene, the Son of Man sayings against Daniel 7, the "Lord" and "good teacher" passages, the Shema-based answer in Mark 12, and Philippians 2 as all pointing to a strictly monotheistic picture in which God anoints and exalts the Messiah. He treats Johannine-style lines in Q, the Trinitarian baptism formula in Matthew 28, and some passages in Josephus and Revelation as later additions shaped by theological disputes. Tabor cites Kermit Zarley and Bart Ehrman as support.

The talk ends with Tabor's view of judgment as described in Matthew 25 and Revelation 20, where people are judged by their deeds rather than by doctrinal belief. Williams closes by summarizing that the early sources are theocentric rather than Christocentric and non-Trinitarian. Throughout, Tabor presents his reading as an argument from the texts and says he is describing what the sources say, not offering a settled verdict.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Q, the earliest sayings source, is strictly monotheistic

Tabor says Q is reconstructed from the material Matthew and Luke share that is absent from Mark. He prints a Lukan-based extract, which he describes as about 50 pericopes, and notes that scholars debate whether Q was a document or an oral tradition (07:54 to 09:23). He argues that Q has no cross, no atoning blood, and no death-of-Jesus theology, and that its opening scenes are theocentric: John the Baptist's call to repent before the God of Abraham (10:08). In the temptation scene, Tabor says Jesus answers Satan with Deuteronomy-style monotheism ("You shall worship the Lord your God, and him only shall you serve") (12:44). He concludes that calling Jesus "son of God" cannot mean he is God, because Adam is also called a son of God in Luke (12:44).

### 2. "Son of man" comes from Daniel 7 and does not mean divinity

Tabor argues that the title son of man is commonly misread as a divine figure. He reads Daniel 7:13-14 (Aramaic), where Daniel sees the Ancient of Days on the throne and a "bar enosh," a human figure, approaching him. He says the interpretation given in the vision is that "the people of the most high" will take over the kingdoms and that the human figure represents them (20:05 to 20:51). He compares Isaiah 6, where seeing the throne of God does not make Isaiah God. He also reads Jesus' sayings about the coming of the son of man as the eschatological vindication of God's people, and he links this to Mark 14:62 and to the reply he attributes to James in Hegesippus (58:10 to 59:48). Tabor adds that the Q apocalyptic urgency ("the kingdom of God has come upon you") did not play out as expected (27:22).

### 3. Q's Isaiah 61 and Malachi 3 passages present Jesus as a human agent

Tabor argues that when John the Baptist's emissaries ask whether Jesus is "the one who is to come," Jesus answers by pointing to his works and Isaiah 61. Tabor says the Isaiah text speaks of an anointed figure distinct from the Lord who anoints him, which makes "the Lord has anointed me" a statement of commission, not identity (16:02). In the John passage (Luke 7:24-35 as Tabor cites it), he reads "among those born of women, none is greater than John" as incompatible with Jesus claiming to be God, and he calls the added clause "yet he who is least in the kingdom is greater than he" a probable later editorial gloss (17:27 to 18:58). He points to Matthew's Hebrew version, which lacks that clause, as evidence (18:58).

### 4. "Lord" in English translations causes confusion

Tabor argues that the English word "Lord" blurs the Hebrew divine name (rendered LORD in RSV and KJV) and the Greek *kurios*, which can mean "master" or an honorific. He says that when Christians hear "the Lord" in the New Testament they often assume Jesus is meant. He suggests that "master," "rabbi," or "teacher" would avoid the problem, and he notes that a good-teacher address in Mark 10:17 is "good teacher," not "good God" (49:40). In Mark 10:18, Jesus' reply "No one is good but God alone" is read by Tabor through the Shema's emphasis on God alone (33:06).

### 5. The greatest commandment in Mark 12 is the Shema

Tabor argues that Mark 12:28-34 gives the first commandment as the Shema ("Hear O Israel... the Lord is one") with love of God as its response, and the second as loving one's neighbor. He says that Christians often name the second commandment as the first, which he finds puzzling, and he connects the Shema's oneness (*ahad*) to Quranic usage (37:13 to 40:23). He says the scribe's approval and Jesus' reply "You're not far from the kingdom of God" show that the passage is Torah-centered, not Christological (38:26).

### 6. Paul's language is monotheistic; Philippians 2 is read through Genesis 3

Tabor argues that Paul is the earliest Christian writer and that his letters share the early Jewish monotheism of Q and Mark. He cites 1 Thessalonians (turning from idols to serve the living God) and 1 Corinthians 8:4-6 (one God, the Father, and one master, Jesus, "through whom are all things") (44:32 to 47:51). He argues that Paul's "one master" language still points to the one God.

On Philippians 2:5-11, Tabor says the passage is often read as the incarnation of a pre-existent divine being. He argues that the passage's sequence works against that reading when read against Genesis 3, where the serpent tempts Adam and Eve to grasp equality with God (*Elohim*). On Tabor's reading, Jesus is contrasted with Adam: he does not grasp for equality but takes the form of a servant, and God then exalts him so that people give glory to God the Father (52:04 to 56:38). Tabor also says the cosmic naming of Jesus in Philippians is Paul's teaching and would have been a problem for James or the early Jewish followers (57:24).

### 7. Textual additions and the Johannine "bolt from the blue"

Tabor argues that several passages were added or altered by later scribes. He identifies Q's Luke 10:21-22 (no one knows the Son except the Father) as a "Johannine bolt from the blue," a phrase he attributes to Bultmann, and says it conflicts with the surrounding Q material (28:55 to 30:29). He says Matthew 28's baptism "in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit" is absent from the Hebrew Matthew that George Howard studied and from Origen's copy (1:03:01 to 1:04:42). He cites Bart Ehrman's *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* and *Jesus Interrupted* and says Ehrman documents Christian scribal changes to Christological texts (1:04:42 to 1:07:10). He gives Josephus's Testimonium (*Antiquities* 18) as an example, saying that removing the likely interpolations leaves a coherent account (1:05:55). He also says Revelation has about two dozen "Jesus" insertions in its visionary sections, which he says he has removed in a version posted on his blog (1:08:45 to 1:09:30).

### 8. Judgment in the early sources is by deeds

Tabor argues that Matthew 25 portrays the final judgment of the nations by how people treated others, with no mention of baptism or atoning blood. He says Revelation 20 similarly judges people by "what they had done," and that Paul also says God judges each person by deeds (1:12:56 to 1:13:41). He contrasts this with popular evangelical "fire insurance" thinking and mentions Pascal's wager, which he rejects (1:14:37 to 1:15:26).

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Messiah was expected to be God.** Tabor says no Second Temple messianic movement expected a divine Messiah; the Messiah is one anointed and commissioned by God (02:19, 1:01:50).
- **The "Lord" of the New Testament is Jesus.** Tabor says the English "Lord" obscures the divine name and the Greek *kurios*, which can be an honorific, and that Christians often read Jesus into it (49:40).
- **"Son of man" means a divine figure.** Tabor argues it comes from Daniel 7 and refers to a human figure representing God's people (20:05).
- **Jesus claimed to be God in Mark 2.** Tabor says the passage has Jesus declaring delegated authority to forgive sins, and that Matthew reads it as God giving such authority to humans (42:01 to 43:42).
- **The Trinitarian baptism formula is original to Matthew.** Tabor says it is absent from the Hebrew Matthew and from Origen's copy (1:03:01).
- **Philippians 2 describes the incarnation of a pre-existing God.** Tabor reads it against Genesis 3 as contrasting Jesus with Adam, who grasped for equality with God (52:04 to 56:38).
- **The greatest commandment is love of neighbor.** Tabor says Mark 12 gives the Shema first and love of neighbor second (39:13).
- **Salvation depends on believing the right things.** Tabor says Matthew 25 and Revelation 20 judge people by their deeds (1:12:56 to 1:13:41).

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor argues that Q, Mark and Paul share a monotheism in which God anoints and exalts the Messiah, so Paul's language does not depart from Jesus' teaching on the question of divinity (44:32 to 47:51). He does not discuss how Paul differs from Jesus on the law or the sacrificial system.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor relies on Hegesippus's account of James's reply in Jerusalem and reads it as an appeal to Daniel 7 rather than a claim that Jesus is God. He says Paul's exalted-Christ language "would have been a real problem for James or any of the early followers" (57:24 to 59:48).
- **Nazarenes, Ebionites, Essenes:** Not discussed in this talk.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet, nonviolence:** The Shema and Mark 12 are presented as Torah-centered. Sacrifice is discussed only as Paul's 1 Corinthians 8 concern about meat offered to idols (45:25 to 46:58).
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Tabor reads Daniel 7 and Q as apocalyptic expectation that God's people will take over the kingdoms of the world, and he notes the urgency that did not materialize (20:51 to 27:22).
- **Resurrection, afterlife, hell:** Tabor says Matthew 25 and Revelation 20 judge people by deeds, not by belief, and he contrasts this with popular ideas of a guaranteed heaven (1:12:56 to 1:15:26).
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor's answer is a commissioned, anointed human agent, not God (16:02, 1:01:50).
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor argues that Matthew, Luke, Josephus and Revelation were altered by later scribes to support Christological views, and he cites Ehrman's work on this (1:04:42 to 1:09:30).
