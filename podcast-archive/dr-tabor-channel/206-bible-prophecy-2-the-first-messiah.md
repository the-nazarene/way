## Summary

Tabor asks whether there was a Messiah before Jesus. He answers yes, in a generic sense: the Teacher of Righteousness, a leader of the Dead Sea Scrolls community who, he says, flourished about a century before Jesus. Tabor notes that Josephus records many messianic claimants but not this figure, and that the man is never named in the Scrolls. He uses "Messiah" in the broad sense Michael Wise and Israel Knohl used in their 2000 book *The First Messiah*: a figure who is anointed, chosen or designated to bring history to its climax.

His evidence is a chain of Scroll texts. The Community Rule (cave 1, column 8) has the group go into the wilderness "to prepare the way," quoting Isaiah 40:3, and Tabor links this to the opening of Mark. The Damascus Document places the Teacher's appearance 410 years after Nebuchadnezzar, following 20 years of groping. Tabor reads the Teacher's death as the start of a 40-year countdown to the final judgment, which he finds in the Psalm 37 commentary (4Q171). He then combines this with the 49-year Jubilee cycle in 11Q13 and Daniel 9's 490 years, and reads Josephus's account of Jewish expectations as showing that many people shared the same timetable.

The talk ends with the failure of that timetable. The 40 years pass without the end. Tabor shows that the Habakkuk commentary reinterprets Habakkuk 2:3 to say the final age "shall be prolonged," so the prophecy is kept alive. He closes with the line "if it tarries wait for it," which he suggests was written over the community's doorpost. He presents all of this as the group's own interpretation, not as settled history. The talk is a single presentation by Tabor, and he promises a follow-up on Daniel and a course on the Scrolls.

## Key Teachings

### The Teacher of Righteousness as a pre-Jesus Messianic figure
Tabor argues that the Scrolls' Teacher of Righteousness (which he says can also be translated "the right teacher" or "the true teacher") is the first Messiah in Western history, and that the community saw him as the prophet like Moses who revealed the mysteries of prophecy. Tabor says the Teacher was raised up by God to guide the community, that he was persecuted by the Jerusalem establishment, and that the Scrolls describe him as killed. He is not named in the texts. Tabor also emphasizes that the Scrolls expect three figures: the prophet and two Messiahs, "of Aaron and Israel" (Community Rule, column 10).

### Wilderness preparation and the parallel with John the Baptist
In the Community Rule (column 8), the group separates from "the habitation of unjust men" to "prepare the way in the wilderness," quoting Isaiah 40:3. Column 9 ties this preparation to a set time. Tabor says Mark opens with the same idea applied to John the Baptist, and that the community was "calling out people to repent of their sins to be immersed in water." He presents the parallel as striking, while noting that he is not developing the wider parallels in this segment.

### The chronology: 410 years, 20 years, and a 40-year countdown
The Damascus Document's opening describes God punishing Israel, giving them over to Nebuchadnezzar, and then visiting them "after 390 years." The group then spent "20 years" like blind men until God raised up the Teacher, which Tabor reads as 410 years after Nebuchadnezzar. Tabor says that the Gathering in of the Teacher (his death) in Damascus Document copy B begins a period of about 40 years until the coming of the Messiah "out of Aaron and Israel." The Psalm 37 commentary (4Q171) applies "a little while" to "the end of 40 years," when the wicked will be blotted out. Tabor stresses that the group's own dating system was not ours and that the Scrolls can be dated to the first century BCE on paleographic and other grounds, though a precise year cannot be fixed.

### Jubilees, 490 years and Daniel 9
Tabor reads 11Q13 (the Melchizedek text) as referring to the end of the tenth Jubilee, when the sons of Light will be atoned for. He argues that a biblical Jubilee is 49 years, so ten Jubilees come to 490 years, which he links to the 490 years in Daniel 9. He reasons that the group placed the Teacher's death within the tenth Jubilee and therefore within the final 40 years. He also notes 4Q174, which refers to "the time of which it is written in the Book of Daniel," and to the wicked not understanding while the righteous purify themselves.

### Josephus on the Jewish expectation of a ruler
Tabor reads from *Jewish War* book 6 that Josephus said the Jewish revolt was driven by an oracle that "at that time one from their country would become ruler of the world." Tabor takes this as a reference to Daniel 9. He says that Josephus historicizes the prophecy by applying it to the Roman emperor and the destruction of the Temple, removing it from its apocalyptic and messianic setting. Tabor also says the Romans (the Kittim in the War Scroll) entered Egypt and then took the land under Herod, which he presents as the kind of event that made the countdown seem to be running out.

### Prophecy reinterpreted: the Habakkuk commentary
Tabor reads the Habakkuk commentary, where the "wicked" is the Wicked Priest and the "righteous" is the Teacher, and the council in Jerusalem "stayed silent" at the Teacher's persecution. He says the group "read themselves into" Habakkuk, which he presents as a pattern common among prophetic movements. He says the commentary claims God told Habakkuk to write the final generation but not when it would end, and that the Teacher knew "all the mysteries of the words of his servants the prophets." Habakkuk 2:3 is read as saying that "the final age shall be prolonged," so the 40-year failure is absorbed. Tabor reads Habakkuk 2:4 ("the righteous will live by his faith") as meaning faith in the Teacher, and contrasts this with how Paul uses the verse. He also compares the delay to 2 Peter's "a day with the Lord is a thousand years."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That Jesus was the first Messiah.** Tabor argues that the Teacher of Righteousness, about a century earlier, is a Messianic figure in the generic sense (anointed, chosen, designated to bring history to its end), and that the Scrolls describe him in those terms.
- **That Josephus documents all the pre-Jesus Messiahs.** Tabor says Josephus names many messianic claimants, usually with their names and fates, but not this figure, which is why he was little known until the Scrolls were found in 1947.
- **That the Jewish Jubilee is 50 years.** Tabor says that, for the purposes of the Scroll chronology, a biblical Jubilee is 49 years, with the 50th year counted as the first year of the next cycle.
- **That Josephus simply recorded Jewish messianic expectations accurately.** Tabor argues that Josephus reinterpreted the Daniel 9 oracle, applying it to Rome and the Temple's destruction and removing its apocalyptic and messianic meaning.
