## Summary

Tabor asks what makes a messiah and whether anyone has ever done what the Hebrew Bible says the future Davidic ruler will do. He starts with the word itself: *mashiach* comes from the verb *mashak*, "to smear with oil," and he cites Exodus 30:25 and the anointing of Aaron and his sons. He then shows that anointing in the Hebrew Bible applies to priests, to kings such as Saul and David (1 Samuel 10:1), and even to the patriarchs (Psalm 105:13) and to the Persian king Cyrus (Isaiah 45). From this he argues that "Messiah" is a broad term, not a title reserved for one figure, and that "Christ" should not be read as Jesus's surname.

The core of the talk is his claim that only about ten passages in the Hebrew prophets speak specifically of a future ideal king from David's line, and that none of them calls this figure "the Messiah." He says he will cover six of the ten in this episode (Isaiah 9:6-7; Isaiah 11; the Isaiah 16 passage on the "tabernacle of David"; Jeremiah 23:5-6; Zechariah 3; and Zechariah 6) and the other four in a second part. For each he summarizes what the ruler will do: establish justice and righteousness, gather the scattered tribes of Israel, bring peace to nature and the nations, and, in the Zechariah texts, build the temple alongside a priest. He also notes that some Christians read Isaiah 9:6 as naming the Messiah "Mighty God," and says he tries to fit that into a Trinitarian idea, but he reads the child as a human birth.

The episode ends without a verdict. Tabor says the second part will treat the remaining four passages and draw conclusions, and he leaves his audience with the closing question: has anyone in history from David's line sat on David's throne and done these things? ## Key Teachings

### The meaning of "anointed" and the broad use of the term
Tabor argues that *mashiach* means "anointed one," from the act of pouring or smearing oil on the head. He traces its first use to Exodus 30:25, where Moses is told to make a holy anointing oil, and to Aaron, whom Moses anoints as the first priest. He says the anointing in ancient Israel was a "performative ritual," in which the words and the oil confer the office, and compares it loosely to a presidential inauguration. He states that the Hebrew term is used for priests, for kings (Saul, then David, in 1 Samuel 10:1), for the patriarchs (Psalm 105:13), and for the Gentile king Cyrus (Isaiah 45). His conclusion is that "Christ is not a special word, it's not Jesus's last name," and he recommends keeping to the English "anoint" to avoid the Greek *Christos*, which he says "gets very misleading."

### Two things God is said not to want: a king and a temple
Tabor contends that the Hebrew Bible presents kingship and the temple as things God did not originally want. He cites 1 Samuel, where Samuel is told that asking for an anointed king is "like the Nations" and "not really a positive thing," and 2 Samuel 7, where God tells David he has been content in a tent and asks what house David will build for him. He notes that many modern Jews and Christians nonetheless look forward to the Messiah's return and the rebuilding of the temple, and he presents this as a tension in present-day expectations.

### Ten texts about a future Davidic king, none calling him "Messiah"
Tabor's central claim is that only ten passages in the Hebrew prophets speak directly of a future ideal ruler from David's line, as distinct from priests, earlier kings, Cyrus, or other anointed figures. He says he numbers them one through ten and that none of the ten uses the word "Messiah" for this figure, so that "our concept and our vocabulary" are out of step with the texts. He presents the ten as a "job description" and says they are "quite consistent" with one another.

### Isaiah 9:6-7 and Isaiah 11: justice, peace, and a transformed creation
Of the Isaiah 9 child, Tabor says the passage "seems pretty clear" that the child will be a Davidic king who sits on the throne of David and rules with justice and righteousness forever. He notes that the child's name ("wonderful counselor," "Mighty God," "Everlasting father," "Prince of Peace") is praise of God in Hebrew naming, and that the "Mighty God" reading is a Christian one he tries to fit into a Trinitarian idea. In Isaiah 11, he reads the "rod" and "branch" from the stump of Jesse as the Davidic line restored after it was "cut off" in the Babylonian captivity. He describes the reign as spirit-filled, judging the poor with equity, and bringing peace even among animals. He also says the branch will "stand as an ensign to the peoples" and gather exiles of Israel and Judah "from the four corners of the earth," so that the message is worldwide and not local.

### Jeremiah 23:5-6 and the "Branch" name
Tabor reads Jeremiah 23:5-6 (repeated nearly verbatim in Jeremiah 33) as a righteous "branch" from David who will reign, execute judgment and righteousness, save Judah, and let Israel "dwell safely." He stresses the name "Yahweh is our righteousness" and the regathering of all the tribes.

### Zechariah 3 and 6: a king and a priest together
In the Zechariah passages Tabor finds a stone laid before Joshua, the high priest of Zechariah's time, with seven eyes or facets and the promise that God will "remove the iniquity of that land in one day." He says the Persians permitted the temple's rebuilding but not an indigenous Davidic king, so the prophet uses the stone image instead. In the sixth passage, the man "whose name is the Branch" builds the temple, sits and rules on his throne, and has a priest beside him, a "Council of Peace" between them. Tabor reads this as two anointed figures, a king and a priest who acts as spiritual adviser.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That there are dozens of prophecies about the Messiah.** Tabor says most people think so, but only about ten passages in the Hebrew prophets speak of a future Davidic ruler, and he says one of them "might be in doubt."
- **That "Christ" is Jesus's last name.** He says "Christ is not a special word, it's not Jesus's last name," and that the word simply translates "anointed."
- **That the Messiah is a unique figure.** He says the Hebrew Bible uses "anointed one" for priests, kings, the patriarchs, and even the Persian king Cyrus, so the term is broader than the popular idea.
- **That the Hebrew Bible endorses a king and a temple.** He argues that God says he did not want a king or a temple, and that the present-day hope for both is not what the texts emphasize.
- **That Isaiah 9:6 names the Messiah as "Mighty God."** He says some Christian readers take it this way and try to read it Trinitarianly, but he takes the child as a human born to the Davidic line, with the titles as praise of God.
