## Summary

Tabor asks what the Hebrew Bible says about a future davidic Messiah, a descendant of King David who will come "at the end of days," and whether anyone has ever fulfilled that job description. This is part two of a two-part episode. In part one he covered the wider Messianic texts. Here he works through what he calls ten basic Hebrew prophetic texts usually read as references to a davidic king.

He carries the whole talk himself, using slides and his blog. He states at the outset that Muslims and Christians identify this figure with Jesus of Nazareth, who would return a second time, while most Jews still await him. He then goes through the texts in order: Isaiah 9, Isaiah 11, Isaiah 16, Jeremiah 23, Zechariah 3 and 6, Psalm 110, Zechariah 9, Amos 9, and Micah 5. For several he discusses translation options and what the text says the king will do: rule in peace, end warfare, and govern from sea to sea. He also argues that Psalm 110 should not be read as a reference to the Melchizedek of Hebrews, and that Micah's "Bethlehem Ephrathah" may name a clan descended from Judah rather than a town.

He ends without a verdict. He says he will map the ten texts in a grid in the next episode, and closes with the question he leaves to viewers: "has anyone ever fulfilled any of these things yet."

### Key Teachings

### The ten-text framework
Tabor says he counts ten basic texts in the Hebrew prophets that are usually taken as references to a future davidic king. Numbers 1 to 6 cover Isaiah 9, Isaiah 11, Isaiah 16, Jeremiah 23 (repeated in 33, not counted twice), Zechariah 3, and Zechariah 6. Number 7 is Psalm 110, which he calls new to this talk. Numbers 8 to 10 are Zechariah 9, Amos 9, and Micah 5. He says he will tabulate the characteristics of all ten in the next episode.

### Isaiah 9:6 and the names
On Isaiah 9 he prefers a translation in which the names "wonderful counselor," "Mighty God," "Everlasting father," and "Prince of Peace" are attributes of God, not the child's titles. He explains this by pointing to other Isaiah passages where children have long meaningful names.

### Zechariah's "branch" and the priest beside the throne
In Zechariah 3 and 6, the "branch" (Hebrew *tsemach*) is a descendant of David. Tabor notes that Cyrus, though anointed, was allowed to free the exiles but not to anoint a king, and that Zechariah 6 pairs a davidic ruler who will build the temple and sit on his throne with a priest beside him, with "the council of peace" shared between them.

### Psalm 110 and Melchizedek
Tabor calls Psalm 110:1-7 "a difficult text" and "very triumphant." He says it is the text most quoted in the New Testament about Messianic hope, and that Jesus cites it in the temple in Jerusalem, asking why the scribes say the Messiah is David's son. On the priestly line, he argues that "you are a priest forever ... after the order of Melchizedek" does not have to refer to the Melchizedek of Hebrews, because Genesis does not give a new priestly order. He reads the king as someone of davidic lineage, not Aaronic, who is given priestly standing.

### Humble and victorious: Zechariah 9 and Solomon's donkey
Tabor argues that the Zechariah 9 figure rides an ass, which he reads as signaling humility and service, and he compares it with Solomon riding a donkey to the Gihon spring. The passage promises that chariots and war bows will be cut off, that the king will command peace to the nations, and that his dominion will run "from sea to sea" and "to the ends of the Earth." He notes that the New Testament claims Jesus fulfilled this, but says the peace has not yet happened.

### Amos 9 and the fallen tent
Amos 9 speaks of raising up "the booth [tent] of David." Tabor connects this to the davidic line being cut off at the Babylonian conquest and to the last Davidic kings, including Zedekiah and Jeconiah.

### Micah 5:2 and "Bethlehem Ephrathah"
Tabor asks whether Micah's "Bethlehem Ephrata" is a town or a person. He argues from 1 Chronicles 4 that Ephrathah was a firstborn son of Judah's line and the father of Bethlehem, so the phrase names a clan of Judah, not a place. He notes that the passage's context is the northern captivity and an ideal future, and that the ruler will shepherd in the strength of Jehovah. He says this figure is a Jew who worships God.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Bethlehem as the birthplace of a king.** Tabor says Micah 5:2 names a clan of Judah, not a town, citing 1 Chronicles 4 to show "Bethlehem" is a person in the lineage.
- **Psalm 110 as a Melchizedek prophecy.** He argues Psalm 110 addresses a davidic king and does not refer to the Melchizedek of Hebrews.
- **Messiah always means one future davidic king.** He notes that "Messiah" (anointed) is applied to Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Cyrus, and that priests and kings were both anointed.
