## Summary

Tabor asks whether he has located the latrines of the community that wrote the Dead Sea Scrolls at Qumran, and what their choice of location says about how they understood holiness and Torah purity. He frames the question as a matter of method: he wants the site and the texts to be read as two sources that do not override each other, and he distinguishes his approach from the older "Bible in one hand, spade in the other" style of biblical archaeology. He treats the community as Essene, following Josephus's description, and calls the site a probable spiritual headquarters.

His main argument rests on texts. He reads Deuteronomy's instruction to relieve oneself outside the camp, Josephus's account of Essene practice (a small pit about a foot deep, dug away from others, then covered), and the Damascus Document's Sabbath limit of 1,000 cubits. He also cites the Temple Scroll's latrine precinct and the War Scroll's distance rule. Together these lead him to predict that the latrines lay northwest of the settlement, beyond the Mikveh and out of view, on a bluff. With Joe Zias and others he took soil samples there to test the hypothesis. The samples contained human fecal microorganisms and tapeworm parasites, and Tabor reports that Zias links this contamination to the community's ritual immersion and to a low adult male survival rate at the cemetery. Tabor says the result is a "possibility" that he has not proven, because the material was not dated and other users of the area are possible. He ends by inviting viewers to his online course on the Scrolls and saying he hopes future work will revisit the question.

## Key Teachings

### The method: site and text as separate checks
Tabor argues that archaeology should not be done to prove the Bible. He says he is not an archaeologist by training (his PhD is in texts), but he has worked on about a dozen projects and co-directed four or five excavations. He points to the 1998 renaming of the American Schools' journal from *Biblical Archaeologist* to *Near Eastern Archaeology* as a sign of the field's preference for pure archaeology. He still accepts Wright's 1938 aim of "a readable non-technical yet thoroughly reliable account of archaeological discoveries as they are related to the Bible," so long as the work is driven by the evidence.

### Deuteronomy and the camp
Tabor reads Deuteronomy's instruction that a latrine be placed outside the camp, with a tool to dig a hole and cover the excrement, because "the Lord your God walks in the midst of your camp." He cites the caption's chapter-and-verse as "23:2", but the passage he reads concerns the camp latrine. He argues that the Scrolls' community imagined itself as a wilderness camp replicating the days of Moses, so the same separation of the holy from the ordinary applied to its latrines.

### Purity categories in the Hellenistic world
Tabor explains purity as separation between the sacred and the ordinary, and says that blood, sex and death were the main sources of impurity. He quotes a rule for visitors to a temple of Athena that required abstention from intercourse before entering, and he says the concern is not that sex is bad but that it makes one ritually unfit for the sacred space. He sees the Qumran practice as one expression of a widespread Mediterranean pattern.

### The Scrolls' ideal future and the wilderness setting
Tabor reads the Temple Scroll's future temple, with latrines in a designated precinct northwest of the city, as an ideal that he thinks the community was already applying in the desert. He reads the War Scroll's statement that the children of light's latrine lies 2,000 cubits away, and the Damascus Document's Sabbath limit of 1,000 cubits, as supporting evidence. He says the War Scroll does not specify northwest, and treats the northwest direction as an inference drawn from the drawings and the other texts.

### Josephus's description of the Essenes
Tabor quotes Josephus on the Essenes' stricter Sabbath observance, their refusal to carry vessels, and their practice of digging a small pit with a paddle-like tool, covering it with a garment, and then covering it with earth in lonely places. He notes Josephus's statement that the Essenes were spread through the country and that they married and had children, which he uses to argue against a simple "monastery" model for Qumran. He suggests the settlement may have been a spiritual headquarters with communal living, not necessarily lifelong celibacy.

### The site: Mikveh, cemetery and the test
Tabor describes the settlement's ritual pool (Mikveh), the cemetery of about 1,000 graves oriented north-south with the heads to the south, and a bluff northwest of the settlement hidden from view. The team tested soil at depth (about a foot down, following Josephus) and at control points labeled areas A and B. The result, which Tabor calls "the high cost of Holiness," was human-specific microorganisms and tapeworm parasites. He notes that people were re-entering the Mikveh for ritual immersion, so contamination could spread through the water.

### Health and the low survival rate
Tabor cites Zias on the skeletal sample at the cemetery, in which only about 6% of adult males survived to 40, compared with roughly 50% at Jericho in a similar environment. Tabor argues that a low survival rate in antiquity is not the same as "everybody died at 40." He says that if people had survived adolescence they often lived to 50, 60 or 70, and that the low overall average reflects high infant and youth mortality. He presents the contamination as a possible contributing factor and says the idea is Zias's to defend.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Biblical archaeology" is a legitimate way to prove the Bible.** Tabor says good anthropology departments reject the "Bible in one hand, spade in the other" approach, and that the site must speak for itself.
- **Ancient people all died at about 40.** Tabor says that if people survived childhood they often lived to 50, 60 or 70, and that the low average reflects high infant and youth mortality.
- **The Essenes lived as celibate monks.** Tabor uses Josephus to say they married and had children, and that inside the settlement the life may not have been lifelong celibacy.
- **Qumran was a "monastery."** Tabor says this "christianizes" the site too much.
