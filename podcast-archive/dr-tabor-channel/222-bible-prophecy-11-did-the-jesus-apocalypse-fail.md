## Summary

Tabor asks whether the "Jesus Apocalypse" of Mark 13, the discourse in which Jesus is said to describe the events of his generation, failed. He reads it as a rewrite of Daniel 11 and 12, and he compares it side by side with its two later retellings in Matthew 24 and Luke 21. He treats Mark as the source that Matthew and Luke revise, and he presents the Jewish War of 66-70 CE and Josephus's accounts as the context in which the prophecy was tested. He dates Mark roughly to the 70s and argues that its "this generation" language was meant to be read as a near-term expectation.

Tabor argues that Matthew and Luke each responded to the same failed expectation. Luke moves the "signs" beyond the destruction of Jerusalem and inserts "until the times of the Gentiles are fulfilled," and Matthew adds parables about a delayed master and bridesmaids. Tabor surveys the strategies that later groups used to handle such failures, including resetting dates, postulating a second fulfillment, dropping date-setting, and spiritualizing the prophecy. He sides with Albert Schweitzer's "thoroughgoing eschatology" and concludes that the expectation did fail. He also links Paul's pre-70 expectations to the same framework, citing Romans 9-11 and the work of Jason Staples.

The talk closes with Tabor's summary of late second-temple expectation and a statement that the next videos will cover the rest of Paul's letters and Revelation, as well as the shift he sees after the late first century. Most of the argument rests on the reader's own comparison of the three texts and on Tabor's reading of Daniel.

## Key Teachings

### Mark 13 as a rewrite of Daniel 11-12
Tabor says Mark 13 "is simply going through Daniel 11 and 12 and interpreting it." The "desolating sacrilege" is modeled on Antiochus Epiphanes (165-163 BCE, the Maccabean period). Tabor argues that because the end did not come in that period, the author applies the pattern to a later oppressor, the Romans, and to the destruction of Jerusalem. He cites Daniel 11:31 for the pattern of foreign forces profaning the temple, stopping the daily offering, and setting up the abomination. He presents the Mark 13 "sign" as a coded instruction to flee Judea, which he ties to the Jerusalem flight in 66 CE.

### Matthew and Luke as two different responses to the delay
Tabor argues that Luke relocates the discourse. He notes that Luke 21:5 has "some" speaking of the temple, not disciples, and that Luke drops the "Mount of Olives" setting. Luke also adds "Jerusalem surrounded by armies" and "days of Vengeance," and he inserts "until the times of the Gentiles are fulfilled." Tabor says this "until" works like "a rubber band" that can keep stretching, and that Luke separates the destruction of 70 CE from the cosmic signs that follow.

Tabor says Matthew "follows Mark real carefully" in this chapter, but adds the "Gospel of the Kingdom will be preached throughout the whole world" line and the "end will come" sequence. Matthew 25 then supplies four delay-themed stories: the thief in the night, the wicked servant who says "my master is delayed," the ten bridesmaids, and the master's long journey. Tabor reads these as Matthew's community being told that the wait would be longer than expected.

### The "generation" and "seal of infallibility" problem
Tabor argues that Mark 13 declares "this generation will not pass before all these things take place," and that this "puts the Seal of infallibility on these predictions." He sees Mark's generation as probably "just after 70," and he reads Luke as shifting "this generation" so that it no longer refers to the same people. He reads Matthew as the most explicit about the coming of the son of man "in the clouds" as a later, cosmic event. In Tabor's account, the text is thus being interpreted in ways that keep it from failing.

### Paul's pre-70 expectation
Tabor cites 1 Corinthians 7 ("the appointed time has grown very short") as the earliest New Testament statement of this expectation, and he links it to Daniel 11-12 language. He cites 2 Thessalonians 2 as Paul's or the Pauline school's interpretation of the "desolating sacrilege" as a ruler setting himself up as God. Reading from his own book, Tabor says that Paul's hope for the redemption of Israel through his Gentile mission "became more and more hopeless by 70 CE." Tabor also mentions Caligula's 41 CE attempt to place his statue in the temple as the kind of event that would have fueled such expectations. He points to Romans 9-11 and to Jason Staples's study of Romans 11 as showing a future expectation "that never happens."

### Strategies for failed expectation
Tabor lists several common responses. (1) Reset the dates, as in Daniel-based calculations, Isaac Newton, William Miller, and Seventh-day Adventists. (2) Create a dual or second fulfillment, which he says is how Daniel itself works, and which dispensationalists (John Darby) use with a rebuilt Jerusalem. (3) Stop setting dates, which he associates with the Dead Sea Scrolls community and with rabbinic and Talmudic material in Sanhedrin 97-98. (4) Spiritualize the prophecy so that it was fulfilled in the church. He associates the last with Augustine, with "full preterists" on YouTube, and with Ellen G. White and Charles Taze Russell, whose 1914 date he discusses. Tabor says he is opposed to this last approach.

### James's followers and the flight to the Decapolis
Tabor says that James was killed in 63, and that James's followers "appointed Simon either a cousin or a brother of Jesus" as second in line. He says they "left the country in the year 66, three years before the destruction" and went to the Decapolis area across the Jordan. He connects this to the flight instruction in Mark 13 and says he will release more detail on a "Jesus Hideout across the Jordan" in a later video.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That Mark 13 contains Jesus's own words about the end, and that billions of Christians therefore read it as his literal prediction.** The description notes that "we are reading the words of the author of Mark, not Jesus directly," and Tabor frames Mark 13 as the Gospel writer's text, shaped after 70 CE.
- **That the Bible's apocalyptic predictions can be seen as simple "contradictions" to be counted.** Tabor says "some people are just into like Bible contradictions that's not the point here"; the point is to compare the retellings as literary and historical responses.
- **That the apocalyptic expectation is unfulfilled only in a literal sense, and can be read as fulfilled spiritually or in a second fulfillment.** Tabor rejects the realized and full-preterist readings and the dual-fulfillment move, arguing that the expectation "failed" and that historians should not use these strategies.
- **That Paul's theology is separate from the apocalyptic Judaism of his time.** Tabor argues Paul "is very much following his apocalyptic Judaism," with an expectation of Israel's redemption that did not happen.
