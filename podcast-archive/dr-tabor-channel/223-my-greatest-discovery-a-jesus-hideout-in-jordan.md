## Summary

Tabor asks where Jesus spent the winter before the Passover he believes ended his life (he places the winter around 29 CE, with the death in 30 CE). He notes that Mark and John both place Jesus across the Jordan at this stage. His answer is a region on the east bank, near the Wadi el-Yabis (which he identifies with the biblical brook Kerith), just north of Pella in the Decapolis and close to the border of Herod Antipas's territory. He calls it "the Jesus hideout in Jordan."

He builds the case from several texts: Mark 1:4-5 and Luke 3 on John the Baptist's preaching around the Jordan, John 1:28 and John 3:22-23 on baptizing near Bethany and Anon/Salim, the Q material on John, and Mark 10:1 and John 10:40 on Jesus crossing the Jordan late in his ministry. He pairs these with Mark 7 and Matthew 4 on activity in the Decapolis. He also draws on the 2004 excavation of a baptismal cave in the Judean hills, which he associates with John, and on a site visit to the wadi with students. He argues that the wadi offered a hiding place with water and caves, like Elijah's refuge in 1 Kings 17. Finally, he links the region to the Eusebius tradition that the Jerusalem community fled to Pella before the war of 66-73 CE, and suggests that James was already dead and Simon had taken over leadership.

The talk ends with Tabor's invitation to study the maps and references, and pointers to his blog, his academic paper, Gibson's book on the cave, and his courses. He presents the identification tentatively ("I think I might have been able to identify") and asks viewers for their reactions.

## Key Teachings

### 1. John's Gospel is a usable source for geography and chronology
Tabor argues against treating John as late and useless for narrative geography. He says he uses John's framing (where Jesus goes and when) alongside Mark, which he calls "our earliest gospel narrative," and that Matthew and Luke use Mark extensively. He reports that groups at Society of Biblical Literature sessions have studied John's chronology in this way.

### 2. John the Baptist and Jesus were a baptizing team, divided north and south
Tabor reads Mark 1:4-5 and Luke 3 as placing John up and down the Jordan, with the Judean and Jerusalem crowds in the south. He reads John 3:22-23 as saying Jesus and his disciples baptized in the Judean countryside while John baptized at Anon near Salim in the north. He calls this a division of labour. He says the Q material shows Jesus teaching a prayer "like John taught his disciples" and reports that Jesus says no one born of women is greater than John, which he treats as an early saying that later gospel editors softened. He describes a "least in the kingdom is greater than he" gloss as a later addition.

### 3. The Judean baptismal cave and the Jesus movement's early art
Tabor describes his 2000-2005 excavation, with Shimon Gibson, of a large plastered water reservoir (mikveh) in the Judean hills west of Jerusalem. Images on the walls, which he interprets as John the Baptist (with a staff, a hair garment and matted hair like a Nazirite), are dated by him to the second or third century CE. The excavated fill included early Roman pottery from the time of Jesus, which he says means the cave was used extensively then. He cites an apocryphal text (unnamed in the talk) about Elizabeth and John hiding from Herod in a cave with steps near Ain Karim. He suggests this cave may be a plausible setting for baptisms in a region with no rivers, though he presents this as a possibility.

### 4. Jesus crossed the Jordan in his last winter, to a refuge from Herod's jurisdiction
Tabor's central argument: Mark 10:1 (Jesus goes into Judea "and beyond the Jordan") and John 10:40 (he goes "across the Jordan to the place where John at first baptized") both place Jesus east of the river in the final months, with John 10 placing this at the Hanukkah winter. He says the two traditions are independent. He identifies the destination with the area of Wadi el-Yabis, on the border of Herod Antipas's territory and the Decapolis. There, according to him, one could leave Herod's jurisdiction and Judea's. Mark 7 (return through the Decapolis) and Matthew 4:25 (crowds from "beyond the Jordan") are cited as further evidence of activity there. The Gerasene demoniac episode in Mark 5 is cited as an example of Jesus's activity in the Decapolis.

### 5. The Elijah parallel: Kerith and the refuge of prophets
Tabor says 1 Kings 17 places Elijah hiding at the brook Kerith east of the Jordan, fed by ravens, to escape Ahab and Jezebel. He notes that Mark presents John as "the Elijah to come." He argues that John, baptizing in that wadi, could cross over to the Decapolis if Herod Antipas pressed him, and that Jesus followed the same pattern. The name "W Elis" in Arabic is offered as the modern survival of the name, with "the hard" or "the rugged" as the meaning of the root.

### 6. Field visit: the wadi as a plausible hiding place
Tabor describes a day walking the wadi with students, followed by four later visits. The lower part is open and watered, then narrows and becomes rugged, requiring climbing. He notes caves above a pool on the north side, with water and space to live. He commissioned an artist to draw a scene for *The Jesus Dynasty* showing Jesus with women and followers there, which he calls "the Jesus Entourage." He estimates a few dozen people were with Jesus.

### 7. Pella, the Eusebius tradition, and the Jerusalem community's flight
Tabor identifies Pella (a Decapolis city, about two miles north of the wadi) as the destination of the Jerusalem community's flight before the Jewish Roman Revolt. His main source is Eusebius, "the church historian fourth Century," who says the followers were "commanded by Revelation" to dwell in Pella. He connects this with Mark 13:14-16 (the "abomination of desolation" and flight to the mountains) and with Revelation 12 (the woman fleeing into the wilderness for "a time, times and half a time"). He says Daniel's three-and-a-half-year chronology correlates. He concludes that "this tradition of fleeing the city and going across the Jordan... might have some historical validity," and that Simon, possibly James's brother or cousin, had succeeded James by 66 CE.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **John's Gospel is late, theological, and useless for geography.** Tabor argues that John's chronology and travel framing can be used alongside Mark for location and timing.
- **"Wilderness" means forest.** He says the Greek word should be understood as "desert" in Mark 1:4 and Luke 3, since modern "wilderness" misleads.
- **Archaeology should prove the Bible.** He warns against "treasure hunters" who walk "with the Bible in one hand and a trowel or a spade in the other," and says text and site must be balanced so neither over-interprets the other.
- **John the Baptist is played down throughout the gospels.** He argues the Q material shows John as very prominent, and that the "least in the kingdom" line is a later addition.
