## Summary

Tabor asks where Jesus was crucified and, by extension, where he was buried. He frames this as a question of both historical accuracy and the meaning of sites for believers. He first surveys the two main candidates. The Church of the Holy Sepulchre rests on a tradition he says has no supporting evidence until about 300 years after Jesus's death, and he notes that Hadrian is said to have placed a pagan temple on the spot, which Tabor reads as an attempt to erase a Jewish site, the tomb of John Hyrcanus. Gordon's Calvary and the Garden Tomb fail, in his view, because the tomb is an Iron Age tomb finished centuries before Jesus, while John says the tomb had never been used.

Tabor's positive case has several parts. He locates the trial at the Herodian pavement (Gabbatha) on the western city wall, where he says first-century steps and paving survive. He argues that Hebrews places the crucifixion outside the Eastern Gate, which he ties to the red heifer altar on the Mount of Olives. He adds Josephus's report of mass crucifixions before the city wall during the Revolt and Quintilian's remark about crucifixion on busy roads as support. He prefers the Mount of Olives while also describing Bible Hill and a tomb in the Hinnom valley as alternatives. He ends by calling the Gospel accounts theologically shaped and full of legend, and says he treats them critically, not as proof of the Bible. ## Key Teachings

### The Church of the Holy Sepulchre lacks early evidence
Tabor says he is not convinced the church marks the site. His main objection is that "there's no evidence for this until 300 years after Jesus death." He notes that Constantine's mother is credited with declaring the site, and that Hadrian, after the Second Revolt, is said to have built a pagan temple there. Tabor argues the temple was meant to erase the site's sanctity and that the tomb under it was that of John Hyrcanus, mentioned several times by Josephus, which would make it a symbol of Jewish independence worth suppressing.

### The Garden Tomb (Gordon's Calvary) fails on archaeology and the Gospel of John
He credits General Gordon with the idea that the skull-shaped hill was the site, and credits N. F. Hutchinson (PEQ notes) with arguing that the crucifixion site and the first burial tomb were near each other. Tabor objects that the tomb is an Iron Age tomb "finished centuries before Jesus was even born," and that John says the tomb "had never been used." He also says the scholarly consensus, based on archaeology, excludes this site.

### The trial site: the pavement (Gabbatha) and Herodian remains
Using John 18-19, Tabor argues that Pilate's judgment seat was at a place called the pavement (Gabbatha) and that the Jewish leaders stayed outside the praetorium to avoid ritual defilement before Passover. He identifies the pavement with a site on the western city wall, excavated by Magen Broshi in the 1970s and visited with Shimon Gibson, where he says first-century steps, Herodian stones, and paving survive. He calls it "the site" and says it is hard to see from the Turkish-era wall until the soil was cleared. He links the location to Herod's Palace, where he thinks Pilate stayed, and to a Roman judgment seat parallel to one he saw in Corinth.

### Crucifixion outside the Eastern Gate: Hebrews and the red heifer altar
Tabor reads Hebrews as describing a crucifixion "outside the camp" and "outside the gate." He argues the only gate that fits the Tabernacle and Temple tradition is the Eastern Gate, which leads to the Mount of Olives. He ties this to a special altar on the Mount of Olives, 2,000 cubits east of the Temple, where the red heifer was burned to make ashes for purification. He cites the Tosefta and Mishnah for this location (both are garbled in the caption). He reads Hebrews as written before the Temple's fall, so it is a first-century witness.

### Mass crucifixions and public display: Josephus and Quintilian
Josephus reports that during the Jewish Revolt (66-70), as many as 500 Jewish captives per day were crucified before the city wall, which Tabor thinks means the Mount of Olives, where Roman forces were camped on Mount Scopus. He also quotes Quintilian that criminals were crucified on "the most frequented roads," with punishment meant as an example. Tabor's reading is that the Mount of Olives was visible to the whole city, so the crucifixion there would send a warning.

### Joseph of Arimathea's tomb as a later addition
Tabor argues that John shows the tomb was simply nearby ("since the tomb was close at hand") and was unfinished and temporary, and that the body was later moved and reburied by Joseph. He says Matthew alone calls the tomb Joseph's "own new tomb," and that this is a theological addition meant to fulfill Isaiah 53's "made his grave with a rich man." He calls the detail a "theological interpolation" and says it makes little sense that a convenient tomb would belong to a secret disciple on the Sanhedrin.

### Critical reading of the Gospels and the empty tomb
Tabor says the Gospels "are full of Theology and they're full of myth making," including angels, earthquakes, and people struck dead. He says the empty tomb found by Mary Magdalene on Sunday morning, in his reconstruction, would simply mean the burial party came back to move a temporarily buried body after Passover. He thinks the gospels were written in the first century and are in touch with the evidence in Mark and in John 18-19, which he considers the source of much of the detail.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Church of the Holy Sepulchre marks the place of crucifixion.** Tabor argues the tradition has no early evidence and that Hadrian's temple was an attempt to suppress a Jewish site.
- **The Garden Tomb (Gordon's Calvary) is the authentic tomb.** Tabor says the tomb is Iron Age, finished centuries before Jesus, and that John says it had never been used.
- **Joseph of Arimathea's tomb was his own, as Matthew says.** Tabor argues this is a later theological addition and that John describes a temporary, nearby tomb.
- **Reading the Gospels critically is the same as proving the Bible.** Tabor says he treats the texts as documents full of theology and myth, not as proof of the biblical account.
