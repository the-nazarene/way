## Summary

Host Derek Lambert interviews James D. Tabor about "suffering Messiahs" and whether the Dead Sea Scrolls contain the idea of a Messiah who suffers. Lambert frames the talk as a preview of a Sunday Zoom Q&A for students in Tabor's online course "Jesus and the Dead Sea Scrolls" (MVP Courses), and much of the conversation is course promotion and Black Friday pricing. The substance, though, is Tabor's, and he carries most of it.

Tabor argues that the Dead Sea community (Qumran) was active from before Jesus into the first century, and that its founding figure, the "Teacher of Righteousness," may have anticipated his own suffering. His main evidence is the Thanksgiving Hymns (Hodayot) from Cave 1, which he reads in the first person and which he thinks the Teacher wrote, at least in part. He also points to Zechariah's "pierced" figure, to Isaiah 53, and to a list of first-century figures in Josephus who claimed messianic status and were killed. He explicitly frames his position as a minority view, and says the "double-sided coin" could be read the other way: that followers reinterpreted a death after the fact.

The talk closes with a survey of martyrdom and "noble death" traditions (Second Maccabees, Philippians, Romans 8, Mark's Gethsemane). Tabor links these to the idea that suffering leads to glorification, and he says the same pattern appears in the Scrolls. He ends by returning to course promotion and the Sunday session.

## Key Teachings

### The Dead Sea movement spans Jesus's time
Tabor says the movement "goes down through Jesus time John the Baptist Jesus James even," and that by his reading of the archaeology it did not leave Qumran until 70 CE, when Jerusalem fell. He says the Community Rule dates roughly 100 to 150 years before Jesus, and that the Teacher of Righteousness probably lived around 75 BCE (he says he follows Michael Wise on this). He presents Josephus, writing in the 70s CE, as looking back on this group. His point is that these communities were not gone when the Jesus movement began, so the Scrolls are relevant to the first century.

### The Teacher's own suffering in the Thanksgiving Hymns
This is Tabor's central evidence. He says the Thanksgiving Hymns (Cave 1) are written in the first person, and that the Teacher is "very aware of the idea that he is going to go through suffering and that he is going to be led to the slaughter and be betrayed." He takes a "minimalist" view that Hodayot 10 through 17 are the Teacher's own work, and says more might be his. He adds that Cave 4 fragments, once placed with the war scroll, are now associated with the hymns. He cites Martin Abegg as arguing that the hymn describing being "exalted to the Council of the Elohim" refers to the Teacher.

### Messianic self-identification and reading Isaiah 53
Tabor calls this the "dynamics of Messianic self-identity": a person reads the scriptures and concludes that he might be the anticipated figure, and then has to face what that implies. He proposes that Jesus may have done the same with Isaiah 53. He notes that the four Servant Songs include individual-sounding lines (God's words about restoring "the tribes of Israel") that do not fit a collective reading. He says the other explanation, that a disappointed community later searched texts to justify a shocking death, is the usual view, but he thinks it less likely.

### Zechariah's pierced figure
Tabor pushes back on the claim that the Hebrew Bible contains no idea of a Davidic Messiah who suffers. He points to Zechariah 12, where the family of David mourns for "the one they've pierced." He notes that Christians read this as Jesus (and Revelation quotes it), but argues it was written well before the Teacher or Jesus, so that later figures could have appropriated it. He also mentions the "strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered" text from Zechariah as something the Damascus Document later applies to the group's own history.

### Josephus's messianic claimants
Tabor reads through the figures Josephus calls bandits (*lēstai*) and says many of them claimed kingship. He names Hezekiah (killed by Herod, 47 BCE), Judas son of Hezekiah (4 BCE), Simon of Perea, and Athronges, all around 4 BCE after Herod's death, plus Judas the Galilean's sons James and Simon (crucified c. 46 CE) and Eleazar. He says these figures "all got killed," and that it is unclear whether they expected to die, because the sources are brief. He observes that he has seen three of these names in the New Testament, though he does not spell that out in this talk.

### Martyrdom, noble death and the Maccabees
Tabor's background is his co-authored book with Arthur Droge, which he says was published in the early 1990s and is about "noble death" traditions. He distinguishes martyrdom (a death that testifies to a cause) from other kinds of self-chosen death, and says the tradition runs from Socrates through the Maccabees. He cites Second Maccabees, where the martyrs say they will be raised, and argues this is a hope of vindication even when the death is final. He links this to Paul in Philippians (the choice between departing and staying) and to Jesus's request that the cup pass in Mark.

### Paul and glorification through suffering
Tabor says Paul extends the martyrdom pattern to his followers: "we will be glorified with him provided that we suffer with him," which he attributes to Romans 8. He says the same logic appears in the Scrolls, where the community says it "will be saved by our faith in the teacher and our suffering." He suggests these parallels show that the pattern of suffering leading to exaltation is "kind of a natural pattern," and he contrasts it with mystery religions, which he says he is not claiming as the source. He also compares it to Joseph Campbell's idea of universal hero archetypes.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Claim:** The Hebrew Bible contains no idea of a Davidic Messiah who suffers. **Correction:** Tabor points to Zechariah 12 and its "pierced" figure, which he says was written long before Jesus and could have been reapplied.
- **Claim:** The Dead Sea Scrolls were just a storage library of worn-out manuscripts (a *genizah*). **Correction:** Tabor says the scrolls were not worn out (the Isaiah scroll is in good condition) and suggests they were the community's archive, though he states this as his own reading.
- **Claim (implicit, from evangelical framing):** The Dead Sea community is contemporaneous with Jesus. **Correction:** Tabor says the Community Rule predates Jesus by 100 to 150 years, so the Scrolls are earlier than the Jesus movement, though the movement continued into Jesus's time.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor argues Jesus may have read himself into Isaiah 53 and Zechariah 12 and anticipated his death, while acknowledging this is a minority position and that the evidence cuts both ways.
- **Paul and the suffering-glory pattern:** Tabor ties Paul's language in Philippians and Romans 8 to the martyrdom tradition and to the Scrolls' language of suffering and salvation, which bears on the question of what Paul taught versus what Jesus taught.
- **Essenes and the Dead Sea community:** Tabor's dating of the Community Rule, the Teacher, and the group's continuity to 70 CE is his framework for relating the Scrolls to Jesus and John the Baptist.
- **Resurrection and afterlife:** The Second Maccabees passages and the "will be raised" language are presented as hope of vindication after death, which Tabor reads as relevant to Paul's view of glorification.
- **Messianic claimants in the first century:** Tabor's list from Josephus of figures who claimed kingship (and in some cases were killed) is his evidence that messianic self-identification and violent death were recurring patterns.
