## Summary

Tabor's question is how far the Dead Sea Scrolls group, which most scholars call Essene, might be related to the John the Baptist and Jesus movement that came roughly a hundred years later. He treats the scrolls as a "test case" for studying Messianic apocalypticism, since they are the earliest group whose own writings document their interpretation of scripture, their expectations, and how they coped when those expectations did not come true. He dates the core sectarian scrolls to roughly the first quarter of the first century BC, following Michael Wise, and argues that the scrolls' vocabulary and patterns (sons of Light, a teacher figure, a council of twelve, a betrayal from within, a "waiting" that outlasts its original timeline) resemble the Jesus movement closely enough to be worth studying together. He explicitly does not argue that the Teacher of Righteousness was Jesus.

Tabor's central evidence is the scrolls themselves, especially the Thanksgiving Hymns, which he reads as possibly the teacher's own first-person writings, and the Habakkuk-based "faith" in a delayed vision. He also draws on Josephus, the New Testament, and the Damascus Document, and he presents Jesus as a scripture-reading prophet who was probably Davidic, probably foresaw his own death, and likely read Daniel as a roadmap. On the Torah, he argues that Jesus was looser than the scrolls' legal strictness, citing the Sabbath sayings, while cautioning that the scrolls' views may have shifted over the roughly century between the two movements.

The conversation is long and wide-ranging. It moves through prophecy failure in Christian and Jewish contexts, modern end-times writers, Tabor's online course and his Mark course, reception of scholars like Eisenman and Ehrman, and the sociology of sects that loosen over time. It ends with Tabor's closing claim that sayings resembling scroll theology are more likely to be authentic, and his reasons for that view.

## Key Teachings

### Prophecy failure and three ways of handling it
Tabor says Messianic apocalyptic groups face a standard problem when prophecy fails, and that groups typically either blame the interpreter, reinterpret the event as fulfilled in a non-obvious way, or push it into the future with a gap. He lists the options as "futurist," "already fulfilled," and "failed." He says that as a New Testament scholar he will "go for failed," while adding that a scholar can never fully say a prophecy has failed, because some future fulfillment can always be proposed. He illustrates this with Daniel 7 and modern writers (Hal Lindsey, John Hagee) who stretched the fourth beast to fit later Romes.

### The Teacher of Righteousness and the Teacher Hymns
The scrolls revere a figure called the "right teacher" (translated "teacher of righteousness"), whom Tabor compares to a prophet like Moses. He points to the Thanksgiving Hymns from Cave 1, which he says are first-person, highly individual texts that speak of a figure given unusual wisdom and suffering rejection. He credits Jeremias (rendered "Garrett jeremus" in the captions) with first proposing in 1963 that these are the teacher's own hymns, and says this view is "getting more and more accepted." He argues the hymns show the teacher anticipating his death and suffering, drawing on Isaiah's Servant passages, and that Michael Wise's book *The First Messiah* supports this reading.

### Dating the scrolls and the generational gap
Tabor follows Michael Wise in placing the sectarian scrolls in the first quarter or so of the first century BC, and he estimates the teacher died by about 50 BC. He notes the group expected the end within about forty years of the teacher's death. Because the scrolls seem to stop referring to contemporary events (Herod, his sons, Roman provincial rule, the 70 CE destruction), he reads their library as a snapshot of the movement's foundation. He then describes a span of four to seven generations between the teacher and Jesus, and asks how a group that expected the end soon would have adapted when the expected time passed.

### Parallels in language, structure and betrayal
Tabor lists shared language and patterns: "sons of Light, sons of Darkness," the "New Covenant," the shepherd and sheep being scattered (which he says appears in Mark and in the Damascus Document), a council of twelve, and a Messianic banquet with a priestly and a Davidic Messiah (the "Messianic rule" fragment). He argues that a betrayal by an inner-circle member is a recurring pattern in religious movements, not necessarily a literary invention, and that the teacher's own Psalm about a "familiar friend who ate bread at my table" reflects a real betrayal. He also argues the "dynamics" of messianic movements (leader, breakaway groups, claims to be the "true" heirs) explain the parallels.

### Jesus and Torah strictness
Tabor argues that Jesus did not maintain the scrolls' legal strictness, though he says Jesus did observe the Torah and wore tzitzit. As evidence he cites the Sabbath saying "the Sabbath is made for people not people for the Sabbath," which he describes as among Jesus's earliest sayings. He contrasts this with a scroll rule against helping an animal out of a pit on the Sabbath, which Jesus's question to the Pharisees appears to reject. He also warns that the scrolls were not monolithic, that they include diverse materials, and that the group's practices may have loosened over the roughly century before Jesus. He draws on sociology of sects, comparing this to Churches of Christ and Baptist groups that became more liberal over time.

### The Kingdom on earth, and the delay
Tabor argues both movements expected the Kingdom of God to arrive concretely on earth, citing Isaiah 2, Isaiah 11, Jeremiah 30-31, and Daniel 9 and 11. He points to Mark's Peter asking what the disciples will receive, and to Jesus's saying about a hundredfold return "in this life," as evidence that Jesus expected an earthly Kingdom rather than only heaven. He reads Second Peter 3 as an early attempt to explain the delay by appeal to God's different timing ("a day with the Lord is a thousand years"), and he compares this to the way later end-time groups stretched their chronologies.

### Jesus, Daniel and Mark 13
Tabor argues that Jesus "certainly is reflecting some of the same" patterns as the scrolls, and that any first-century apocalyptic figure would have worked through Daniel. He says Mark 13 reflects the general scheme of the Book of Daniel, and that Mark's "the time is fulfilled" is a quotation from Daniel. He also says Jesus likely saw himself as a scripture prophet who expounded the prophets (citing Luke's "beginning from the first he expounded all the prophecies"). He describes the Q source as a "provisional government" with twelve thrones, and says Q shares strong apocalyptic features with the scrolls.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Prophecy never fails; the failure was in the interpreter.** Tabor says this is a common response, and that as a historian he would classify the scroll expectations as "failed," not blame the prophecy or push it into the future.
- **Jesus was (or was the) Teacher of Righteousness.** Tabor says Eisenman "never says that" and that the scrolls are better read as a pattern and vocabulary parallel than as a claim that the teacher was Jesus.
- **The Dead Sea Scrolls prove Jesus was derivative.** Tabor rejects the sense that finding parallels makes Jesus less unique, saying "take deep breaths" and that the question is what the texts show.
- **Jesus was as strict as the scrolls on Torah.** Tabor argues Jesus was looser, citing the Sabbath saying and the pit example.
- **Jesus said all who came before him were thieves and robbers.** Tabor says he sees no indication Jesus said this, and suggests later groups could have attributed such sayings to him to elevate him.
- **The Kingdom is only in heaven.** Tabor argues both movements expected a concrete, visible Kingdom on earth, citing the Lord's Prayer and Mark's hundredfold saying.
- **Jesus did not read Daniel.** Tabor says any first-century apocalyptic figure would have read Daniel, and that the phrase "the time is fulfilled" quotes it.
- **Jesus didn't exist (mythicism).** Tabor rejects this, while saying Jesus's life was surrounded by "myth making from day one."

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor reads Paul's "sons of Light," his use of Habakkuk, and his "touch not, taste not" as parallel to scroll language, while noting Paul's use of Habakkuk differs from the scrolls' use. He says Paul is "our earliest reference to Jesus" and that Paul "never met Jesus."
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor says "I think the Book of James we have very little from James or from those circles," and that James quotes scripture about "killed the just one" in a way comparable to the scrolls' use of Habakkuk.
- **Essenes, Nazarenes, Ebionites:** Tabor discusses whether "Essene" is an appropriate term for the scroll group, since they never use it, and whether John the Baptist visited Qumran. He says Scholars think John was "stripped," but he disagrees and calls John a Nazarite.
- **Torah:** The Sabbath saying and pit example, the scrolls' strictness, and the Pharisees' Hillel/Shammai debate are central to his argument about Jesus's legal approach.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Tabor argues the Kingdom was expected concretely on earth, citing Isaiah and Mark 10 (hundredfold).
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** Tabor's discussion of resurrection focuses on Daniel 12 as part of the road map, and on Christian claims that the resurrection explains the delay. He does not treat afterlife or hell in depth.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor says he thinks Jesus was Davidic and "self consciously" believed he had been called, and that Jesus was a scripture prophet.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor discusses Q, Mark 13 as reflecting Daniel, and whether gospel sayings resembling scroll theology are more likely authentic; he says "generally yes."
