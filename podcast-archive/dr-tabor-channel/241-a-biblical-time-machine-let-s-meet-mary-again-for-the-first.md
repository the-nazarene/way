## Summary

This podcast episode, the first of two Christmas specials of the *Biblical Time Machine* show, puts the question "who was the historical Mary?" to James Tabor, a retired professor of religious studies. Tabor argues that Mary has been "best known" yet "least known": later Christian tradition has turned her into a nun-like figure, the Queen of Heaven and a perpetual virgin, and in the process removed her Jewishness, her married life, and her role as mother of a large family. He says he will reconstruct her from "about 10 texts in the whole New Testament" (the birth accounts, Jesus at twelve, Cana, Nazareth, the cross, the tomb, and Pentecost) and from archaeology, while repeatedly stating that much of what he describes is imagination or probability rather than evidence.

Tabor's main lines of reasoning are these. He suggests Mary may have grown up in Sepphoris, the urban capital of Galilee, and cites a fourth-century Byzantine church over a house of Mary there and his own excavations at the site. He pictures her as a widow after Joseph's disappearance from the story, heading a household of seven to nine children and shaping Jesus's teaching. He then reads the Gospel scenes of family tension (Mark 3, Cana) as less negative than usually assumed. He places Mary with James in Jerusalem after the crucifixion, argues that James succeeded Jesus as leader of the movement, and holds that the Gospel of John's Beloved Disciple is probably James rather than John son of Zebedee. Helen Bond contributes questions and a few comments, but Tabor carries nearly all of the content.

The episode ends with a playful "time machine" exercise, in which Tabor chooses to travel to a family discussion in Nazareth in about 26 CE, shortly before Jesus leaves to join John the Baptist. The hosts close with holiday greetings. The talk is a speculative, popular-level reconstruction. Tabor's own qualifications, such as "I'm imagining" and "I don't know," make clear which parts are conjecture.

## Key Teachings

### Mary as a Jewish woman, not a transformed saint
Tabor says Mary is "robbed" of her Jewishness and her womanhood. He calls this a "theological overwrite that comes later." He argues that the Creeds give her credit mainly for the birth, "almost like a vessel the god-bearer," and that the wider Christian imagery (nun's habit, Queen of Heaven, perpetual virginity) obscures the widowed mother she may have been. He treats the perpetual-virginity emphasis as part of the later development.

### Paul's "Dogma" and the marginalizing of family
Tabor claims that the Creed's focus on the birth, death, burial and resurrection of Jesus "comes a lot from Paul." He says that Paul "doesn't think they're vital for the gospel," that Paul "no longer know[s] Jesus after the flesh," and that Paul "has a certain aversion to the family." His summary is that "as Paul goes so goes the church," so that what matters theologically is the death, burial and resurrection, not Jesus's family or teaching. This is the speaker's interpretation of Paul, offered without close citation of Paul's letters.

### Sepphoris and a bustling Galilean upbringing
Tabor proposes that Mary may have grown up in Sepphoris, not in a quiet village. He describes it as a Roman-era urban capital of about 10,000 to 20,000 people, with Herod Antipas's palace, trade routes, and a theater. He notes that Herod's palace was burned in 4 BCE, according to Josephus, and that Sepphoris lies "just to the north" of Nazareth. He cites a fourth-century Byzantine church over a house of Mary on the south side of the city, while saying his students' guesses often remain at the level of "possible." He says the Mary-from-Sepphoris idea rests on "later Church tradition and archaeology" rather than the New Testament, and he calls it a "twig" that is not in the Gospels. He describes the evidence as reaching a "likelihood or even probability" level in his view. He also speculates that Joseph and his sons worked in building trades, and that Jesus's teaching images of stone foundations reflect this.

### Mary as widow and head of a large household
Tabor imagines Mary as a widow in her thirties or forties with seven to nine children, supported by older sons (Jesus, then James, Joseph, Simon, Jude) working in the family trade. He describes her as a matriarch who "takes full responsibility" and possibly taught Jesus, including the instruction to turn the other cheek. He also suggests that she could have helped organize Jesus's movement, for example the sending out of the seventy in Luke 10 and the logistics of Galilean women followers. He presents this as "speculative but not far-fetched," not as established history.

### Reading the family-tension passages more positively
Tabor argues that the Mark 3 scene, where Mary and the brothers come to get Jesus, need not be read as rejection. He notes that Jesus's line "you're all my family" could be taken as inclusive ("if you hear and do the word of God, we're all one family"). He says the wedding at Cana (John 2) is filtered through John's theology and carries little historical weight, and he treats the brothers' skepticism as plausible sibling behavior rather than hostility. He points to Mary's presence at the cross (John 19) and the tomb as evidence of her loyalty, and to her attendance at Jerusalem festivals as family involvement.

### James as leader and Mary's likely guardian in Jerusalem
Tabor argues that James, the oldest surviving brother, "takes charge of the movement" after Jesus's death, and that Mary was with him in Jerusalem. He cites Acts 15, where James presides over the council, and Paul's letters, which list James first among the "pillars" and show him in charge ("it's not Peter it's James," Tabor says). He proposes that the Beloved Disciple of John is James, because a Jewish eldest brother would take over the care of his mother. He links Mary's death tradition to Mount Zion, a Jerusalem site near the upper room, and cites Stephen Shoemaker for the Dormition traditions. He rejects later traditions placing Mary in Asia Minor as late and unsupported. He describes the *desposyni* (the "family of the Lord") as relatives who survived into the second and third centuries, and reports a trans-Jordan connection he says is documented, though this is brief.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mary as a nun-like saint with no ordinary family life.** Tabor argues she was a Jewish widow and mother of a large household, shaped by Galilean life.
- **Mary as passive or disapproving toward Jesus.** Tabor reads the Mark 3 and Cana scenes as neutral or positive and suggests Mary may have helped shape Jesus's teaching.
- **Jesus traveling alone with twelve men.** Tabor argues the movement included women, families and logistics organized by household members.
- **Mary's later life in Asia Minor.** Tabor says the traditions placing her in Asia Minor are late and that he finds no evidence for her travel there.
- **Peter as the leading figure after Jesus.** Tabor argues James led the movement and that Peter was not the successor.
- **Jesus's family ignored in the Gospels.** Tabor argues the Gospels play down the family, and that the family was central to the movement.
