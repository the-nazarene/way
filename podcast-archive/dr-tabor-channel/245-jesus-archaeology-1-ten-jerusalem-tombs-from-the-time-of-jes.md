## Summary

Tabor opens the first episode of his "Jesus Archaeology" series by asking whether material evidence from Jerusalem's late Second Temple tombs (he frames the period from roughly 100 years before Jesus to 100 years after) sheds light on the New Testament texts. He presents ten tombs, counted down from one to ten, drawing on inscribed ossuaries, skeletal remains and DNA. He relies heavily on the Kloner and Zissu catalogue of the Jerusalem necropolis, which he describes as covering over 900 tombs and more than 2,000 ossuaries, with about 704 inscriptions.

The talk's central claim, which Tabor says he is "99% sure" of at about 1:23:24, is that the Talpiot ("topio") tomb is the Jesus family tomb, and that the James ossuary (inscribed "James son of Joseph brother of Jesus") came from it. His main evidence for the link is the names on the Talpiot ossuaries, a claimed statistical argument about name frequencies, and new geochemical soil testing that he says ties the James ossuary to Talpiot. He also argues that the Talpiot "Jonah" tomb, with its fish-and-Jonah image and a Greek inscription he reads as a plea to "raise up," bears on early resurrection belief, and that the presence of bones in a tomb does not negate resurrection faith. He presents several other finds in the same spirit, including the Caiaphas ossuary, a Mount of Olives tomb he proposes as Jesus's temporary burial, the Tomb of the Shroud with its leprosy and first-century DNA claims, and Antigonus-linked and Masada remains.

The talk ends without a formal conclusion. Tabor says he is "not trying to get you to join my patreon," points to unpublished DNA work, and teases the next episode, on Sepphoris ("sephus" in the captions), the Galilean urban capital. Much of the talk is presented as Tabor's own reading of contested evidence, and he acknowledges that many scholars disagree.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The necropolis and the practice of secondary burial
Tabor describes the Jerusalem necropolis as a ring of cave tombs north, east and south of the old city, with most inscriptions on ossuaries that hold family bones. He explains the sequence: the body is laid out on a shelf for about a year, then the bones are collected into an ossuary. About 20% of ossuaries have names scratched on them, sometimes with "son of," "daughter of," or "sister of." He notes that grave goods are rare in Jewish tombs. He stresses that Orthodox Jews are involved in reburying disturbed remains, which he says allows the tombs and ossuaries to be studied.

### 2. The Caiaphas ossuary and high-priestly burial practice
Tabor identifies the ossuary of "Yosef bar Qafa" (Joseph son of Caiaphas), found in 1990 in the Peace Forest south of the old city, and says it is held by the Israel Museum. He ties Caiaphas to the gospel trial of Jesus and to Josephus. He says DNA has apparently not been done on its residue. He reports a Greek coin placed in the mouth of a family member, which he reads as evidence that Judean high priests knew Hellenistic burial customs.

### 3. A Mount of Olives tomb and the burial of Jesus (the "Beth" tomb)
Tabor argues that Jesus was crucified on the Mount of Olives, which he says he has argued in a separate video. He relies on John's passion narrative for detail, noting that John knows of two trials (Annas and Caiaphas) and the Pilate trial. He reads John 19 as describing an emergency burial near the crucifixion site, before the Sabbath and Passover, in an unfinished tomb that was not Joseph of Arimathea's family tomb. Mark, he says, does not say where the burial occurred, and Matthew and Luke follow Mark. He identifies the Beth tomb as this site, pointing to its rolling stone, steps, and early Christian graffiti (a tree of life, an eternity sign, and Greek and Hebrew letters). He identifies the "Beloved disciple" in John 20 as James the brother of Jesus rather than the son of Zebedee, which he calls a later idea.

### 4. The Tomb of the Shroud and first-century DNA
Tabor recounts finding a robbed tomb in Hinnom Valley in 2000 while digging at Subba with Shimon Gibson. Inside, a sealed niche held a burial shroud, which Tabor says was carbon-dated to the first century at the same Arizona lab that dated the Shroud of Turin to the Middle Ages. Skeletal remains were studied and, he says, showed Hansen's disease (leprosy). He says the team obtained DNA profiles from bone fragments, which he calls the first from a first-century Herodian tomb, and points to a published report. He also claims that scraping organic residue from ossuary surfaces may yield DNA, which is the basis for his planned new testing.

### 5. The Talpiot "Jonah" (resurrection) tomb
Tabor describes a tomb about 200 feet from the Jesus family tomb, under a condominium, explored by robotic camera. He says it contains ossuaries, one decorated with a fish spitting out Jonah, with the Hebrew name "Yona" written at the mouth, and another with a Greek inscription he reads as "dios" and "Yo" with "opo" and "agba" (raise up). He argues this is the only tomb among the 900 with inscriptions about raising the dead, and that Jonah's fish-tomb imagery ties to the "sign of Jonah" in the Q source. He argues that Paul's account (1 Corinthians 15) describes a transformed body and that the bones of the dead do not affect resurrection faith.

### 6. The James ossuary and the soil-matching argument
Tabor describes the James ossuary ("Yakov bar Yosef, achui d'Yeshua", which he reads as "James son of Joseph brother of Jesus") as acquired by a private collector in 2002 from an antiquities dealer, and displayed in Toronto (with Hershel Shanks present). He argues the patina in the letters of "Yeshua" shows the inscription is original and that "brother of Jesus" was not added later. He says new geochemical testing of soil in the ossuary matches the Talpiot Jesus family tomb more closely than other Jerusalem tombs tested. He concedes the James ossuary's removal from Talpiot is not documented and that the reasoning is a hypothesis. He also cites a statistical illustration with a stadium of 50,000 people, narrowing by name (Mary, Joseph, Jesus, James, Yose, Mariam), which he calls "a slam dunk" per a statistician friend.

### 7. The Antigonus tomb, Masada, and DNA
Tabor identifies the Abba tomb at Givat ha-Mivtar as the burial of Mattityahu (Matthew) son of Judah, whom he links to King Antigonus, the last Hasmonean king. He says the remains show crucifixion nails through the hands and a neck cut. He describes a second crucifixion victim, Yehohanan, with a nail through the heel bone. He reports DNA on the Abba remains and on one Masada skeleton, and says they match closely, suggesting a shared priestly royal lineage. He contrasts this with the account of the Masada suicide group (Josephus), noting that seven survivors are reported and suggesting a priestly family hid in a cave.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Belief that the Talpiot tomb would negate resurrection if Jesus's bones were found.** Tabor says people assume this; he responds that two tombs (one temporary, one permanent) explain it and that resurrection does not depend on the bones.
- **Belief that "brother of Jesus" was a later addition to the James ossuary.** Tabor argues the patina in the letters shows the inscription is original.
- **Belief that John is the least historically reliable gospel for the passion.** Tabor argues that John's passion narrative contains detail, such as the two trials and the burial, that suggests a good source.
- **Belief that Matthew and Luke's "empty tomb" narrative is about Jesus's own tomb.** Tabor says the accounts say the place was empty, which fits an emergency burial better.
- **Belief that the "leper" in the gospels had a skin disease rather than leprosy.** Tabor says the Tomb of the Shroud skeleton shows real Hansen's disease, which he says challenges that assumption.
- **Belief that no reputable scholar considers Talpiot the Jesus family tomb.** Tabor reports this as what people say about him and says that, in his view, more people think it than assume.
