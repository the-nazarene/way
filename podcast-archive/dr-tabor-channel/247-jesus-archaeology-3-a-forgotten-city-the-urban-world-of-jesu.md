## Summary

Tabor asks what the archaeology of Sepphoris (called Sepphoris, Zippori, or Diocaesarea in the talk) might tell us about the life of Jesus. His central claim is that Sepphoris, the Galilee's urban capital, was a few miles from the village of Nazareth, and that Jesus would have grown up in its orbit. He argues against the model, which he attributes to unnamed "colleagues," of Jesus as a poor, itinerant, illiterate peasant. In its place he proposes an urban Jesus who could have worked in the city's building trade and moved comfortably in Greco-Roman settings.

His evidence falls into three groups. The first is literary: Josephus calls Sepphoris "the ornament of the Galilee," and his *Jewish War* account of the rebellion of Judas son of Hezekiah, the Roman destruction of the city around 4 BCE, and Herod Antipas's rebuilding frame the city's history. The second is textual: Tabor reads the Q saying about people in "King's palaces" (Luke 7:25) as reflecting urban knowledge, and he treats Luke 23's account of Herod Antipas mocking Jesus in "gorgeous apparel" as a echo of that saying. The third is material: the excavated theater, the mosaics (including the so-called "Mona Lisa of the Galilee"), the Dionysus dining hall, and synagogue floors, which he presents as signs of a prosperous, culturally mixed city.

The talk ends as a preview. Tabor says he will address Nazareth, Jaffa (Yafa), Capernaum, and Magdala in later episodes. He also mentions a local tradition, which he treats as unproven and which he will examine later, that Mary grew up in Sepphoris with her parents, and he suggests this would require revising the picture of Nazareth as a backwater. Throughout, Tabor frames these as hypotheses ("I'm suggesting this as a possibility," "we don't know") rather than settled conclusions.

## Key Teachings

### Sepphoris as the forgotten urban capital

Tabor says Sepphoris is "not even included on many Bible maps," though an Oxford atlas places it roughly 3.5 to 4 miles north of Nazareth. He calls it the Galilee's urban capital and a Jewish city rebuilt in Roman style, and he notes its Roman name, Diocaesarea. He points to the road system: Sepphoris sat at a crossroads of the main east-west route from the coast to Tiberias and the north-south road toward Damascus, so that "if you leave Sepphoris Nazareth would be located right here in this crop of Hills." His source for the title and for much of the background is Richard Batty's *Jesus in the Forgotten City*.

### Nazareth as a suburb within the city's orbit

Tabor's main analogy is that a famous figure born in a small town near a major city (he uses Matthews, North Carolina, near Charlotte) would be biographically described without mention of the city. He argues the Gospels have the same silence about Sepphoris. He also argues that from the hills of Nazareth Jesus could have seen the city "gleaming at night with all the Torches and lights," and he connects this to the Q saying "a city set on a hill cannot be hidden" (Matthew 5, which he attributes to the Sermon on the Mount and the Q source).

### The post-4 BCE destruction and Herod Antipas's rebuilding

Tabor reconstructs the sequence from Josephus's *Jewish War*. After Herod the Great died (he gives the date as 4 BCE), Judas son of Hezekiah raided the royal armory at Sepphoris and made the city the center of a revolt. Roman forces under Varus, from Damascus, crushed the revolt, burned the city, and crucified about 2,000 rebels, according to Tabor's reading of Josephus. Herod Antipas was then put in charge of the Galilee and rebuilt Sepphoris as his capital. Tabor says this rebuilding was underway during Jesus's childhood, which he places around 5 BCE, while noting that dates for Jesus's birth vary.

### Jesus as builder rather than carpenter

Tabor argues that the Greek *tektōn* means "builder" or "architect" and that Jesus was more likely a stonemason than a woodworker. His reasons are that the Gospels speak of laying foundations and building with stones, never with wood, and that stone was the main building material of a Roman city. He suggests Jesus may have worked on the rebuilding of Sepphoris, perhaps in a family building business headed by his father Joseph, with the brothers as apprentices and possibly extended family or relatives from Mary's side on the crew. He frames all of this as "a possibility" and says "we don't know." He refers viewers to an earlier video of his, *The Lost Years of Jesus Revealed at Last*, for his fuller argument.

### Q 7:25, Luke 23, and Herod Antipas

Tabor reads Luke 7:25 (from Q, in his account) in which Jesus asks the crowds about those "gorgeously apparel and live in luxury" who are in "King's palaces," and argues that this shows urban knowledge. He then links it to Luke 23:6-12, which only Luke records: Pilate sends Jesus to Herod Antipas, who "treated him with contempt and mocked him and arrayed him in gorgeous apparel." Tabor calls this "an exact reflection" of the Q saying. He reads Herod as hostile to Jesus, citing the Lukan report that Herod wanted to kill him, and he adds that Herod Antipas had John the Baptist killed (he says he will cover this in a later program, on Machaerus).

### The Mary tradition

Tabor reports a tradition that Mary, the mother of Jesus, and her parents (whom he names Joachim and Anna, correcting the garbled captioning) lived in Sepphoris, and that a Crusader church over Byzantine ruins on the southern slopes is supposed to mark her house. He does not endorse it as established. He says that if it were true, Nazareth's image as a "backward Village" would need revising, and that Mary would have grown up urban and married Joseph, "probably from Nazareth," after she was engaged. He defers the full discussion to a later episode.

### Literacy, synagogues, and the Jesus model

Tabor argues that Jewish families in the period may have taught boys and girls to read, citing later rabbinic sources with the caveat that they are later. He points to Gospel stories of Jesus quoting Scripture and reading in the synagogue. He also argues that the claim that Luke invented first-century synagogues is outdated, since "we now have way over a dozen first century synagogues that have been excavated." He says a synagogue at Sepphoris is built on an older one, and that its famous floor mosaic is not from Jesus's time, though he thinks Sepphoris probably had several synagogues and Nazareth likely had one too.

### Urban culture: wealth, Greek art, and mixed identity

Tabor describes Sepphoris as a city with high culture even while being Jewish. He points to a dining hall (triclinium) with a Dionysus wine-harvest mosaic, which he says is not necessarily religious, comparing it to "a statue of Buddha on your mantle." He describes a mosaic of Helios with the Four Seasons labeled in Hebrew and Greek, and a Roman woman panel he calls the "Mona Lisa of the Galilee." He says the Dionysus mosaic is later than Jesus's time, and that the synagogue floor's Helios face was knocked out, probably by iconoclasts, which he notes as an example of "cultural mix." He uses the theater to suggest that Jesus's use of "hypocrite" (*hypokrites*, "play actor") draws on a theatrical culture his audience would know.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus was an illiterate, poor peasant from a tiny village.** Tabor argues for an urban background, suggests that Jewish boys and girls may have been taught to read, and points to Gospel portrayals of Jesus quoting Scripture.
- **Nazareth was an isolated, backward village.** He says it was a suburb of Sepphoris, a few miles away, and that Jesus would have walked to the city regularly.
- **Jesus was a carpenter.** He argues *tektōn* means builder and that the Gospels' building imagery is stone-based.
- **There were no synagogues in first-century Galilee.** He says that claim is outdated given the excavated synagogues, while noting that the Sepphoris synagogue floor is later than Jesus's time.
- **Sepphoris is unimportant because the New Testament does not mention it.** He argues that its absence from the Gospels is itself a reason to study it, and that it matters for reconstructing Jesus's world.
