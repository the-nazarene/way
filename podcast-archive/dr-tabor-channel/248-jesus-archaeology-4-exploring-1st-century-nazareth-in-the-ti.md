## Summary

Tabor asks what the material evidence now tells us about Nazareth, the village where the Gospels place Jesus's childhood and early adulthood. He begins by noting that Nazareth appears in no early non-Christian text, and he treats it as part of the wider Lower Galilee landscape around Sepphoris, the major urban center he discussed in the previous talk in the series. His argument is that Nazareth was a small agricultural village tied geographically and economically to Sepphoris and to the nearby city of Yafia, and that its archaeology is more substantial than popular claims allow. He cites a first-century house excavated under the Maran Center across from the Basilica of the Annunciation, a first-century excavation beneath the Church of the Sisters of Nazareth, and a Caesarea inscription listing Nazareth among the priestly courses. He credits Ken Dark's published work on these excavations.

Tabor also works through the Greek and Hebrew terms behind "Nazareth," "Nazarene," and "Nazirite." He argues that Matthew's "Nazarene" is probably a wordplay on the Hebrew *netzer* ("branch," Isaiah 11:1), and that the Nazirite vow of Numbers 6 is a different word and idea, though he suggests John the Baptist's diet and abstinence may reflect that tradition. He closes by imagining Jesus walking to Sepphoris each morning to work in the building trades, and by saying that the Nazareth-Yafia-Sepphoris geography should change how we think about Jesus's movements and his later choice of Capernaum as a base. The talk ends with a teaser for the next video on John the Baptist's home village.

## Key Teachings

### Nazareth's place in the texts and the name question

Tabor lists Mark 1:9, Matthew 2:23, and Luke's birth and return narratives as the New Testament sources for Nazareth. He argues that Matthew's account of Joseph choosing Nazareth after the return from Egypt is "probably unlikely," and that Luke's account, in which the family is already in Nazareth and returns there, "makes more sense given all of our records." He takes Mary to be from Sepphoris, and Joseph to be from Nazareth. He says the Gospel usages "Jesus of Nazareth" and "Jesus the Nazarene" work like "the New Yorker," a label for someone from a place.

### Netzer, Nazir, and the Matthean "Nazarene"

Tabor separates two Hebrew words that English readers confuse. *Netzer* (branch) appears in Isaiah 11:1, a prophecy about a shoot from the stump of Jesse, which he says is "the davidic Messianic line." He says Matthew "must be referring" to this when he speaks of a prophecy that he "shall be called a Nazarene," though he admits no Messianic prophecy says that in those words. He mentions the Branch Davidians as a later use of the title. *Nazir* (separated one) is a different root, used for the vow of Numbers 6:2 and for Samson in Judges. Tabor says John the Baptist is said to be a Nazirite in Luke, but the text does not say so explicitly, and he does not settle whether John kept the vow.

### Archaeology: the Caesarea inscription and first-century Nazareth

Tabor shows three fragments found at Caesarea in 1962 naming "Nazareth" in a list of the priestly courses. He says the 18th course, called Happizzez, served in Nazareth, which he takes as evidence that the place name existed in the period and was a recognized settlement. He then argues against the view he was taught as a graduate student, that there was "no first century Nazareth." He presents the first-century house under the Maran Center, which he says has been "securely dated," and the excavations under the Sisters of Nazareth, which include bedrock, Byzantine, and Crusader layers. He says the Sisters site is the spot most likely to have been revered as the house of Mary, because of the Byzantine and Crusader churches built above it. He is careful to say he does not know whose house the Maran Center structure was.

### Geography: Nazareth between Sepphoris and Yafia

Tabor insists on the lay of the land. Sepphoris is about four miles northwest of Yafia, and Nazareth is a hamlet close to Yafia, not a remote village. He reads Josephus's description of Yafia as a walled town and says that a 40-minute walk connects it with Nazareth. He estimates a walk of about an hour and a half from Nazareth to Sepphoris, which he uses to argue that Joseph and the boys would have commuted to Sepphoris as stonemasons while building Antipas's city. He says Nazareth's water source in the valley explains why it was settled, and that the village was probably agricultural with a population of "100 200." He proposes that Jesus "didn't just grow up out in the middle of nowhere," and that the regional network shapes what we should think about his later movements.

### John the Baptist, diet, and the Nazirite question

Tabor uses Luke 7:33 (from the Q source, in his account) and a Slavonic Josephus passage to argue that John the Baptist's diet was ascetic, avoiding bread and wine. He notes that Jesus is described as eating and drinking and accused of being a glutton and drunkard. He says the locusts of Matthew and Mark may have been a desert plant or honey wafer, and cites the Ebionites for an alternative reading. He also says that James the brother of Jesus did not drink alcohol "according to our early text," while Jesus apparently did. He presents these as possible connections to the Nazirite tradition, not as settled conclusions.

### Village life and the synagogue

Tabor describes Nazareth Village, a reconstruction near the hill, built with first-century methods based on archaeological findings. He reads Luke 4 in a reconstructed synagogue there, and says that the synagogues at Migdal and Modi'in (his garbled name "Mada") have similar features. He addresses the claim that there is no precipice at Nazareth by saying he takes tour groups to one, which corresponds to the precipice of Luke 4:29. He portrays Jesus walking the hills around Nazareth "thinking about his mission."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **There is no archaeological evidence for first-century Nazareth.** Tabor says excavations such as the Maran Center house and the Sisters of Nazareth site show a first-century settlement, though modern building limits further digging.
- **Nazareth was a remote, isolated village.** Tabor argues it was close to Yafia and about an hour and a half's walk from Sepphoris, and that Jesus would have had a regional network.
- **There were no first-century synagogues.** Tabor cites the synagogues at Migdal and Mada, and the reconstruction at Nazareth Village, as evidence against this view.
- **There is no precipice at Nazareth.** Tabor says he takes groups to one and that it is "dizzy" to look down from.
- **John the Baptist was clearly a Nazirite with long hair and a wild appearance.** Tabor says the text does not say this, and that the movie image may not match the historical figure.
- **"Nazarene" is simply a place label.** Tabor argues Matthew's wording may point to *netzer* (branch) as a Messianic wordplay, though he hedges with "I think."
