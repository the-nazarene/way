## Summary

Tabor asks what the Suba cave, a rock-cut cave west of Jerusalem near Ein Kerem, can tell us about John the Baptist and about Jesus. Gibson first reported the cave to him as possibly holding the earliest artwork of John the Baptist, and the two co-directed excavations there, which Tabor says ran from 2000 through 2006 with later work in 2008. Tabor frames the talk as one installment of a series he calls "Jesus archaeology," which pairs ancient texts with material evidence.

On the textual side, Tabor argues that John should not be treated only as a forerunner of Jesus. He reads Mark 1:9 and 1:14 together with John 3:22 (Jesus and his disciples baptizing in Judea while John baptized near Aenon) and with a Q saying in which Jesus calls John "more than a prophet." He proposes a joint "John the Baptist Jesus James movement" in which the two baptized in different regions. On the archaeological side, Tabor reports first-century Roman pottery, thousands of fragments of juglets, a foot-shaped basin, and a spring-fed pool, which he reads as evidence of repeated ritual use with no sign of habitation. He is explicit that the cave's link to Jesus is not proven: "I cannot prove that the subba cave is the place but boy does it fit the idea."

The talk closes with a plug for Gibson's book *The Cave of John the Baptist* and Tabor's *The Jesus Dynasty*, and with an announced forthcoming Israel Exploration Society volume covering the excavation.

## Key Teachings

### John as more than a forerunner
Tabor says that John "is usually seen as a forerunner of Jesus," appearing briefly and then pointing the way to Jesus, but that the historical sources give a fuller picture. He draws on a Q saying (found in Matthew and Luke, not in Mark) in which Jesus asks about John, "what did you go out to see a prophet," and answers that John is more than a prophet. Tabor links this to Malachi, the last prophet in the Hebrew Bible, and to Isaiah 40:3, which he reads as John inaugurating the messianic last days.

### A joint baptizing campaign
Tabor reads John 3:22 as evidence that "Jesus and his disciples went into the land of Judea and he remained with them and baptized," and takes this to mean that Jesus and John "teamed up." He reasons that John was baptizing at Aenon near Salim, a place with many springs, while Jesus worked in Judea, and that John's movement was located at a crossroads where travelers from Galilee would pass. He also suggests that some of Jesus's core disciples, later among the Twelve, may have been first followers of John.

### Using John's Gospel for historical material
Tabor argues that scholars increasingly recognize "very very reliable material" in the Gospel of John, especially its geography and chronology, which Mark does not supply. He proposes reading Mark as the base text, with John's narrative portions (not the developed theological speeches) filling in travels and dates. He gives a chronology in which Jesus's baptism took place in the fall of 26 CE, John was arrested early the next year, and the joint baptizing ran for months before that. He says he treats the disciple "whom Jesus loved" in John as James the brother of Jesus, though he calls this "another topic."

### The Q gloss on John's greatness
Tabor argues that the statement "among those born of women there's none greater than John" is a later editorial addition, a "theological gloss," made to promote Jesus over John. He says the original saying probably ended after "born of women," and that the fuller saying survives in a Hebrew Matthew text he names as published by George Howard (captioned "Evan Bohan"). He presents this as his view, not as settled.

### The cave: physical evidence
Tabor describes Suba, a natural swelling overlooking the main road from Jerusalem into the Judean hill country, with an Iron Age reservoir cut into bedrock, plausibly from the time of King Hezekiah. Excavators started in March 2000 and found first-century Roman pottery near the bottom of the fill, which surprised them since they expected later material on top. Tabor reports almost two meters of accumulated fill, with first-century material at the lower levels, and Byzantine, early Islamic and some Ottoman material above.

Specific features he describes:
- A foot-shaped basin cut into rock, with a channel running off it, which he reads as an anointing ceremony, probably of the right foot, before baptism.
- Thousands of fragments of small juglets, many apparently broken on purpose, which he takes as evidence of a ritual that ended with the vessel being left in the cave.
- A spring-fed trough and shaft that fill with winter rainwater, which he calls clean for ritual purposes.
- Flotation sampling that found no cooking or eating remains, and his statement that "this is not a cave that was inhabited by anybody."

### The image of John and the Byzantine tradition
The cave wall carries a Byzantine-era image of a figure identified as John, with raised arm, staff and small vessel. Tabor says the face has been chopped away, possibly by rival religious groups, and that the hair is wrapped in a bun that he links to Nazirite practice. He ties Byzantine clay medallions showing Elizabeth fleeing with John (with a Herodian soldier grabbing at her) to the Protoevangelium of James, an infancy text he dates to the late second or early third century, and which describes Elizabeth hiding the child in the hill country, where "the mountain was rent asunder and received her." He notes that this Byzantine tradition does not appear in the New Testament.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **John is just a forerunner who points to Jesus and disappears.** Tabor argues the sources show John with his own function and a larger role, including the Q saying that he is "more than a prophet."
- **The Gospel of John has no historical value.** Tabor says more scholars now see reliable material in its narrative portions, especially geography and chronology, though he sets aside its later theology.
- **The statement "none greater than John" reflects the original tradition.** Tabor calls the clause a later editorial gloss meant to favor Jesus.
- **The Suba cave is confirmed as John's baptism site.** Tabor says it is a fitting hypothesis but cannot be proven.
