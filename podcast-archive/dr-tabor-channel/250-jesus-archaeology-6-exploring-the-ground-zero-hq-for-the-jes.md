## Summary

Tabor asks why Capernaum on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee, rather than Jerusalem, became the base of the Jesus movement, and what archaeology can add to that. He argues that Capernaum was the movement's first headquarters, "until things moved to Jerusalem after the death of Jesus." His case rests on three kinds of evidence. The first is the Gospel of John's account of Jesus' early months with disciples from Bethsaida before his Galilean campaign, which he says the Synoptics do not know. The second is the repeated appearance of Capernaum in the Synoptic and Johannine narratives as Jesus' residence and the place where he returns from travels. The third is the excavated remains at Capernaum: a first-century house under a later Byzantine church, the nearby synagogue ruins, and ancient docks that have become visible as the lake level dropped. Tabor says the house is "most likely" Peter's, and he calls the identification "pretty solid," while also noting that it rests partly on church tradition.

The talk walks through the textual sequence, then the map, the harbor, and the site. It ends with Tabor's reading of Capernaum as a strategic center on major trade routes, from which Jesus ran a "provisional revolutionary government of the kingdom of God," and with his statement that the movement's birthplace was "really in a house on the shores of Capernaum."

## Key Teachings

### 1. John preserves a prehistory that the Synoptics omit
Tabor's central textual claim is that the Gospel of John shows Jesus working with Philip, Andrew and Peter (all from Bethsaida) as disciples of John the Baptist before Jesus began his own work in Galilee. He says "what John knows that the synoptics don't know Mark Matthew or Luke not aware of this is that Jesus has a prehistory with the disciples." He cites John 2:12 (Jesus goes down to Capernaum with his mother, brothers and disciples), John 3:22-24 (baptizing in Judea before John's arrest), and then Mark 1:14 (Jesus comes to Galilee after John's arrest). He dates Jesus' baptism to the fall of 26 CE and places the movement's Galilean phase in 27 onward, with the final Capernaum visit in winter 29 before Jerusalem.

### 2. Capernaum as a base of operations
Tabor reads the Gospel texts as showing Jesus moving his family (mother, four brothers, at least two or three sisters, per Tabor) to Capernaum, where he "dwelt" (Matthew 4:12 and Mark's "he was at home" account). He treats Jesus as head of the family after Joseph's death, and says Jesus moved in "high circles," citing the centurion (Luke 7) and the royal official in John 4:46. He links Capernaum's position at the junction of north-south and east-west roads and at the lakeside toll station (the tax collector in Mark 2:14) to its strategic value.

### 3. The archaeology of the Capernaum house
Tabor describes the excavated house as a first-century dwelling of black basalt, now under an octagonal Byzantine church and a modern Catholic church with a glass floor. He reports that the house was venerated from the second century and visited by pilgrims through the fourth to sixth centuries, and that the "worship use of this building began as early as the second half of the First Century" (per the description's summary). He says there are "about twelve" first-century houses in the cluster. He stresses that the house foundation is what is preserved, not the Byzantine layers above it.

### 4. The synagogue and the Byzantine layers
Tourists often assume Jesus preached in the visible synagogue, but Tabor says that building is a fourth- or fifth-century Jewish synagogue built over the earlier foundation. A probe below it, he says, shows the earlier synagogue's placement was kept, so the first-century building lies beneath. He treats this as evidence that Capernaum stayed a Jewish town.

### 5. Harbor, docks and the "Jesus boat"
Tabor cites a Biblical Archaeology Review article on ancient docks exposed by the falling lake level. He argues that Capernaum's dock was the major one on the lake, and that this shows why the town mattered economically. He describes a first-century fishing boat recovered from mud and says it was carbon dated; he notes it is a relatively large boat, not a rowboat, capable of carrying Jesus and the Twelve.

### 6. Jesus' program: a provisional government
Tabor says that in his book *The Jesus Dynasty* he presents Jesus as "setting up a provisional revolutionary government of the kingdom of God." He reads the commission of the Twelve as a claim to regather the tribes of Israel (citing Isaiah 11 and the promise of judging the twelve tribes from twelve thrones) and says Capernaum was chosen as the operational center of that project. This is an interpretive claim built on the texts and the site, and Tabor presents it as his own reading.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jerusalem was the birthplace of the Jesus movement.** Tabor says that "when you think of early Christianity and you go to Capernaum we always think of Jerusalem as the birth of the Jesus movement," but that the movement's birthplace was "really in a house on the shores of Capernaum."
- **Jesus was a poor, illiterate peasant.** Tabor says "I don't think Jesus was a poor itinerate illiterate peasant" and places Jesus in the prosperous urban center of Galilee.
- **Jesus called the fishermen out of the blue.** Tabor says movies show Jesus walking up unannounced, "as if they've never seen him or even heard of him," but John shows they had already worked with him for months.
- **The visible synagogue at Capernaum is the one Jesus preached in.** Tabor says the visible building is a fourth- or fifth-century Jewish synagogue, with the first-century foundation below.
