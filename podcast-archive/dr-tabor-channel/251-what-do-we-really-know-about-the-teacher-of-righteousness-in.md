## Summary

Tabor asks what can be known about the Teacher of Righteousness, the unnamed leader of the community behind the Dead Sea Scrolls, using only what the scrolls themselves say. He describes his method as gathering everything the texts say about the figure and then testing what can be drawn from it, without assuming the community's claims are literal history. He offers five points: when the Teacher lived, that the community expected a prophet like Moses, that the Teacher gave the correct interpretation of the Sabbath and festivals through a 364-day calendar, that he was given inspired knowledge of the prophets' mysteries, and that his death was taken to mark a countdown of about 40 years to the end.

For dating, Tabor relies on the Damascus Document's "390 years" and "20 years" chronology, which he says traditionally pointed to about 176 BCE. He follows Michael Wise's work, which tallies the historical figures named in the scrolls, in placing the Teacher around 70 BCE, in the first century BCE rather than the second. He ties the prophet-like-Moses expectation to Deuteronomy 18:18 and the Community Rule, and the "star and scepter" language to Numbers 24. He describes the Teacher's calendar as tied to Genesis 1 and the Book of Jubilees, and presents the Habakkuk commentary and the Psalms commentary as showing a "final 40 years" framed by Daniel 9's 490 years. Tabor notes that the countdown failed, and that the community's texts respond by saying God has "prolonged" the end.

The talk ends with a teaser rather than a conclusion. Tabor says he will save for a Zoom session the "teacher hymns" (first-person psalms possibly from the Teacher), questions about whether the Teacher died violently or naturally, how the community handled his absence after the 40 years, and whether the Thanksgiving Hymns imply he was raised to heaven. ## Key Teachings

### 1. When the Teacher lived (c. 70 BCE)
Tabor reads the opening of the Damascus Document: after God gave Israel into the hand of Nebuchadnezzar, "390 years" later, the group was "groping for the way" for 20 years, and then God "raised up for them a teacher of righteousness." Adding 390 and 20 to the traditional date for Nebuchadnezzar's conquest (586 BCE) gives about 176 BCE, which he says was long the standard view, near Antiochus Epiphanes. Tabor says that Michael Wise, in the Oxford Handbook article, has made it "pretty persuasive" that the chronology reflects a different reckoning, and that tallying the historical figures named in the scrolls (Herod the Great and others) points to the first century BCE. He mentions Alexander Janneus as a reference point, and says he would place the Teacher "around 70 BCE." He is careful: "I don't know if we can absolutely nail it down."

### 2. The expectation of a prophet like Moses
The Community Rule says members should keep the existing instructions "until there shall come the prophet and the Messiahs of Aaron and Israel." Tabor links this to Deuteronomy 18:18, where Moses promises a prophet like himself, and notes that Joshua, a possible candidate, consults the priest Eleazar and the Urim rather than acting as a prophet like Moses (Numbers 27). He says Acts 3 and Acts 7 identify Jesus with this figure, and that the Damascus Document's "Interpreter of the Torah" (a "star" from Numbers 24, with the "scepter" as the Davidic Messiah) reflects the same expectation a century earlier. He suggests the Teacher is a final figure, not one rabbi among many, and that John Reeves argues the title should be read as "the true teacher" rather than "teacher of righteousness."

### 3. The correct calendar and festivals
The Damascus Document says the community keeps the Sabbath and festivals "according to the findings" of the New Covenant members. Tabor describes the calendar as the Jubilees calendar: a 364-day year of twelve months, with 30-day months and some 31-day months, so that festivals fall on fixed weekdays. He says the Teacher sought "perfect harmony" because God would not create the cosmos "in such disharmony," and that Genesis 1 is the rationale for the weekday arrangement. He contrasts this with the lunar calendar of rabbinic Judaism, which he says is about 11 days off the solar year, and argues that the Teacher's calendar set his community apart.

### 4. A teacher who has inspired knowledge of the prophets
Tabor cites the Habakkuk commentary: God told Habakkuk to write what would happen to the final generation, but the "mysteries" were revealed to the Teacher, "to whom God made known all the mysteries of the words of his servants the prophets" (Habakkuk 2). He says other texts claim God put his words in this prophet's mouth, and that no other Jewish leader of the period claims revelation of this kind. He also says the Habakkuk commentary says "the righteous will live by faith" (Habakkuk 2:4), and that faith in the Teacher was what counted.

### 5. The 40-year countdown and its failure
The Damascus Document says that "from the day of the gathering of the Teacher until the end of all the men of war who deserted to the liar" about 40 years will pass. A Psalms commentary says "a little while the wicked will be no more" (Psalms 37) and interprets this as the wicked "blotted out" after 40 years. Tabor calculates 390 + 20 + 40 + about 40 years of the Teacher's ministry, totaling 490, which he links to Daniel 9's 70 weeks and to the "ten jubilees" of 49 years. He notes the Scrolls speak of an "atonement of all the sons of Light" in the first week of the final jubilee, and concludes that "the date doesn't come": prophecy failed, and the community responded with the claim that God "has prolonged" the days. Tabor says the Habakkuk commentary quotes Habakkuk 2:3-4 ("it will surely come... don't give up") to explain the delay, and he says this is where "we kind of trail off with the teacher."

### 6. Teacher hymns and the open questions (deferred)
Tabor says some 11 or 12 Psalms, which many scholars call "teacher hymns," may be first-person writing from the Teacher himself, and that they give a sense of a personality, including an enemy who sits at table and lifts a hand against him, and the striking of the Shepherd (Zechariah 13:7 is alluded to as "strike the shepherd and the sheep will be scattered"). He names the questions he will take up in the course: whether the Teacher died violently, whether the death was expected or a disappointment, how the group handled the missing leader, and whether the Thanksgiving Hymns imply he was taken up to heaven and given a throne among the gods. He says he will not settle these in the talk.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Teacher is so anonymous that nothing can be known about him.** Tabor says that although his name is unknown, the scrolls yield a set of reasonably supported points about when he lived, what the community expected of him, and how they understood his death, if the texts are read critically.
- **The Teacher lived in the second century BCE, around 176 BCE.** Tabor says that once the chronology is read correctly, the figures named in the scrolls point to about 70 BCE, in the first century BCE.
- **"Prophet like Moses" in Deuteronomy 18 just means any successor to Moses, such as Joshua.** Tabor argues that Joshua consults the priest Eleazar and does not act as a prophet like Moses, so the expectation in the scrolls is for a final, Moses-like figure.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Jesus and early Christianity compared with the Teacher.** Tabor draws parallels with the "prophet like Moses" in Acts 3 and 7, the "way" language (the Damascus Document's "groping for the way" versus Acts' "the Way"), the "voice crying in the wilderness" in John, and the new covenant motif. He says the Teacher movement predates Jesus by about a century, but he cautions that this is parallelism, not proof of direct influence.
- **Torah, calendar and festivals.** The Teacher's authority is presented as deciding the correct Sabbath, festival and calendar practice, including the Jubilees 364-day year; the community treated the Jerusalem calendar as false.
- **Who the Teacher thought he was.** The talk frames the Teacher as a final, inspired interpreter of Torah and prophecy, with his death linked to an end-time countdown. Tabor says the Teacher is "not a Messiah in the davidic sense" and uses "anointed" in a broad way.
- **Resurrection, exaltation and afterlife.** Tabor raises, but does not resolve, the possibility that the Thanksgiving Hymns speak of the Teacher being raised to heaven and given a throne among the gods. He defers the discussion.
- **Violence and death of the leader.** Tabor poses whether the Teacher was killed or died naturally, and whether his death was expected or a disappointment, but says the texts need closer reading before any answer.
