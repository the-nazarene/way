## Summary

Tabor asks whether Magdala (Migdal), a first-century harbor town on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee, was the hometown of Mary Magdalene. He calls the "Mary from Magdala" reading the majority view among scholars and says he leans toward it, while noting alternatives drawn from the name. He works through three layers: what Josephus reports about the town, what the excavations have uncovered, and what the Gospels say about Mary.

On the archaeology, Tabor describes a large commercial center rather than a village. He cites Josephus for a population of about 40,000, a Roman-era fish-processing industry (the Greek name Taria and the garum sauce), military clashes, and a rebellion-era massacre. He describes excavated remains including a marketplace with 28 shops, 300 fishing weights, 40 pools likely used for fish processing, about 4,000 coins, and two first-century synagogues. The second synagogue's identity and the "mystery Stone" with menorah, gates, fish, and palm motifs are discussed as open questions. He stresses that only a small part of the site has been excavated.

For Mary, Tabor reads Mark 15:40-47, Mark 16:1, Luke 8:1-3, and John's account of the empty tomb. He argues that Mary's presence with Jesus's mother and family, and her prominence in Luke, fit a Galilean origin. He then turns to the empty-tomb narrative, arguing that Jesus was crucified on a Thursday and that the earliest tradition had Mary, Peter, and the Beloved Disciple (whom he identifies as James the brother of Jesus) respond without yet expecting a resurrection. He closes by saying he now thinks there is evidence that Mary was a close companion of Jesus, possibly his wife, a view he says he once dismissed, and he refers listeners to his book for the argument. The talk ends with a reflective note on visiting the synagogue and the value of touching the first-century past.

## Key Teachings

### Magdala as a major first-century urban center

Tabor argues that Magdala was a prosperous city, not a quaint village. He relies mainly on Josephus, who reports about 40,000 residents at times and says the town was second only to Tiberias in the region. He ties its wealth to a salted-fish industry, citing the Greek name Taria and the Roman delicacy garum. He describes military history at the site: Maccabean fighting around 165 BCE, Herod the Great's campaigns, and battles during the First Jewish Revolt, where Josephus describes large naval and land forces and a massacre or enslavement of the population. Tabor says the excavated finds (fishing weights, pools, shops, and wide commercial links to Jerusalem, Caesarea Maritima, and Mediterranean ports) support a city of commerce. He notes that the theater and hippodrome Josephus mentions have not been found.

### The name "Magdala" and alternative readings of "Magdalene"

Tabor explains that migdal means "tower," so the town may have been named for a watchtower on the shore. He lists three possible readings of Mary's byname: (1) "from Magdala," the majority view; (2) a nickname like Jesus's other nicknames (Peter/Cephas, the "sons of thunder," Thomas the "twin"), where "Tower" could mean a tower of strength or faith, linked to Micah's "tower of the flock" (he cites a verse 8 passage and later Micah 5 for the Davidic Messiah theme); and (3) a Hebrew-root reading, Miriam mdala, related to "to grow" or to a "baby tender" or hairdresser. He labels the nickname readings speculative and says he tends to favor Magdala.

### The two synagogues and the mystery Stone

Tabor presents the synagogues as major evidence. He says that when he began his career it was textbook knowledge that there were no first-century synagogues, so references in the Gospels and Acts were considered possibly literary inventions. He now counts at least ten known first-century synagogues across the region (including Capernaum, Masada-era Herodian sites, Gamla, and Magdala) and says Magdala has two. The first synagogue is small, with benches, plastered walls, and geometric mosaics without figurative motifs. The stone in its center has a menorah, what he identifies as temple gates, palm trees, and what he says are two fish (he rejects the view that they are loaves of bread, pointing to the tail, body, and head). He says the stone's purpose is unknown. A replica is displayed, and the original is said to be in Jerusalem and still under study. Tabor presents the stone as a possible symbolic link to Jerusalem.

### Mary Magdalene in the Synoptic and Johannine accounts

Tabor reads Mark 15:40 as the earliest mention of Mary Magdalene at the crucifixion, listed with Mary the mother of James and Salome, whom he treats as very likely Jesus's mother and sister. He reads Mark 15:47 and 16:1 for the burial and spices, and Luke 8:1-3 for Mary "from whom seven demons had gone out" and her support of Jesus with Joanna, wife of Chuza (Herod's steward), and Susanna. He says Luke's wording implies Mary was a woman of means. He stresses that in John she is at the tomb alone while still dark, and that John's account, with Peter and the Beloved Disciple, fits a version in which the body was moved the previous night.

### Thursday crucifixion and the early empty-tomb tradition

Tabor argues that Jesus was crucified on a Thursday, not a Friday, so the body would have been moved after two back-to-back Sabbaths (the Passover Sabbath and the weekly one), with decay as the motivation for a permanent reburial on Joseph of Arimathea's property. He reads John 20:1-10 as a realistic account: the body was moved, the grave left closed, Mary reported the empty tomb to Peter and "the other disciple," whom he identifies as James the brother of Jesus. He notes the disciples did not yet know the scripture that he must rise, so they did not think he had risen. He says the disciples then returned home and cites the Gospel of Peter (a fragment he says was discovered in 1886 in Upper Egypt) as an early tradition of the disciples returning to Galilee after the feast, with Peter and Andrew going back to fishing. He argues this fits a timeline that the Acts narrative, which keeps the disciples in Jerusalem, later changes.

### Mary as a close companion of Jesus

Tabor says that after writing The Jesus Dynasty he had dismissed the idea that Mary Magdalene was Jesus's wife, but he now thinks there is evidence for it. He points to his book The Jesus Discovery, chapter 5, for the argument. He links this to the Jerusalem-area tomb inscriptions he discussed in an earlier episode (a "Mariam" and a "Jesus son of Joseph" ossuary) and to a child named Judah, but says he will not argue it in this video. He also notes that the earliest Church tradition describes Mary as possibly the "Apostle of the Apostles," and says later Gnostic texts such as the Gospel of Mary present her in a prominent role, though he says the historicity of those texts is unknown.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Magdala was a quaint village.** Tabor says it was a large urban port and industrial center, with commercial links across the region, and that Josephus gives it a population of around 40,000 at times.
- **There were no first-century synagogues.** He says the older textbook view has been overturned by finds, with at least ten first-century synagogues now known and two at Magdala.
- **The fish on the mystery Stone are loaves of bread.** He says the tail, body, and head show they are fish, though the stone's purpose remains unknown.
- **Mary Magdalene's name simply means she was a sinner or a hairdresser.** Tabor lists the hairdresser and "tower" readings as speculative alternatives, while he tends toward "from Magdala."

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor identifies the "other disciple" in John 20 as James the brother of Jesus, and places him among the pillars of the early church, reading an early tradition that James and Peter were the first to respond to the empty tomb.
- **Jesus's family:** Tabor reads Mark's list of women at the cross as including Jesus's mother and possibly his sister, and argues that the early tradition places Jesus's family with the disciples in Galilee at Passover.
- **Resurrection:** He argues that the earliest empty-tomb account had the disciples going home without expecting resurrection, based on the Gospel of Peter and John 20, and that the resurrection expectation was a later development.
- **Gospels and canon:** He contrasts the Gospel of Peter fragment and the Acts narrative (disciples staying in Jerusalem) with the earlier tradition he reconstructs, and treats Luke's account of the women's support as preserving a tradition that the Synoptic writers handled differently.
- **Kingdom and who Jesus was:** Tabor says Jesus's followers included wealthy women and that the Jesus movement had a Galilean base, and he raises but does not settle the question of Mary's marriage to Jesus.
