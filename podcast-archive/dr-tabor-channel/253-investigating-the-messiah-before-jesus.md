## Summary

Tabor, interviewed by Derek Lambert of the MythVision Podcast, asks whether the Dead Sea Scrolls contain a figure who expected to suffer and be exalted before Jesus, and whether Mark's portrayal of Jesus follows that pattern. His main evidence is the Thanksgiving Hymns (the "teacher hymns") from the Dead Sea Scrolls, which he reads as autobiographical first-person texts by or in the name of the Teacher of Righteousness. In them the speaker echoes the Isaiah servant songs ("a Man of Sorrows," rejection, being "a ruined vessel"), claims a glorified heavenly status ("there's none comparable to me in glory"), and describes being "drawn up" from the pit. Tabor reads this alongside Mark's account of Jesus predicting suffering, rebuking Peter's expectation of a triumphant kingdom, and the Transfiguration, and he suggests Jesus may have held a similar view of suffering followed by exaltation.

Tabor says the Scrolls are not direct proof about Jesus. He argues that the Teacher's self-understanding makes it plausible that the "suffering then glory" pattern is older than the gospel writers, and he says he thinks it "likely that it goes back to the historical Jesus." He also points to Morton Smith's reaction to the hymn texts in the 1990s, to Michael Wise's book on a pre-Christian Messiah, and to Zechariah's "two anointed ones" and the Adon/Messiah pairing in Psalms 2 and 110 as possible frameworks for the expectations of the period. He notes uncertainty throughout: whether the Teacher was killed or simply died, and whether the hymns were written by him or by his followers.

The talk ends as a promotion for Tabor's Dead Sea Scrolls course and a Sunday Zoom session on Mark and the Scrolls. Its closing discussion turns to Revelation 11 and Zechariah, and to the idea that the movement expected a transformed world in which marriage and commerce would pass away. Tabor presents this as the expectation of the Scroll group and of early Christians alike.

## Key Teachings

### The Teacher hymns as autobiography and the Isaiah servant
Tabor's central claim is that the Thanksgiving Hymns (about 40 in total, with the teacher hymns usually numbered roughly 10 to 17) speak in an unusually personal first-person voice, which is why many scholars call them the "teacher hymns." He argues the Teacher reads himself into the Isaiah servant songs: he speaks of being "not esteemed," "driven from my land," "like a bird from The Nest," and having his friends turn against him. Tabor notes that Isaiah 53's "many" recurs in the hymn, and that the hymn's language of being "called from the womb" echoes Isaiah 49. He also reads the Isaiah scroll from Cave 1, which has marginal marks, as possible evidence that someone was studying these passages closely.

### Exaltation and a heavenly throne
Tabor reads the hymns as claiming a heavenly status. He quotes lines about having "a mighty throne in the congregation of angels," being unmatched "in glory," and having his "Abode" in the holy congregation. He reads the lines about the "wonderful counselor" (Isaiah 9) as the speaker identifying himself with that title. He calls this a "Jesus 1.0" self-consciousness, a term the host supplies, and he uses it to ask how such a self-understanding would relate to Mark's Jesus. Tabor cites Morton Smith's claim, which Tabor reports second-hand, that the text showed a pre-Christian figure "exalted to a throne in heaven."

### Mark: suffering, rebuke, and the Transfiguration
Tabor's reading of Mark has three parts. In Mark 8 Jesus predicts his fate and Peter rebukes him, which Tabor reads as the disciples expecting a Jerusalem takeover and a kingdom. He says Jesus instead points to the pattern of rejection in Isaiah and Zechariah. He treats the Transfiguration (Mark 9) as a "proleptic" vision of the kingdom and Jesus's glory, and he suggests Mark may present resurrection as a transfigured state rather than a body walking around Jerusalem. He cites "quite a few Scholars" who see resurrection as apotheosis, a term he uses without naming the scholars. He also notes Mark 10, where James and John ask for the places on Jesus's right and left.

### Two Messiahs, the Adon, and Zechariah
Tabor connects the teacher's exaltation to a broader Second Temple pattern of multiple anointed figures. He cites Zechariah's "two anointed ones who stand before the Lord of the whole Earth," and notes that the Hebrew "Adon" in that phrase differs from the divine name, which he links to Psalms 2 and 110 and to Jesus's question in Mark about why David calls the Messiah "Lord." He suggests the possibilities were a priestly Messiah, a Davidic Messiah, and a figure who was himself "the Adon." He presents these as candidates the various groups tried to assemble into a "Messianic Mosaic," and he admits that the expected events did not happen as predicted.

### Suffering, nonviolence, and the expected transformation
Tabor argues that a figure who expects to be rejected and to die, like the lamb "led to the slaughter" of Isaiah 53, would not gather an armed resistance but would rely on God for vindication. He compares this to Jesus's teachings on loving enemies, which he attributes to the earliest teaching tradition, and to Luke's portrayal of forgiveness at the cross. He reads the Scrolls' "Sons of Light" war material as depending on God's intervention rather than human strength. He also reads Romans 8 (being "glorified with him" provided "we suffer with him") and the Philippians 2 hymn as the same pattern of reversal. Tabor cites Paula Fredriksen's description of the world transformed, with death and suffering ended, and he argues that Paul's advice to "not even get married" reflects the expectation that "the schema" of this world is passing away.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Scrolls are proof that Jesus was a copy of an earlier figure.** Tabor says the Teacher is not a direct source for Jesus, and the Scrolls show a pattern of expectation, not a fixed template. He also says the hymns may have been written by followers in the Teacher's name.
- **Isaiah's servant is simply Israel.** Tabor says that in four hymns the servant becomes individual and speaks in the first person, so a messianic reading is available.
- **Two anointed figures or a dying Messiah were foreign to Judaism.** Tabor argues that the Zechariah two-anointed pattern and the Psalms 2 and 110 royal texts were available to the period, and that a dying figure appears in Isaiah 53.
- **The prophecies were fulfilled literally by early Christians.** Tabor says groups read texts like Zechariah 12 as fitting a "Messianic Mosaic," and that the expected events, such as a return in the clouds, did not happen as predicted. He calls this a "historical" rather than "cynical" comment.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor reads Romans 8 and Philippians 2 as sharing Mark's pattern of suffering followed by glory, and he suggests Paul's advice about marriage reflects an expected passing of the world.
- **The Essenes and the Dead Sea Scrolls:** The talk's whole focus is the Teacher of Righteousness and the Thanksgiving Hymns, read as possibly autobiographical. The talk does not address whether the Teacher was an Essene, nor the Nazarene or Ebionite communities.
- **Nonviolence:** Tabor links the suffering servant pattern and Jesus's teaching on enemies to a refusal of armed resistance, while noting the War Scroll's reliance on God's intervention.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Tabor reads Mark's kingdom as an expectation of a transformed world, and he argues Revelation's New Jerusalem is a renewed earth.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and heavenly exaltation:** Tabor reads resurrection in Mark as transfiguration and apotheosis rather than a bodily return, though he acknowledges the scholarly question is open.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** The talk raises the possibility that Jesus saw himself as the exalted figure in a line of messianic claimants, but Tabor presents this as likely rather than established.
