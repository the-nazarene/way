## Summary

Tabor asks where Jesus spent the months between his northern journey in Mark's Gospel and his arrival in Jerusalem for Passover. His answer is that Jesus spent the last winter, probably December 29 CE through March, in hiding east of the Jordan, in a wadi near Anan (Aenon) and Salim, outside the territory of Herod Antipas. He builds the case in layers. He first traces Mark's route on a map: Capernaum, Caesarea Philippi, a "high mountain" he identifies with Mount Hermon rather than Mount Tabor, then back through Galilee to Capernaum and south. He then turns to John 10:40, where Jesus "went away again across the Jordan to the place where John had first baptized and there he remained," and to John 3, which places John's baptizing at Anan near Salim. He connects this area to the brook Kerith, where 1 Kings says Elijah hid, and to Pella, where he says the Jerusalem followers of Jesus fled in 66 CE according to early Christian sources.

Tabor's main evidence is textual and geographical, with fieldwork he describes as preliminary. He reports a 1992 visit prompted by the John 10:40 verse, five later return trips, caves he describes as defensible and hard to reach, and pottery collected and studied (he says Professor Strange found mostly Iron Age, Hellenistic and early-to-late Roman material). He ties this to the Copper Scroll's cross-Jordan hiding places, the Damascus Document's "place of Refuge," and Mark 13's instruction to flee to the mountains. He is explicit that none of this is proven: "I can't prove any of this yet."

The talk ends with Tabor inviting the audience to respond ("what do you think"). He points viewers to Jesus Archaeology #10, "A Jesus Hideout in Jordan," for the site details. He also says he will next cover Jesus in Jerusalem, beginning with the raising of Lazarus in John.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Mark's northern itinerary and the "high mountain"
Tabor reads Mark 8 through 10 as a route: Bethsaida, then the villages of Caesarea Philippi (outside Herod Antipas's territory, under Philip), then a "high mountain" six days later where the Transfiguration occurs (Mark 9:2-8), then back through Galilee to Capernaum and south. He rejects the traditional Mount Tabor identification. He says it is "not very high" and "really doesn't fit the description or the Geography," and that the tradition arose in the Byzantine, Crusader and pilgrim periods. He proposes Mount Hermon, which he says has snow for much of the year.

### 2. Expectation of the kingdom and the suffering
He notes Mark 9:1 ("some standing here who will not taste of death before they see the kingdom of God has come with power") as evidence that Jesus expected the kingdom's imminent arrival. He says most scholars think Mark created the idea of necessary suffering (drawing on Isaiah's suffering servant passages). Tabor disagrees, arguing from the Dead Sea Scrolls and the "Teacher of Righteousness" that Jesus could have anticipated suffering while still expecting deliverance and resurrection. He says he develops this in his Dead Sea Scrolls course.

### 3. John 10:40 and the "last winter"
Tabor argues that Mark has no chronology for the stay beyond Galilee, while John (which he assumes was written later, perhaps aware of Mark) gives dated Jerusalem visits: the Feast of Tabernacles, a clandestine trip at the Feast of Dedication (Hanukkah, which he dates to December 29 CE), an escape from an arrest attempt (John 10:39), and then John 10:40 placing Jesus across the Jordan at the site of John's early baptizing. Tabor says he had not noticed the verse before a class in 1992, and that when he connected it to John 3's "Anan near Salim," he decided to go check the area.

### 4. Geography east of the Jordan: Wadi Yabis, Salim and Pella
He describes Tel Salim (never excavated, he says, apart from a cut through the middle by archaeologists), the wadi (Wadi Yabis, which he associates with the brook Kerith of 1 Kings), and the cliffs and caves along the ravine. He notes that a border he says separates Perea from the Decapolis lies nearby, meaning that crossing east puts one outside Herod's jurisdiction. Pella is less than four miles north. He suggests the flight of the Nazarene community to Pella in 66 CE (after James's murder in 63 and the Roman revolt) may have involved the same region where Jesus had stayed.

### 5. Jerusalem's Essene quarter, Mount Zion and Qumran pottery
Tabor proposes that the Jerusalem community (which he calls "the Nazarene followers") had a stronghold in the Essene quarter on Mount Zion, where a Jerusalem upper room may have been available to Jesus. He mentions the property, probes by Bargil Pixner that found small Jewish dwellings with mikvaot and coins around 68 CE, and his hope that pottery matching Qumran types might link the Mount Zion and Wadi Yabis sites. He is candid that this is hypothesis: "we don't do archaeology to prove theories but we have to have theories." He says Scrolls are unlikely to survive in this wet climate, so pottery and inscriptions are the realistic evidence.

### 6. Flight texts: Mark 13, the Copper Scroll, the Damascus Document and Revelation 12
Tabor reads Mark 13:14 ("let those who are in Judea flee to the mountains") as a memory of a flight at the time of the Jewish-Roman war, and he links it to Daniel's "desolating sacrilege." He cites the Copper Scroll's cross-Jordan hiding places, the Damascus Document's "teacher" fleeing to a place of refuge, and Stephen Goranson's argument (presented at a SBL meeting in 1991) that "land of Damascus" refers to Perea east of the Jordan. He also reads Revelation 12's woman who flees "into the Wilderness to her place" as possibly symbolic memory of this flight. He calls Revelation 12 "the most speculative one."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Transfiguration happened on Mount Tabor.** Tabor says the Mount Tabor identification is a later tradition from the Byzantine, Crusader and pilgrim periods, and that the mountain is too low and the geography does not fit. He proposes Mount Hermon.
- **Mark's account of a straight, brief trip from Galilee to Jerusalem is the whole story.** Tabor says that Mark's route can look like a "straight shot," but that John 10:40 and John's other visits imply a longer period, including a winter stay east of the Jordan.
- **The suffering of Jesus was entirely Mark's invention.** He says this is the majority scholarly view, and he argues instead that Jesus could have anticipated suffering while expecting deliverance, based on the Dead Sea Scrolls.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor says James was murdered in 63 and that Simon, identified as Jesus's brother, took over; the community then fled across the Jordan to Pella during the 66 CE revolt. He calls this a "Nazarene" flight tradition.
- **Nazarenes, Ebionites and Essenes:** Tabor ties the Nazarene community to the Essene quarter on Mount Zion, and to Dead Sea Scrolls references (Copper Scroll, Damascus Document) to hiding places across the Jordan. He does not discuss Ebionites in this talk.
- **The Kingdom on earth and apocalyptic expectation:** Mark 9:1 and Mark 13 are central. Tabor reads them as evidence that Jesus and the early movement expected the kingdom soon and anticipated a Jerusalem catastrophe.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** The Caesarea Philippi confession (Mark 8:27-30) is the starting point, though Tabor's focus is geographical.
- **How the gospels were formed:** Tabor treats John as later than Mark and possibly aware of Mark, and treats John's details (dated visits, the John 10:40 location) as potentially independent evidence of the winter stay.
