## Summary

Tabor introduces the video as an interview with Dead Sea Scrolls scholar Robert Eisenman, whose views he calls outside the mainstream, and says the interview shows Eisenman's life and his basic theory of Christian origins, Paul, James and Jesus. The interview itself is mostly Eisenman speaking. He describes his path from Cornell, Paris and Islamic-law doctorate work into teaching courses on the Dead Sea Scrolls and on Paul and James at Cal State Long Beach, where he says teaching the texts repeatedly is what led him to his views.

The core question is what the historical Palestinian movement behind the Scrolls was, and how it relates to the Jesus, James and Paul of the New Testament. Eisenman argues that the Scrolls describe a militant, apocalyptic "Palestinian messianism," best understood as messianic Sadducees, not the peaceful Christianity familiar to most readers. He reads Acts' "we" passages as the most reliable historical material, uses them to present James as the leader of the church and Paul as a zealot for the law who was nonetheless compromising, and argues that Paul was a Herodian kinsman. He says the Gospels are literature, while the Scrolls give a more accurate picture of the historical Jesus, and especially of his execution.

The talk ends with Eisenman stepping back from doctrine. He says his only faith is in righteousness, which he thinks people know intuitively, and closes with "But don't tell me that that's history." ## Key Teachings

### The Scrolls as messianic war literature, not recognizable Christianity
Eisenman says scholars who are Christians could not see the Scrolls as connected to the Christianity they knew, because the Scrolls are "apocalyptic," "full of anger," and say "hate the sons of the pit." He contrasts this with the "love turn the other cheek" Christianity of the Gospels, which he calls "highly polonized" and a Greco-Roman production. The War Scroll's "sons of light against the sons of darkness" and the holy warrior angels joining the community's camps against Rome are his main examples. He calls the movement "Palestinian messianism" and says even "Palestinian Christianity" is the wrong term.

### Messianic Sadducees and the Zadokite identity
Eisenman argues that the Scrolls' "sons of Zadok" should be read as Sadducees, using wordplay on Zadok (priesthood) and *ZDK* (righteousness). He says the Sadducees of Josephus and the Gospels were establishment collaborators with the Herodian monarchy, so the community must be an opposition, anti-Roman, anti-Herodian Sadducee group, which he calls "messianic Sadducees." He says the editors of the Scrolls rejected this reading because it did not fit their Christian expectations.

### Sicarii, Zealots and the mass suicide at Masada
Eisenman cites Hippolytus, whom he calls a second- or third-century Roman historian, as listing four kinds of Essenes. He identifies the last two with the Zealots and the Sicarii, names the Sicarii after the Latin word for the curved dagger, and says they assassinated Herodian collaborators. Josephus, he says, describes the Sicarii withdrawing to Masada and committing suicide rather than submit to Rome. He argues the Sicarii Essenes were the authors of the Scrolls, which were also found at Masada.

### Acts' "we" passages, James, and Paul the zealot
Eisenman says the first fifteen or so chapters of Acts resemble the Gospels, but after about chapter 15 the narrative shifts to first-person plural, which he reads as an authentic travel document from someone with Paul. He treats Acts 21:21 as the key case: James speaks, Peter is not the leader, and Paul is confronted with the claim that "we are all zealots for the law." He then reads Paul's willingness to pay expenses for Nazirites in the Temple as a sign of flexibility and a lack of principle, and he says the Temple crowd's reaction in Acts shows the episode is historical.

### Paul as a Herodian kinsman
Eisenman says the greetings at the end of Romans include a "kinsman" named Herodion, which he takes to mean the "littlest Herod," a member of the exiled Herodian family. He connects this to Paul's Tarsus origins and Roman citizenship, and says Paul's Roman passport rescued him in Jerusalem. He calls this "not my prophet" and says he sets the Pauline corpus aside for historical purposes but not for "spiritual enlightenment."

### James the Just as the Teacher of Righteousness
Eisenman says the Latin "Justus" and the Greek "dikaios" for James the Just are parallel to Hebrew *Zadik*, and that the community's leader, the "Teacher of Righteousness," is the model for James. He says the Scrolls explain who the Sadik was, and that someone in the early community knew James was Jesus' successor, as the Gospel of Thomas reflects. He stresses he does not take every Thomas saying as authentic, and says a method of starting with a few reliable passages, such as Acts 21:21, is the way forward.

### Gospels as literature, and Pilate's crucifixion
Eisenman says that "literature is a very facile form of history," and the Gospels are "three four variations on basically the same theme." He argues the Gospels' account of Jesus' execution, in which the Romans made a mistake and the Jews plotted against him, is "hocus." Pilate, he says, crucified more people than almost any governor according to Josephus and knew exactly whom he was crucifying. He says the Scrolls offer a more accurate picture of the historical Jesus because they explain the execution and the Jamesian community.

### Righteousness, purity, and wilderness camps
Eisenman describes the Damascus Document's wilderness camps, where members stayed celibate, bathed daily, and maintained purity so the heavenly host could live among them, and he says they survived in part and may have taught Muhammad in caves. He then sets aside doctrine and argues that righteousness is the central core of the prophets and the Scrolls, that people know right and wrong "in my soul," and that no book tells him how to live. He criticizes "Christian nations" for warfare that contradicts the love-your-enemy ethic.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Scrolls are a Christian or quietist Essene document.** Eisenman argues they are apocalyptic, militant "Palestinian messianism," and that the Christian scholars who edited them could not recognize this.
- **The Sadducees were only establishment collaborators.** Eisenman argues that some Sadducees were an opposition, anti-Roman messianic group, and that the Scrolls' "sons of Zadok" were such Sadducees.
- **Paul was an opponent of the law.** Eisenman reads Acts' "we" passages as showing Paul and the Jerusalem church were both "zealots for the law," and he says Paul's Temple conduct was compromise, not principle.
- **The Gospels are an accurate history of Jesus' execution.** Eisenman argues Pilate did not make mistakes, and that the Gospels' account is contrived.
- **Christian nations have lived out love of enemy.** Eisenman says Christian nations have been among the most warlike, so their behavior does not match the doctrine they claim.
