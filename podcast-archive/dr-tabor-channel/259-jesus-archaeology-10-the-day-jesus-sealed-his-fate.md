He presents this episode as an introduction to that week, centered on Jesus's action in the Temple. He describes the route Jesus took from Bethany over the Mount of Olives, his ride down on a donkey, and his entry into the Temple Mount, where he drove out buyers and sellers and blocked passage through the courts. ## Key Teachings

### The Donkey Entry as a Messianic Claim
Tabor argues that Jesus's ride down the Mount of Olives was deliberate. He ties it to Zechariah 9:9, where the king comes "riding on an ass," and notes that the same passage speaks of cutting off the chariot and the battle bow. He admits that some scholars think the scene was added later by Mark, but he says he considers it "an accurate account of what most likely happened." He reads the act as a claim to be the Messiah, and he links it to the Son of Man imagery in Daniel 7 and to the Dead Sea Scrolls' Teacher of Righteousness, whose expectations also went unfulfilled in his own time.

### The Temple Action as the Trigger
Tabor's main argument is that the Temple is what drove the authorities. He describes the Temple action as a "shutdown," in which Jesus blocked the entrances and exits so that people could not carry things through the Temple. He reads Mark 11 as the earliest account and cites Zechariah 14 for the expectation that there will be no traders in the house of the Lord. He concludes that the chief priests and scribes "sought away to destroy him" because the Temple was threatened.

### The Money Changers and the Tyrian Shekel
Tabor disputes the common explanation that the coins had to be changed because they were "Pagan money." He says the priests required that all coins be converted to Tyrian shekels because they were about 99.99% pure silver, and that the Temple wanted its donations in the purest silver. He notes that the coins could carry Pagan images on the back and human images on the front, which were not allowed on Jewish coins. He also suggests the exchange rate was steep, comparing it to airport prices.

### House of Prayer versus Den of Robbers
Tabor says Jesus is quoting Jeremiah 7:11, and he questions the usual translation "den of robbers." He argues that the Greek word *lēstōn*, usually rendered as robbers or brigands, can mean a violent person who will "jump you and stab you," and that the Hebrew word he discusses, paratz, means to break open or shred. He reads Jeremiah 7 and Isaiah 35:9 as describing a "layer of shredders" or ravenous beasts, so that the Temple has become a place of bloodshed rather than prayer.

### Sacrifice Condemned by the Prophets and by Ebionites
Tabor cites Jeremiah 7 (where he says Jeremiah speaks sarcastically about burnt offerings) and Amos, and he quotes Hosea 6:6 ("I desire loving kindness not sacrifice"). He reports that Epiphanius records a saying of Jesus among the Ebionites: "I have come to abolish sacrifices." He presents this as a Jewish-follower tradition distinct from the Pauline wing of the movement. He then argues that Jesus's action directly challenged the sacrificial Cult of the Temple, and he compares the danger to what Jeremiah faced, who was arrested and nearly killed.

### Jesus Among the Messiah Claimants
Tabor places Jesus within the wider pattern described by Josephus, who mentions a dozen messianic figures, all of whom were largely wiped out. He names Judas the Galilean and Simon of Perea as examples of others who acted in various ways. He argues that Jesus differed because he came to Jerusalem, the capital, and confronted the Temple authority directly. He also draws a parallel: Jeremiah spoke against the Temple 40 years before the destruction of the first Temple, and Jesus did the same 40 years before the destruction of the second.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The money changers were needed because the coins were "Pagan money."** Tabor says the Temple required conversion into Tyrian shekels because of their purity, and that the exchange was costly.
- **"Den of robbers" means a den of thieves dividing loot.** Tabor argues the Hebrew and Greek suggest a place where ravenous, violent animals shred prey, and that the usual translation misses this.
- **The donkey entry is a domesticated "home Sunday" event.** Tabor calls the entry a "revolutionary move" tied to Zechariah 9:9, not a pious procession.
- **The Western Wall is the Temple.** Tabor says it is actually part of the Pavilion (Portico) that Herod built around the Temple.
- **The Mount of Olives donkey scene was invented by Mark.** Tabor acknowledges that some scholars hold this view but argues the account is likely accurate.
- **Jesus traveled with twelve men.** Tabor says the entourage included his mother, sisters, brothers, the apostles' families, women, and children, and that the Twelve were the core leadership.
