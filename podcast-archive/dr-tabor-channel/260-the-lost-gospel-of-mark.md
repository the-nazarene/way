## Summary

Tabor opens a promotional video for his online course on the Gospel of Mark, "Creating Jesus," and asks why Mark is so rarely read on its own terms. His explanation is that readers meet Matthew and Luke first, treat Mark as a shorter version of Matthew, and move on. He says Mark lacks a birth story, opens without a clear account of who Jesus is, and ends abruptly at the empty tomb with the women fleeing in fear and saying nothing to anyone.

Tabor argues that Mark is the original Gospel story, that Matthew and Luke "overwrite" it, and that Mark's ending and portrait of Jesus were later smoothed over. He gives three examples: Jesus's parables explained as a way of keeping the crowds from understanding, Jesus rejecting the title "good" from a man who calls him "good Rabbi," and Peter's confession that Jesus is the Christ, which Tabor calls a false confession inspired by Satan. He also says that scribes added three other endings to Mark. The video is mainly a sales pitch, so these claims are asserted with little supporting evidence. ## Key Teachings

### Mark is the original Gospel story, and Matthew and Luke overwrite it
Tabor says that "Mark creates Jesus" and "gives us the only Jesus story that we have really." He says Matthew and Luke "use it but they're not happy with it," and that they "overwrite" it rather than simply rewrite it. As an example he notes that Luke removes the failure of the disciples. He says Paul "didn't give a story" while Mark "gives the story." No manuscript evidence or textual comparison is shown in the clip.

### Mark's ending and the tacked-on endings
Tabor describes the empty-tomb ending (Mark 16): a young man, "not an Angel by the way," tells the women that Jesus "was taken up," and the women "flee from the tomb" and "said nothing to anyone." He calls this a "non-gospel" ending that is an embarrassment to later Christians, who he says added "three other endings... by Pious Christian scribes" because they could not leave Mark ending this way. He presents this as a reason Mark was marginalized, but does not name the manuscripts or scholars involved.

### Jesus's parables and the hidden meaning
Tabor says Mark's Jesus explains his use of parables by saying he does not want the crowds to understand or be converted, which he calls a puzzling portrait. He presents this as one of several features that do not fit the later Gospel picture of Jesus, though the clip gives no further explanation.

### "Why are you calling me good?"
Tabor cites the exchange in which a man addresses Jesus as "good Rabbi" and Jesus replies, "why are you calling me good? There's none good but God." Tabor uses this as an example of Mark's distinctive presentation of Jesus, and as a sign that the Gospel "doesn't seem to fit" a simple devotional picture.

### Peter's confession as a "false confession"
Tabor says that in Mark, after the question "who is he," Peter answers "you're the Christ," and Jesus replies, "Get Behind Me Satan." Tabor says he teaches his students that "Peter's confession of Jesus as the Christ is a false confession inspired by Satan." He acknowledges this is "kind of shocking" and says the course works out how and why. This is his reading of Mark, not a point settled by evidence in the clip.

### Mark's "secret" and the reader's experience
Tabor presents the course as a way for readers to "get your own text of Mark" and read it "without assuming anything," so they can see the puzzles of the text for themselves. He describes the approach as recovering something "buried and forgotten" in the history of ideas.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mark is just a shortened version of Matthew.** Tabor says that many readers treat Mark as a "cutdown version kind of of Matthew" and that this is wrong. He argues Mark is the earlier, original story and that Matthew and Luke build on it.
- **Mark is unimportant because it lacks a birth story and an ending that matches the others.** Tabor argues these features are why Mark has been "forgotten and overwritten," not a reason it is minor. He says the ending was altered by later scribes.
