## Summary

Tabor opens part 11 of his "Jesus Archaeology" series, the "Final Days of Jesus" section, by asking what the Jerusalem Temple looked like during the last week of Jesus' life, as Mark 11-16 narrates it. He treats the week as seven clusters of temple confrontations, from the question about Jesus' authority through the widow's offering. He argues that the physical setting helps explain the stories. His main evidence is topographical and architectural: Herod's Temple compound, the Antonia Fortress, the Hulda Gates and the steps, the court system with its barriers and warning inscriptions, and the Western Wall. He draws on Leen Ritmeyer's work and on a virtual reconstruction by Jeremy Park, which he shows on screen.

Tabor says that in the temple Jesus confronted the authorities over their legitimacy, taxes, resurrection, and the greatest commandment, and claimed a Davidic role tied to the Daniel 7 "son of man" vision, which he reads as a collective people of God rather than a single figure. He also takes up the Mark 13 temple prophecy. He presents two readings: that Jesus wanted to purify the temple rather than see it destroyed, which he calls the majority view, and that Jesus had Daniel 9's "desolating sacrilege" in mind. He leaves the question open.

The talk ends with the tour and a teaser for the next episode, which will cover the evening meal the "Last Supper" and a building Tabor thinks may be identifiable. Tabor carries the whole talk alone, and he presents his interpretations as his own views and as scholarly opinion, not settled fact.

## Key Teachings

### The seven confrontations in the temple (Mark 11-12)
Tabor sorts the week's events into seven units: the question about Jesus' authority, with its counter-question about John the Baptist; the parable of the wicked tenants; the tax question about rendering to Caesar; the Sadducees' question on resurrection; the scribe's question on the greatest commandment; Jesus' question on how the Messiah can be David's son; and the poor widow's two coins. He says the stories make sense only when the setting is clear, since each is staged in the temple's courts and porticos.

### Jesus' claim: Daniel 7 and the "son of man" as a people
Tabor argues that the Davidic-Messiah question leads to Daniel 7. He says the "son of man" coming on the clouds is often read as Jesus alone, but in that chapter it is "interpreted... as the collective people of God." On this reading Jesus' kingdom would involve his disciples and followers sharing rule, with the Twelve gathered together, which he connects to Isaiah 11 and other texts. He cites Jesus' trial answer ("I am... you'll see the son of man coming with power") as confirming this group-centred expectation.

### Taxes and Roman rule: "notice the context"
On the tax question Tabor reads the saying as a boundary drawn inside the temple. Outside the temple, he says, the Romans' affairs are the Romans' business, but inside the temple the concern is "the things of God not with money and commerce." He ties the question to Judas the Galilean's revolt around 6 CE, which he says shaped the atmosphere Jesus grew up in, though he does not name a source for the revolt's details.

### Herod's Temple as a "third Temple"
Tabor describes Herod's expansion as so vast that "it almost could be called a third Temple," with new massive stone enclosures, gates and porticos. He stresses that the temple compound was entirely rebuilt, and that the foundations and some steps and an arch from Herod's period survive. The Hulda Gates and their tunnel approach, the Court of the Women with its thirteen offering boxes, and the warning inscriptions at the barrier are his examples of physical evidence.

### Mark 13 and the destruction of the temple: two readings
Tabor sets out the scholarly question of whether Jesus expected the temple to be destroyed. He says the majority opinion is that Jesus "doesn't really anticipate that the temple is going to be destroyed," and that he wanted to purify it into "a house of prayer for all people." He notes a second view: that Jesus read Daniel 9's "ruler" and "desolating sacrilege" as a coming destruction of city and sanctuary, perhaps as defilement followed by purification in the days of the Maccabees. He does not settle between them.

### Sacrifice and the court system
Tabor describes the sacrificial system as the focus of Jesus' challenge, since he says Jesus "condemns the sacrificial system" when he disrupts commerce in the temple. He explains the graded access in the courts (Gentiles in the outer court, with warning inscriptions, and Israelite men, women and priests in inner courts) to show what was at stake in Paul's arrest in Acts.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Jesus said not one stone would be left on another, so the stones are all gone."** Tabor says that the temple itself is gone and the area is now rubble, but "the foundation of the Box does remain" and some Herodian stones and steps survive.
- **"Son of man in Daniel 7 is Jesus alone."** Tabor argues that Daniel 7 interprets the son of man as the collective people of God, so the expectation involves Jesus together with his followers.
- **"Jesus clearly predicted the temple's total destruction."** Tabor says the question is contested: the majority view, in his account, is that Jesus meant to purify the temple, not see it destroyed, while another reading ties the saying to Daniel 9.
- **"The widow's coins were worth a great deal."** Tabor says the two lepta were tiny, "about a quarter of the size of an American dime," and "not worth hardly anything," which makes her gift's value the point.
