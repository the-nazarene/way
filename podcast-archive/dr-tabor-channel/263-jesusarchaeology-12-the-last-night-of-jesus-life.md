## Summary

Tabor asks how Jesus spent his last evening, and whether the meal in the Synoptic and Johannine accounts was a Passover seder. He argues it was not. In his reading, the meal took place on the night before Passover (Nissan 13), and Jesus was crucified on the afternoon of Nissan 14, when the Passover lambs were being slain. His evidence is textual and comparative: John 13:1 and Luke 22:14-15, Paul's wording in 1 Corinthians 11, a Babylonian Talmud reference to "the eve of Passover," the Gospel of Peter, and a claim that Josephus places the lamb slaughter in the afternoon. He treats Mark's "first day of unleavened bread" wording as possibly confusing rather than decisive.

The second half is archaeological. Tabor follows the route from Bethany over the Mount of Olives, through the Siloam pool area, to Mount Zion. He presents Barill Pixner's proposal that an Essene quarter stood on Mount Zion near an "Essene Gate," and that the Last Supper took place there. He describes the Dormition Abbey and the Crusader-era "Room of the Last Supper," which he says preserves the outline of a first-century house. He ties this to a Byzantine basilica shown on the Madaba map and to the later Jerusalem community of James. He repeatedly hedges, noting that "everybody doesn't agree with pixner but it's a proposal." He ends by placing the arrest in Gethsemane, which he says is probably not the traditional site, and promises the next episode will continue the story.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The meal was the night before Passover, not the Passover itself
Tabor's main claim is chronological. He cites John 13:1 as making the meal "before the Feast of Passover," and reads John's account as having Jesus' enemies avoid the Roman Praetorium that morning so they could eat the Passover that evening. He adds Luke 22:14-15 ("I've earnestly desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer... I will not eat it until it's fulfilled in the Kingdom") as Jesus saying he would not eat it. He also reads Luke's cup saying as a normal Jewish blessing, not a Eucharistic rite. On the Passover timing, he argues that the Lambs were slain on the afternoon of Nissan 14, and that Jesus died in the afternoon of that same day. He says John's Passover framing "could be a theological thing that John creates," but thinks it "fits other sources."

### 2. Paul calls Christ the Passover, not the meal
Tabor notes that Paul in 1 Corinthians 5 calls Christ "the Passover Lamb," yet in 1 Corinthians 11 he describes the Last Supper as taking place "on the night when he was betrayed" and does not call it a Passover meal. Tabor also points out that the bread word Paul uses is artos (leavened loaf), not unleavened bread. He suggests Paul's account and John's are independent witnesses supporting an evening meal before Passover. He adds that Luke's Western text (Codex Bezae, "D") omits some verses about the bread and cup, and he proposes that these may have been added later to match Paul.

### 3. Mark's wording and the water-carrier
Mark 14:12 and 14:16 say the disciples "prepared the Passover," which Tabor says most readers take as a Passover meal. He argues Mark may simply be unclear, since Passover preparation "can take longer than just that afternoon." He notes Mark's detail about a man carrying a jar of water who leads the disciples to a furnished upper room. Tabor locates the water-carrier's route at the Siloam pool, a major pilgrim purification pool, and he presents the Pilgrim's Path, recently uncovered, as the road up to the Temple Mount. He pauses on why a man rather than a woman carries the water, because this detail leads into Pixner's theory.

### 4. The Essene quarter on Mount Zion (Pixner's proposal)
Tabor presents Pixner's argument, which he says is disputed, that Herod gave a special area of Mount Zion to the Essenes, that an "Essene Gate" existed there, and that the water-carrier leads toward this quarter. Tabor describes a field now owned by the Greeks and a Iron Age mikveh (ritual bath) he believes was later cut by Christians. He says Pixner's 1990 Biblical Archaeology Review article led him to identify the area as the headquarters of the Jesus movement, which Pixner called the "synagogue of the Apostles." Tabor also credits Jim Strange, now deceased, as a companion in this investigation.

### 5. The Crusader building and the first-century house
Tabor describes the Room of the Last Supper (Crusader-period upper room over the Dormition Abbey) and says the lower floor and courses of reused Herodian stones suggest a first-century house. He reports excavation finds in the lower room: Roman-period floor levels and judeo-Christian graffiti. He points to the Madaba map (mid-fifth century), which shows the Hagia Sion Basilica built in the 4th century under Theodosius I, and argues that the Crusader church kept the foundations of this earlier house. He concludes that "this is very possibly the headquarters of the Jesus movement in Jerusalem so James would have lived here." He ties this to Acts 12 (Peter telling the disciples to "go tell James and the brothers"), suggesting the family of Jesus lived in a separate place.

### 6. Gethsemane and Judas
Tabor says the traditional Garden of Gethsemane is a medieval Crusader site, not the real place. He draws on Mark 14:26 ("they went out to the Mount of Olives"), Luke's reference to a place "they knew," and John 18:1 (crossing the Kidron). He argues that Judas knew the place because Jesus often met his disciples there, and that the high priests paid Judas to reveal the location. He suggests the garden was somewhere on the Mount of Olives, possibly near a cave, but says "I don't think we have any way of knowing."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Last Supper was a Passover seder.** Tabor argues it was an evening meal the night before Passover, with Jesus dying the next afternoon as the lambs were slain.
- **The Lord's Supper ceremony in Luke 22 reflects the original meal.** Tabor suggests the bread and cup sayings may be a later addition, given the Western text variant and their similarity to Paul.
- **The traditional Garden of Gethsemane is the real site.** Tabor says it is a medieval Crusader site, and that Jesus was more likely arrested somewhere on the Mount of Olives.
- **The traditional Room of the Last Supper on Mount Zion is first-century.** Tabor says the current room is Crusader-era, but he argues it preserves the outline of a first-century house, which he calls "very possibly" the headquarters of the Jesus movement.
