## Summary

This short video is a narrated virtual tour of the Herodian Temple in Jerusalem, the building as it would have looked in Jesus's time. Tabor frames the tour around the places where the Gospels and Acts put their events: the entrances used by pilgrims, the outer court where commerce went on, the Court of Women with its offering boxes, and the barrier that restricted non-Jews. He presents the model as close to how the building "must have looked," pointing to surviving Herodian stonework as a comparison and to the reconstruction as the work of Jeremy Parks, with input from Leen Ritmeyer.

Most of the tour is Tabor describing what the animation shows. He links specific features to New Testament episodes: the money changers and animal sellers at the temple action, Jesus teaching in the temple courts over the final week, the widow's two coins, the Gate Beautiful in Acts, and Paul's arrest after he is accused of bringing a Gentile past the barrier. He also reads the entrances and inscriptions to support the picture of a building with strict access rules. The talk's arguments are mainly topographical and descriptive; Tabor does not develop a broader theological claim.

The video ends with a drone overview of the complex and a credit to Ritmeyer, whom Tabor calls "the expert" after decades of excavation and study, and to Parks for the animation. Tabor then points viewers to Ritmeyer's book and Parks's channel.

## Key Teachings

### The Huldah Gates as tunnel entrances
Tabor says the double and triple gates on the south side are called the Huldah gates. Some, he notes, argue they are named for a prophetess named Huldah in the Hebrew Bible. He proposes instead that "hula" also means a mouse or a burrowing animal, and that the gates make more sense as tunnels: "they're the tunnel Gates basically the hula Gates." He says this is where the crowds would come up from the south into Jerusalem.

### Layout, purity areas, and the barrier warning
He points to steps and pools he believes were used for ritual purity, and to the Herodian architecture, which he compares to structures still standing at the Tomb of the Patriarchs in Hebron. Moving inward, he describes the outer court as open to all, with a low barrier beyond which, "at each gate there's a very Stern warning sign no Gentiles allowed upon penalty of death." He describes the sequence: women and children and Israelites in the inner court, the Court of the Women, the Court of Israelite men, and finally the priests and sacrifices. He mentions a Greek inscription from one of these warning stones, which he says was found under Ottoman rule and is now in Turkey, and says Israel has tried to get it back.

### The Court of Women and the widow's coins
In the Court of Women, Tabor describes the tiled floor, the four lamp pillars, and the thirteen offering boxes, which he says are "according to our records historically." He ties this to the Gospel scene of the widow dropping in two copper coins. He reports that Jesus, observing the offering, tells his disciples the woman gave more than anyone, and calls this "one of his most famous teachings," which he links to Jesus's sense of the kingdom of God and to the blessing of the poor and meek. He treats the setting as the place where Jesus taught and was questioned during the last week.

### Acts scenes: the Gate Beautiful and Paul's arrest
Tabor connects the temple gates to Acts. He suggests a gate on the temple's east side may be the "gate beautiful" where the disciples went to pray, preach and teach after Jesus's death. He also says that Paul, arrested after being accused of bringing a Gentile past the barrier, was taken up the steps into the Fortress Antonia. He says this is how Paul "causes a riot in the temple," and why he later flees toward Caesarea and ends up in Rome, according to Acts.

### The temple as the setting for Jesus's last week
Tabor ties the physical space to the Gospel account of the temple action. He says that on the day Jesus "took over the temple" and closed it for a day, the money changers, commerce and animals were in the outer court. He describes the space where animals were examined and sold, and says that the same courts were where Jesus taught and preached "over the next week." He describes crowds gathering in these areas as the setting for the teaching and questioning.
