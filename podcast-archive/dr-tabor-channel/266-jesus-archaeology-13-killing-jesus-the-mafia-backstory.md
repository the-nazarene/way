## Summary

Tabor asks why the accounts of Jesus's arrest and trial in Matthew, Mark, Luke and John seem confused: who arrested Jesus, who questioned him, and who handed him to Pontius Pilate. He proposes sorting this out by comparing the texts with each other, with Josephus, and with first-century Jerusalem archaeology. He argues that Mark, the earliest gospel, names no high priest and describes a night session that was preliminary and illegal, followed by a formal morning consultation of the council (the Sanhedrin) that handed Jesus to Pilate. John, in his account, adds that Jesus was first taken to Annas, the former high priest and father-in-law of Caiaphas. Tabor presents Annas and his sons as a family that held the high priesthood and controlled the temple for much of the first century, with Caiaphas as a figurehead. He calls this the "Priestly Mafia."

Tabor's material evidence includes the Jewish Quarter excavations, the Mount Zion priestly mansion that his team has been excavating since 2006, the 1990 discovery of a Caiaphas family ossuary, and the excavated gate and pavement of Herod's palace, which he associates with Pilate's judgment seat. He is careful to say he is not claiming the Mount Zion house was the house of Caiaphas. He notes that three sites are proposed for it. The ossuary inscription "Joseph son of Caiaphas" is, in his reading, a match for Josephus's account of the high priest who presided in Jesus's time.

The talk ends as a preview. Tabor says the next episode will cover crucifixion, the burial, the empty tomb and the sightings of Jesus, and the final episode will explain his method.

## Key Teachings

### A night hearing versus a formal morning session
Tabor reads Mark as describing two stages. After the arrest, which he places after midnight, Jesus is brought before the chief priests, elders and scribes. Tabor calls this a preliminary hearing "or a setup," not a legal procedure. Only at morning does "the whole Council" (which he identifies as the Sanhedrin) hold a consultation, bind Jesus, and deliver him to Pilate. Tabor's point is that the Jewish authorities wanted a death sentence but lacked the right to carry one out, which is why they needed Pilate. He describes the night session as illegal and marked by abuse, including spitting, blows and mockery of Jesus as a prophet.

### John's Annas and the Priestly family
Tabor says John alone names Annas as the first stop: "they led him to Annis for he was the father-in-law of Caiaphas who was high priest that year." He suggests John may rely on a source "more involved apparently" than Mark's. Tabor says that in Luke "there's more material that does seem to shed light on this scenario," but he does not go into it in this talk. He uses John, Mark and Josephus together to reconstruct the sequence: Gethsemane, then Annas's house, then Caiaphas, then the council in the morning, then Pilate.

### The "Priestly Mafia" under Annas
Tabor gives a succession list from the Annas family: Annas (6 to 15 CE), his son Eleazar (16), Joseph Caiaphas (18 to 36), and then Jonathan (36), Theophilus (37), and Maias, with a later Annas son who "murders James the brother of Jesus" in 62. He says Caiaphas had a long rule and presided over Jesus's death sentence, but that Annas "is really in the background really running things," so that Caiaphas was "basically a figurehead." Tabor presents the family as corrupt and self-interested in its management of the temple. He links their hostility to Jesus with their earlier rejection of John the Baptist, citing the exchange in which Jesus asks about John's authority.

### The Jerusalem Jewish Quarter and the Priestly Mansion
Tabor explains that Jordanian control of the Old City (1948 to 1967) led to the destruction of much of the Jewish Quarter, including old synagogues. After Israel retook the city in 1967, excavators uncovered first-century levels beneath the rebuilt quarter. Tabor says the Annas house is located there, with steps, a bathtub, a mikvah, storage areas and a mosaic floor, all damaged by the Romans in 70 CE. He describes a reconstructed "Judgment Hall" and courtyard in the Annas house, where Peter's denial is placed. He calls the house "a kangaroo court" setting.

### The Mount Zion priestly mansion (his team's dig)
Tabor's team began excavating a first-century priestly house on Mount Zion in 2006 and paused for COVID in 2020. He lists finds: a mikvah, a bath "with the ceiling intact," a kitchen with three bread ovens, and a room he believes is the only first-century one found with its ceiling intact. He describes it as "a Priestly Mansion" based on its luxury and features, and stresses: "I'm not claiming it's the house of Caiaphas there are three candidates for the house of Caiaphas." The other candidates he names are the Roman Catholic St Peter in Gallicantu site and an Armenian site.

### The Caiaphas ossuary
Tabor describes a family tomb found in November 1990 by a bulldozer during construction in the Hinnom Valley, which he says appears to be the tomb of Caiaphas's family. It holds an ossuary with the inscription "Joseph son of Caiaphas," now in the Israel Museum. He says the New Testament only uses "Caiaphas," while Josephus gives the full name. He argues that the ossuary connects the gospel figure with Josephus's record of the high priest who ruled from 18 to 36 CE.

### Pilate's judgment seat and Herod's palace gate
Tabor says Pilate, as governor, came to Jerusalem from Caesarea during Passover to keep a military presence. He identifies a gate and steps into Herod's palace on the south side, with first-century bedrock steps, and a paved area he links to the "gatha" (the pavement) in John. He describes the crowds outside not entering the Judgment area so they could eat the Passover, and Pilate going in and out "three different times" before sending Jesus to Herod Antipas, then back. Tabor says Jesus was scourged before the final verdict. He points to his blog article "Ecce Homo Revisited" for more on this.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That one event with the Jewish priests and then Pilate is the whole story.** Tabor says the accounts differ and that there were several trials and stages: a night hearing before Annas and Caiaphas, a morning council, and then Pilate, with Herod Antipas in between.
- **That Caiaphas alone ran the high priesthood.** Tabor argues Annas and his sons controlled the office for much of the first century, and that Caiaphas was basically a figurehead.
- **That the Last Supper and traditional sites are as old as the events.** Tabor says the Last Supper room is a Crusader structure above the first-century space, and that tourist sites such as Gethsemane are not necessarily the original locations.
- **That the Jewish Quarter is only what tourists see at street level.** Tabor says the first-century city lies beneath the modern streets, and that it is now accessible underground.
