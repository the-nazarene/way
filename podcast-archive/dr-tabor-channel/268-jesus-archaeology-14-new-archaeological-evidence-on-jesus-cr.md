## Summary

Tabor, presenting alone in the 14th episode of his "Jesus Archaeology" series, the last of the "Last Days of Jesus" episodes, asks where and how Jesus was crucified and buried, and what the material and textual evidence can contribute. He argues that the crucifixion took place on the Mount of Olives, east of the Old City, rather than at the traditional Golgotha site of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre or at Gordon's Calvary. He also argues that Jesus was given an emergency burial in a nearby new tomb because Passover was approaching, and that the body was later moved to a permanent place, which he links to the Talpiot tombs. His evidence includes a first-century heel-nail crucifixion find, a Roman-era hand bone with a crucifixion nail, Josephus's report of mass crucifixions, the Gospel of John's account of the burial, a shroud-wrapped burial from a Hinnom valley tomb, the Talpiot ossuary inscriptions, a statistical argument about names, a soil test on the "James ossuary," and a Jonah image in a neighboring tomb.

Tabor presents the Talpiot material as a plausible but unproven link. He says the James ossuary's soil profile matched the Talpiot tomb, and he calls the Jonah image "the earliest image of Jonah ever found." He acknowledges that the James ossuary has been disputed and that the Talpiot tomb needs further study, including DNA work. He also says Paul's resurrection language describes a transformed spiritual body, and that the gospel appearance stories are later and developed after 70 CE.

The talk ends with Tabor pointing viewers to his blog (james.com) for the full case on Golgotha and Talpiot, and previewing episode 15, which will cover method in judging texts and archaeology. He recommends a book he co-wrote with Simcha Jacobovici and a review of James Strange's book on archaeology and the Gospels.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Crucifixion method: nails in the heel and forearms, not the feet or wrists
Tabor describes the 1968 tomb north of the Old City, containing the remains of a crucified man named Yohanan (written "Yokan" in the captions). A crucifixion nail was found through his heel bone. He explains that a nail through the foot's front would cause massive bleeding and rapid death, which would defeat the purpose of prolonged crucifixion, so the heel placement "makes more sense." He also says that he had been taught in graduate school that the arm nails went through the wrists, and that this is not correct. He presents a hand bone from Antigonus Mattathias, the last Hasmonean king, executed by Mark Antony, with a nail still in it, and suggests the nails were tacked into the back of the hand while the arms were tied with ropes to carry the weight.

### 2. Golgotha: Mount of Olives over the traditional sites
Tabor surveys three proposals. The majority view, supported by Roman Catholic, Greek Orthodox, Coptic, and Armenian traditions, places the crucifixion at the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, which he says was built in the early fourth century under Constantine and Helena. He argues that the site's ancient tomb and rock were a later identification. Gordon's Calvary, near the Damascus Gate, he rejects because the tomb there is dated to the Iron Age, which conflicts with the Gospel idea of a new, unfinished tomb. He favors the Mount of Olives. His reasons include Josephus's report that the Romans crucified outside the city's eastern gate, and that Josephus mentions 500 crucifixions a day during the Roman revolt. He argues that the Mount of Olives would have been visible across the city, and that the Kidron road follows an ancient bedrock road said to date to the time of King David (2 Samuel 15, where David walks barefoot up the road). He also suggests that the Hebrew word "skull" might refer to the summit's shape, to a skull-shaped place of execution, or to a skull associated with Goliath. He says that Helena's search, about 300 years after the event, may have garbled earlier stories, possibly linked to a tomb of John Hyrcanus that Hadrian had torn down.

### 3. The burial: an emergency tomb, not Joseph of Arimathea's
Tabor argues that the Gospel of John (19:41-42) is more reliable on geography and setting than Mark, even though John is more theological and later. John says the crucifixion site had a garden with a new tomb "in which no one had ever been laid," and does not name Joseph of Arimathea. Tabor says Matthew (27) names Joseph and cites Isaiah 53 to fulfill prophecy. He says John's choice of tomb was driven by the Passover and Sabbath timing, which created an "emergency burial" and a "burial of convenience." He proposes that after the Sabbath, the body was retrieved and permanently buried. He reads John 20:1-10, where Mary Magdalene finds the tomb empty and asks "Where have they taken him," as evidence that the empty tomb was explained by a reburial and not by a resurrection.

### 4. Burial practice: the Hinnom valley shroud and the Talpiot tombs
Tabor describes a first-century tomb in the Hinnom valley, south of Jerusalem, where a man with leprosy was buried in a shroud. Fragments of the shroud survived because the tomb was sealed. He says the shroud has been carbon-dated and is "the only burial shroud ever found in Jerusalem." He then turns to the Talpiot tombs, which he says contain ten ossuaries, six with inscriptions. Among the names he lists are Yeshua bar Yosef (Jesus son of Joseph), Maria, Mara, Mariamene, Matthew, and Yehuda bar Yeshua (Judah son of Jesus). He notes that the decorative style of the Mariamene and Maria ossuaries is similar, and suggests a family relationship. He argues that the "Yose" inscription matches the nickname of Jesus's brother in Mark. He acknowledges that some inscriptions are contested and that the tomb's contents need further study, including DNA work and excavation to bedrock.

### 5. The name statistics and the James ossuary
Tabor offers a rough estimate. Among 50,000 people, about 1,000 might be named Yeshua, about 150 might have a father named Joseph, and about 50 might also have a mother named Mary. Adding a brother named James reduces the number to "less than one." He calls this "a very oversimplified view" and says the argument is meant as a visual aid. He discusses the James ossuary inscription ("Yakov bar Yosef achui Yeshua," James son of Joseph brother of Jesus), which came onto the antiquities market around the time of the Talpiot discovery. He acknowledges that the phrase "brother of Jesus" has been disputed, with some arguing it was added to give the ossuary value. He reports a recent soil test, carried out by the Israel Antiquities Authority with blind control samples from other Jerusalem tombs, that he says matched the soil in the Talpiot tomb. He presents this as evidence that the James ossuary came from the Talpiot area.

### 6. The Jonah image and resurrection as transformation
Tabor describes a second tomb on the same estate, less than 200 feet away, which was sealed in 1981 and which he and a team examined with a robotic camera through drilled holes in a basement corridor. One ossuary shows a figure coming out of a fish, labeled "Yona" (Jonah), and a four-line Greek inscription with prayers to "God" and "raise up." He calls the Jonah image "the earliest image of Jonah ever found," says that Jewish art has no images of Jonah, and suggests that resurrection belief may explain the image. He argues that the early Christian view of resurrection was not the "resuscitation of a corpse." He draws on Paul's account in 1 Corinthians 15, the list of appearances to Peter, the Twelve, 500 people, James, and "last of all to me," and on 2 Corinthians 5, to argue that Paul described a transformed spiritual body. He says the Gospel appearance stories (Matthew, Luke, John) are later and developed after 70 CE.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Nails through the feet or wrists.** Tabor says the traditional image of nails through the front of the feet is incorrect, and that the wrists were not the nail site. He says the nails went through the heel and the hands, with the arms tied.
- **Golgatha as the Church of the Holy Sepulchre.** He says the majority view and the most visited site are not necessarily correct, and that the traditional tomb and rock were identified centuries later.
- **Joseph of Arimathea's tomb.** He says John does not name Joseph, and that Matthew did so for theological reasons. He says the tomb was a burial of convenience.
- **Resurrection as a resuscitated corpse.** He says early believers did not think of resurrection that way, and that the Gospels' defensive stress on a real body is later.
- **Scholars often think Gordon's Calvary or the Garden Tomb is the site.** He says this is not the case, because the tomb is dated to the Iron Age.
- **Wreath and gabled facade on Talpiot tombs as Jesus-specific symbols.** He says these were common Roman-period motifs for the house of death.
