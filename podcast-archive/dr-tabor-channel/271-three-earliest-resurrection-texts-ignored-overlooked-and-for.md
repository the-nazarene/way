## Summary

Tabor asks why three early texts about Easter morning, two from the canonical gospels (Mark 16:1-8 and John 20:1-10) and one from the Gospel of Peter (fragments discovered in Egypt in 1886), are so often ignored, and what picture of the resurrection they support. He opens by saying these texts differ from "the common view of both Conservative Christian Believers as well as apologists," which he argues "doesn't really stand up to historical investigation." He reads Mark's ending as originally stopping at verse 8, with a tomb already open, the body gone, and a young man (not an angel) telling the women that Jesus has been raised and will meet the disciples in Galilee. He treats the three later endings of Mark as forgeries and says "the weight of Scholarship today does not accept those bogus endings."

Tabor argues that John 20:1-10 preserves an earlier, self-contained tradition in which Mary Magdalene finds the stone moved and reports that "they have taken the Lord out of the Tomb." He treats the Gospel of Peter as independent of the canonical gospels, a view he says he is "persuaded of," and reads its account as close to Mark's ending, with later details (such as a week's interval and the disciples going fishing) that he takes as evidence of a non-appearance tradition. He then proposes a reconstruction: Joseph of Arimathea took Jesus' body down before Passover began, placed it temporarily in a nearby tomb, and later reburied it on his own property. In his account, the disciples only began to believe in the resurrection when they had visions of a glorified Christ back in Galilee, a pattern he connects to 1 Corinthians 15 and Acts 7. The talk ends with his statement that the resurrection as a "resuscitated corpse" is "not the view of the earliest texts."

## Key Teachings

### Mark 16:1-8 as the original ending
Tabor argues that Mark originally ended at verse 8 and that the three appended endings are spurious. He reads the women's visit at dawn, the stone already rolled back, and the young man in a white robe who says the crucified Jesus "has risen" and "is not here." He stresses that the text has no angel, no dazzling appearance, and no sightings. He summarizes: "no sightings there no appearances they simply find a tomb that's already been open the body is gone." The earlier prediction that Jesus would meet the disciples in Galilee is what the story points toward, and the women "fled" in fear.

### John 20:1-10 as an earlier embedded tradition
He treats the first ten verses of John 20 as a self-contained, earlier story that the later Johannine material continues beyond. In this version Mary Magdalene alone finds the stone removed and reports that "they" have taken the body. Peter and "the other disciple" run to the tomb; Tabor identifies the second disciple as James, though he says he has covered this in other programs and writings. He suggests James, as a priestly figure, does not enter the tomb during Passover, while Peter enters and sees the linen cloths and a separately rolled head covering. Tabor reads the phrase about believing as meaning that the second disciple believed the body had been moved, not that he believed in resurrection, "for as yet they did not know the scripture that he must rise from the dead." The disciples then "went back to their homes," which he takes to mean Galilee.

### The Gospel of Peter as an independent witness
Tabor says the Gospel of Peter, whose fragments were found in Egypt in 1886 "buried with a monk," is disputed among scholars as to whether it depends on the canonical gospels. He is "persuaded" that it is independent "because it's too different." He reads its tomb scene as resembling Mark's ending, with the young man saying "he's risen and gone away" and the women fleeing. He then notes later features: the disciples' eight-day weeping, Peter and Andrew going fishing, and an appearance tied to Jerusalem. He contrasts these with the Matthean story of an earthquake, an angel rolling back the stone, and guards who "pass out," and argues that "all of this embellishment" is legendary and "doesn't hold up for historical critical studies."

### Joseph of Arimathea and an emergency burial
Using Mark 15:42-46 as his main text, Tabor describes Joseph as a respected member of the Sanhedrin, "looking for the Kingdom," who asked Pilate for the body, bought a linen shroud, and laid Jesus in a rock-hewn tomb. He points out that Mark does not say the tomb was Joseph's own, and he reads John 19 (the garden tomb near the crucifixion site) as explaining a temporary burial "because of the Jewish Day of preparation." He argues that the tomb was close at hand because of the Passover and Sabbath, and that a family tomb would normally be on family property, which Joseph would not have had at Golgotha. He proposes that the body was moved on Saturday evening to a permanent burial, most likely on Joseph's land, and that the women arriving Sunday found it gone.

### Galilean visions and the Pauline evidence
Tabor's reconstruction moves faith in the resurrection to Galilee: "faith in Resurrection only began back in the Galilee when people began to have visions." He cites 1 Corinthians 15 as "earlier than any of our gospel accounts" and lists the witnesses (Peter, the Twelve, groups, James, and Paul). He reads Paul's visions as of "a glorified Heavenly Christ" and quotes Paul's claim that the resurrection body is "not flesh and blood." He cites Acts 7, the vision of Jesus at God's right hand given to Stephen before his stoning, as an example of the kind of experience the disciples had. He notes Paul "equates his visions of Christ to that of the Apostles."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The resurrection was a resuscitated corpse.** Tabor says this is a later apologetic development and "not the view of the earliest texts," which he says describe a glorified, exalted Christ seen in visions.
- **The Easter story is a single, consistent account across all four gospels.** He argues the accounts were expanded over decades: Mark ends at 16:8, John 20:1-10 is an earlier unit, and Matthew, Luke, and the later Johannine material add an earthquake, angels, guards, and appearances.
- **Mark's gospel originally included appearances of Jesus.** He says the three additional endings of Mark are spurious and that the original ends with no sightings.
- **The empty tomb proves a miraculous resurrection.** Tabor reads the empty tomb as the result of a burial party moving the body, and he treats the disciples' belief as beginning only with Galilean visions.
- **Joseph of Arimathea's tomb was a family tomb at the crucifixion site.** He says the tomb was an emergency burial near Golgotha and that a permanent burial was likely on Joseph's own property.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor treats Paul's visions of a glorified Christ in 1 Corinthians 15 as the earliest literary evidence and says Paul equates his visions with those of the apostles. He does not discuss Jesus' teachings in this talk.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** He identifies the "other disciple" in John 20 as James, "the just," a priestly figure who would not enter a tomb during Passover. He presents this as his own reading, one he says he has argued elsewhere.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** The talk centers on the nature of the resurrection. Tabor argues the earliest accounts describe an exaltation to heaven rather than a bodily resuscitation.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** He presents Mark's short ending as original, the appended endings as forgeries, John 20:1-10 as an earlier unit, and the Gospel of Peter as an independent witness, with later expansion in Matthew, Luke and parts of John.
