## Summary

Tabor presents a side-by-side, "historical critical" reading of Mark and Matthew, with Luke used as a comparison point, to ask whether Matthew edited Mark and whether harmonizing the Gospels into one story damages them. He frames the talk as a 30-minute excerpt from a longer roundtable with host Derek Lambert and guest Kipp Davis, and he is the main speaker for most of the segment. His working hypothesis is that Mark was written first and that Matthew rewrote it, sometimes by adding material and sometimes by smoothing away difficulties. He presents six examples, five of them drawn from comparing Matthew, Mark and Luke: the death of John the Baptist, the baptism of Jesus, the Sabbath grain episode, the parables and the "secret," Peter's confession, and "Why do you call me good?" He says Matthew tends to abbreviate Mark unless he has a problem with it, in which case he "mitigates" it, while Luke usually removes passages that are troublesome.

Tabor's central claim is that harmonizing these texts (merging them into one composite narrative) "destroys" them, because each evangelist has a distinct voice and purpose. He argues that apologists read the Gospels to make them say what the reader already believes. He says this is often done by sincere believers, including himself earlier in life, and he presents his method as a more credible alternative. The talk ends with his summary of the pattern and a remark that he is "sticking to" his position. The discussion that follows, which is not in this transcript, is described as Part 2.

## Key Teachings

### Matthew compresses Mark's account of John's death
Tabor reads the passage in Mark 6:17-29 (the death of John the Baptist) against Matthew's version. He says Mark is not shorter than people assume and is dense with detail, including the banquet, the birthday party and Herodias's daughter. Matthew, in his view, keeps the essentials but reduces the story to "here's the story, John got his head cut off," and then moves on. He argues that this is a "cliffnotes" pattern consistent with Matthew working from Mark. He says a Matthew-first theory is possible in theory but "doesn't hold up when you look at the content."

### The baptism: Matthew adds the objection and the voice
In Mark 1:9-11, Tabor says, Mark has one line about the baptism and does not address whether Jesus, in a baptism "for remission of sins," needed forgiveness. Matthew, he argues, sees the problem and adds an exchange in which John objects to baptizing Jesus. Tabor also contrasts Mark's voice from heaven ("you are my beloved Son with you I am well pleased"), which he calls personal and which he says fits Mark's "secret" about Jesus's identity. In Matthew the same declaration becomes, in his phrase, a "loudspeaker announcement" that tells the world, which Tabor thinks Matthew can do because he has already established Jesus as Messiah through the genealogy. Luke, he notes, keeps the words of Mark's voice.

### The Sabbath grain: Mark's reasoning versus Matthew's
In Mark 2, the disciples pick grain on the Sabbath and are challenged by Pharisees. Mark answers with the David episode (which Tabor says Matthew reduces by omitting the high priest Abiathar, whom he says was not the high priest), and then with the saying that "the Sabbath was made for man" and that "the son of man is Lord even of the Sabbath." Tabor reads this as Mark's appeal to the original purpose of the Sabbath and to human need. Matthew, by contrast, removes the saying and gives a different argument: priests work in the Temple on the Sabbath, and "someone greater than the temple is here," so the disciples are justified by Jesus's authority. Luke, he says, drops the whole debate because his audience was not interested in halakhic argument. Tabor adds that Hillel and Shammai, and rabbinic sources generally, would be relevant to how a Jewish reader would have argued the question.

### Parables, "the secret," and the hardened hearers
In Mark 4:10-12, the disciples ask why Jesus speaks in parables, and Jesus answers that to outsiders everything comes in riddles "so that seeing they may not see." Tabor says the usual Sunday-school explanation (that Jesus meant they were hardened, fulfilling Isaiah) is not what Mark says; in his reading, Mark presents the parables as a test that outsiders fail. Matthew, he argues, adds an explanation that makes the riddle easier, and Luke removes the problematic phrasing. He also notes that in Matthew's Q-source material, which Tabor says Matthew uses "all the time," sayings such as "don't think I came to bring peace but a sword" are softened into a call to love God more than family, when Tabor thinks the original says "you gotta hate them."

### Peter's confession and "Why do you call me good?"
Tabor argues that Peter's confession in Mark 8:27-33 is followed by Jesus's rebuke of Peter as "Satan," which Matthew keeps but surrounds with a blessing for Peter (Matthew 16:17) that Tabor describes as making Peter the foundation of the church. Luke omits the confession's harder ending. In Mark 10:17-18, a man addresses Jesus as "good teacher," and Jesus replies, "Why do you call me good? No one is good but God alone." Tabor argues that Matthew cannot leave this unqualified and removes or reshapes it, while his own ending (Matthew 28) presents Jesus with all authority in heaven and earth. Luke keeps the same wording as Mark. Tabor describes this as Matthew's theology pressing against Mark's more "raw" presentation.

### The method: Luke as control and the cost of harmonizing
Tabor uses Luke as a "control," comparing it to a control in an experiment, to test whether Matthew or Mark is more likely to be the earlier text. His general rule, as stated in the talk, is that when two accounts differ, the one that adds an explanation or a mitigating detail is usually the later one, and that "if something's missing" the more likely pattern is that a later author has solved a problem. He says a harmonized text is a new document ("The Gospel According to Joe Blow") that matches none of the four evangelists, and that "respect the texts" means letting each Gospel keep its own voice.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Harmonizing the Gospels is a faithful way to read them.** Tabor argues that harmonizing destroys each Gospel's distinct voice and produces "The Gospel According to Joe Blow," not any evangelist's account.
- **Mark is shorter than the other Gospels.** Tabor says Mark's account of John's death is dense with detail, and that people who think otherwise should count the words.
- **Sunday-school readings explain the parables as hardening.** Tabor says Mark 4 presents the parables as a test that outsiders fail, not as a fulfillment of Isaiah about people who cannot see.
- **Apologists are insincere.** Tabor says he is not sure all apologists are insincere, and that many believers are sincere; his objection is to the method, not the motive.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Gospel formation and canon:** The talk's central question is literary dependence: Tabor argues Mark was written first, Matthew edited it, and Luke edits both. He presents this as a reason not to read the four Gospels as one harmonized text.
- **Torah and Sabbath:** The Sabbath grain episode is the clearest Torah-related example. Tabor contrasts Mark's appeal to the Sabbath's purpose and human need with Matthew's appeal to Jesus's authority over the Temple.
- **Who Jesus thought he was (christology):** The baptism voice, Peter's confession and "Why do you call me good?" are discussed as places where Matthew moves Jesus toward a more exalted identity than Mark presents, which Tabor links to Matthew's rising theology.
- **Kingdom and secrecy:** The parable and "secret of the kingdom" passage (Mark 4:10-12) is Tabor's example of Mark presenting the teaching as a test rather than an open message.
