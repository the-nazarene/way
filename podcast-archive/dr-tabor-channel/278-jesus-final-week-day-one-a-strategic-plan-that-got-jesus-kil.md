## Summary

Tabor opens a short series on Jesus' final week, asking why Jesus was crucified and what led up to his death. He says his method is to start with Mark as the control text, comparing Matthew and Luke against it, and to draw on sources beyond the Gospels where he can. This first installment covers "Day One," traditionally Palm Sunday. He uses Burton Throckmorton's *Gospel Parallels* laid out in synoptic columns, with Mark in the center, to show where Matthew and Luke add or change material.

Tabor argues that the entry into Jerusalem happened on a Sunday after a Sabbath spent at Jericho, that the route over the Mount of Olives fits the geography, and that the Palm Sunday tradition is "probably valid." On Mark's account, Jesus sends two disciples for a colt, enters Jerusalem to a crowd crying "Hosanna," goes into the temple, looks around "as it was already late," and returns to Bethany. Tabor treats Matthew's two animals as an expansion made to fit Zechariah 9:9, possibly through a misreading of Hebrew parallelism, and treats Luke's additions (the disciples as the crowd, the Pharisees' rebuke) as Luke's own adjustments. His most original proposal is that Mark's scene may echo the coronation of Solomon in 1 Kings 1 (a mule, the Gihon spring, anointing, and shouting), with Mark's Jesus reenacting a Davidic succession. Tabor presents this as a new idea that he finds possible and "maybe even likely," not as established.

The talk ends before the temple action or the crucifixion plot. Tabor says Mark's Jesus is "checking it out" and "planning something," that the plan is what many people miss, and that the anointing by a woman in Mark will come later. He closes by pointing viewers to his blog post on the last days of Jesus, the Throckmorton PDF, and his courses.

## Key Teachings

### Mark as the control narrative
Tabor says the synoptic parallel layout shows that "Mark is kind of like our control." He treats Matthew as one that "cuts to the chase" and often drops Mark's detail, and Luke as one that "tends to follow Mark more closely unless he sees something that makes a big difference." He says he will "let each account stand alone" rather than merge the three into one composite. He also says he thinks Mark wrote first, though he does not argue that point in this talk.

### Geography, timing and the approach to Jerusalem
Tabor reasons from the route. Jesus comes up from Jericho, about 1,200 feet below sea level, to Jerusalem at about 2,400 feet above sea level, so Tabor concludes it is "probably afternoon" when the colt is sought. He says the Sabbath observance makes a Sunday entry likely, and that the Galilean crowds travelling the Jordan Valley to Jerusalem before Passover would explain the size of the gathering. He places Bethany on the eastern slope of the Mount of Olives, not the Jerusalem-facing side, and says he has walked the route many times.

### Matthew's two animals and Zechariah 9:9
Tabor notes that Mark mentions one colt, while Matthew has both an ass and a colt. He ties this to Matthew's quotation of Zechariah 9:9 and offers two explanations: that Matthew does not recognize Hebrew parallelism (where "an ass and on a colt" may describe one animal in two phrases), or that Matthew knows this and reads the prophecy literally. He points to other places where Matthew doubles figures found in Mark, such as the two demon-possessed men and the two blind men, as evidence that Matthew "likes to emphasize things." He rejects the harmonizing reading that makes Jesus ride two animals.

### Luke's additions
Tabor says Luke follows Mark closely but changes the crowd into "the whole multitude of the disciples," and adds the Pharisees' request that Jesus rebuke his disciples and Jesus' reply that "the very Stones would cry out." Tabor links this to Luke's later, longer passage on the destruction of Jerusalem in Luke 21, which he says picks up a theme first raised in the entry scene.

### The Solomon coronation hypothesis
This is the talk's main original idea. Tabor reads 1 Kings 1, where Solomon is set on a mule, taken to the Gihon spring, anointed, and proclaimed, with the crowd's noise so loud "that the Earth was split." He notes that Jesus' route down the Mount of Olives to the temple passes the Kidron Valley near the Gihon spring. He says the crowd's cry about "the kingdom of Our Father David" would fit a succession scene, since the Davidic line had not ruled Jerusalem since the Babylonian captivity. Tabor says Mark does not mention Solomon or Zechariah, so the parallel is his suggestion, not something Mark states.

### Mark's reconnaissance and the planned action
Tabor argues that Mark's detail "as it was already late" shows Jesus only looking around the temple on Day One, with the major events falling on the next day. He says Mark presents a pre-planned event that Matthew and Luke compress or omit. He says this is "the thing that many many people miss," and that Mark's "plan" is the key to why Jesus was killed. The talk ends before he sets out what the plan was.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That Jesus rode two animals.** Some Bible literalists insist he rode both an ass and a colt. Tabor says this comes from conflating the Gospels. He says each account should stand alone, since Mark has one colt and Luke has one, and Matthew is retelling Mark to fit the Zechariah prophecy.
- **That Palm Sunday involved palm branches in the synoptic accounts.** Tabor notes that "even though we call this Palm Sunday there no Palms mentioned" in Mark. He says palms appear only in John, which he will discuss later.
- **That the entry scene was a single, simple event with the temple cleansing right after.** Tabor argues that Mark's "as it was already late" means the temple visit was only a look-around, with the main events on the following day. He says Matthew and Luke compress the timeline, and that they are not interested in day-by-day chronology.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Jesus' self-understanding and messianic claims:** Tabor reads the crowd's cries ("blessed is the kingdom of Our Father David," "son of David") and the possible Solomon echo as the public moment when Jesus' messianic status becomes publicly claimed, which he says Mark had previously forbidden.
- **How the Gospels and canon were formed:** The talk is a concrete example of Mark as the earlier narrative, with Matthew and Luke adding prophetic fulfillment language, extra characters, and expanded details.
- **The Gospel evidence versus later theology:** Tabor's stated goal is to separate what the sources say from what theology has layered on, using the animals and the Zechariah citation as his first case.
