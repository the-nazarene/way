## Summary

This is the second part of a live stream hosted by Derek Lambert (MythVision Podcast) with Kipp Davis as co-host. James Tabor, the featured speaker, is the one who carries the substance. The question is whether apologists who merge the four gospels, especially Matthew and Mark, damage the distinct meaning of each text. Tabor argues that they do. Each evangelist had a purpose, and reading the gospels "carefully," one at a time, reveals it. He frames this as scholarship, not a demolition project, and says the aim is "what is the author of the text intending you to understand," not "did it happen."

## Key Teachings

### Each gospel has its own purpose, and merging them destroys it
Tabor's central claim is that Matthew, Mark, and Luke each had a distinct purpose, and the way to see it is to read each text whole. He says Matthew's purpose is stated in its last verse: "all authority and power belongs to Jesus Christ in heaven and on earth," and the church's mission is to "go and teach and baptize and make disciples Until the End of the Age." Mark, by contrast, presents Jesus's teaching as mysterious, with the disciples never understanding. Luke's purpose is to move the mission from Jerusalem outward (Samaria, Judea, Antioch, Greece, Rome). Tabor compares merging them to merging three biographies of Lincoln, one of which admires him and another which does not, and then publishing the result as your own work.

### Matthew adds, explains, and multiplies; Mark withholds
Tabor's recurring examples: Matthew has "two donkeys" where Mark has one; Matthew names the Transfiguration disciples' question about Elijah as John the Baptist, where Mark leaves it for the reader; Matthew cites Daniel by name for the "desolating sacrilege," where Mark only alludes to it ("as it is written... let the reader understand"). Tabor says Matthew "helps out the reader," which is fine, but Mark wants the reader to work it out. He presents Matthew's changes as deliberate. He also notes that Matthew drops Mark's praise of Herod's respect for John, and that Matthew's paralytic story strips out Mark's detail. In Mark, he says, the healing is praised for the four men's faith, while Matthew emphasizes Jesus's authority. He also says Matthew adds the name "Jesus Barabbas" in the trial scene. He calls this "a punt on the word," with no early sources supporting the name, and says Matthew sets two "Jesuses" side by side deliberately, along with the line "those who live by the sword will die by the sword," which he says appears only in Matthew.

### Mark's ending and the resurrection appearances
Tabor argues that Mark does not lack resurrection appearances. He says Mark "clearly believes Jesus was raised" and "believes their appearances," since the young man at the tomb says "you will see him in the Galilee." Mark is writing for a community that found faith in Galilee. Luke, by contrast, keeps the disciples in Jerusalem until Pentecost, which Tabor says rules out a Galilee appearance. He treats the ending as a deliberate choice that adding Matthew's or Luke's appearances would undo. He presents Mark 16 as "the one that's usually talked about," and says he will not claim it as the original ending, only that Mark's purpose is what counts.

### The Zechariah 9 and 1 Kings donkey, and the Solomon motif in Mark
Tabor's view is that Mark is not reading Zechariah 9:9 at all. He says Mark's source is First Kings 1:38, where Solomon is placed on a donkey at the foot of the Mount of Olives and declared king. He reads Mark's triumphal entry as a repeat of Solomon's coronation, with a new Solomon who will build a new Temple. He says the crowd's cry "blessed is the kingdom... of our Father David" fits this reading. He warns against "harmonizers" who read Mark through Matthew's Zechariah quotation. Kip Davis, in turn, discusses how Matthew handles Zechariah and whether his Hebrew text was close to the Masoretic text. He cites Emanuel Tov on the variety of Greek and Hebrew texts in the Second Temple period. Davis says the question of Matthew's Hebrew "doesn't make a big difference" to the donkey problem, which he sees as Matthew's own literary choice.

### The "Mark's Christ" argument: the centurion, suffering, and discipleship
Tabor says Mark's true confession of Jesus comes from the centurion at the cross, "truly this is the Son of God," and that this is not sarcasm, as Bart Ehrman reads it. He calls it "the great confession" of a "god forsaken dead Messiah." He ties this to Mark's theme of suffering discipleship and to Paul: Romans 8:17 ("provided we suffer with him that we may be glorified with him") and the baptism of Romans 6. He contrasts Mark with second-century "glory Hallelujah" Christianity and cites Ignatius's letter to the Romans, and Dietrich Bonhoeffer's *The Cost of Discipleship*, as examples of the cross-centered alternative. He also cites the Isaiah 53 "suffering servant" as a collective call to a faithful remnant to bear abuse.

### The Hebrew Bible on sacrifice and the Temple
Tabor uses the Hebrew Bible to show that later texts revise earlier ones, in a way apologists often overlook. He cites Jeremiah 7 (the Temple as a "den of robbers," which he reads as condemning sacrifice), and Isaiah 56, where later editing adds "your burnt offerings will then be acceptable." He cites Psalm 51, where the penitent king says he could bring sacrifices but they would not help, followed by a verse about rebuilding Jerusalem's walls and then offering sacrifices. He takes these as evidence that editing shapes the meaning of texts, which he links to the endings of Jeremiah, Job and Ecclesiastes. He recommends the Jewish Study Bible and the New Oxford Annotated RSV for readers.

### Hebrew Matthew, the Aramaic background, and the Sabbath saying
In the Q&A, Tabor discusses the "Hebrew Matthew" text (Evan Bohan) published by George Howard, which he thinks is an independent Hebrew tradition rather than a translation of the Greek Matthew. He says the Greek Matthew is not inferior, and that Hebrew Matthew does not replace it. Davis explains the Aramaic-background argument (Maurice Casey, Bart Ehrman) for Mark 2:27-28: "the Sabbath was made for man," where "man" can be read as "human being" (*bar nasha*). He says Daniel 7:13-14 is not a Jesus-specific figure but a group ("the saints of the Most High"), and that Genesis 2:1-3 supports the reading that the Sabbath was made for humans.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The four gospels are "in lockstep" and differences reflect no different ideas.** Tabor says each evangelist had a distinct purpose, and that merging their texts (for example, adding Zechariah's second donkey to Mark) distorts Mark's meaning.
- **Matthew's Zechariah 9 quotation should be used to read Mark's donkey scene.** Tabor says Mark's source is First Kings 1:38 (Solomon's coronation), not Zechariah.
- **Mark has no resurrection appearances, so the ending is "incomplete" or lacks belief.** Tabor says Mark's ending is deliberate and that Mark believed in Galilee appearances.
- **The centurion's confession is sarcasm.** Tabor says it is the "great confession" of a suffering Messiah.
- **Daniel 7 refers to Jesus.** Tabor says the "son of man" figure is a collective (the saints of the Most High), not a Messianic individual.
- **The Bible is "Lincoln" in one voice; read all the gospels together.** Tabor says one should read each gospel for its own purpose before asking about history or harmonizing.
- **Matthew's Hebrew original is the true gospel.** Tabor says the Hebrew Matthew is a separate tradition that does not replace the Greek text.
- **The Sabbath saying concerns the Son of Man as Lord.** Davis's Aramaic reading says "son of man" means "human being," so the saying refers to humans and the Sabbath, not to Jesus's lordship specifically.
