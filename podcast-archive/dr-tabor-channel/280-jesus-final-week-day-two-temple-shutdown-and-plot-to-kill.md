## Summary

Tabor opens part two of his Holy Week miniseries by proposing that the Temple action in Mark 11 on the Monday of Jesus's final week, the "temple shutdown," is the event that led to Jesus's death. He treats Mark as the earliest and most usable narrative source, notes that Matthew and Luke rewrite Mark's chronology (placing the Temple action on the day of the donkey entry), and says John has an independent account placing a similar incident at the start of Jesus's ministry. He argues that the action was a deliberate, sustained blockade of the Temple's southern entrance and its commerce, backed by a large crowd, just days before Passover, which he thinks gave the chief priests and scribes motive to act.

His main evidence is textual. He reads Mark's quotation "my house shall be called a house of prayer for all nations" (Isaiah 56:7) alongside "a den of robbers" (Jeremiah 7), and argues that these two prophetic texts express an ideal of worship without sacrifice. He cites Jeremiah 7, Amos 5, Psalm 51, Psalm 103, Isaiah 65, and Solomon's dedicatory prayer in 1 Kings 8 as evidence that some strands of the Hebrew Bible subordinate sacrifice to justice, mercy, and intimate prayer, and that the sacrificial material in other strands looks like later Priestly additions. He also draws on a video tour of the Temple Mount, which he presents as showing the Herodian structures and the Huldah Gates, and he credits Leen Ritmeyer's archaeological work for the reconstruction.

The talk ends with Tabor's claim that the Temple action reflects the historical Jesus rather than Mark's invention, which he says he will test in the rest of the series against the Ebionite and Nazarene traditions and the teaching of James the brother of Jesus and John the Baptist. He previews day three, the next day's Temple confrontations. Throughout, Tabor frames his conclusions with "I think" and "I'm convinced," and he presents the sacrifice question as a larger topic he will develop later.

## Key Teachings

### The Temple action as the trigger for the plot
Tabor reads Mark 11:15-19 as describing three acts: driving out those who sold animals, driving out those who bought, and overturning the money changers' tables and the seats of the pigeon sellers, with a blockade of the gates so that no one could carry goods through the Temple. He argues this was a day-long takeover (from morning until evening) before Passover, with a large multitude behind Jesus. He says the chief priests and scribes, who ran the Temple and were its main beneficiaries, saw this as a threat, and that "they feared him" (Mark 11:18) reflects that. He adds that he thinks the Pharisees and other groups may have been sympathetic to the priests' position, though he gives no source for this.

### Chronology: Mark against Matthew, Luke and John
Tabor argues that Mark gives a day-by-day account (Sunday, Monday, Tuesday) and that Matthew and Luke compress the events, placing the Temple action on the same day as the donkey entry. He says Matthew and Luke are less interested in chronology and more in theology, and that Luke in particular avoids presenting Jesus as a revolutionary. He notes a textual variant in Mark 11:19 ("they" versus "he" going out of the city) and prefers "they," since Jesus travels with the Twelve. He describes John's Temple cleansing as an earlier, separate incident (John 2:13-17) and proposes that John's version and Mark's act as bookends to Jesus's public work.

### The fig tree as a prophetic sign, not a failed hunt
Tabor reads the fig tree episode (Mark 11:12-14) as deliberately odd to the disciples, and argues that ancient readers would recognize that figs do not ripen in April. He treats the curse as a sign-act rather than a reaction of hunger, and links it to the Temple action, though he states the link briefly and does not develop it in this talk.

### Jeremiah 7 and the den of robbers
Tabor says Mark's phrase "a den of robbers" comes from Jeremiah 7, where Jeremiah stands at the Temple gate and warns that the people trust in "the Temple of the Lord." He points to the Hebrew words *me'arat* (den or cave) and *paratz* (to rip apart or shred, used of a voracious animal in Isaiah), and argues that the sarcastic line "add to your burnt offerings and your sacrifices, eat the flesh" shows Jeremiah rejecting the sacrificial system. He reads verse 22-23 (in his translation) as God saying he did not command burnt offerings when he brought Israel out of Egypt, and that the true command was to obey his voice. He notes that Jeremiah 7 is set roughly 40 years before the Babylonian destruction in 586-587 BCE, and says Jesus's action is similarly about 40 years before the Roman destruction of the Second Temple.

### The Isaiah 56 "house of prayer for all nations" and a Priestly editor
Tabor reads Isaiah 56:6-7, which speaks of foreigners who join the Lord and keep the Sabbath and the covenant, and says Mark combines Isaiah 56 with Jeremiah 7. He argues that the phrase "a house of prayer for all people" in Isaiah 56:7 is an ideal of universal prayer that conflicts with the Temple's commerce and slaughter. He also argues that the sacrificial clause in Isaiah 56:7 and the sacrificial and blood additions in Zechariah 14:20-21 are Priestly insertions that "stick out like a sore thumb." He presents this as evidence for competing traditions in the Hebrew Bible: a Priestly sacrificial tradition and a prophetic tradition that rejects sacrifice as God's real desire.

### Psalm 51, Amos 5, and the prophetic ideal of justice
Tabor cites Amos 5:21-24 ("I hate, I despise your feasts... let justice roll down like waters") and Psalm 51 (David's prayer after the Bathsheba affair), and argues that the verse "you have no delight in sacrifice... a broken spirit, a broken and contrite heart" was followed by a later addition ("build the walls of Jerusalem... then you'll delight in sacrifices") that he says was added by a Priestly writer. He treats this as a recurring pattern in which sacrifice is accommodated after the fact. He also cites Psalm 103:8-14 for God's merciful character and says that in this strand the human requirement is "a poor and a contrite heart," not blood. He contrasts this with the parable of the tax collector and the Pharisee in Luke 18:9-14, where the justified man "didn't need to bring any sacrifices or offerings."

### Solomon as the new king, and the Temple's purpose
Tabor argues that Mark's donkey image is not mainly Zechariah 9:9 (which he says Matthew adds) but Solomon, who rode a donkey to the Kidron Valley to be anointed king (1 Kings 1). He says Jesus is "bringing back" Solomon's ideal, and that Solomon's prayer in 1 Kings 8 (the dedication of the Temple) contains no sacrifices, which he calls "not accidental." He emphasizes that the Ark held the tablets of the Ten Words, and that Herod's Temple had an empty Holy of Holies, so the Temple's purpose for Jesus was to be a house of prayer and a witness to the one God. He describes this as a claim about what the historical Jesus wanted, which he says he will test later.

### Who carries the anti-sacrifice tradition
Tabor says that the strongest evidence that the historical Jesus opposed sacrifice comes not from Mark but from the Jewish-Christian groups he later discusses: John the Baptist, James the brother of Jesus (who "took over the leadership of the group"), and the Ebionite and Nazarene traditions that survive into the second through fourth centuries. He says these followers, headquartered in Jerusalem, held that God never commanded sacrifice and that sacrifice was an accommodation imposed by the Priestly and cultic groups. He says he will reconstruct this material in later videos, and he states this view as his position, with the evidence coming later in the series.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Temple action was a symbolic protest against commerce only.** Tabor argues it was a day-long takeover that blocked the gates and stopped sacrifice, and that the target was the sacrificial system itself, not just the trade.
- **Mark's donkey image is the humble king of Zechariah 9.** Tabor argues it is Solomon's anointing, and says Zechariah 9 is a Matthean addition.
- **Sacrifice was God's commanded will throughout the Hebrew Bible.** Tabor argues that Jeremiah 7, Amos 5, Psalm 51, and Solomon's prayer present sacrifice as secondary or non-commanded, and that the sacrificial texts are later Priestly insertions.
- **The fig tree is simply a case of Jesus being irrational.** Tabor argues that ancient readers would understand that figs do not ripen in April, and that Mark presents the episode as a sign for the disciples.
