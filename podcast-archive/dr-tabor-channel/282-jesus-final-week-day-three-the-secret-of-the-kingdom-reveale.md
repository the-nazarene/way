## Summary

Tabor's question is what Mark's account of Jesus' third day in Jerusalem reveals about the "secret of the kingdom," and how the disciples and enemies respond. He works through Mark 11:20 to 12:44, which he treats as a single day of seven encounters in the Temple: a challenge about Jesus' authority, the parable of the wicked tenants, the tribute question, the Sadducees on resurrection, the scribe on the greatest commandment, the question about David's son, and the poor widow's offering. He frames the day as Tuesday, based on a projection from the year 30 CE and an astronomical program, and he says this dating is not absolutely demonstrated.

His central claim is that the scribe's answer in Mark 12:28-34 is the climax of the whole gospel. Tabor argues that Jesus recognizes the scribe as "not far from the kingdom of God" because he has grasped that loving God and one's neighbor matters more than the Temple and its sacrifices. He reads this as the "secret" or "mystery" of the kingdom, and he links it to the widow's gift and to Isaiah 66, where God says he wants a person of a poor and contrite heart. He also argues that Mark's resurrection is transformation, not resuscitation, and that Mark's Jesus treats John the Baptist as his teacher.

The talk ends with Tabor's warning that the disciples miss all of this. They admire the Temple buildings, and Mark 13 turns to the coming destruction of Jerusalem. Tabor says he will continue with day four in a later video.

### Key Teachings

### 1. The Authority question and John the Baptist

Tabor argues that Jesus' counter-question about John the Baptist in Mark 11:27-33 is a deliberate answer, not evasion. He notes that Josephus records that Herod Antipas had John arrested out of fear of an uprising, which he uses as evidence of John's popular influence. Tabor says, "I believe all the evidence shows that Jesus looked to John as his teacher." He adds that the sayings in Matthew and Luke's shared material, that John is more than a prophet and that none born of women is greater, are not in Mark. He also says Christians often want John to step aside once Jesus appears, which he rejects. He cautions that Mark's crowd sizes may be inflated, though he thinks John's popularity is independently attested.

### 2. The parable of the wicked tenants (Mark 12:1-12)

Tabor reads the vineyard owner's sending of servants and finally a "beloved Son" as a transparent allusion to the prophets and to Jesus, drawing on the baptism and Transfiguration voices. He says no reader of Mark would doubt that the parable concerns God sending prophets to Israel. He does not develop the theme further in this talk beyond noting that the enemies understood it as aimed at them.

### 3. Tribute to Caesar and the Zealots (Mark 12:13-17)

Tabor says the question about taxes is loaded. He ties it to Judas the Galilean, whose 6 CE revolt, in his account, included a call not to pay taxes. He describes that revolt as a call to arms against the client rulers set up after Herod the Great, and he says Jesus, then around ten years old, would have remembered it. He treats the answer "render to Caesar..." as a question that has been debated for nearly two thousand years, and notes that it leaves the matter of what belongs to God open.

### 4. Resurrection as transformation (Mark 12:18-27)

Tabor argues that the Sadducees, who reject resurrection and the age to come, are answered by Jesus from the Torah: at the burning bush (Exodus 3), God says "I am the God of Abraham," not "I was." He says Jesus defends resurrection, but not the revival of corpses. In Mark, resurrection is a transformation into a body suited to "the age to come," and Tabor links this to Mark 16 and to Paul, whom he says is allied with Mark on this point. He also notes that Luke expands the saying to say that all live to God, and that Luke adds that they will live forever.

### 5. The scribe and the greatest commandment (Mark 12:28-34)

This is the core of the talk. Tabor praises the scribe as the only figure in Mark whom Jesus commends for insight. He compares Matthew 22:34-40, where Matthew turns the scribe into a hostile tester and stops the quotation before the scribe's own words. He says Luke omits the scene and gives a similar but different exchange with a lawyer in Luke 10. Tabor reads Deuteronomy 6:4-5 (the Shema) and Leviticus 19:18, and presents the scribe's words that loving God and neighbor is "much more than all the whole burnt offerings and sacrifices." He concludes that Jesus' reply, "You are not far from the kingdom of God," reveals the secret that the kingdom is the will of God done on earth, expressed as love over Temple ritual. He calls this, with Mark 10:45, one of two climactic statements in Mark before the crucifixion.

### 6. The Son of David and Psalm 110 (Mark 12:35-37)

Tabor says Jesus' question about how the Messiah can be David's son, using Psalm 110:1, is not a question of genealogy. He argues that it concerns who the final Davidic anointed one will be and his role in restoring things. He rejects reading "Melchizedek" as a proper name in Psalm 110, citing the Jewish Publication Society translation and study-Bible notes, and reads the phrase as "righteous King." He also connects the Son of Man in Daniel 7 with a collective group of faithful servants, not a lone figure.

### 7. The widow's offering and the Temple (Mark 12:38-44)

Tabor reads Jesus' warning against the scribes who devour widows' houses as a closing denunciation. He then presents the poor widow's two lepta as the true model, while the disciples remain blind. He says the Hebrew Bible shows God never wanted a Temple or a human king, and cites Isaiah 66:1-2. He sets this against the disciples' admiration of the Temple's stones in Mark 13:1. He reads Mark 13, with Daniel 9 as background, as shaped by the destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE, which he says was probably written after that event. He says Mark 13 should be read in light of the widow.

- **John the Baptist is a minor figure who steps aside for Jesus.** Tabor argues that Jesus looked to John as his teacher and that the sources do not present John as subordinate in this way.
- **Psalm 110's "Melchizedek" is a proper name.** He says it should be read as "righteous King."
