## Summary

Tabor asks who anointed Jesus as Messiah, and answers by working through Mark's account of "Day Four," the Wednesday before Passover, which he says has only two events: a plot to kill Jesus and the anointing at Bethany (Mark 14:1-10). He argues that Mark signals something messianic in the anointing of Jesus's head, performed by an unnamed woman in the house of Simon the leper, and that Mark frames the act as preparation for Jesus's burial. Tabor also notes that Mark's unnamed women are heroines while the male disciples fail, a pattern he attributes to Mark's design.

Tabor then compares the two other anointing accounts: Luke 7, a sinful woman anointing Jesus's feet in a Pharisee's house in Galilee, and John 12, where Mary of Bethany anoints Jesus six days before Passover. He says that Mark and John cannot be harmonized, though they can shed light on each other. He points to his blog post on whether Mary Magdalene and Mary of Bethany are the same person, but he does not state a conclusion and leaves viewers to read the posts themselves.

The talk ends with a calendar argument. Tabor assumes the year 30 CE, places Day Four on Wednesday, and says that by Jewish reckoning Wednesday night begins Thursday, so the Last Supper falls on Wednesday night. He promises to cover the Last Supper, arrest, trial, crucifixion and burial on Thursday in the next episode.

## Key Teachings

### Day Four is brief and Mark's account is the anchor
Tabor describes Day Four as "two events" in Mark 14, in contrast to the packed days before it. He says the plot to kill Jesus and the anointing are told "in just a few verses," and that Mark's account should be read first, before Matthew, Luke and John are brought in. He assumes the year 30 CE and dates Nisan 13 to Wednesday, April 3, "by my calculations at least."

### The anointing of the head as a messianic signal
Tabor's central reading is that the anointing "over his head" carries messianic meaning. He links it to Jesus riding a colt into Jerusalem, the crowd's cry about "the kingdom of Our Father David," and the claim that Jesus is bringing David's kingdom. For Tabor the irony is that a woman anoints Jesus, which he says fits Mark's pattern of reversing expectations. He reads Jesus's words in Mark 14:8 ("she has done what she could... she's anointed my body beforehand for burial") as presenting the messianic anointing as preparation for his death. Tabor says Mark wants Jesus proclaimed Messiah "dead on the cross," so his anointing functions as anointing for burial.

### The unnamed woman and Mark's literary pattern
Tabor stresses that the woman is not named, and says this is consistent with Mark: "these unnamed women that are heroins in the gospel of Mark whereas all the male disciples are complete failures." He also notes that Mark's opening line ("the beginning of the Gospel of Jesus Christ") introduces the story, and that Jesus's words in Mark 14:9 predict the story will be told "in memory of her." He says he does not think the name is needed for Mark's meaning.

### Three anointing accounts, which do not harmonize
Tabor sets Mark 14 beside Luke 7 and John 12:
- **Mark 14:** Bethany, house of Simon the leper, two days before Passover, the head anointed, objections from unspecified "some," then Judas goes to the chief priests.
- **Luke 7:** Galilee, a Pharisee's house, a woman of the city, feet anointed and wiped with her hair, an objection about the woman's character, and the parable of forgiveness and love.
- **John 12:** Bethany, Mary the sister of Martha, six days before Passover, feet anointed and wiped with her hair, with Judas as the one objecting.

He says the chronology differs and that most people would harmonize them into two anointings at Bethany, but "I don't think you can harmonize Mark and John." He adds that John sometimes has material Mark lacks and seems to have more accurate chronology, though he does not claim certainty.

### The Mary identification question
Tabor says that the feet-anointing and hair-wiping in John resemble Luke's story, and that people have merged these with the figure of Mary Magdalene as "the Sinner" and "the Prostitute." He calls this a mixing of "completely different occasions according to these texts." He points viewers to his blog post "In Memory of Her: Mary's Forgotten Memorial," which he describes as one of his most-read posts, and to a guest post by Jeffrey Butz arguing that Mary Magdalene and Mary of Bethany are the same person. He says he will leave the reader to decide and does not state his own verdict in the talk.

### Day reckoning and the Last Supper
Tabor argues that by Jewish reckoning, Wednesday night is the beginning of Thursday, so Jesus "actually does eat the Last Supper on Wednesday night which is then Thursday." He acknowledges that the church tradition puts the Last Supper on Thursday and the crucifixion on Friday, and says he is "moving it one day earlier." He notes that other years (29, 31, 32, 33) would produce different weekdays, and that his argument assumes 30 CE.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The three anointings are one event, or Mary Magdalene is the woman in them.** Tabor says the texts describe different occasions, and that merging the Mary of John with Luke's sinful woman and the figure of Mary Magdalene is a later mixing. He does not, however, settle the identity question in this talk and directs viewers to the blog posts.
- **Mark and John can be harmonized into two Bethany anointings.** Tabor says "most people" would do this but that he does not think Mark and John can be harmonized, though they can illuminate one another.
- **The Last Supper was on Thursday and the crucifixion on Friday.** Tabor says this is church tradition, and argues on calendar grounds that Jesus ate the Last Supper on Wednesday night, which is Thursday by Jewish reckoning.
