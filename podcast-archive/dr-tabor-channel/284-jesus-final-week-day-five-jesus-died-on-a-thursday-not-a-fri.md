## Summary

Tabor asks on which day of the week Jesus was crucified, and argues that the standard Good Friday tradition is wrong: Jesus died on Thursday, Nisan 14, in the year 30 (he gives April 4 as the date). He presents this as the fifth video in a short series on Jesus's final week. His case rests on three kinds of evidence: the Gospel texts (Mark 14:1, John 13, and Luke 22), the Jewish calendar (days begin at sunset, and Nisan 15 is a "double Sabbath" that would make a Friday crucifixion awkward), and a few outside sources (Josephus, the Hebrew Bible, a Babylonian Talmud reference to "Ar pesak," and Paul's letters). He argues that the Last Supper was held on Wednesday night, before Passover, and was not a Seder. He says Jesus was arrested and executed before the feast so that there would be no riots, and he presents Jesus's death as occurring at about 3 p.m. as the lambs were being slaughtered.

Tabor ends by pointing viewers to his blog post "Last Days of Jesus," to a post titled "Was Jesus' Last Supper a Passover Meal," and to Jonathan Klawans's article on whether the Last Supper was a Seder. He says the next video will cover the arrest, trials, and crucifixion. He closes by naming Jim Bishop's 1957 book *The Day Christ Died* as the inspiration for his title. The talk is a single presenter's monologue; no guest or host speaks.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The calendar: Thursday, Nisan 14, with days beginning at sunset
Tabor argues that Jewish days begin at sunset, so the "Wednesday night" meal already belongs to Thursday in Jewish reckoning. He places the arrest after midnight, the trials in the early morning, and the crucifixion at 9 a.m., with Jesus dead by about 3 p.m. on Nisan 14. He also says the lambs were slaughtered on the afternoon of Nisan 14, which he links to Josephus.

### 2. "Before the feast": Mark 14:1 and John 13
Tabor reads Mark 14:1 ("two days before the Passover") as placing the plot on Wednesday. He reads John 13 ("before the Feast of the Passover") as showing that the Last Supper was not a Passover meal. He points to the trial-site detail in John, where the officials do not enter Pilate's praetorium "so that they might not be defiled but might eat the Passover" that night. He takes this to mean the Passover meal was still ahead of them. He describes the "Seder" reading of the Last Supper as a misunderstanding of Mark.

### 3. The "double Sabbath" argument
Tabor argues that people wrongly move the Sabbath to Saturday. He says Nisan 15 is the first day of Unleavened Bread, an annual "high day" Sabbath (one of seven, as he counts them), so there was already a holy day on Friday. This is why the bodies had to be taken down quickly "before the Sabbath," and why Jesus stays in the tomb through both days.

### 4. Paul's evidence: "the night he was betrayed"
Tabor says Paul, "our earliest witness," does not call the meeting a Passover. In 1 Corinthians 11:23 Paul speaks of "the night he was betrayed." Tabor also cites the line "Christ Our Passover is sacrificed for us," which he says refers to the day before the Passover lamb is killed. He presents this as a sign that Paul knew the Last Supper was not Passover night.

### 5. Talmudic reference: "Ar pesak"
Tabor says the Babylonian Talmud records that they "hung Yeshua" on "Ar pesak," the day of preparation before Passover, with Passover day coming after. He treats this as an independent, non-Christian confirmation of a Friday-before-Sabbath timing that falls on the preparation day.

### 6. Jesus as "Passover" and the Last Supper as a Messianic meal
Tabor separates the theological claim that Jesus "was the Passover" from the historical claim that he died as the lambs were being killed. He says it is possible the early Christians added the theology, but he thinks the historical timing is probably correct. Luke 22 is used to show Jesus saying he wanted to eat the Passover before he suffered but would not eat it "until it's fulfilled in the kingdom of God." Tabor reads this as Jesus knowing he would not share the Passover meal.

### 7. The motive: arrest before the feast to prevent unrest
Tabor argues that the timing reflects a plan to arrest and execute Jesus before the festival, so that crowds would not riot in his support. He says that once Passover began, arrest and crucifixion on the holiest day of spring would not have happened.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus was crucified on a Friday (Good Friday).** Tabor argues he died on Thursday, Nisan 14, because the traditional reading misplaces the Sabbath and the Passover days.
- **The Last Supper was a Passover/Seder meal.** Tabor says it was held the night before Passover, citing John 13, Mark 14:1, and Paul's wording.
- **The Sabbath was Saturday, so the Friday was simply preparation.** Tabor argues Nisan 15 was an annual Sabbath, creating a double Sabbath that made the Friday a holy day in its own right.
- **Christians "read the Passover into" the story.** He says that once Christians lost touch with Jewish festivals, they misunderstood the terms (such as "first day of unleavened bread," "Passover," and the bread used), and this confusion persisted.
