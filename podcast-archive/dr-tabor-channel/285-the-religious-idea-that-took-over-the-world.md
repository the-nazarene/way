## Summary

Tabor asks when and how the Western idea of death as a passage to a true, eternal home came into Jewish and Christian thought. He builds on the categories of his teacher Jonathan Z. Smith: an "archaic" view, in which the cosmos is ordered and every being has a fixed place, and a "utopian" view, in which the human being is a stranger in this world and salvation means escape to a true home. Tabor argues the second view entered the Mediterranean world in the Hellenistic period and is the basis of the religion that followed. He says he is speaking mainly about Western thought, and he presents the talk as an informal, unedited session for his research community.

His evidence runs in three stages. First, the older Hebrew texts (Psalms 74, 88, 115, 139; Job 14) show a three-tier cosmos in which the dead go down to Sheol and do not return, with Job's protest (Job 14 and 19) as the first crack. Second, Tabor sets this against Gilgamesh, Homer's Odyssey Book 11, and the Greek "golden plates" for the dead, which tell the soul to drink from the lake of memory and say its race is of heaven alone. He also cites Cicero's *Dream of Scipio* and Plato. Third, he traces Jewish texts that move toward the new view: Ben Sira (which he treats as the older position), the Wisdom of Solomon, 2 Maccabees, and 1 Enoch 39. He also says the earliest Christians reworked resurrection in this direction, citing 1 Corinthians 15.

Tabor ends by returning to Smith's idea that gnosticism is a "structural possibility" rather than a Christian heresy, and he points listeners to his own book on Paul's ascent to paradise. The talk is almost entirely Tabor speaking. The captions also include a few brief remarks from people in his audience (for example, a participant who clarified the Psalm 22 reading), but Tabor's own argument carries the talk.

## Key Teachings

### The archaic Hebrew cosmos and Sheol
Tabor describes a three-tier universe: the God of the heavens above the firmament, the earth as the place for humans, and Sheol as the world of the dead, with waters of chaos outside the earth's disk. He grounds this in Psalm 74 (the sea and Leviathan), Genesis 1 (the waters that must be moved so dry land appears), and Psalm 115 ("the dead do not praise the Lord"). In Psalm 88 the dying man is "reckoned among those who go down to the pit." In Psalm 139 David says "if I make my bed in sh[eol] thou art there," which Tabor reads as God's presence reaching into Sheol without any promise of rising from it. Tabor's point is that the dead simply go to the grave, and any return is temporary (a "resuscitation"). He suggests human sacrifice may have arisen from fear of cosmic disruption, but he presents this as his own view, leaning on Velikovsky.

### Job's protest and the redeemer
Tabor calls Job "the pivotal text" where someone begins to object to how the cosmos is arranged. Job 14:7 compares a tree that can sprout again after it is cut down with a human who does not come back (Job 14:10-12 is read the same way). Tabor says Job 14:13 turns to a wish: "Oh that you would hide me in shield" and "appoint me a set time." Job's protest is about divine justice (theodicy), since the righteous and the wicked lie together in the grave. Tabor then reads Job 19 as Job's hope for a "redeemer" who will vindicate him after his death. He says the passage is not about Jesus and is not a full resurrection, but a demand that a just God must act.

### Gilgamesh, Homer and the "house of no return"
Tabor reads the Gilgamesh epic as showing the same universal view. Siduri advises Gilgamesh to enjoy life because humans were given death and immortality belongs to the gods. In the underworld, the dwellers are "bereft of light." In Odyssey Book 11, Achilles tells Odysseus he would rather be a hired hand on earth than "lord over all the dead." Tabor's conclusion is that across the ancient West, death was seen as being "consigned to a realm" of no return, not as nonexistence. He also says Egypt's afterlife developed mainly for the elite.

### The golden plates and the soul's heavenly origin
Tabor calls the golden plates (lamellae) the first archaeological evidence he can find for the new view, and he calls them "Q cards for the dead." He describes one from Petelia, which he dates to about 350 BCE on the basis of grave goods. The dead person says, "Parch with thirst am I and dying," and asks for water from the cold spring on the right by the white cypress. The guardians ask who he is, and he answers that he is "a child of earth and starry heaven, but heaven alone is my house." Tabor reads the lake of Lethe as the cause of forgetfulness and reincarnation, and the lake of memory as the way out. He argues the soul does not belong to the lower world and has fallen into it. Smith, he says, read "my race is of heaven alone" as a claim to origin, not as a promise of joining the heroes.

### Cicero, Plato and the heavenly self
Tabor presents Plato (the "weep not for me" scene and the cave), Empedocles ("I was once a bird, a fish, and now a man... I wept when I saw this dreadful place"), and Cicero's *Dream of Scipio* as Hellenistic texts that reverse the cosmos. In Cicero the body is a prison and the true self is the spirit: "Know then that you are a God." Tabor says that his own experience of watching people die (including his son) supports the sense that the self, not just the body, is what matters. He treats this as a human intuition, not as proof.

### Jewish texts that move toward the new view
Tabor traces a sequence. Ben Sira keeps the older position: trust God, live righteously, and do not expect eternal life. The Wisdom of Solomon is "very Hellenized," with an eternal spark within. 2 Maccabees has a resurrection of the seven martyrs, whose tongues are restored, though Tabor doubts they meant a simple reassembly of the body. He says the Pharisees moved toward resurrection, the Sadducees held to the older view, and the Essenes probably leaned Pharisaic with some Sadducean adjustments. 1 Enoch 39 places the dead with God in heaven, which Tabor calls "seven levels of heaven" in the Hellenistic model, against the Jewish model that places resurrection and final things at the end.

### Paul, the earliest Christians and the reversal
Tabor says the earliest Christians did not reject the idea of death; they moved it toward resurrection. He lists Paul's terms in 1 Corinthians 15: the present body is "perishable, lowly, weak" and the resurrection body is "imperishable, glorious, and power." Tabor says Paul does not claim that death itself transforms, but that the dead sleep in the dust and God may transform them. Tabor also reports a saying of Jesus about the woman with seven husbands, where Jesus tells the questioners they misunderstand resurrection and that in the age to come people "are like angels." Tabor is not certain of the chapter (he says "Mark 10," then "Mark 11"), so verify the passage. He also says Christianity moved to see death as an enemy that is "conquered" but not a friend. He presents this as the revised Hebrew view.

### Smith's utopian and archaic categories and "gnosticism"
Tabor's frame is Smith's contrast: archaic religion finds order in a cosmos created and maintained through ritual and classification, while utopian religion sees that order as oppressive and seeks escape. Tabor quotes Smith that gnosticism is "a structural possibility" and a mode of being in the world, not a Christian heresy, and that it can produce an evil creator in its extreme forms. Tabor adds that "just about every Christian on the planet is a Gnostic, at least with a small G."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Gnosticism is a Christian heresy.** Tabor, following Smith, says it is a "structural possibility" in the Hellenistic world, a way of viewing the cosmos, and that "just about every Christian on the planet is a Gnostic, at least with a small G."
- **The Hebrew Bible already contains a clear afterlife.** Tabor argues the older texts (Psalms 88, 115, 139; Job 14) show the dead go to Sheol with no return, and that resurrection and eternal life come later, through Hellenistic influence.
- **Job 14:7 teaches reincarnation.** Tabor says the tree image is only that a tree can sprout again, while a human does not; he does not read it as reincarnation.
- **Ancient Egypt had a general afterlife for everyone.** Tabor says Egyptian afterlife developed first for the elite, and only later was democratized.
