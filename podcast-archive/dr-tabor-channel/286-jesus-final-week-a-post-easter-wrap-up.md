## Summary

Tabor asks how to reconstruct the events from Jesus' arrest through his burial, and what method can sort out sources that disagree. He works through the passion narrative in Mark 14 to 15 using Burton Throckmorton's *Gospel Parallels*, setting Mark, Matthew, and Luke side by side, then comparing Mark with John 18 to 19. He treats Mark as the earliest account and Matthew and Luke as rewritings of it, with John as an independent "alternative passion account." He says Luke softens the failures of the disciples and the guilt of the Romans, and that Mark presents a forsaken Jesus.

Tabor's main conclusions are that Jesus ate a final meal before Passover, not at the Passover meal; was arrested on the Mount of Olives; was tried first before Annas and then Caiaphas, then before Pilate; and was crucified on a Thursday, before the Passover. He argues that Joseph of Arimathea's tomb was an emergency burial near the crucifixion site, and that Jesus was reburied permanently after the Sabbath. He supports these points with the geography of John's account, archaeological work he covered in an earlier series (the Mount Zion house, the pavement at the Praetorium), the Mark 15:21 reference to Simon of Cyrene's sons, and the Jewish Passover calendar. He also suggests that Mark's crucifixion scene draws on Psalm 22 and may not be wholly historical.

The talk ends as a wrap-up of the miniseries. Tabor sets aside the empty tomb and the post-resurrection appearances for a separate video, "What Really Happened Easter Morning," and promotes it at the close.

## Key Teachings

### Mark as earliest source, with Matthew and Luke as rewritings
Tabor says "Mark is our earliest record as I understand it," and he judges the arguments for that to be strong. He reads Matthew as following Mark closely, and Luke as using Mark as "his main narrative source" while changing it. In Gethsemane, Tabor says Luke omits the three-disciple rebuke, has an angel comfort Jesus, and has the disciples sleep "for sorrow," so the disciples' failure is softened. Luke also has Jesus sent to Herod Antipas and omits the Roman soldiers' abuse, which Tabor reads as an effort to shift blame from the Romans to the Jewish authorities.

### The trial: Annas, Caiaphas, and Pilate
Tabor argues that Mark's "two-stage trial" can be filled in from John. In his reading, the night hearing was at the house of Annas, who "controls everything" and was the "Godfather of this Priestly Mafia," with Caiaphas as Annas's son-in-law in office. He says the Sanhedrin's early-morning session was before dawn, and that the group then handed Jesus to Pilate. He cites Mark's "not during the feast" (Mark 14:2) as evidence that the arrest was planned before the festival.

### The chronology: Thursday crucifixion, Passover on Friday
Tabor's central claim is that the crucifixion took place on a Thursday, "which is not the Passover yet." He reasons from the Passover week itself: Mark has the authorities say "not during the feast," and Mark 15:21 has Simon "coming in from the country," which Tabor says would not happen on the Passover itself. He says the traditional Good Friday timing "just doesn't work." He also reads John's "sixth hour" (John 19:14) as Roman time counting from midnight, against the third hour (9:00 a.m.) in Mark.

### Gethsemane and the Mount of Olives
Tabor locates the arrest on the Mount of Olives, in a private "hiding place" where Jesus often met his disciples, based on John's garden detail and Judas's knowledge of it. He says the traditional Gethsemane at the foot of the Mount of Olives, "where anyone could see you from the road," "was definitely not the place." He takes the "young man" who flees naked (Mark 14:51-52) as a literary figure for Mark, following his teacher Norman Perrin, and he does not treat it as a historical event.

### The crucifixion and Psalm 22
Tabor reads Mark's crucifixion as shaped by Psalm 22. He notes that Psalm 22 ends in rescue, but "Mark does not have God deliver Jesus." He argues that the dividing of garments and the "forsaken" cry (Mark 15:34) show that Mark may have built the scene from prophecy, and that the historical question is therefore open. He also notes that the crowds in Mark "heard him gladly," which he finds hard to square with the mockery, and he is "somewhat skeptical" that the scene is built wholly from events. He also points to Luke's version: Luke drops the forsaken cry, has Jesus forgive his executioners and a thief, and has the centurion call Jesus innocent.

### Two burials: an emergency burial and a permanent one
Tabor argues that Mark does not say the tomb belonged to Joseph of Arimathea, and that Matthew adds "rich man" because of Isaiah 53:9. He holds that John's account describes a burial "of convenience" in an unused garden tomb near the crucifixion site, done because the Passover and weekly Sabbath prevented further handling of the body. Afterward, "the Joseph party" moved the body to a permanent tomb. He says the common belief that Joseph's own tomb received Jesus is a later tradition, carried into the Gospel of Peter.

### Method: comparing Mark and John
Tabor describes his method as a "dual" textual comparison: Mark as the earliest synoptic account and John as an alternative passion narrative, compared closely on places, times, and situations, while setting aside theological additions. He admits that John also has theological material, but he treats the geography and timing as the parts most worth comparing. He says this approach yields "a rather coherent story."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Last Supper was the Passover meal.** Tabor argues it was a private meal before Passover, as shown by Mark's "not during the feast" and the Passover-week timing.
- **Good Friday, a Friday crucifixion on Passover.** He argues for a Thursday crucifixion, since Simon's coming "in from the country" and the Passover timing do not fit a Friday.
- **Gethsemane is the traditional garden at the foot of the Mount of Olives.** He says that site "was definitely not the place," and argues for a hidden spot higher on the Mount of Olives.
- **Joseph of Arimathea's tomb was Jesus' burial place.** He says that tradition comes from Matthew's "rich man" reading of Isaiah 53, and that John describes a temporary, emergency burial.
- **The Gospels agree on the arrest and trial.** He says Luke revises the passion narrative to excuse the disciples and the Romans, and that Mark and John must be compared to reconstruct events.
