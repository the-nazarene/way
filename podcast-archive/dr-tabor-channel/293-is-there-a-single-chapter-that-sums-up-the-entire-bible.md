## Summary

Tabor asks whether one chapter of the Hebrew Bible sums up the core of "Abrahamic Faith." His answer is Psalm 103, which he calls the "heart" of Book Four of the Psalms (Psalms 90-106). He presents Book Four as a neglected collection, noting that its opening heading assigns Psalm 90 to Moses. He argues that Psalm 103 supplies three things he identifies as the foundation: who God is, what "the way" is, and what the plan is.

His main evidence is textual. He traces the divine self-description in Psalm 103:8 ("merciful and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love") back to Exodus 34:6-7, where God proclaims his name, and he lists parallel uses in Numbers 14:18, Psalm 86:15, Joel 2:13, Jonah 4:2 and Psalm 145:8. He also argues that the divine name YHWH should be understood through Exodus 3:14 as a statement of being and character rather than a magic word, and that "Elohim," used with a singular verb, is better rendered as "force of all forces." From there he reads Psalm 103's description of human mortality ("we are dust") as the human counterpart to God's character.

The talk ends without a full resolution. Tabor says the chapter's ethics follow from the character of God, that the Hebrew Bible is not very interested in detailing an afterlife, and that he has published a longer treatment of this material (first in 1991, later expanded) with a new book forthcoming. He closes by inviting questions about how such a faith relates to fundamentalism, science and history, and says he will share a list of sources later.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Book Four of the Psalms and the choice of Psalm 103
Tabor points out that the Psalter is divided into five books, and that each book ends with a doxology (he cites Psalm 41, 72 and 89). Book Four begins with a heading, "a prayer of Moses the man of God" (Psalm 90). He says he does not claim to know whether Moses wrote it and calls the headings "partly editorial." He argues that the Psalm 103 contains "the entire Bible in one chapter" and that Book Four as a whole contains creation, Exodus and other material, so that "every concept is there."

### 2. The character of God: Exodus 34 and its repetitions
Tabor reads Psalm 103:7 ("he made known his ways to Moses") as pointing back to Exodus 34. In his reading, God passes before Moses, proclaims the name, and describes his character: "the Lord God merciful and gracious slow to anger abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness." He notes the same formula recurs in Numbers 14:18, Psalm 86:15, Joel 2:13, Jonah 4:2 and Psalm 145:8. He says the Hebrew word translated "truth" or "faithfulness" (*emet*) here is a character trait, meaning someone who is "true" and not a "fake" or "hypocrite." He also connects the "ways" (plural) of God with the human 10 words given in Exodus 34:27, which he describes as a summary of how humans should apply that character.

### 3. The name YHWH and "I am who I am"
Tabor argues that Exodus 3:14 ("I am who I am") should not be read as a simple name tag. He notes that the verb is in the imperfect aspect, which he glosses as "not stopping," and that the translation could be "I will be what I will be." He says most scholars take the form as third person ("he is/will be"), and that he is not a Hebrew specialist. He argues that to know the name in Hebrew thought is to know its meaning or reputation. He criticizes using the name as a "Talisman or something you know magic," and suggests the name should be understood as a designation or understanding rather than a formula.

### 4. Elohim as "force of all forces" and El Shaddai
Tabor explains that Elohim is a plural form with a singular verb, and he uses "force of all forces" as a translation to convey an ultimate reality. He argues that if the ultimate force behind the universe is consciousness, it must also be personal, since otherwise the universe would be superior to any mind within it. For El Shaddai in Psalm 91:1, he offers a reading connected to the image of a nursing or protective breast, though he does not give a source for the word's full range of meanings. He presents the "most high" (*Elyon*) as another term pointing to the same reality.

### 5. Humans as "soil creatures" and mortality
Tabor translates Adam as "soil creature" or "soil man" (red earth), linking it to Psalm 103:14 ("he knows our frame, he remembers that we are dust"), Psalm 103:15-16 (human days are "like grass") and Genesis 18:27 (Abraham as "dust and ashes"). He says the Hebrew Bible does not elaborate on the afterlife, and cites Job's struggle in Job 14 (the tree that can sprout again, but a human who "lays low") without giving full detail on how Job's question is resolved. He does not offer a view on Sheol or resurrection beyond this.

### 6. Ethics as a reflection of God's character
Tabor argues that the Ten Words are a human application of God's character, and that negative commands are "actually positive." He contrasts George Bernard Shaw's version of the Golden Rule (which he rejects because tastes differ) with the negative form attributed to Hillel ("whatever is hateful to you do not do to others"). He also cites an unnamed first-century Jew who said Gentiles without the written Torah have it "written in their heart," and that they will be judged by conscience. He applies this to a general moral self-judgment.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Blessing God" or "blessing the name" is a magical act.** Tabor argues that *barak* means to bow the knee, and that blessing God's name means understanding it.
- **The divine name works like a magic formula or tag.** He says the name is a designation or understanding of being, and warns against treating it as a talisman.
- **God will fix your problems if you pray (e.g., a gas tank filled by prayer).** He calls this a "more magical universe" view and says he does not think it works that way. He also says the Bible is not a book of dogma that requires you to "swallow this and shut up."
- **Elohim is just a generic "god" or the name of a Mesopotamian deity.** He acknowledges the Mesopotamian connection but argues Israelites used the word to describe a personal, ultimate force, with singular verbs.
- **The Hebrew Bible offers a detailed afterlife doctrine.** He says it is "not very interested" in details, and that Job's question about life after death is not answered in a settled way in the cited passages.
