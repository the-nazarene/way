## Summary

Tabor, speaking alone to camera, closes a seven-part series on what he calls "fateful" New Testament texts. He asks whether a single underlying cause explains the harm done by six earlier texts, which he says were lifted out of first-century context and applied as universal rules about gender, marriage, celibacy, slavery and government. His answer is the expectation that the end of the age and the arrival of the kingdom of God would come within the lifetime of Jesus' generation, roughly the mid-to-late first century CE. He calls this the "imminence of the parousia" and says it is "absolutely clear" across the New Testament documents, citing the Synoptic sayings, Acts, Paul's letters, Hebrews, James, 1 Peter and Revelation, along with the Dead Sea Scrolls' Habakkuk Pesher as evidence that such groups dealt with delayed expectations by claiming God had extended the time.

Tabor's argument for why this matters rests on the claim that Paul's instructions in 1 Corinthians 7 and Romans 13 are interim advice for a world he thought was ending, such as staying in one's state, remaining celibate, obeying slave masters and honoring the emperor. Tabor argues that once these instructions were detached from that expectation and treated as permanent principles, "lots of harm is done to people." He explicitly rejects the full preterist view that everything was fulfilled in 70 CE, and says he holds a preterist view in the sense that these texts belong in their own first- and second-century setting.

The talk ends without a debate or guest. Tabor proposes that the expectation's failure should be weighed "in the name of justice and fairness and equality," says that "history speaks fairly clearly" about the results of the full preterist reading, and signs off with a pointer to his other series.

## Key Teachings

### The imminence of the end as the common thread
Tabor says the early followers of Jesus, whom he calls the Nazarenes, expected the parousia within their generation. He traces this to Jesus' own preaching that the kingdom of God was at hand and to a reading of Daniel 9 and 11. He says the hope begins to wane only in the latest New Testament writings, naming 2 Timothy (which he notes most scholars do not think Paul wrote) and 2 Peter. He calls the delay answer in 2 Peter ("a day is like a thousand years") a way of coping with a failed prediction.

### Textual evidence for the expectation
He cites Revelation's closing words "I am coming soon," Mark's saying that "some standing here will not taste of death before they see the kingdom come with power," Acts 3:19-21, and Luke 21 with Acts' "this generation will not pass" language. For Acts, he notes that the author also says "it's not for us to know the times and the seasons," but he argues the overall expectation still points to that generation. He reads the Synoptic "coming of the Son of Man" passages as judgment on Jerusalem, which is where the full preterist reading starts.

### Paul's interim ethics and the appointed time
Tabor's central Pauline text is 1 Corinthians 7, where Paul says "the appointed time has grown very short" and advises people to remain as they are, including not marrying. He ties the phrase to Daniel 11 and says the world's "present form is passing away." He argues Paul's advice on women's silence and submission, slaves obeying masters, and obeying governing authorities (Romans 13) is of the same kind: a status-quo posture for people who believed the old categories would soon disappear. He argues Paul detested the Roman world and was not an "accommodationist" with Rome.

### Paul's expectation of resurrection and transformation
Tabor describes Paul's expectation, drawn from 1 Thessalonians 4 and Philippians 3:20-21, that the dead would rise in glorified bodies and the living would be changed "in a moment of time," and that Paul expected to be among the living at that event. He says Paul did not live to see it, and that in the later letters attributed to Paul the apostle speaks of dying and receiving a crown. He notes that the dead are described as "asleep" in both Daniel and Paul.

### How apocalyptic groups handled delay
Tabor offers the Dead Sea Scrolls' Habakkuk Pesher as a case study. He reads its treatment of Habakkuk 2:3 ("if the vision terries wait for it it will surely come") as a community that had passed the expected period, calculated the 70-weeks prophecy as 490 years, and still held that God had "prolonged the time." He contrasts this with dispensationalism (John Darby's system), which he says places the prophetic clock on hold until a final seven-year period. He also says the historical Jesus, following Albert Schweitzer, expected the imminent end, and he includes John the Baptist and James in that picture.

### Why the Pauline texts became "fateful"
Tabor's core claim is that the harm came from taking the texts "out of their ancient context" and making them universal. He says the same approach would apply to any ancient document: "you can't take an ancient historical document and just rip it from its context and then reapply it verbally and literally down Through the Ages." He concludes that the apocalyptic expectation and its failure must be factored into any honest reading.

### Rejection of the full preterist view
He acknowledges the full preterist claim that all prophecy was fulfilled in 70 CE, and says the question is practical: slavery, mistreatment of women and similar practices persisted in the church afterward. He says rational readings after the Enlightenment helped, and that the claim the kingdom has existed for "nearly 2,000 years" should be tested against history.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That Paul's advice on marriage, slavery, women and government is a timeless moral code.** Tabor says these are interim instructions for a world Paul thought would end soon, and that making them universal caused harm.
- **That Paul accommodated himself to Rome.** Tabor says Paul "detest[ed] the Roman world" and expected it to be overthrown, so his submission advice was a temporary posture, not endorsement.
- **That the prophecies were simply fulfilled in 70 CE (full preterism).** Tabor says he does not share this view and argues the texts belong to their first- and second-century context, with the failure of expectation still unresolved.
- **That the delay can be explained by "time is nothing to God."** Tabor treats this as a later rationalization and points to the Habakkuk Pesher as an earlier example of an apocalyptic group extending the timetable.
