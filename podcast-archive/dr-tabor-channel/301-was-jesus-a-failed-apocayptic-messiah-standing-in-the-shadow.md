## Summary

Tabor asks what can be said about an apocalyptic Jesus "standing in the shadow of" Albert Schweitzer, whose *Quest of the Historical Jesus* (1906) he treats as the classic statement of an eschatological reading of Jesus. He says he is not debating whether Jesus was apocalyptic, but asking what follows from that view for how we understand Jesus, his first followers, and the movement that became Christianity. He frames the talk around his 2006 trade book *The Jesus Dynasty*, which he says drew wide public attention and whose dedication to Schweitzer gave the title.

Tabor argues that Jesus, John the Baptist, James, Peter and Paul all "lived and violently died with this imminent hope of cosmic reversal on their lips," and that what they expected "never came about." He describes himself as seeing Jesus as "a full and willing participant and key agent in these failed apocalyptic hopes and dreams," while still allowing for Crossan-style realized-kingdom teaching about a social order already breaking into history. He reviews three standard responses to failed prophecy (postponement, marginalization, allegorizing), contrasts symbolic apocalyptic language with apocalyptic movements that act on it, and then argues from Paul's letters, which he calls the earliest unmediated source, that Paul's own apocalyptic expectations were tied to the years before 70 CE. He presents this as his own assertion and argument, not as settled fact.

The Q&A turns to the embarrassment of Jesus's imminence sayings, the Dead Sea Scrolls' habakkuk pesher as an example of a community that kept its expectation, and whether 2 Thessalonians refers to or refutes 1 Thessalonians, including a forgery hypothesis. Tabor closes by saying Paul's specific apocalyptic vision was the source of "a new and very different story" after 70 CE. The talk ends with the moderator's closing remarks.

## Key Teachings

### Schweitzer's failed Messiah and the eschatological question
Tabor reads Schweitzer as holding that the union of "thoroughgoing skepticism" and "thoroughgoing eschatology" is an obstacle to traditional Christian theology. He paraphrases Schweitzer's image of Jesus laying hold of the wheel of the world, which "refuses to turn," and being crushed by it. Tabor says he shares most of Schweitzer's view. He notes that Schweitzer's own final chapter ("results") nonetheless affirms that Jesus means something through "a mighty spiritual force that streams from him," so Schweitzer ends with theological statements rather than pure negation. Tabor credits Johannes Weiss's 1892 work on the kingdom of God as the key step for the eschatological question.

### Apocalyptic as a cosmic worldview, and Jesus's failed hope
Tabor defines late Second Temple apocalyptic as focused on "the imminent End of the Age" and "a cosmic takeover through the power and agency of God," followed by a new world order and the vindication of God's people. He stresses a "very thick kind of world view," including the unseen world of Satan and demons. He argues the central historical fact is that what these figures expected did not happen, and that the Roman destruction and "terrible destruction of Jewish life in the land of Israel" followed. Tabor says Jesus's message that "the kingdom of God has come upon you" can coexist with a realized, social-political eschatology. The problem, he says, is the "windup": what Jesus expected did not happen.

### Three responses to failed prophecy
Tabor lists three ways communities handled "when prophecy fails":
- **Postponement**: "we were wrong but it will happen," which he finds in the Dead Sea Scrolls and in the Talmud (Sanhedrin 96, where the rabbis say calculations are exhausted).
- **Marginalization**: avoid apocalyptic books such as Daniel and Revelation, and do not become a calculator of the "70 weeks prophecy."
- **Allegorizing**: reread the texts as symbolic of an eternal struggle between good and evil, citing Augustine's *City of God*.
Tabor says each "is an attempt to affirm that failure is actually success."

### Symbol versus operation (projection versus agency)
Tabor distinguishes apocalyptic language used symbolically from apocalyptic thinking that becomes a social program. He gives the David Koresh group at Waco, which he says he studied closely, as an example of the second, and contrasts it with a Freudian "projection" explanation. He cites H.H. Rowley's 1942 *The Relevance of Apocalyptic*, written as Nazi forces advanced, to argue that expectations of imminent deliverance repeatedly prove mistaken. He also says dispensationalist Evangelical apocalypticism is socially "safe" in some ways, but that its support for Zionism as fulfilled prophecy is "more troubling."

### Paul as the earliest unmediated source
Tabor calls Paul's seven core letters the earliest and most "unmediated" source, because they have no redactor layer, followed by Q and Mark (if Mark is earliest). He reads 1 Thessalonians 4 on the living and the dead being "caught up in the clouds," 1 Corinthians 6 on the saints judging the world, and 1 Corinthians 7 on celibacy as "a celibacy of apocalyptic necessity." He describes Paul's outlook as a Hellenistic scheme of "apotheosis or immortalization" set within late Second Temple apocalypticism, with a specific expectation of Israel's conversion and the imminent parousia (Romans 9-11). He argues Paul's vision is particular to Paul and that "from the standpoint of the history of Judaism he belongs in those crucial years of Hope and promise" before 70.

### Mark 13, the 70 CE break, and Paul's "appointed time"
Tabor argues that Paul wrote in the early 50s expecting the end "had indeed drawn very near," and that events such as Gaius's attempt to place a statue in the Temple (41), the revolts of the 60s recorded by Josephus, and Nero's persecution would have heightened apocalyptic expectation. He reads Mark 13's "this generation shall not pass" as a text from that climate. He says Paul's mission to bring Israel to accept Jesus "became more and more Hopeless by 70 CE," and that "what emerged in his name... was the beginning of a new and very different story."

### The Q&A: Thessalonians and the embarrassment argument
Questioners raise two points. The first: Jesus's imminence sayings would be embarrassing to the early church, which might support their authenticity. Tabor answers that the group that kept them could affirm postponement, as in the Dead Sea Scrolls, and that later texts such as 2 Peter address "the so-called delay." The habakkuk pesher's promise that the vision "will surely come" is his example of a community that "in no way gives up the vision." The second point is about 2 Thessalonians, which an audience member argues disputes a claim that the day of the Lord has come, possibly by calling 1 Thessalonians a forgery. Another questioner suggests 2 Thessalonians may instead be a follower of Paul warning against jumping the gun, since Mark 13 requires a figure sitting in the Temple first. Tabor's response is brief and partly agrees.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Apocalyptic belief is harmless fantasy.** Tabor argues that the Waco and Zionism-linked examples show apocalyptic belief can have "profound social and political implications," and that the distinction between symbol and operation matters.
- **Prophecy never fails, so failure is really success.** He presents this as the motive behind allegorizing, and argues the historical record of failed expectations (Paul, Jesus, James, John the Baptist, Peter) should be faced squarely.
- **The apocalyptic Jesus was only a message of end-of-world doom.** Tabor says Jesus's "the kingdom of God has come upon you" can be read as a present, social-political kingdom alongside imminent expectation.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor's central claim is that Paul, not Jesus, is "more properly seen" as founder of Christianity, and that Paul's specific apocalyptic and Hellenistic vision set the course of what followed.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor names James as the figure who "took charge of things" after Jesus's death, and in the Q&A notes that the expectation of imminence was kept by communities. He does not develop James's role in this talk.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** Tabor argues Jesus's hope that "corpses don't rise from the dead" failed, and reads Paul's 1 Thessalonians 4 and 1 Corinthians 15 as bodies rising and transformed at the Lord's coming.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor calls Jesus "a key agent" in failed apocalyptic hopes, and says Schweitzer's Jesus lays hold of the wheel of the world to set it moving.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor ranks Paul's letters as the earliest unmediated source, followed by Q and Mark, and the Q&A raises the authenticity and forgery questions about the Thessalonian letters.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Tabor sees the kingdom as both imminent and already breaking into history, combining Schweitzer with Crossan.
