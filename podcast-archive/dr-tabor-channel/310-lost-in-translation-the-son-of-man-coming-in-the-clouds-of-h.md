## Summary

Tabor asks whether "the Son of Man coming in the Clouds of Heaven" in Daniel 7:13-14 refers to Jesus, as Christians have usually taught, or to a Messiah figure, as some Jewish interpreters have held. He argues it does not. In his reading, Daniel's vision is about four beasts, a persecuting "little horn," and the judgment of God, and the human-like figure is interpreted within the chapter itself as "the saints of the Most High" who receive the kingdom. He supports this with the meaning of "son of man" as "human being" in Hebrew and Aramaic, with Psalm 146:3 and with Ezekiel's use of the title for the prophet. He then applies the same reading to Mark 14:62, where Jesus's answer to the high priest invokes the figure, and to a report from the Jerusalem period, attributed to Hegesippus, in which James is asked about "the gate of Jesus" and answers by speaking of the Son of Man. Tabor concludes that the Son of Man here is an exemplar of a persecuted group, not a christological title, and that the hope it offers is vindication for the suffering people of God, Jesus and the martyrs included.

The talk is delivered by Tabor alone; there is no host or guest. Its positions are Tabor's own, and several of its historical claims (the identification of the little horn, the dating of the James report, the reading of Mark's expectations) are his interpretation of the sources he names.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The Daniel 7 vision and its interpretation within the chapter

Tabor walks through Daniel 7 in order: four beasts rising from the Mediterranean, a little horn that blasphemes God and persecutes the saints, the throne scene with the "Ancient of Days," and then the figure "like a son of man." He notes that most interpreters identify the little horn with Antiochus Epiphanes, which he links to the Maccabean period, and that later readers reassigned the beasts (Babylon, Persia, Greece, Rome) as the empires changed. He stresses that Daniel is puzzled by the vision and asks for its interpretation, which he says is given in the chapter: the four beasts are kingdoms, the little horn wars against the saints, and judgment is given "for the Saints of the Most High" until "the saints received the kingdom." For Tabor, the decisive point is the chapter's own explanation, which he says should govern how the figure is read.

### 2. "Son of man" means a human being, and the figure is collective

Tabor says the Aramaic phrase means "like a human being," and that its appearance in heaven is what draws Daniel's attention. Because the chapter's own explanation speaks of the saints receiving the kingdom, Tabor concludes: "they collectively are the son of man that's how it's interpreted in the Book of Daniel." He acknowledges that someone might say the collective of saints could have "a head or a roof like a messiah," but argues the text is not about a Davidic figure or any Messiah.

### 3. Other uses of "son of man" in the Hebrew Bible

Tabor offers two supports for reading the title generically. Psalm 146:3 ("put not your trust in princes... in a son of man in whom there's no help") uses the phrase in Hebrew parallelism for a mortal human. He also notes that Ezekiel is addressed as "son of man" in his visions, meaning simply "Earthling" or human being. He reads Daniel 7 as saying that the world's power is placed in the hands of God's people, who are glorified and given an everlasting kingdom. He ties this to Daniel 2:44, the stone that breaks the image of world kingdoms and sets up a kingdom that "will never be destroyed," which he says is a parallel vision.

### 4. Mark 14:62 read in its trial context

Tabor says that in Mark, Jesus answers the high priest's question about being the Messiah with "I am," followed by the Son of Man "seated at the right hand of power and coming with the Clouds of Heaven." He acknowledges that later Christians took this as a promise of Jesus's return and judgment, and says Mark himself believed Jesus would return. Tabor's reading is that, in context, Jesus is saying that the high priest, "backed up by Roman power," is acting like the beasts who persecute the saints, and that the Daniel vision of vindication will come regardless of what happens to Jesus. He describes Mark's broader framing: Mark 1:15 ("the time is fulfilled... the kingdom of God is at hand") means "the overthrow of the kingdoms of this world has drawn near," and Mark, Tabor says, is "very much involved in the Book of Daniel," with a chapter correlating Daniel with the destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE. (The caption renders this as "Daniel chapter 13"; the context suggests Tabor means the apocalyptic discourse in Mark 13, but he does not say so explicitly.)

### 5. James, the "gate of Jesus," and the Son of Man

Tabor cites a report, which he attributes to Hegesippus and which reached us through a church historian (the caption garbles the name), that James the brother of Jesus was questioned in the Temple by Pharisees around 62 CE and then killed. They asked "what is the gate of Jesus," and James replied, "why do you ask me concerning the son of man," describing him as sitting at the right hand of great power and about to come on the clouds of heaven. Tabor notes that the Greek text is the only version available, and that in Hebrew the words for Jesus and salvation are close, so the question might mean "the way of salvation" or "the way that Jesus taught." On his reading, James's answer shows that the Daniel 7 event was expected imminently and was not a claim about the Messiah.

### 6. Daniel 7 is not christology; it is about suffering and vindication

Tabor's conclusion is that Daniel 7 "is not about christology who is the Messiah it's about suffering and suffering servants." Jesus is an "exemplar of a type": crucified and "blotted out," yet part of the group whose persecution the vision answers. The vision offers hope that the persecuted saints, including Jesus and "all the other Martyrs of faithful Israel throughout history," will take over the kingdom.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Belief:** The "Son of Man coming in the Clouds of Heaven" in Daniel 7 is a prophecy of Jesus's Second Coming.
  **Correction:** In Daniel 7 the figure is interpreted within the chapter as the saints of the Most High who receive the kingdom; the text predates Jesus and is about the vindication of the persecuted people of God.
- **Belief:** The figure is a Davidic or Messianic figure, as some Jewish interpreters have held.
  **Correction:** Tabor says the text is not about a Davidic figure or any Messiah; a collective reading is what the chapter gives.
- **Belief:** Jesus's answer in Mark 14:62 is a prediction of his own return in judgment.
  **Correction:** In context, Jesus is confronting the high priest, and the reference is to the vindication of the saints, which Tabor says Mark also expected imminently.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor's main independent evidence is the James report, which he presents as showing that the Jerusalem leadership, in the years before 70 CE, expected the Daniel 7 event soon and did not identify it with a Messiah figure.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Tabor reads Daniel 7 and Daniel 2 as describing the kingdom of God set up over the world, with the saints receiving dominion, and reads Mark 1:15 in the same terms.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor's argument is that Jesus, in Mark 14:62, speaks as a representative of the persecuted saints rather than as a christological figure; he calls Jesus an "exemplar of a type."
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** Tabor says the vision offers vindication for the persecuted, including martyrs, but he does not discuss resurrection or afterlife in these terms.
- **How the gospels were formed:** Tabor's reading of Mark as a text that interprets Daniel and expects Jerusalem's destruction bears on Mark's composition and expectations. He does not discuss the wider canon.
