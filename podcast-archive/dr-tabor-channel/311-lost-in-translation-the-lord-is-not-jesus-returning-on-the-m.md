## Summary

Tabor asks what Zechariah 14 actually says and who it describes. Christians often read its account of "the battle against Jerusalem" and of a deity's feet standing on the Mount of Olives as a prediction of Jesus' return. Tabor argues that the chapter is about Yahweh, the personal name of the God of Israel, not about the Messiah. His main evidence is the Hebrew text itself: the chapter uses the four-letter name in the key verses, and the Messiah does not appear in it. He reads the name as a personal name, not a title, and notes that English translations render it "the LORD" in capitals, which hides the distinction from readers. He supports his reading by pointing to the Hebrew Bible's ten Messiah texts, which he says always describe the Messiah as God's anointed agent, and to New Testament passages (Mark 10, Mark 12, Luke and Matthew's temptation account, 1 Corinthians 8, Revelation 11:15) where, he says, the Lord in the Greek is the one God of Israel and not Jesus. He also argues that the prophecy has never been fulfilled in history.

The talk is a single presentation with no host or guest. It ends with a brief explanation of the meaning of the divine name (from the verb "to be") and a pointer to his videos on the Messiah texts, with the promise of another passage in the next episode.

### Key Teachings

### 1. "The LORD" in English is a translation problem
Tabor says the capitalized "LORD" in most modern English translations stands for the Hebrew personal name, usually pronounced Yahweh (also written Jehovah). He says the name is not a title and is not the Greek word *kurios*, which is used in the New Testament for Jesus. His example is "Behold a day of Yahweh is coming," which he says should not be read as "a day of the Lord" in the generic sense. He notes that some older or Catholic translations (the American Standard Version and the Jerusalem Bible) print the name as "Jehovah" or "Yahweh," which avoids the confusion.

### 2. Zechariah 14 describes a judgment by Yahweh, not a Messianic return
Tabor reads through Zechariah 14 in English: the battle for Jerusalem (14:1-2), Yahweh fighting against the nations and his feet standing on the Mount of Olives (14:3-4), the mountain splitting, the earthquake "in the days of Uzziah king of Judah" (14:5), continuous light, living waters flowing east and west from Jerusalem (14:6-8), and Yahweh becoming "king over all the Earth" with "his name one" (14:9). He says the text never mentions a davidic Messiah. He compares it with the Book of Daniel, which describes books opened and judgment held, also without mentioning the Messiah. He adds that the Babylonian and Roman sieges of Jerusalem, the second Jewish revolt, and Hadrian's rebuilding as Aelia Capitolina did not fulfill it, so the prophecy is still unfulfilled in his view.

### 3. The ten Hebrew Bible Messiah texts make the Messiah God's agent
Tabor says the ten Hebrew Bible passages on the davidic Messiah (he does not list them in the talk; they are in his notes) all present the Messiah as an anointed, chosen, blessed descendant of David who serves the Lord God. He describes the Messiah as "an executive of God's will," like a ruler or king carrying out God's purpose. The Messiah is therefore distinct from the God who is judging the nations. Tabor says this is consistent across the texts and points to his separate videos for the detailed treatment.

### 4. The early Jesus movement never identified Jesus with Yahweh
Tabor says the earliest followers of Jesus, Jewish and Gentile, who acknowledged him as Messiah "never confused Jesus with the one God of Israel." He says the modern equation "Jesus is God, Jesus is Yahweh" is a later development and not a feature of the earliest tradition.

### 5. The New Testament keeps the Shema and the one God
Tabor cites Mark 10 (the rich man calls Jesus "good teacher"; Jesus replies that no one is good but God alone), Mark 12 (Jesus quotes the Shema, Deuteronomy 6:4-5), and John 17:3, which he reads as Jesus praying to "the only true God." He reads Paul's summary in 1 Corinthians 8 as distinguishing "one God the father" from "one Lord" (*kurios*), and he compares the Greek *kurios* with the Hebrew *Adon* ("sir"), which can be used of a waiter. In Revelation 11:15, "the kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of Our Lord," he reads "Lord" as Yahweh. He concludes that the New Testament does not overturn the Hebrew view of God but speaks of Jesus as an exalted Messiah who sits at God's right hand, while God keeps his own throne.

### 6. The meaning of the divine name
At the end, Tabor explains that the name Yahweh is built from the verb "to be" and may reflect the past, present and future tenses ("he will be," "he is," "he was"). He cites Exodus 3 and Exodus 6, where God answers Moses' question about his name, and says the name is a designation for the ultimate, living Being, which Revelation's "who is and was and is to come" echoes. He says Orthodox Jews do not pronounce the name and use *Hashem* ("the Name").

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Zechariah 14 predicts Jesus returning to the Mount of Olives.** Tabor says the chapter is about Yahweh's intervention, and the Messiah does not appear in it.
- **"The Lord" in the Bible always means Jesus or a generic lord.** He says the capitalized "LORD" in English usually stands for the personal name Yahweh, while the Greek *kurios* is a different word used for Jesus and for ordinary masters.
- **Jesus is Yahweh, and the early followers taught this.** He says the earliest Jesus movement never confused Jesus with the one God of Israel, and that the New Testament keeps the Shema and the one God.
- **The New Testament overturns the Hebrew view of God.** He argues that the New Testament presents Jesus as an exalted Messiah while keeping Yahweh's sovereignty and the one-God framework.
- **The Battle of Armageddon.** He says the name "Armageddon" is not in the Hebrew Bible and the passage speaks of a battle for Jerusalem, not a battle at Megiddo, and that the New Testament does not use the term "Armageddon" for this passage (he mentions the gathering of armies at Armido/Megiddo).
