## Summary

Tabor asks what the War Scroll from Qumran Cave 1 (read in Geza Vermes's translation) expected for the end of the age, and how those expectations failed. He calls it "the first grand failure of apocalyptic expectations" and dates it roughly a century before Jesus, around the Maccabean period and after. He argues that the scroll's group had calculated a final 40-year period before a war between "the sons of light and the sons of darkness," and that the teacher was already dead when the text was written. He suggests the scroll was written shortly after the teacher's death, though he notes this depends on how the teacher is dated.

His main evidence is the scroll's reliance on biblical texts: Psalm 83, Ezekiel 38-39, Zechariah 14, and especially Daniel 11:40-12:3, which he reads as the template for the scroll's "final scenario" with a northern king, Kittim (which he reads as Rome), and a resurrection of the dead. He then traces the same expectations forward into Mark 13, 1 Thessalonians, 2 Thessalonians, and 1 Corinthians, arguing that early Jesus-movement and Pauline writers moved the timetable forward and reused the scroll's imagery when the Roman period failed to deliver. He presents Caligula's 41 CE order to place a statue in the Jerusalem temple as a plausible trigger for such expectations.

The talk ends as a long close reading of the scroll's battle formations, trumpet inscriptions, purity rules for the camp, and the "delivered" survivors "written in the book." Tabor says he finds the text compelling and repeatedly declines to rule on whether the scroll's battle was ever meant to be literal or was a ritual or "pantomime" expectation of divine victory.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The 40-year clock and the failed war
Tabor reads the scroll as organized around a 40-year period. He says the group "calculated a final 40-year period" before the war, and that the first seven years were already gone when the scroll's ritual calendar was described, leaving "33 years." He then reads the scroll's phases as "35 years of service," "six" years of war by the whole congregation, and "29 years" when "the war will be divided," with ten years against the sons of Ham and ten years against the sons of Japheth, the nations of Genesis 10. For Tabor, this schedule shows how confidently the group expected a worldwide military campaign. He stresses that "nothing happens," and that such failure "deflates prophetic expectations considerably."

### 2. Daniel 11 as the template
Tabor says the War Scroll's scenario is built on Daniel 11. He reads Daniel 11:30 and the "ships of Kittim" as a reference to an Antiochus Epiphanes-like figure of the Maccabean period, and treats Daniel's "until the time of the end" as a deliberate gap. He argues that the figure in Daniel 11:40 who "exalts himself" above every god must be a final ruler, because "if the end doesn't come, then you're going to have to have another candidate." He says Antiochus does not fit the description of the one who "will give no heed to the God of his fathers," and that this figure "fits what Christians later call a kind of an Antichrist figure." He identifies the "King of the North" (Seleucids) and "King of the South" (Ptolemies) as generic terms, and says the scroll reads Daniel's northern king as the one who will overrun Egypt, which he links to Rome's takeover of Egypt in 31.

### 3. Other prophetic sources for the scroll: Psalm 83, Ezekiel, Zechariah
Tabor argues the scroll's enemy list comes from Psalm 83 ("Come, let us wipe them out as a nation"), that its final battle draws on Ezekiel 38-39 (Gog of Magog, with weapons and allied peoples including "Persia," "Cush, and Put"), and that its vision of all nations gathered against Jerusalem is taken from Zechariah 14. He describes Ezekiel 37's regathering of the tribes as the context for the scroll's expectation that the twelve tribes would return. He notes that Revelation draws on the same material, and says New Testament eschatology is "utterly dependent and derivative" on Daniel's interpretive tradition.

### 4. The battle as ritual and divine intervention
Tabor describes the scroll's rules for the camp (no boys, women, or men with impurity; no sex; latrines 2,000 cubits outside camp; priestly linen clothing; exclusion of the lame, blind, or afflicted) and its trumpet inscriptions ("The mighty deeds of God shall crush the enemy"). He says the rules are "priestly" rather than military, and that the angels' presence explains the purity requirements. He compares the scroll to the walls of Jericho and to Assyrian camps undone by plague or confusion, and concludes that "they're expecting God to give them the victory." He repeatedly says "I don't think they ever did it" and that he does not picture the sons of light dying in battle.

### 5. Paul, Mark 13, and the shifting timetable
Tabor argues that the scroll's logic reappears in early Jesus-movement texts. He calls Mark 13 "a commentary on this," says the "little apocalypse" reflects "our earliest Markan community," and notes that the 70 CE destruction "wasn't fulfilled" in the way the scroll expected. He argues that Paul's 1 Corinthians advice about marriage ("you don't have time") and his expectation that the Lord would come "in the clouds" reflect the same timetable, and he presents 2 Thessalonians as "very Pauline" and possibly from Paul's school. He links Paul's argument that the "time appointed of the end is going very short" to the scroll's sense of an approaching liberation. He also draws a parallel with Matthew 26's "twelve legions of angels," which he reads as evidence that angelic battle was part of the same expectation even though Jesus did not resist arrest.

### 6. Resurrection and the "book" of the living
Tabor reads Daniel 12:1-3 as the scroll's resurrection frame: "many of those who sleep in the dust will awake," and "everyone whose name is written in the book" will be delivered. He says the scroll's group considered themselves the elect because their names were inscribed as community members. He argues that Paul's idea of the dead being raised and meeting the Lord "in the air" should be read with Zechariah 14 and the battle, not as a simple flight to heaven. He says the Dead Sea community "believed" the teacher had been taken up to heaven and would return "on the day of Melchizedek," though he presents this as an inference.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Battle of Armageddon" is a fixed final battle.** Tabor says "there is no Battle of Armageddon." He says the text speaks of an assembly at Megiddo and that the battle is called "the great day of the Battle of our God" in Revelation.
- **Paul's eschatology is purely heavenly.** Tabor argues that "everybody wants to make Paul so heavenly," and that if the Lord returns "in the air," he would then fly over with the saints to the battle, as in Zechariah 14.
- **New Testament battle language is only spiritual.** He says Christians "gave this up because it's too crazy," and that the war in the texts is treated as literal by the scroll's community.
- **The Dead Sea community was quietist.** Tabor says that the "quietist, meditative spirituality withdrawn to the desert" view is wrong for this text, which is "war."
- **The "Antichrist" figure comes from Antiochus.** He argues the description of the final ruler in Daniel 11:36-37 (no regard for the God of his fathers) does not fit Antiochus well and instead fits the later Christian "Antichrist" reading.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor treats Mark 13, Paul's 1 Thessalonians, and 1 Corinthians as sharing the same imminent-war framework, and argues that Paul's "sons of light" language is unusually close to the scroll. He does not separate Jesus's sayings from Paul's on this point.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Not discussed directly. Tabor's Jerusalem references concern the Mount of Olives and the scroll's camping route, not James.
- **Nazarenes/Ebionites/Essenes:** Tabor identifies the scroll's community with Qumran (though he says "we think" they were at Qumran) and links the "poor ones" in the scroll to the Ebionites and to Jesus's followers.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet, nonviolence:** The scroll's camp purity rules, "seven forbidden nations" from the Torah, and Deuteronomy 20 appear. Matthew 26's "put away your swords" is set against the scroll's war, and Tabor says "Jesus does not resist."
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Tabor says the scroll's group expected the kingdom to be set up by military victory, and that Mark 13 and Paul do the same. He says that when "the Kingdom of God wasn't set up... in this dramatic way of a military victory," later groups reinterpreted the failure.
- **Resurrection, afterlife, and hell:** Daniel 12 is read as the basis for resurrection "to everlasting life and some to shame and everlasting contempt." Tabor argues Paul expected the raised dead to join a battle rather than go to heaven.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Not directly addressed.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor attributes Mark 13 to "our earliest Markan community" and says Jesus's words are not known "word for word," but he does not discuss canon formation.
