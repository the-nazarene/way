## Summary

Tabor asks whether Jeremiah 17:9 really teaches that human beings are hopelessly depraved. The verse is familiar from the King James Version ("the heart is deceitful above all things and desperately wicked"), and he notes that the American Standard (1901), Revised Standard (1940s-50s) and Jewish Publication Society renderings differ only in wording ("exceedingly corrupt," "desperately corrupt," "perverse"). He argues that the problem is both translation and context. He says the Septuagint (which he dates to the second or third century BCE) renders the heart as "deep" and "very human" rather than "wicked," and that the Hebrew, read with the verses around it, describes human frailty and complexity rather than total wickedness.

His main evidence is the surrounding text. He reads Jeremiah 17:7-8 as a blessing on the one who trusts the Lord, and Jeremiah 17:10 as God searching the mind and heart to repay each person according to their ways. He then compares Romans 2 (God judging each person by their works, and Gentiles whose conscience bears witness) and cites Psalm 51, the prophets' call to repentance, and Luke 18:9-14 to argue that the Hebrew Bible and Jesus both allow for forgiveness. He sets this against Paul's argument in Romans 3-4 and Galatians 3-4 that no one is righteous, which he says he does not follow.

The talk ends with Tabor's claim that the verse has been used to support harsher views of human nature, including Calvinist "total depravity," and that it has caused a lot of guilt. He closes by telling viewers to read all of Jeremiah 17 and promises the notes as a free PDF.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The Greek and Hebrew do not say "desperately wicked"
Tabor reads the Septuagint line as "the heart is deep," with a word he glosses as "very human" (anthropos, "human being"). He compares the English idiom "I'm only human," which he says does not mean "I'm only desperately wicked." On the Hebrew, he says the word translated "desperately wicked" is "aav or aov" and means "weak or sick" or "crooked," in the sense of winding or complex, not perverse. He links it to the Hebrew word for human being (enosh, which he connects to "son of man" in Daniel) and to the "heel" image in the story of Jacob's birth in Genesis. He concludes the passage is about human mortality, frailty and complexity, which need compassion and grace.

### 2. The immediate context is a blessing
Tabor says Jeremiah 17:7-8 describes a blessed person who trusts the Lord, using the image of a tree planted by water, which he compares to Psalm 1. He reads verse 10 ("I the Lord search the mind and try the heart, to give every man according to his ways") as the sentence that follows the verse in question. For him, the passage pairs the complexity of the human heart with God's fair judgment.

### 3. Paul's Romans 2 is a close parallel, but Romans 3 reverses it
Tabor says Romans 2:6-11 uses similar language: God will repay each person by works, giving eternal life to those who seek glory and immortality through patient well-doing, and wrath to those who do evil, "the Jew first and also the Greek." He cites Romans 2:14-16, where Gentiles without the written Torah show the law's requirement "written in your heart," with conscience bearing witness. He reads this as a hypothetical standard that Paul then undercuts in Romans 3, where he says "none is righteous" and everyone has gone astray. Tabor calls the chapter 2 standard a fair way to judge the world, and he says the chapter 3 claim is the one that lost the translation's point.

### 4. The Hebrew Bible allows for forgiveness
Tabor argues that the Hebrew Bible never says any person cannot be forgiven, even someone called righteous. He points to Isaiah's "our righteousness is like a filthy rag" (which he says is better rendered as a "minous cloth," something to be cast away) and to Psalm 51, where David, after adultery and murder, says God does not desire sacrifice and wants "a broken and contrite heart." He says the prophets repeat the pattern that turning to God brings forgiveness.

### 5. Jesus in Luke 18 teaches the same
Tabor summarizes Luke 18:9-14: the Pharisee lists his good deeds, while the tax collector, "a sinner," will not raise his eyes and says "God be merciful to me a sinner." Tabor stresses that Jesus says nothing about "sacrifice, blood, cross" and declares the tax collector justified. He presents this as Jesus reflecting the Hebrew Bible's teaching that repentance brings grace.

### 6. Why it matters: the doctrinal consequences
Tabor argues that the idea of humans as "hopeless Sinners" bound for hell, needing the blood of Jesus, comes from Paul (Romans 3-4, Galatians 3-4) rather than from the Hebrew Bible or from Jesus. He says he does not go along with that reading. He notes that some forms of Christianity, including Calvinism, teach that even good deeds count for nothing. He says this has produced guilt for centuries and that Jeremiah 17:9 is often cited to support it.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jeremiah 17:9 teaches that every human heart is desperately wicked.** Tabor says the Septuagint and the Hebrew point to depth, complexity, frailty and mortality, and the verse sits within a blessing on the one who trusts God.
- **The English translations ("desperately wicked," "exceedingly corrupt") reflect the original.** He argues that the wording comes from translation choices, and that the Greek and Hebrew do not support the harsher reading.
- **The Hebrew Bible teaches that humanity is hopelessly sinful and cannot be forgiven without a sacrifice.** Tabor argues the reverse, citing Psalm 51, the prophets and Luke 18, where repentance brings forgiveness and God does not require sacrifice.
- **Paul's view of universal sinfulness is the view of the Hebrew Bible and of Jesus.** Tabor says Paul's Romans 3-4 and Galatians 3-4 argument is his own, and that it does not match the Hebrew Bible's teaching or Jesus' parable.
- **Total depravity, as in Calvinism, is grounded in Scripture.** Tabor says this teaching has been supported by this verse and has caused a lot of guilt, but he says the verse does not support it.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor's central contrast. He presents Luke 18 and the Hebrew Bible's repentance theme as the teaching he follows, and he explicitly sets Paul's Romans 3-4 and Galatians 3-4 aside.
