## Summary

Tabor asks whether Job 19:23-27, the passage behind the hymn "I Know that My Redeemer Lives," refers to Jesus and to a final resurrection and judgment, as many Christian readers have held. He argues that it does not. He compares the King James Version (KJV), the Revised Standard Version (RSV, 1952), and the Jewish Publication Society (JPS) Tanakh, and says the Christian translations inherited a reading that the Hebrew does not require. His central point is that the Hebrew word usually rendered "Redeemer" (*goel*) is a legal term for a vindicator or defense advocate, the opposite of the prosecutor (the "satan" or opposer). He also reads Job 19 alongside Job 14:7-12, where Job says a dead man does not rise, and treats Job's "resurrection-like" language as a wish, not a prediction. Tabor presents his own literal rendering, which stays close to the JPS, and argues that Job wants to see God "from my flesh" while still alive, not after his skin is destroyed.

Tabor notes that the Hebrew preposition could be read as "without my flesh," but he says "from" fits the context better. He places Job within a development toward resurrection and final judgment, citing Daniel 12 and what he calls "the Apocalypse of Isaiah" as the few later Hebrew passages that describe it. He says he has a longer series on death and afterlife, which he does not summarize here.

The talk closes with Job's encounter with God at the end of the book, where Job repents and God speaks from the position of Creator. Tabor says the book's answer to suffering is essentially that "we simply don't know," with God in control. He ends by previewing his next video on Isaiah's messianic titles, which he says are translated in a way that sounds almost Trinitarian. The talk is a solo presentation; there is no guest or host.

## Key Teachings

### 1. "Redeemer" should be read as "Vindicator"

Tabor argues the Hebrew *goel* means a legal advocate who clears a wronged person's name. He says it is "basically a legal term the opposite actually of a prosecutor," and he places this within a courtroom picture: the accusers (the friends, and the opposer in the prologue) against a defense. In his reading, Job imagines a record of his case, written permanently, that a future vindicator could read long after he is gone. He presents this as the sense the JPS reflects and as the sense he finds most natural.

### 2. Job wants to see God in his lifetime, not after death

Tabor's main translation claim concerns the phrase usually rendered "from my flesh I will see God." He says the Hebrew can be read as "without my flesh," but that "from my flesh" is clearly the context. On this reading Job says he wants to see God with his own eyes now, while still alive, "and not another." Tabor says Job is not expecting a Redeemer to raise him; he is saying that he does not want to wait until his skin is stripped and someone stands over his dust. He then says that the Book of Job does give Job what he wants: God appears to him and vindicates him.

### 3. Three possible vindications in Job

Tabor reads Job as laying out three possibilities. First, Job wants to see God in life. Second, if he dies, God might "remember" him at a set time after Job has been hidden in Sheol (the grave), a point Tabor draws from Job 14. Third, a future vindicator might read a permanent written record of his case. Tabor says the first is what Job most wants, the second is possible but unverified, and the third is what he calls "kind of" granted, since the words have ended up in the Bible.

### 4. Job 14 shows the idea of resurrection is not yet present

Tabor cites Job 14:7-12, the tree that may sprout again after being cut, but he says Job uses it to compare humans unfavorably: "man lies down and Rises not again till the heavens are no more." He says Job "wishes" that humans could rise like trees, but "he doesn't think it is" so. Tabor describes this as an early stage in a development toward resurrection and final judgment. He says the idea appears elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible only in a few passages, which he names as Daniel 12 and "the Apocalypse of Isaiah," and points to his longer series on the topic.

### 5. The "Satan" of Job is not the later devil

In the prologue, Tabor says, the figure called Satan is "the opposer," a kind of prosecutor, and one of the Elohim or members of the council of Yahweh. He is not the devil of later Christian belief. Tabor draws the connection to the courtroom reading of Job 19, where the vindicator is the reverse of this accuser.

### 6. The answer to suffering in Job is God's sovereignty

At the end of the book God speaks to Job, asking what he knows of the universe, and Job repents "in dust and ashes." Tabor says that the book's answer to the problem of evil is "we simply don't know," with God as Creator in control. He calls this a common answer that many people accept, while presenting Job as a more interesting book in its refusal to say more.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Job 19 predicts Jesus and his return to raise the dead.** Tabor says this reading depends on the KJV/RSV translation tradition; in his view the Hebrew describes a vindicator in a legal sense and Job's wish to see God during his lifetime.
- **"Redeemer" in Job means Jesus the Messiah.** Tabor argues the Hebrew *goel* is a legal term for a defense advocate, not a savior figure.
- **Job expects bodily resurrection.** Tabor says Job 14 explicitly says the dead do not rise, and that Job's hope is a wish, not a belief in resurrection.
- **The "Satan" of Job is the devil.** Tabor says the figure is a member of the divine council who plays the opposer's role, not the later Christian devil.
