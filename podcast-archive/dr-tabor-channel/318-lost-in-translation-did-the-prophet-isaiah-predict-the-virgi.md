## Summary

Tabor asks whether the prophet Isaiah predicted that the Messiah would be born of a virgin, as Matthew 1:22-23 implies by quoting Isaiah 7:14. He notes that the Apostles' Creed and later creeds affirm the virgin birth, and that many conservative Christians treat the Isaiah prophecy as settled. He also says that Genesis contains no virgin birth: the miraculous births it describes, such as Isaac's, have Abraham as the father.

Tabor argues that Isaiah does not predict a virgin birth. His main evidence is linguistic and contextual. The Hebrew word *alma* and the Greek *parthenos* (which he spells "paranos") both mean a young woman of marriageable age, and only come to mean "virgin" through the assumption that such a woman is a virgin. He then reads Isaiah 7 against the Syro-Ephraimite crisis in Judah around the 730s BCE, when King Ahaz was threatened by the kings of Israel and Aram. In his reading, the sign is that a child born in the near future will grow up to the age of moral discernment before the threat ends. He also argues that Matthew's word "fulfill" means "this is like that," not a prophecy predicting a distant event. He ends by saying that the 1952 Revised Standard Version's translation of "young woman" was not an attack on the faith, and promises another installment of the "Lost in Translation" series.

The talk is almost entirely Tabor speaking alone; no guest or host appears.

## Key Teachings

### 1. "Alma" and "parthenos" mean a young woman, not necessarily a virgin
Tabor says the Hebrew *alma* "doesn't specifically mean virgin. It means a young woman of marriageable age who of course is expected to be a virgin but it doesn't mean virgin." He says the Greek word is the same in effect: "everyone will tell you that means virgin," but in the best Greek lexicons "it's assumed that a virtuous young woman who's going to get married and conceive is going to be a virgin." On this reading, the words describe a marriageable young woman who conceives, and "virgin" is an inference from that status, not its meaning. He concludes that "young woman" is the better translation in Isaiah 7:14. He does not name the specific lexicons he consulted.

### 2. The historical context of Isaiah 7 is the Syro-Ephraimite crisis
Tabor places the chapter in the reign of Ahaz, king of Judah, "730s BCE." Two kings are attacking Jerusalem: Pekah, king of the northern Kingdom of Israel, and the king of Assyria, named "resin" in the transcript (Rezin, though he does not spell it). Tabor says Ahaz is terrified of losing the city and his family. He points readers to 2 Kings 16 and to Isaiah 7 itself for the background. Tabor's point is that Isaiah's sign has to be understood as addressed to this specific crisis.

### 3. The sign is a child who will grow up before the threat passes
Tabor reads Isaiah 7:15-16 as describing a child who eats "curds and honey" and will be old enough "to refuse the evil and choose the good." He treats this as a sign of timing: "it's how this child is going to grow up from infancy and before he's approaching adulthood and knows the difference between good and evil" the threatening kings will be gone. He ties "knows good and evil" to Genesis and Deuteronomy 1:39 to suggest an age of moral accountability, perhaps around "8 10 12 years old maybe a teenager." He says the prophecy's fulfillment is found in the removal of the two kings, quoting the phrase "the land before whose two kings you are in dread will be deserted."

### 4. Matthew's "fulfill" means "this is like that"
Tabor argues that the modern idea of fulfilled prophecy as a predicted future event does not describe what Matthew means. In his account, Matthew's formula "fulfill" means "this is like that," so that just as Isaiah's child was a sign of deliverance in Ahaz's day, Jesus's birth becomes "a saving sign" because Jesus "is going to save his people from their sins." He presents this as a reading of Matthew's own usage, not an outside theory.

### 5. Matthew 2:15 uses Hosea in the same way
Tabor gives a second example. Matthew says Joseph and Mary took Jesus to Egypt "to fulfill what was spoken by Hosea." Tabor says Hosea is clearly describing the nation of Israel coming out of Egypt, "there's no way he's talking about ancient Israel" referring to Jesus, so Matthew is again applying an earlier text to a new event. He does not give a chapter reference in the talk.

### 6. The translation controversy around the Revised Standard Version
Tabor recounts that when the Revised Standard Version appeared in 1952, with "young woman" in Isaiah 7:14, "there were book burnings primarily over this verse," because conservative critics "insisted that this was the Devil's Bible." He describes earlier English versions, the King James (1611), the American Standard Version (1901), and the English Revised Version, as reading "a virgin will conceive," and says the Geneva Bible had "virgin" too. He says the reading was not controversial until Matthew's citation became the focus of Christian doctrine. He presents the RSV rendering as the more accurate reading of the Hebrew and its context.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Isaiah 7:14 predicts the virgin birth of the Messiah.** Tabor says the verse is about a young woman in Ahaz's time and a sign tied to the defeat of two kings, not a prediction of Jesus's birth.
- **The Hebrew *alma* and Greek *parthenos* mean "virgin."** Tabor argues both words mean a young woman of marriageable age, and that "virgin" is an inference from the assumption that such a woman is chaste.
- **The Revised Standard Version removed the virgin birth from Isaiah.** Tabor argues the RSV rendering is the more accurate translation and that the objection came from doctrinal anxiety, not from the Hebrew text.
- **"Fulfilled prophecy" means a text predicting a later event.** Tabor says Matthew's "fulfill" means "this is like that," so an earlier text is applied to a new situation as a sign.
- **The virgin birth is a liberal invention undermining the faith.** Tabor closes by saying his reading is "a responsible academic historical way to read the ancient languages and the historical context of these passages."

- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** The talk shows how Matthew uses Hebrew Bible texts (Isaiah 7:14, Hosea 11:1) by applying them to Jesus through the "fulfill" formula. Tabor's claim is that this is a reuse of scripture, not a predictive citation.
