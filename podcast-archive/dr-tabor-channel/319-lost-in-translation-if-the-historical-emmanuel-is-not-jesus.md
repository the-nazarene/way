## Summary

Tabor asks whether the "Emmanuel" of Isaiah 7 is Jesus, as Christian tradition has read it since the Gospel of Matthew, or whether the passage means something else in its original setting. He argues for the second option and says the answer becomes clear if the sign is read with chapter 8 and against the Syro-Ephraimite crisis of the 730s BCE. Tabor describes King Ahaz of Judah threatened by Rezin of Syria and Pekah of Israel, and he reads the offered sign as a child whose birth would show that the two kings were no danger. He identifies the child as Isaiah's own second son, Maher-shalal-hash-baz, born to "the prophetess" in chapter 8. Tabor's main textual points are that the Hebrew word *alma* means a young woman of marriageable age, not necessarily a virgin, and that the naming of the child fits the sign, not a messianic prophecy.

Tabor presents this as a plain reading of the text in context, not as a verdict from other scholars. He also says the passage has been "christianized" and that the meaning is "actually quite simple" when read historically. The talk is a single presenter's lecture, with no guest or interview. It ends with a short excerpt of Handel's *Messiah* and a promise that chapter 9 will be covered in the next video.

## Key Teachings

### The historical setting of the sign
Tabor places the scene in the days of Ahaz, king of Judah, who is threatened by Rezin, king of Syria, and Pekah, king of Israel (the northern kingdom). He says the two kings came against Jerusalem but could not conquer it, and that Isaiah was sent to meet Ahaz with his son. Tabor reads Rezin and Pekah as "two smoldering stumps of fire brands" that are about to go out, and he uses this image to argue that the threat is minor and temporary.

### The sign: a child with a meaningful name
Tabor stresses that Isaiah's children have symbolic names. The first son, Shear-jashub, means "a remnant will return," which he treats as a sign of coming exile and return. The sign offered to Ahaz is the birth of another child, named Immanuel, meaning "God with us." Tabor says the sign is to assure Ahaz that God is with Judah and that he does not need to fear the two kings.

### Timing: the child is born in chapter 8
Tabor argues that the birth is narrated in chapter 8, not in some distant future. He says commentators "seem to ignore that" when they cite chapter 7 alone. He reads the lines about the child eating curds and honey, and about the child knowing "how to refuse the evil and choose the good," as a time frame for the removal of the two kings' threat. He says the Assyrian invasion that follows fits the period before the child is old enough to be accountable. He dates the Assyrian capture of the northern kingdom to 721 BCE, which he treats as the event the text anticipates.

### *Alma* means a young woman, not a virgin
Tabor argues that the Hebrew word *alma* (rendered "virgin" in the Greek of the Septuagint and in Matthew) means "a young woman of marriageable age." He says it does not require that she has never had children, and that it "doesn't even mean virgin." He treats the translation "virgin" as a later, theologically driven choice, and he says the Christian reading has held for about 2,000 years without checking the wider context.

### The name and the figure of Maher-shalal-hash-baz
In chapter 8 Tabor reads a second, symbolic name: Maher-shalal-hash-baz, meaning "the spoil speeds, the prey hastens." He says it refers to Damascus and Samaria being carried off before the king of Assyria. He treats the child's two names as connected: the child's name points to the coming judgment, and "Immanuel" points to God's presence with Judah in the meantime. Tabor says Isaiah then calls the child's birth "a sign and portent in Israel" from the Lord of hosts, which he takes as the key to the passage.

### The "famine of the word" and the appeal to Torah
Tabor ends with the closing verses of chapter 8. He says that because God is hiding his face, the people will have no prophetic word and will turn to mediums and wizards. He reads the "teaching" and "testimony" of Isaiah 8:16 as the Torah and the Ten Commandments, and he takes the passage as a warning that the nation will be "thrust into thick Darkness." Tabor's conclusion is that the message is about exile and disappointment, not about a messianic redeemer.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Immanuel in Isaiah 7 is Jesus or a messianic figure.** Tabor says the child is Isaiah's son, born in chapter 8, and the sign is about the removal of the threat from Syria and Israel.
- **Isaiah 7:14 is a prophecy of the virgin birth.** Tabor argues that *alma* means "a young woman of marriageable age," not "virgin," and that the translation "virgin" is a later, theologically driven rendering.
- **The sign refers to a distant future event.** Tabor says the child's birth and the Assyrian invasion are narrated within chapters 7-8 and are meant to happen in the near term.
- **Commentators read chapter 7 alone.** Tabor says the birth is in chapter 8 and that the wider context has been overlooked.
