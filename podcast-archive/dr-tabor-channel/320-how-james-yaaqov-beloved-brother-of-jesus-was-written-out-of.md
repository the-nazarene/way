## Summary

Tabor asks what can be reconstructed about James, the brother of Jesus, and why he has largely disappeared from the standard account of early Christianity. He says he suspects the disappearance is purposeful and that it reflects a rift between the Jerusalem group led by James and developing forms of Pauline Christianity. He leans toward F.C. ## Key Teachings

### 1. The brothers in Mark and the Johannine cross scene

Tabor argues from Mark 6:1-3 that the four named brothers (James, Joses, Judas, Simon) and unnamed sisters show that Jesus had a real family, and he thinks the brothers were children of Mary rather than cousins or the sons of Joseph by a prior marriage (he says the church holds these options, and he "goes with brothers"). He reads Mark's omission of the names when Luke retells the synagogue scene as "purposeful." He identifies "Mary the mother of James the younger and Joses" (Mark 15:40, 16:1) with Jesus' mother, arguing that Mark is muting the family relationship, and he says he will "conflate" these figures and call the James in question "James the kid" or "James the just." In John 19:25-27, he reads the disciple "whom he loves" as James, on the grounds that the scene describes the transfer of the mother's care to the next eldest son, a practice he compares to the role of a second son when a father has died, as happens in the current war.

### 2. The Twelve and the three "mystery" disciples

Tabor's classroom exercise lists the Twelve, noting that only Peter, Andrew, James, John, Philip, Thomas, Matthew, Bartholomew (possibly Nathanael), and Judas Iscariot can be named easily, leaving three apostles with little narrative. He suggests these three (James son of Alphaeus, Judas or Thaddaeus "of James," and Simon the Canaanite/Zealot) may be Jesus' brothers. He notes objections, since the Gospels list the brothers separately from the Twelve, and he says the traditions "either don't want you to know that or don't accept it." He cites an article by a mathematician in the Charlesworth volume arguing that the name pattern is statistically plausible. He suggests that "Alphaeus" (Greek for "replace or supplant") could refer to a brother of Joseph who married Mary after Joseph died, an idea he attributes to Eisman and develops in *The Jesus Dynasty* (his own book, which he mentions).

### 3. James in Acts and the Jerusalem leadership

Tabor points to Acts 1:14 (Mary and "his brothers" with the Twelve, with no leader named), Acts 12:17 (Peter tells the group to "tell this to James and the brothers"), and Acts 15 (James speaks at the council of about 49 CE, calling Peter "Simon," quoting Amos, and issuing the rules for Gentiles: avoiding sexual immorality, meat sacrificed to idols, what is strangled, and blood, which he links to the Noahide laws and Genesis 9). He also reads Acts 21:18, where Paul meets James and all the elders, as evidence that James is in charge. He presents Acts as using earlier material while smoothing over tensions. He describes his own earlier ignorance of James's role.

### 4. James in Paul's letters

Tabor reads Galatians 1:19 (Paul saw "James the Lord's brother" in Jerusalem), Galatians 2:9 (James, Cephas and John, "those who were reputed to be pillars," gave the right hand of fellowship), and Galatians 2:12 (men "from James" pressure Peter). He notes 1 Corinthians 9:5 (the "brothers of the Lord" travel with wives) and 1 Corinthians 15:7 (the risen appearance to James, which Paul says he received as tradition). He suggests that Romans 14 may address Jamesian sympathizers in Rome who abstain from meat and wine and observe a holy day, and that Paul calls such a person "weak" while saying everything can be done to honor the Lord. He presents this as a possibility, not a settled reading.

### 5. Hegesippus, as quoted by Eusebius

Tabor calls Hegesippus "our best source on James" and reads the account of James as "holy from his mother's womb," drinking no wine or strong drink, eating no flesh, never cutting his hair, never anointing himself or bathing, wearing only linen, and entering the temple alone, where his knees became hard "like those of a camel" from prayer for the people. Tabor says the title "the Just" (Greek *oblias*, which he links to a wall or bulwark) and the "Rock" connect him to Peter's role, and that Hegesippus's report of James's teaching, that Jesus is "the Gate of Salvation," can be read against Jesus's words in John 14:6. He says Eusebius tends to avoid calling James "bishop" in a full sense, though Tabor reads that word as meaning "overseer." Tabor's reading is that this material shows a Torah-keeping, priestly-styled leader, and he links the linen to the "linen cloth" in Mark 14 and the Last Supper.

### 6. Clement of Alexandria and the Gospel of Thomas

From Clement's *Hypotyposes* (as quoted by Eusebius), Tabor reads that after the resurrection the Lord imparted knowledge to James the Just, John and Peter, and that they chose James as bishop of Jerusalem. He understands this as a Council of Twelve headed by James, with the seventy from Luke 10:1 beneath them, and he compares it to the twelve tribal leaders and seventy elders of Moses. He reads Gospel of Thomas saying 12 ("whoever you have come, you will go to James the just, for whose sake heaven and earth came into being") as a Jewish hyperbole about the 36 righteous (lamed-vav), in which James is one of the hidden righteous whose presence sustains the world.

### 7. The Gospel of the Hebrews and the resurrection appearance

Tabor reads the Gospel of the Hebrews (via Jerome) in which James vows to eat no bread from the Last Supper until he sees the risen Lord, after which the Lord has bread brought and gives it to James, saying "my brother, eat your bread." He compares this to the Emmaus story in Luke 24 and asks whether it is an apparition, a vision, or a report by James himself. He notes that Scholars often treat this as derived from 1 Corinthians 15:7, and says he is connecting "things that line up" without claiming certainty.

### 8. The Pseudo-Clementines, the Ascent of James, and the death of James

Tabor describes the Pseudo-Clementines (*Homilies* and *Recognitions*, with a Syriac version) as a literary complex in which Peter debates a figure named Simon Magus, whom Tabor says stands for Paul. He reads the Epistle of Peter to James, which warns against false teaching, and he argues that Acts 8 and Paul's Damascus vision are being critiqued. He identifies a source he calls the *Ascent of James* (known through Epiphanius), in which James denounces the sacrifices, opposes the temple's use, and teaches baptism for the forgiveness of sins. In a narrative set seven years after the crucifixion, James is thrown down the temple steps by "a certain enemy" (which Tabor takes as Paul), and is then buried, with 5,000 people, "in Jericho." Tabor reads the burial location as the Qumran cemetery area and invites a comparison with Acts 7:2 (the burial of Stephen by "devout men") and the Dead Sea Scrolls. He also says the *Ascent* account has a Passover gathering in 37 CE, which he uses as a dating point.

### 9. Josephus and the death of James

Tabor presents Josephus's account (*Antiquities* 20) as "more historical and less theological" than Hegesippus. In it, the high priest Ananus (whom he calls Annas) exploits the interval between procurators (between Festus and Albinus) to convene a Sanhedrin, bring "the brother of Jesus called the Christ, whose name was James," and have him stoned on a charge of breaking the Torah. Tabor thinks the charge may relate to halakic interpretation, and he links it to Qumran-related texts such as the Temple Scroll and MMT, which he mentions without elaborating.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Peter as the obvious leader of the early church.** Tabor says he himself assumed Peter was "the pope" until he noticed James's role in Acts 15, Galatians 2, and the post-New Testament sources, and he argues the Jerusalem leadership was James's.
- **The Beloved Disciple is John the son of Zebedee.** Tabor argues the John 19 and 21 material points to James, and he says "nobody ever accepts this," which he presents as a minority view.
- **James the Less is a small or minor figure.** Tabor says "less" is an offensive or ambiguous rendering of the Greek (possibly "younger," "shorter," or "smaller in age") and that the James in these texts is not minor.
- **James the brother of Jesus is the same as the fisherman James.** Tabor reads Hegesippus as saying there were "many that bore the name James," and treats the Jerusalem James as a separate figure.
- **The brothers of Jesus did not believe in him.** Tabor notes John's statement that the brothers did not believe early on, and asks when James came to faith, but he says "we don't know" and offers a speculative answer.
- **The brothers are necessarily children of Joseph by an earlier marriage or cousins.** Tabor says the church holds these views but he prefers the reading that they are Mary's children.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor links Mark's Last Supper and Christology to Paul, and reads Galatians 2 and Romans 14 as evidence of a Jamesian alternative to Paul's law-free Gentile mission.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** The talk's main thread is James as the Jerusalem leader, including the council in Acts 15, Paul's visits in Acts 21 and Galatians, and James's death under Ananus.
- **The Nazarenes/Ebionites/Essenes:** Tabor refers to the "Ebionite" movement (his transcript has "ianite") and the Pseudo-Clementines, and links the Ascent's burial location to Qumran.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet and nonviolence:** Tabor reads the Acts 15 requirements, Hegesippus's ascetic diet, and the Ascent's critique of sacrifice (citing Jeremiah 7 and Isaiah 56) as a Torah-observant, sacrifice-critical movement.
- **Resurrection and afterlife:** The Gospel of the Hebrews appearance to James, the Sadducean denial of resurrection in the Ascent, and Hegesippus's report that some sects denied resurrection are the relevant material.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor notes that James reportedly called Jesus "the Savior" and "the Gate of Salvation," and that James's teaching included the Son of Man returning on the clouds.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor argues that the Johannine material may derive from James, and that Mark and Luke suppress or omit family names and leadership. He also suggests the Pseudo-Clementines show a rejected Jamesian strand.
