## Summary

Tabor asks what the Temple Scroll, the longest of the Dead Sea Scrolls, says about the Temple, and whether it describes an ideal Temple for "the last days." He treats the scroll as a rewritten Torah built largely from Deuteronomy, Exodus and Leviticus, but with much material that is not in the Torah, especially on ritual purity, the Temple building, and the holiness of Jerusalem. He reads it in the first person as God's own speech.

His main claims: Yigael Yadin concluded that the scroll describes an earthly, not heavenly, Temple. The scroll's architectural detail (for example, a courtyard measurement of 120 cubits, and a ditch 100 cubits wide, which he works out to roughly 150 feet) goes beyond Exodus and so, in Tabor's view, shows a plan that people believed could be built. He argues, following Shiffman, that the Qumran community itself may be the "sanctuary of men" that stands in for the Temple until the final one is built. He reads the scroll's laws on a king, the army, and the purity of Jerusalem as a program for a restored and holy state. He also dates the scrolls to the first century BC, which he says is his own view, not a consensus.

The talk moves through purity and sex laws, the "three nets of Belial" from Isaiah 24 (quoted in the Damascus Document), and the king's restrictions (no multiple wives, no horses, no silver and gold). In its last section Tabor compares the Temple Scroll's ritual rules to Gospel accounts of Jesus's burial, arguing that Joseph of Arimathea's rapid burial fits Jewish purity law. ## Key Teachings

### The Temple Scroll as a plan for an end-time Temple
Tabor says the scroll speaks in the first person as God ("I'm God," in his paraphrase), unlike the Torah's framing, and he links this to the first-person voice of Moses in Deuteronomy. He cites Yadin's view that the scroll is not an ideal heavenly Temple but an earthly one "of the last days." He contrasts it with Solomon's Temple and with Herod's Temple, which he says Herod did not build according to it. He compares it with Ezekiel 40-48, which he says has often been drawn as a Temple unlike Herod's, and with 1 Chronicles 28, where David gives Solomon a written plan. Tabor frames the scroll as a set of building instructions that the Torah itself does not supply.

### The sanctuary of men and the Qumran community
Drawing on the Community Rule and the Damascus Document, Tabor reads the phrase about building "a sanctuary of men" as a reference to a community that offers "the works of the law" while the true Temple is absent. He cites Shiffman for the view that "the Kuman Community itself is the sanctuary." He admits the Qumran group may not have offered sacrifices there, but says they still believed in sacrifice. He is clear that they were not Ebionite-style opponents of sacrifice, and he argues that the Temple Scroll itself affirms sacrificial worship.

### Purity laws and the holiness of Jerusalem
Tabor reads the scroll's rules on sexual intercourse, nocturnal emission, menstrual blood, and corpses as ritual purity rules, not hygiene. He links them to anthropological work on "signs of mortality" (sex, blood, death), which he says are found across cultures. He notes that the scroll seems to extend the Temple's holiness outward: every four cities must have a burial area, and lepers and others must be kept outside. He compares the table of a Jewish Sabbath meal to a sanctuary, and he suggests the Pharisees began this kind of extension. He also questions whether the scroll's "city of the sanctuary" can mean all of Jerusalem, and concludes it probably means the Temple compound.

### The king, the army, and the law of the king
Tabor reads the scroll's king as an administrator ("think of it as an executive"), not the final Messiah of Isaiah 11, and he says the king "is a messiah" in a lower-case sense. The king may not take many wives, horses, or great wealth, and must consult the high priest before war, as the scroll describes. Tabor notes that this passage is based on Deuteronomy 17 and links it to his argument that David did not read the hidden law. He says that Deuteronomy 29 (he is unsure of the verse) refers to a second covenant, and that the scroll's "law of the king" was hidden until Zadok.

### Isaiah 24 and the three nets of Belial
Tabor reads the Damascus Document's quotation of Isaiah 24 (the "Apocalypse of Isaiah") as describing three nets of Belial: fornication, riches, and profaning the Temple. He calls Isaiah 24-27 an outline of the end of the age, and says Paul quotes it in 1 Corinthians 7. He compares the Qumran interpreters' method of linking Hebrew phrases across texts to David Kish, the Branch Davidian leader. He then links the Damascus Document's teaching against a second wife while the first is alive to Jesus's teaching on divorce, which he says quotes Genesis's male-and-female creation principle. He asks whether any other rabbi taught this, and answers that Jesus did.

### Jesus's rushed burial and Jewish purity law
Tabor argues that the Gospel of John's account of a Sabbath or "high day" fits the scroll's purity rules. He says that Joseph of Arimathea put Jesus in an nearby, unfinished tomb because the feast was approaching and a body in a house would defile it. He calls this first burial a "stashing" of the body, to be completed after the feast. He says the Romans often left corpses up, but that in Jerusalem during Passover they had permission to take them down "according to the texts." He disagrees with Crossan and Ehrman, whom he says see the claim that bodies were left to rot as legend. He places Jesus's death on a Thursday in the year 30, which he gives as "my view."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Herod's Temple followed Ezekiel's plan:** Tabor says Herod did not follow the Temple Scroll or Ezekiel 40-48, and that Herod's Temple looked more like a Hellenistic and Roman temple.
- **The Qumran community rejected sacrifice like the Ebionites:** Tabor says they were "not ebionites." He argues that they believed in sacrifice, even if they did not sacrifice at Qumran.
- **The Romans always left crucified bodies to rot:** Tabor disputes Crossan's and Ehrman's view, arguing that Passover permission allowed the bodies to be taken down, and that the Gospels reflect this.
