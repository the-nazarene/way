## Summary

Tabor asks whether Isaiah 9:6 (9:5 in the Hebrew numbering) calls the coming Davidic Messiah "Mighty God" and "Everlasting Father," as many Christian translations and Handel's *Messiah* have assumed. He presents this as the latest entry in his "Lost in Translation" series, arguing that the traditional reading is a mistranslation. He also says the passage is a rare case of a Hebrew Bible text that could be read as applying divine titles to the Messiah himself.

His answer is that the four names ("Wonderful Counselor," "Mighty God," "Everlasting Father," "Prince of Peace") celebrate God and God's acts, and that the child is named by God rather than being identified as God. His evidence has three main parts: a grammatical reading of the verbs ("has been born," "has been given," "one has named his name"), which he says is supported by the Hermeneia commentary, the Dead Sea Scrolls reading and the 1917 Jewish Publication Society translation; a survey of how Isaiah names children, where a name often states what God does; and a comparison of the same divine epithets elsewhere in Isaiah (10:20-21, 28:29), Jeremiah 32:18, and Isaiah 11:2, where the Messiah's wisdom and might come from the Spirit of Yahweh. He reads the passage in its context in Isaiah 7 and 8, where the child sign follows the threat of Assyria and the hiding of God's face, and Isaiah 9 opens with the darkness breaking.

The talk ends with a short look ahead. Tabor says he will move to New Testament passages in the next part of the series, which he says have been similarly mistranslated. He describes the Isaiah 9 passage as one of ten Hebrew Bible texts about the coming Davidic Messiah, and points viewers to his separate series on those ten passages.

## Key Teachings

### Grammar: the verbs describe a completed act, and God is the one naming
Tabor reads "to us a child is born and to us a son is given" as a finished action ("has been born," "has been given"). He says the phrase "his name will be called" is not truly passive in the Hebrew, and renders it as "one has called his name," which he takes to mean God. He cites the Hermeneia commentary, the Dead Sea Scrolls and the 1917 Jewish Publication Society translation as supporting readings in which the names are attributed to God's action, not to the child's own identity.

### How Isaiah names children: names state what God does
He argues that names in Isaiah usually say something about God or what God will do. He gives "Isaiah" (Yahweh saves), "Hezekiah" (Yahweh strengthens) and "Emmanuel" (God is with us) as examples. He describes Shear-jashub ("a remnant shall return") and Maher-shalal-hash-baz (the spoil speeds, the prey hastens) as sentence-length prophetic names that announce future events. On this view, the four names in Isaiah 9:6 are a declaration about God, read out with praise, and not a claim about the child's nature.

### Parallel uses of the same epithets: they describe God
Tabor sets the Isaiah 9:6 titles beside other Hebrew Bible passages. "Mighty God" (El Gibbor) appears in Isaiah 10:21, where a remnant returns "to the Mighty God," and in Jeremiah 32:18, which he cites as "Great and Mighty God, whose name is the Lord of hosts." "Wonderful in counsel" appears in Isaiah 28:29 with reference to Yahweh. He notes that Isaiah 10:20 ("lean upon Yahweh") is clearly about God, and that "Everlasting Father" fits Yahweh as "Rock of Ages," an eternal one. He also connects the name Yahweh to the verb "to be."

### The Messiah as bearer of God's Spirit, not as God
Drawing on Isaiah 11:2, Tabor says the Messiah will have the Spirit of the Lord resting on him, giving him wisdom, understanding, counsel and might, and the fear of Yahweh. In this reading the Messiah's attributes are derived from God's Spirit and do not make him God. He summarizes the child's name as a "Divine messenger of Mighty counsel," so that the Messiah represents God and reflects God's attributes in judging with justice and righteousness.

### Context: Isaiah 7, 8 and 9 as a sequence
Tabor reads Isaiah 7 (a young woman will conceive; the child is "God with us"), Isaiah 8 (the child named Maher-shalal-hash-baz; the hiding of God's face; the threat of Assyria, and then of Babylon in the sixth century BCE) and Isaiah 9 (darkness broken in Zebulun and Naphtali, then the Davidic child bringing peace, justice and righteousness) as one connected argument. He says Isaiah 9 celebrates what Isaiah 11 later describes, and that Jews have read the passage as a messianic prophecy for centuries.

### Translating "Prince" and "ruler"
Tabor argues that the Hebrew *sar* means "ruler," "leader" or "champion," not the English distinction between a king and a prince who will become king. He prefers "ruler of Peace." He presents this as part of why the traditional English rendering misleads readers into a royal-heir picture and into thinking God is not a prince.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Popular belief:** Isaiah 9:6 calls the Messiah "Mighty God" and "Everlasting Father," so the Hebrew Bible itself identifies the Messiah as God. **Correction:** Tabor argues the names are God's names, given by God and celebrating God's attributes, and that the Messiah bears them only as God's representative and through God's Spirit.
- **Popular belief:** The English "Prince of Peace" is a title for a royal heir who will later become king. **Correction:** Tabor says *sar* means ruler, leader or champion, and prefers "ruler of Peace."

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Who Jesus thought he was (christology):** The talk's central question is whether the Hebrew Bible itself treats the Davidic Messiah as God. Tabor argues it does not; the Messiah is a ruler who carries God's Spirit and reflects God's attributes. He does not discuss Jesus directly in this talk, and says the New Testament will be the subject of a later part of the series.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Tabor reads Isaiah 9:7 as describing a Davidic ruler who will establish and uphold the throne "with justice and righteousness" on earth. This is a messianic kingdom reading, not a claim about Jesus.
