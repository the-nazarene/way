## Summary

Tabor asks whether Mark 7:19 really has Jesus declaring "all foods clean," as English readers have long concluded. He argues that the verse is badly translated and that the joke in Jesus's saying has been missed. His main claim is that the Greek clause usually rendered "thus he declared all foods clean" is a translator's or editor's commentary, not part of Jesus's words. Jesus's saying in Mark 7:15 concerns what passes through the body and goes out into the latrine, which Tabor reads as a toilet joke that undercuts the handwashing tradition the Pharisees and scribes are defending.

Tabor builds the case from context. He describes the dispute over ritually unwashed hands at a meal, explains the difference between ritual defilement (linked in the Hebrew Bible mainly to blood, sex and death, and to going up to the Temple) and the dietary restrictions on animals in Deuteronomy 14 and Leviticus 11, and compares several English translations (King James, Revised Standard, American Standard of 1901, New American Standard). He also points to the private explanation in Mark 7:17-23, where the disciples ask about the saying and Jesus rebukes them, and to the list of thirteen vices that follows. He adds that Matthew, which he takes to follow Mark, drops the "declared all foods clean" clause altogether.

The talk ends as a teaser for the next episode of the "Lost in Translation" series and for a parallel series on how to detect interpolations (later scribal additions) in New Testament manuscripts, including ones that appear in every surviving copy.

## Key Teachings

### The handwashing dispute is about tradition, not dirt
Tabor says the question in Mark 7 is why Jesus's followers eat with hands not ceremonially washed. He compares it to the Orthodox Jewish handwashing before a Sabbath meal, and says the issue is ritual, not hygiene. In his reading, Jesus answers that the critics have added human customs (he gives the Hebrew term *minhag*) that have nothing to do with the intent of God's commandments.

### Jesus's saying is a riddle about the body's process
Tabor treats Mark 7:14-15 as a riddle ("whatever goes into a person does not defile them but what comes out of a person that's what defiles") that Jesus leaves unexplained. He argues that the riddle has a concrete answer in the body's digestive process: what enters from outside goes into the stomach and then out into the latrine, so it cannot reach the heart and cannot defile. He reads the wordplay on the katharo root (the group of words for cleansing) as part of the joke, saying "he's in effect making a kind of a toilet joke here." He paraphrases the point as: whatever worry you have about food is handled by the body, and "go look in your toilet because that cleanses all foods."

### The "declared all foods clean" clause is a commentary, not Jesus's words
Tabor's central textual claim is that Mark 7:19b ("thus he declared all foods clean") is a later editorial addition. He says "it's understood by translators to be something the author inserted as a commentary not part of Jesus declaration." He shows how English versions make this worse: the King James's "purging all meats" and "into the draught" are closer to the Greek, while the American Standard Version (1901) and New American Standard add "thereby" or "thus he declared" and italicize or parenthesize, which cuts the clause off from the preceding sentence about passing into the latrine. He observes that the Greek word translated "meat" in this context means food in general, so the passage is about eating a meal, not about which animals are permitted.

### Ritual defilement differs from dietary prohibition
Tabor explains that ritual defilement in the Hebrew Bible concerns blood, sex and death ("normal life of mortal human beings"), and that it does not make a person sinful or dirty. He says this has "absolutely" nothing to do with meat, and he contrasts it with the animal laws. For land animals he gives the criteria of chewing the cud and having a split hoof, explains pork's exclusion by its digestion, and gives fish criteria (fins and scales). He cites Deuteronomy 14 and Leviticus 11 as the main lists. He notes that some interpreters link the bird and animal prohibitions to health, and that he is willing to grant "a case for that," but he distinguishes the category of abominations (Hebrew *to'evah*) from defilement that requires purification when going to the Temple.

### The thirteen vices as Jesus's ethic
Tabor says that Mark 7:21-22 lists thirteen things that defile from within (including theft, murder, adultery, coveting, deceit, envy, slander, pride and foolishness), and he calls this "a nice little set of Ethics" that reflects the character of a person. He stresses that these are inner moral failings, which is why he reads the riddle's moral point as "what comes out of the heart" matters more than what goes in.

### Mark's priority and Matthew's silence
Tabor states that Matthew follows Mark and that he holds Mark to be the earliest Gospel, a view he says he has argued at length on his channel. He notes that when Matthew rewrites the passage, he omits "thus he declared all foods clean," and leaves the toilet image to imply the conclusion. He presents this as a sign that Matthew's editor read the passage as a joke rather than as a declaration of dietary freedom.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Popular belief:** Mark 7:19 shows that Jesus declared all foods clean and abolished the food laws. **Correction:** Tabor argues the clause "thus he declared all foods clean" is a later commentary inserted by an editor, and that Jesus's own saying is a riddle about the body's digestive process, not a dietary ruling.
- **Popular belief:** Ritual defilement means dirt or sin, as in "your hands are dirty, wash them." **Correction:** Tabor says the Hebrew Bible's ritual defilement concerns blood, sex and death in the context of Temple access, is a normal part of mortal life, and carries no sin, and that the handwashing dispute is a matter of tradition, not hygiene.
- **Popular belief:** The Hebrew "meat" in the English Bible means flesh. **Correction:** Tabor says the Greek word for food in this passage is general and refers to a meal, not specifically to animal flesh, so the passage is not about which animals may be eaten.
