## Summary

Tabor opens by reading Walsh's bio and then frames the difference: a theological approach "is not pejorative" but "is going to ask different questions," and Tabor argues that reading ancient texts back through a developed, present-day religious identity risks treating them as confirmation of what the reader already believes ([01:33]). ## Key Teachings

### Historical and theological approaches ask different questions
Tabor sets up the core distinction. A theological reading that begins from a developed present-day faith tends to find that faith reflected back in the text. A historical approach asks what the early movement looked like in its own setting. Walsh says the aim is to understand Christianity, which emerged from Jewish sources and writers, "as a new religious movement but a ancient Mediterranean religion," with its social formation examined in context ([03:06]). Both speakers stress that this is not an attack on faith.

### Romans as an appeal to a community Paul had not founded
Walsh calls Romans Paul's latest letter and says he had not yet been to Rome. She describes it as a presentation of his "best foot forward," meant to win sponsorship for a visit to Rome. In her reading, the Roman assembly is a group of "Jesus curious" people, not a Pauline church that accepts his authority ([04:38]). She says this challenges the assumption that every Pauline letter addresses an established community that treats Paul as final authority.

### Corinthians and Galatians show social tension and interests
Walsh says that Corinthians shows "little cohesiveness" in the community and that Galatians shows Paul angry at the start, with visible tensions among different social spheres. She argues that political, economic and social interests should be considered alongside religious ones when reconstructing how these groups formed ([06:14]).

### Paul's philosophical and cultural setting
Walsh says Paul's Greek is shaped by Middle Platonism and familiarity with Stoicism, and that his terms are sometimes translated in ways not found in other Greek philosophical texts. She describes Jewish scripture as ancient and interesting to outsiders, and notes that Rome's expansion produced renewed interest in colonized peoples' religions, including Egyptian ones. She says that *pneuma* read as a physical substance in Stoic cosmology explains its function in Paul and the Gospels better than a later Trinitarian "personified actor" model ([20:11]; [21:46]; [23:19]). She adds that Mark, too, does not fit that later model.

### Antiquity as a competitive claim: Philo and Josephus
Walsh and Tabor discuss how Philo and Josephus argued that Jewish wisdom predates Greek philosophy. Tabor describes Josephus's *Antiquities* as running from Genesis 1:1 to Alexander the Great, and Walsh says such writers treated antiquity itself as a claim to authority. Walsh also says Paul was not from the Alexandrian elite of Philo's type, and suggests he was closer to "middle management" in social standing ([10:51]; [12:25]).

### Paul and "conversion," and faithfulness vs. faith in Jesus
Walsh says Paul's letters do not use "conversion" as a concept, and that Acts presents a later picture. She also cites a point Stanley Stowers made about the subjective and objective genitive: Paul writes of the faithfulness of Abraham and Moses, but translations often render Jesus's "faithfulness" as "faith in Jesus," which she calls a theological decision not present in the Greek ([13:57]; [23:19]).

### 1 Corinthians 15 and Paul's cosmology
When asked for a favorite passage, Walsh names 1 Corinthians 15, noting Paul's references to the moon, stars and heavens and the "third heaven," and linking this to her Harvard Theological Review article. She also mentions Romans 8, where creation is described as subjected to "futility," and connects this to Stoic ideas of cosmic decline. She says Paul appears to believe the cosmos will be saved, and that middle Platonists placed the soul's destination in the sphere of the moon ([26:27]; [28:01]; [29:34]).

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Historical criticism is a negative project that destroys faith.** Tabor says students commonly hold this view, and both speakers reject it as a framing; they describe historical work as adding new insight ([15:31]; [24:52]).
- **Paul "converted" to Christianity, as a fixed event.** Walsh says conversion is not Paul's concept, and that Paul saw the God of Israel acting in the Messiah within a Jewish framework that now included Gentiles ([13:57]).
- **Pauline letters address cohesive churches that accept Paul's authority.** Walsh argues that Romans was written to a community Paul had not founded, and that Corinthians and Galatians show tension and fragmentation ([04:38]; [06:14]).
- **Judaism was a monolithic relic superseded by Jesus.** Walsh says the movement was flowing out of Jewish traditions, even where groups disagreed about who the Messiah was ([24:52]).
- **"Faithfulness of Jesus" simply means faith in Jesus.** Walsh says the Greek allows the objective and subjective readings, and that rendering it as "faith in Jesus" is a theological choice ([23:19]).
- **Pneuma is a personified actor in a Trinitarian sense in Paul and Mark.** Walsh argues that the Stoic physical-substance reading explains these texts better ([21:46]; [23:19]).
