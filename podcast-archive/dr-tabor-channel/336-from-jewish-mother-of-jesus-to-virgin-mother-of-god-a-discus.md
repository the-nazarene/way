## Summary

Tabor, a retired professor of religious studies, is interviewed by host Shirley Paulson about his forthcoming book *The Lost Mary* (Knopf, due fall 2025, per the description). The talk was recorded as an August 2024 "Bible and Beyond" discussion and includes a live audience question session. Tabor says he asks why Mary, the mother of Jesus, has been so thoroughly lost from the historical record, and he argues she should be understood as a Jewish woman of her own time, not only as a theological figure. His answer rests on four kinds of evidence: the Gospels (chiefly Mark, with John and a verse in Acts), Paul's letters, archaeology on Mount Zion and at Sepphoris, and "educated imagination" to fill gaps. He proposes that Mary was a teacher to her children and a matriarch of the Jerusalem community, that Luke's genealogy traces her line, and that the "Beloved Disciple" of John was her son James.

Tabor attributes the loss of Mary's story partly to Paul's dominance in the New Testament and partly to later doctrinal development (perpetual virginity, sinlessness, and the Immaculate Conception). Much of the talk is Tabor reasoning from thin evidence, and he says so repeatedly, for example that "I can't prove it" when attributing James's teaching to his mother. The talk ends with Paulson and Tabor agreeing it has raised more questions than it settled, and with Tabor promoting his book, documentary plans, and his online community.

## Key Teachings

### Mary as a Jewish woman in her historical setting

Tabor begins with chronology. He places Mary in Nazareth around 4 BC, after Herod the Great's death, nursing an infant Jesus, and describes the revolts that followed, including the Roman suppression by Varus and the burning of Sepphoris, the urban capital of Galilee. He contrasts the village of Nazareth (which he says had perhaps 200 people, from archaeology) with the modern city and says Mary may have seen Sepphoris burning from the hill above Nazareth. He presents this to show that Mary lived through real violence, and that she later lost sons James and Jesus to violent deaths. He says she may have lived past 70, but that "we just don't know when she died." He also says Joseph seems to disappear after Jesus is 12, leaving Mary as a single mother of seven children, and he uses this to argue that she needed courage and skill to survive.

### Mary as teacher, and the "educated imagination" method

Tabor admits his method goes beyond the texts. He describes four sources: his "educated imagination," New Testament texts, later texts, and archaeology. From the Gospels he weighs Mark as the earliest, with John as a major but later source and Acts 1 as a single verse placing Jesus's mother and brothers with the disciples. He argues that Jesus's sayings on love of enemies and turning the other cheek (as opposed to the rougher sayings in Proverbs, which he says also circulate) must have been taught in the home. He points to material shared by Matthew and Luke that does not come from Mark, which he accepts as real whatever it is called ("Q" or otherwise), and he says the letter of James reads like the Sermon on the Mount. He concludes that Mary may have shaped these teachings. He acknowledges that critics will call this speculation.

### Mary's marginalization and the Pauline shift

Tabor argues that Mary and James were pushed aside by the dominance of Paul. He notes that Paul barely mentions James (in Galatians, he describes meeting James briefly and calls the "pillars" meaningless to him), and that Paul's emphasis on the heavenly Christ, expressed in the line "we no longer know Jesus after the flesh," left no room for the family. He says that Paul's thirteen letters, with the Epistle to the Hebrews sometimes attributed to him, overshadow James, who is mentioned once in Acts 15 without introduction at the Jerusalem council, where James "stood up" and gave the decision on Gentiles. Tabor says Mary's disappearance was "deliberate," but he frames the motive as a theological one, not a conspiracy.

### James as the Beloved Disciple and Mary in Jerusalem

Tabor proposes that James, not John, is the "Beloved Disciple" in the Gospel of John, and that the John tradition about Mary going to Ephesus "makes no sense." He argues that Jesus, at about 30, passed leadership and care of his mother to his younger brother, and that the John Gospel's eyewitness detail (for example the Pavement where Pilate's praetorium was) would fit a Jerusalem source. He describes Mount Zion, where Bargil Pixner's excavations (which Tabor says he has posted as PDFs on his website) identified a first-century house church on the site traditionally linked to the Last Supper, with later Byzantine, Crusader, and modern Dormition Abbey structures built over the site of Mary's death. Tabor cites Stephen Schumacher's view that the death traditions for Mary are located in Jerusalem alone. He also describes Mary as the "hostess" of the Jesus movement and the matriarch of a house at Pentecost, as shown in later paintings.

### Mark 3:31-35 is not a rejection of the family

When asked directly, Tabor reads the scene in which Jesus says his mother and brothers are those who hear and do God's will as a crowded-house incident. He argues that Jesus is telling his family to leave because the house is full, and that the words "all of you who listen and hear are my mother and brothers" are positive, not a repudiation of blood family. He reads Mark 6:3 as listing Mary's own children (James, Joses, Simon, Judas, and sisters), and says the phrase "son of Mary" is the key verse for his book. He describes the later Catholic and Eastern attempts to read these as cousins or children of Joseph by an earlier wife as attempts to protect Mary's sanctity, and he calls the perpetual virginity idea a later imposition on Judaism's emphasis on procreation (citing the commandment to be fruitful and multiply, as he locates it in Genesis).

### Luke's genealogy as Mary's line

Tabor argues that Luke's genealogy is Mary's, while Matthew's is Joseph's. He says the early church held this view, and that critical scholars of the 19th century rejected it as harmonization. He points to Levitical and priestly names in the list (including Eleazar and Matthan) and to Nathan, son of David, to argue that Jesus carried both priestly and Davidic descent through Mary. He presents this as a point his colleagues will "slam," and says DNA evidence might one day test it.

### Virgin, young woman, and Matthew's Joseph story

On Isaiah 7:14, Tabor says the Hebrew word means a young woman of marriageable age, not necessarily a virgin, and that Matthew's use of the Greek parthenos inherits the translation question. He cites Raymond Brown's *The Birth of the Messiah* as a careful study that does not let dogma control the evidence. He reads Matthew's account as Joseph's dilemma, with the dream as possible grounds for marriage to a woman carrying another man's child, and he suggests Joseph may never have announced an adoption if no one knew the father. He compares this to the divinely ordained birth of Isaac in Genesis, where the language also suggests an unusual conception. He describes the Proto-Evangelium of James as late and legendary, containing a story of Mary's birth and Anna's pregnancy, and says he does not think it preserves much history beyond the wealthy Sepphoris household of Joachim and Anna.

### Original sin, Immaculate Conception and the sinless mother

Tabor says the Virgin birth, in the early church, meant both virgin conception and birth, and that the Immaculate Conception developed to make Mary sinless so that she could bear a sinless child. He argues that "original sin" as an inherited guilt rests on a misreading of Romans 5, where, he says, the next verse ("all have sinned") is ignored. He does not date the doctrine's development, and says that he is "not up on the history" of it and that Augustine may not be the first to teach it. He associates the later emphasis on bodily purity with Neoplatonism and sees women as the first casualties of that view.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mary as merely a holy figure with no ordinary life.** Tabor argues she was a Jewish woman, mother, and survivor of violence, with a family, a household and a role in the early movement.
- **Mark 3:31-35 as Jesus rejecting his family.** Tabor reads the scene as a crowded-house moment, with "my mother and brothers" a positive statement of spiritual kinship.
- **John 2 as a negative portrayal of Mary.** Tabor argues the Greek "woman" is respectful, not pejorative, and that Mary is in charge of the event.
- **Isaiah 7:14 as proving a virgin birth.** Tabor says the Hebrew term means a young woman, and the Greek translation in Matthew inherits the question.
- **The Immaculate Conception as the same as the Virgin birth.** Tabor says the two are distinct, and that the Immaculate Conception concerns Mary's own conception and sinlessness.
- **Original sin as inherited guilt from Adam.** Tabor argues this rests on a misreading of Romans 5 and does not reflect Paul's point.
- **Luke and Matthew's genealogies as harmonized.** Tabor argues that the two lists reflect two different family lines, Mary's and Joseph's.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor says Paul's focus on the heavenly Christ and his "apocalyptic celibacy" pushed the family out of the story, and he contrasts Paul's emphasis with the family-centered teaching he attributes to Mary.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor argues James was the leader of the movement after Jesus, the Beloved Disciple, and the decision-maker in Acts 15. He describes Jerusalem as the center of Mary's life and death.
- **The Desposyni (families of the Lord):** Tabor mentions the group and their stories, including Jude's grandsons and the crucifixion of Simon, as evidence that the family continued in Jerusalem.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Tabor says Jesus proposed an egalitarian society where wealth is shared and social barriers fall, and that Jesus expected the kingdom to break into history.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor's discussion of Mark as earliest, Matthew and Luke as later, and John as major but later, and his treatment of the Proto-Gospel of James as legend, bear directly on how the canon developed.
