## Key Teachings

### 1. Locative versus utopian religion (Smith, via Tabor)
Tabor reads Smith's Britannica article as describing two modes of ancient religiosity. The first is "locative": the archaic religions of the Mediterranean and ancient Near East, centered on a cosmos "conceived as a vast network of relationships" in which each part "must know its place and fulfill its appointed role." Tabor illustrates this with the Gilgamesh Epic, where Gilgamesh is told to accept his mortality and "be satisfied with your place with your lot." The second is "utopian," which Smith associates with the Hellenistic period from roughly 300 BCE. Tabor says the older structures of "Celebration and Conformity to place" no longer answered the questions of the time, and a "mood" of "escape and Liberation from place" took over.

### 2. Dualism and the Hellenistic salvation pattern
Tabor reports Smith's view that "the characteristic religion of the helenistic period was dualistic." Humans were "Strangers In a Strange Land" on a low material earth beneath expanded planetary spheres, and they sought to ascend to a world of freedom. Tabor says this is what people today mean by going "to heaven when they die," and he adds that Christianity "was taken over essentially by this view." He treats this as a point to test, not settled fact.

To illustrate the pattern, Tabor cites the Gnostic Theodotus, whose "fundamental questions of Life" include "who were we" and "what is rebirth." He also quotes a line attributed to a Greek philosopher (the name is garbled in the captions) about having been "a fish a bird and now a man," and an unnamed sailor's epitaph from Marseilles in which the dead sailor says he belongs to the company "among the choruses of stars" because he "obtained a God for my guide."

### 3. Three views of Paul
Tabor distinguishes three readings of Paul:
- **Juristic:** the core is how sins are forgiven. Tabor associates this with Luther and partly with Augustine, and describes students who ask whether he is sure he would "absolutely go to heaven" and who recite "four spiritual laws."
- **Participatory:** Schweitzer (in *The Mysticism of the Apostle Paul*) and E. P. Sanders (in *Paul and Palestinian Judaism*) center Paul on "being in Christ" and on participation in a "Cosmic process."
- **Cosmic:** Tabor credits Smith and Morton Smith (his outside reader on Tabor's Chicago dissertation on Paul) with the view that Paul's salvation extends to all creation.

Tabor's own question, "Justified for what," pushes past the juristic reading toward the purpose of salvation.

### 4. Cosmic redemption grounded in Genesis
Tabor says Paul, as a Jew, grounded salvation in "the Book of Genesis chapter 1" and the Garden of Eden in "chapters 2 and three." On this reading, salvation is not "pulling you out of the material world" but "transforming the material world into its New Birth." Tabor says the creation is "being redeemed from what Paul calls its bondage to Decay," and promises to examine those passages later. The video does not cite a specific verse for that phrase.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Popular belief:** Paul's core message is that sins are forgiven through Jesus' blood so that individuals go to heaven. **Tabor's correction:** this is the juristic reading, and it may not be Paul's center. Schweitzer, Sanders, and Smith put the center in being "in Christ" and in a cosmic process, with the whole creation redeemed.
- **Popular belief (implied):** Christian salvation means escaping the material world to go to heaven at death. **Tabor's correction:** this resembles Hellenistic dualism, which he says Christianity absorbed. He argues that Paul's salvation is cosmic and transformative, grounded in Genesis, not escape from matter.
