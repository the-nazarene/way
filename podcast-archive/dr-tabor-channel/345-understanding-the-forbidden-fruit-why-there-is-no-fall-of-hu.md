## Summary

Tabor asks whether the Genesis story of the forbidden fruit in Eden, the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, really describes a "fall" of humankind from God's favor, as Christian theology has long read it. He says it does not. He argues that the Hebrew points to coming of age rather than disobedience, and he supports this with translation notes on the phrase "knowledge of good and evil," the Genesis 3 narrative, and comparisons with Deuteronomy 1:39 and Isaiah 7. The talk is delivered by Tabor alone, as a segment of his "Lost in Translation" series, and it previews his own translation of Genesis, which he says has over a thousand notes.

His central claim is that "know" (Hebrew *yada*) means discerning or having experienced something, not merely knowing it intellectually, and that "good and evil" is better rendered "good and bad," meaning right and wrong decisions. On this reading, the couple's eyes being opened and their recognition of their nakedness marks the transition from childhood innocence to adult moral awareness. Tabor describes the expulsion from Eden as the loss of access to the tree of life, not an act of wrathful punishment. He reads the "you shall die" warning as a loss of access to eternal life, since the couple did not physically drop dead. He ties this to the way children "who this day have no knowledge of Good and Evil" are described in Deuteronomy 1:39, and to the child in Isaiah 7 who grows to refuse evil and choose good. He ends by noting that Jews and Christians read the same Genesis text, and that a Christian reading of Revelation 21-22 is a separate later interpretation.

The talk ends with Tabor inviting viewers to his Genesis translation for the full notes and promising further installments on other passages. Because it is a single presenter's argument with few cited sources and no engagement with other scholars, the interpretation should be treated as Tabor's position, not as established consensus.

## Key Teachings

### The phrase "knowledge of good and evil" means discernment and experience
Tabor argues the phrase is "probably better translated as Discerning or knowing good and bad rather than good and evil," since "evil sounds a little too sinister." He calls it a matter of right and wrong decisions ("right turn wrong turn"). He says "to know" in Hebrew means to discern or to have experienced something, and he points to Genesis 4 usage, where "Adam knew his wife Eve and she conceived," as proof that the verb carries experiential meaning. (The talk does not give a chapter or verse for that example.)

### The eating is coming of age, not a failed test of obedience
Tabor reads Genesis 3 as showing that the couple's eyes were opened and they recognized they were naked, which he takes as a change in perception, mainly about human sexuality. He says "nothing is said about" physical bodily change but describes their realization as coming of age. He rejects the reading of the command as a simple test: "it's not so much that they've failed a test of obedience but rather that they've grown up and come of age." He also notes that the warning against touching the tree is a stricter addition made by the serpent, though he says he sees "no big difference" in practice.

### Deuteronomy 1:39 uses the same idea for children coming of age
Tabor's main comparative text is Deuteronomy 1:39, which speaks of the children born during the forty years in the wilderness. He reads Moses as saying "your children who this day have no knowledge of Good and Evil shall go in there into the land," meaning those children, not the adults who left Egypt, will enter Canaan. He says this shows "knowledge of good and evil" is a marker of moral accountability and adulthood, and notes that English still uses similar ideas when judging whether someone is an adult.

### "You shall die" means loss of access to eternal life
Tabor says the warning "in the day that you eat of it you will die" (Genesis 2:15) was fulfilled in that "they did die that day," but not by dropping dead. He reads God's words outside the garden ("you are dust and to dust you will return") as the couple losing access to eternal life. In Genesis 3:22, God worries the human might "take also of the tree of life and eat and live forever," so the expulsion blocks that access. He says the story "doesn't promise anything for the future."

### Parallel in Isaiah 7
Tabor compares Isaiah 7, where a child will eat curds and honey "before he knows how to refuse evil and choose good." He says this child goes through the same stage as Adam and Eve, reaching moral agency at a particular age. He uses this to support the coming-of-age reading.

### Eden as a territory, not only a garden
Tabor opens by noting that Eden is "a geographical location" described by rivers including the Tigris and Euphrates, and that the Garden is the place within that territory. He stresses that "Elohim" is a plural word taking a singular verb, which he reads as a collective of "powers that be." He notes that other "sons of God" (Benei Elohim) passages are a topic for another lecture.

### Humans are not presented as fallen
Tabor concludes that "humans didn't fall and get expelled in some wrathful punishment of God." He describes humans in Genesis as "disposable dust beings" with the capacity to become gods, to discern good and evil, and to act as autonomous agents, but without access to the tree of life. He says the new-creation tree of life in Revelation 21-22 is a Christian interpretation that Jews and Christians do not share as the same reading of Genesis.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The "fall of man" is in Genesis.** Tabor says the story does not describe a fall from grace; it describes humans growing up and leaving Eden.
- **"Good and evil" means moral sin.** He argues the phrase means discerning or experiencing right and wrong, and that "evil" is a sinister-sounding rendering of what is better read as "bad."
- **The forbidden fruit was a test of obedience.** He argues the story is about perception changing and coming of age, not about a rule being broken.
- **"You shall die" meant immediate death.** He notes the couple did not drop dead and reads death as loss of access to the tree of life and eternal life.
- **Original sin passes from Adam to all humans.** Tabor's reading of "we've all eaten the forbidden fruit" presents becoming morally accountable as the universal experience, not inherited guilt. He does not directly discuss the doctrine of original sin beyond saying the fall idea became "a great theological Doctrine."
