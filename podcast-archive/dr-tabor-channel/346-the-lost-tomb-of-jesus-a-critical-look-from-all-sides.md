## Summary

This is an unedited 2007 outtake from a post-broadcast discussion hosted by Ted Koppel, held after Discovery aired Simcha Jacobovici and James Cameron's documentary *The Lost Tomb of Jesus*. The program argued that a tomb in Talpiot, Jerusalem, held the family of Jesus, including an ossuary inscribed "Mariamne" (Mary Magdalene) and a "Jesus son of Joseph." The first half of the debate asks whether the evidence (DNA, patina, names, statistics) supports that claim. Jacobovici defends the film as a journalist's compelling story that scientists should test; he repeatedly says he followed the evidence and that the hypothesis is open to challenge. William Dever and Jonathan Reed press the critique: Dever calls the program damaging to archaeology and says the name database is too small, and Reed argues the conclusions rest on a chain of "ifs." Tabor, a consultant on the film, joins the exchanges over the name analysis and the James ossuary patina, and he argues for the name and statistical case while conceding points such as the late attestation of the "Mary omna" form.

The first half is also contested on specific facts. A forensic archaeologist (Carney Matheson, quoted by the film's critics) says DNA cannot show marriage, and Jacobovici concedes the "husband and wife" remark was overstated. The "match" between the James ossuary patina and the Talpiot patina is disputed, with a crime-lab email quoted to limit the word "match" to fingerprints and DNA. The Joseph Gat notes and Amos Kloner's account are disputed over whether the tenth ossuary was unmarked, and the statistical 1-in-600 figure (Feuerverger) is disputed over its assumptions.

The second half, with David O'Connell, Darrell Bock and Judy Fentress-Williams, turns to religious implications. The panelists agree the program does not disprove faith but disagree over whether a remaining body would challenge bodily resurrection; they also criticize the film's dramatizations. Koppel closes by saying faith does not require physical evidence, and that speculative hypotheses need far more rigorous testing.

## Key Teachings

### The DNA "chain" and what the non-match shows
Jacobovici says he followed the DNA, statistics and epigraphy "as far as I could," and that the evidence is "compelling" because the alternative (two unrelated families sharing the same name cluster) is possible but less likely. He notes that only two ossuaries (the "Jesus son of Joseph" and the one marked "Mariamne") yielded accessible DNA; others, such as the "Judah son of Jesus" ossuary, appeared cleaned. Reed argues the method is a chain where each link is "nailed" and then the next is taken up, and says that "there are so many ifs in that chain." Forensic archaeologist Carney Matheson, quoted in the film, says DNA cannot establish marriage, so a non-match shows only that the two are not maternally related. Jacobovici concedes that "husband and wife" was a step beyond what the test proved.

### The patina "match" and the James ossuary
Jacobovici cites Suffolk County crime-lab analysis (Charles Pellegrino) finding a similar trace-element composition between the patina on the James ossuary and the Talpiot patina, and calls it a "match." A later email from the lab (Robert Genna) is read on air, saying the lab would say the samples are "consistent" but not a "match," and that the term applies only to fingerprints and DNA. Koppel presses this distinction, and Jacobovici responds that the lab's wording still establishes a link that courts accept. Dever and Reed challenge the inference that the samples came from the same tomb.

### The missing tenth ossuary
Koppel reports that Amos Kloner told him he counted and cataloged ten ossuaries in the tomb, and that the missing one (which Jacobovici suggests could be the James ossuary) was unmarked and placed in a courtyard. Jacobovici reads from Joseph Gat's notes listing nine ossuaries received. Dever argues that Kloner, a careful archaeologist, would not have missed the rosette and inscription on the James ossuary, and that Kloner's 1996 report used the file rather than his own observation of the one in question. Jacobovici responds that two feet of soil could have hidden the inscription, and that the item may simply have been lost.

### The names and the statistics
The film's argument turns on the rarity of the name "Mariamne" on the ossuary, which the film says supports identifying the woman as Mary Magdalene. Feuerverger's calculation, as reported in the film (Koppel reads it), is about 1 in 600 that the tomb is not the Jesus family tomb, after discounting assumptions. Feuerverger, as Koppel reports him, says the result depends on the interpretation of the name: if it is simply the common form "Mariam," the significance "would wash out considerably." Dever argues the film claims a complete name list while the database is only a few hundred names, and that the sample is too small and unrepresentative. Tabor, on the film's side, replies that the database is much larger. Reed says the Mary Magdalene identification rests on the Acts of Philip (a document Reed dates to the fourth century, citing François Bovon). Tabor answers that the earliest place he found the form is Hippolytus (second century), and concedes "we have no evidence" for its use in the first century. He calls the link a matter of "correlation."

### Recreations and docudrama
Dever says the film's dramatizations give the hypothesis a power it would not otherwise have and that the film "puts archaeology in a rather bad light," reading as "archaeo porn." Jacobovici replies that he is a filmmaker, not a journalist, and that the film is a "dramatization of a hypothesis," which he says is common on television. Fentress-Williams argues that the storytelling, with its cliffhangers and staged scenes, moves the viewer away from critical thinking because it is selective and does not explain what archaeology can and cannot show. O'Connell says the dramatized frosting is "a real problem" while the excavation process is well done. Jacobovici defends the casting of Jesus and the "blond Jesus" images as a deliberate correction.

### Bodily resurrection and what a remaining body would mean
Bock argues that the Christian faith's resurrection claim is "absolutely" bodily, that Judaism already held a bodily hope (citing 2 Maccabees 7), and that the film's suggestion of a body left on earth would be "absolutely problematic." He also cites Paul's distinction of material and spiritual bodies and the seed analogy. Bovon-style objection (from the panel) holds that the church has opposed a spiritual-only resurrection since the second century. O'Connell says many Christians believe in a physical ascension and that he sees no evidence for the film's claim, but that his faith is not contingent on the tomb. Bock and O'Connell both say the hypothesis would not necessarily undermine resurrection belief if the theology is understood correctly, while others on the panel say a remaining body would force a reinterpretation.

### Faith and evidence (Koppel's closing)
Koppel concludes that faith "does not require physical evidence to survive or even flourish," that the speculative hypotheses need to be tested more vigorously, and that Christianity, Judaism and Islam survive because they are part of the fabric of culture even for non-believers. This is the host's own framing, and it is not a claim the guests settled on air.

- **That the name "Mariamne" is definitively Mary Magdalene.** Reed and Dever argue the identification depends on a late source (Acts of Philip), and Tabor concedes the first-century use is not attested.
- **That the film's name database is complete.** Dever says the film implies a full list of names when the database is small; Tabor disputes the size of the database.
- **That a dramatization is neutral journalism.** Dever and Fentress-Williams argue that the recreations lend credibility to a hypothesis.
- **That a remaining body is no challenge to Christian belief.** Bock argues that bodily resurrection is central to the Christian faith; O'Connell and others disagree on how much it matters.
