## Summary

Tabor asks whether the hypothetical Q source, the material Matthew and Luke share that is not in Mark, preserves the earliest version of Jesus' teachings, and whether Luke's version of it is more original than Matthew's. He says he accepts "a version of the Q hypothesis," though he does not defend it fully in this video. He presents Luke's Q as independent of Matthew and as the closer witness, and he treats Matthew as an editor who shortens and alters both Mark and Q.

His main evidence is a set of side-by-side comparisons: Matthew's misattributed Isaiah quotation (which Tabor says is from Malachi), the voice at Jesus' baptism, the Beatitudes and woes, the Lord's Prayer, the "hate your father and mother" saying, the three would-be disciples, the "sword" and "division" sayings, and the healing of the paralytic and Sabbath controversy. For each, he argues that Matthew's version shows the marks of revision (shortening, removing, softening, or adding theology), while Luke's is more rough, repetitive and "singular" in style, which he takes as a sign of earlier material. He also says Luke tends to keep Q largely intact, placing about 75% of it in one block at 9:51 onward.

The talk ends without concluding. Tabor says he will not have time to cover the Griesbach and Lindsey alternatives in full, and the transcript closes after a brief remark that a later passage (Gethsemane, the empty tomb) is not needed for this presentation.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Q is the shared Matthew-Luke material outside Mark, and it was mostly teaching
Tabor defines Q (from the German word for "source") as the material Matthew and Luke have in common that is not in Mark. He says that if one extracted this material, "you would largely get teachings of Jesus," the material Christians call "Red Letter." He pictures an early scroll of about fifty sayings, which he has seen as a printed graph-paper copy of Luke's Q, and calls it "a lost gospel" in a teaching joke. He also notes that material shared by Matthew and Luke could have come from another source, so a Q reconstruction is only what the two share.

### 2. Luke's Q is earlier and more original than Matthew's
Tabor's central claim is stated in the opening: "Luke seems to have a more accurate or original version and Matthew is the one who's shortening and editing and altering it." He says Matthew and Luke are independent of each other for Q, and he defends Luke's Q as preserving the original. Luke's order and wording are, in his view, closer to the source, and Matthew's Q passages are arranged into five blocks that reflect Matthew's own shaping.

### 3. Matthew's editorial method, first shown on Mark
Tabor says he can show how Matthew edits Mark, and so can infer how Matthew treats Q. He lists three strategies: "shorten abbreviate and get to the point," remove "extraneous things" or whole passages, and modify passages "by adding something that will soften it or mitigate it." His examples are the Isaiah/Malachi misquotation (Matthew keeps the Isaiah label and moves the Malachi quote later), the baptism of Jesus (Matthew adds John's objection, which Tabor says resolves a theological problem Mark leaves open), and the Sabbath controversy (Matthew reframes "the Sabbath is made for man" and changes the reason the disciples can pick grain, tying it to the temple's priestly work and to Jesus being "greater than the temple"). Tabor is careful to say he is describing what the texts say, not what anyone historically said: "I did not say John didn't say it to Jesus."

### 4. The baptism voice and the "Messianic secret"
Tabor compares the voice at Jesus' baptism. In Mark (and Luke) the voice says "you are my beloved Son" in a form addressed to Jesus, while Matthew has the voice declare it publicly. He reads Matthew's version as removing Mark's secrecy motif, and he treats Luke as keeping the voice private by placing the heaven opening during prayer after Jesus "had also been baptized." He presents this as evidence of how each writer thinks about the event.

### 5. The Beatitudes and woes: a single literary voice
Tabor argues that Luke's blessings are followed by woes, which Matthew lacks in this form (Matthew has woes in chapter 23), and that Matthew's blessings drop and rearrange Luke's terms. He claims the style of Luke's Q is "singular," and he says that reading the passage aloud reveals the "blow blessed blessed blessed but whoa whoa whoa" rhythm. He concedes the hypothesis is his own: "my hypothesis is that Luke's Q preserves the original collection of the teachings of Jesus," and that this is "as close as we're going to get to Jesus."

### 6. The Lord's Prayer as Q's shorter form, and the hard sayings
He argues that Luke's shorter prayer (Father, hallowed be your name, let your kingdom come, daily bread, forgiveness, do not lead us into temptation) is closer to Q, and that Matthew's longer version is a liturgical expansion. He reads the "hate father and mother" saying as hyperbole and rhetorical emphasis (noting "all good teachers are hyperbole teachers"), not a command to hate. He treats the sword/division saying (Luke 12:51-53) and "fear him who can destroy soul and body in Gehenna" the same way: as rhetoric that Matthew softens or cuts down, which he calls "Matthew's Cliff Notes on Q."

### 7. Method: harmonizing destroys the text
Tabor describes Kermit Zarley's *The Gospels Interwoven*, which blends the four gospels into one narrative. He says it "destroyed" the separate voices of Matthew, Mark and Luke by making them impossible to hear in their own terms. He says he uses the book as a workbook, asking students to colour-code three synoptic sources on an unseen passage (the arrest, trial and crucifixion), and that students quickly see Mark's emphasis on the disciples' failure, which others drop.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Luke's extra material (prodigal son, Good Samaritan) was just added to Mark and Matthew, so the four gospels are a single "revelation" to be combined.** Tabor says gospels should be read separately, and that harmonizing (as in Zarley's book) destroys each writer's voice.
- **The voice at Jesus' baptism said the same thing in all gospels.** Tabor says Matthew and Mark differ in the wording and in whether the voice addresses Jesus or the crowd.
- **"Hate" your father and mother in Luke 14 means to literally hate family.** Tabor reads it as hyperbole and rhetoric, which Matthew softens.
- **Matthew's Beatitudes and Luke's Beatitudes are the same teaching.** Tabor argues Luke's version is economic and social, and Matthew's is spiritualized, with Luke also including woes.
- **The Lord's Prayer as usually recited is Jesus' original prayer.** Tabor suggests Luke's shorter form is closer to Q, and that the longer form developed liturgically.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. the later gospels:** This is the core of the talk. Tabor argues Luke's Q is the nearest available witness to Jesus' sayings, and that Matthew and Mark reshape them. He presents this as a hypothesis built on comparative editing, not as settled fact.
- **Torah:** The Sabbath controversy (Mark 2 and Matthew 12) is used to show how Matthew changes the reasoning on Sabbath observance to fit his theology. Tabor says Matthew adds the priestly-work argument and the claim that Jesus is "greater than the temple."
- **The Kingdom:** Tabor reads the Lord's Prayer's "let your kingdom come" and Mark's greatest commandment as linked to what he calls a "spiritual realization" of God's will, in Mark.
- **Afterlife and hell:** The Luke 12 saying about fearing the one who can destroy soul and body in Gehenna is discussed only as an example of Matthew's compression, not as a doctrinal claim.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** This is the main thread. The talk describes the two-source model, Matthew's editorial habits, Luke's claimed care in "investigating everything" (he cites Luke's preface on writing an "accurate" account), and the harmonizing problem.
