## Summary

Host Helen Bond opens by asking how the ancient Near East pictured the cosmos and what happened to people after death, and how those ideas changed over time. James Tabor carries most of the talk. He describes a three-tier universe (heavens above, earth, and a dark underworld) and argues that the Hebrew Bible originally had no heaven, no hell, and no conscious reward or punishment after death. In his account, the dead went to Sheol, a shadowy, silent existence that the Psalms and Job describe in bleak terms. He traces a gradual shift through Job, Isaiah, and Daniel toward a hope of bodily resurrection, which he distinguishes from the Greek idea of an immortal soul. He ties the Greek contribution to Hades, its compartments, and the 4th-century gold tablets, and he contrasts this with the Hebrew and early Christian view of resurrection with a new body on a renewed earth.

Tabor's main evidence is textual: he reads and comments on passages from Job, Ecclesiastes, Psalms 115 and 139, Isaiah 24-29, Daniel 12, 2 Maccabees, Wisdom of Solomon 2, Luke 16, Romans 2, and Revelation 20. He also draws on Homer and Greek and Babylonian material, and on his own visits to Jerusalem's Hinnom Valley when discussing Gehenna. He presents these as his reading of the sources, and several of his datings are explicitly tentative ("I can't" date it, "we don't know").

The second half turns to Paul and to the later Christian picture of hell and heaven. Tabor argues that Paul expected an imminent end, that later generations shifted to a "vertical" picture of going straight to heaven or hell, and that the old eschatological hope was largely forgotten. The episode closes with the "time machine" segment, in which Tabor says he would like to ask Paul about the delay of the end and about slavery and women, and then the hosts give closing announcements about a book club.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Sheol as a silent, non-punitive underworld
Tabor describes the ancient cosmos as three decks, with the dome of the sky above, the earth in the middle, and the underworld below. The dead went down to Sheol, which he says is "a shadowy silent existence" and "dusty and drab." He says Sheol is "not torture" and "not hell." He cites Job's lament ("man dies and is laid low... Till the heavens are no more he will not awake") and Ecclesiastes' comparison of human and beast death. He also cites Psalm 115 ("the dead do not praise Yahweh") and notes that the living are the ones who will praise God. He stresses that Sheol is not non-existence, pointing to Psalm 139 ("if I make my bed in Sheol you are there") as an early hint that God still attends to the dead.

### 2. Nephesh and the "living breather"
Using his own translation of Genesis, Tabor argues that Adam becomes a *nephesh*, which he renders "living breather" rather than "living soul." He says the word is biologically grounded (linked to breathing and the throat) and that the same term applies to animals, so Genesis does not present a separable immortal soul. When a breather stops breathing, the breath "goes forth" and the person returns to dust.

### 3. Saul and the medium: a folk-level exception
Helen raises the story of Saul and the medium (the "Necromancer of Endor"). Tabor reads the passage, in which the woman sees "a Elohim" rising from the earth and Saul recognizes Samuel. He reads the episode culturally, as a folklore belief that the "shades" of the dead could be summoned, and he says the text is "a far cry" from life after death. He compares the Odyssey, book 11, where Achilles asks Odysseus what he is doing in the underworld.

### 4. Theodicy and the slow emergence of hope (Job 19, Isaiah)
Tabor argues that the impulse toward an afterlife grew out of theodicy, the problem that the righteous and the wicked all go to the same place and never return. He reads Job 19 ("I know that my redeemer lives") not as a resurrection promise but as Job's wish that vindication were possible, and says most translations mislead. He then identifies what he calls the "Isaiah apocalypse" (Isaiah 24-27), with a brief passage in chapter 29 that he reads as "thy dead will live," while the wicked stay dead. He says the book also contains the promise that a person would live to a hundred, which he distinguishes from the resurrection of the dead. He says he cannot date the apocalypse.

### 5. Daniel 12:3, the Maccabean martyrs, and the general resurrection
Tabor treats Daniel 12:3 ("many of those who sleep in the dust will arise") as a "huge change," but notes that "many" does not clearly mean all. He places the next step in 2 Maccabees, where martyrs under Antiochus expect to have their limbs restored in the resurrection, while their persecutors will not. He presents the view that resurrection was first only for the righteous and later extended to all, which he connects to Paul's language about "the resurrection of the just and the unjust." He ties the later sifting to judgment, the "last day" (John's Gospel), and Revelation 20's great white throne.

### 6. Greek Hades and the gold tablets; resurrection versus immortality
Tabor argues that the Greeks introduced the compartmentalized Hades, the "Isles of the Blessed," a middle state for souls needing another chance, and the notion that the soul could rise to "the couches of the Gods." He describes the 4th-century gold tablets (about 17 found, by his count) that guide the soul past the Lake of Forgetfulness to a second lake of memory, with a confession: "I am a child of Earth and Starry heaven but Heaven alone is my home." He cites Empedocles on the soul's fall. He contrasts this with the Hebrew and early Christian pattern: "resurrection of the Dead is not the same as immortality of the Soul." Earth is "a good Earth," and the righteous will be raised with a transformed body on a renewed earth, while the Greek soul "fell down to the dark Earth." He cites Wisdom of Solomon 2 as showing the transition, since the "fool's" view of death is followed by the righteous "in the hand of God."

### 7. Paul, the delayed end, and the vertical turn
Tabor argues that Paul expected the Lord's return in his lifetime, and that the early hope was "brimming" with imminence. Over the centuries the hope became a "vertical" picture of going to heaven or hell at death, which he says is "more effective probably for preaching." He reads Philippians 1 as Paul expecting to be "with Christ" at death without yet being raised, and compares this to the "bosom of Abraham" held in Luke 16. He interprets Gehenna as a garbage-dump image, not a place of torture ("you don't really torture garbage"), and says Dante and later Christians developed the burning-hell imagery.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Hebrew Bible already teaches heaven and hell.** Tabor argues that the early texts describe only Sheol, a silent shadowy existence, with no reward or torment after death.
- **The soul is immortal by biblical design.** Tabor argues that the Hebrew and early Christian hope is resurrection of the body on a renewed earth, not the Greek immortality of a soul that fell from heaven.
- **Job 19 is a resurrection promise.** Tabor says that reading comes from translations; in context Job is wishing his vindication could happen.
- **Paul taught that the dead go straight to heaven at death.** Tabor reads Paul as expecting an imminent end with resurrection, and sees the "go to heaven now" view as a later development.
- **Gehenna is an image of eternal torture.** Tabor reads it as a garbage dump outside Jerusalem, an image of being cast out; the burning-hell imagery he attributes to later Christianity and Dante.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor reads Paul as expecting the end imminently and Jesus's Luke 16 parable as a holding area; he says these views shaped later preaching, but the talk does not fully separate Jesus's own teaching from later reception.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** This is the talk's central theme. Tabor's main claim is that the earliest Hebrew view was Sheol, that resurrection emerged from theodicy and Daniel, and that immortality of the soul came from Greek thought.
- **Kingdom on earth:** Tabor says the Hebrew and early Christian hope centers on "a good Earth" and a new heavens and new earth where God comes down, rather than heaven as the destination.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Wisdom of Solomon and 2 Maccabees appear as Apocrypha-type texts that show the transition; Tabor notes the Wisdom book was in the original King James Version.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Only lightly touched. Tabor says Paul believed in Christ's presence in the afterlife, and reads Christ as "Lord" filling heaven and earth.
