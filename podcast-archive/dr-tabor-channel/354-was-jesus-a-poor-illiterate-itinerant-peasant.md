## Summary

Tabor asks whether the common scholarly picture of the historical Jesus as a poor, illiterate, itinerant Mediterranean peasant from a tiny village fits the evidence in the Gospels and in Josephus. He proposes what he calls a "literate Urban Cosmopolitan Jesus," and says this is a possibility he derives from the texts and their geography, not a settled conclusion. He frames the talk as a slideshow with maps and photographs from his own excavations and tours. The talk is entirely his presentation, with no guest or host.

His main evidence has three parts. First, geography: he places Nazareth about four to four and a half miles from Sepphoris, the Herodian capital of Galilee, and argues that the town was an urban suburb rather than an isolated hamlet. He says Sepphoris was burned in 4 BCE and that its rebuilding during Jesus's lifetime could have given work to a builder's family. Second, the John the Baptist movement: he reads Mark, Luke, Q, John and Josephus as showing a large, popular movement whose preaching Jesus continued, with crowds from Galilee, Judea, Transjordan, Tyre and Sidon, and the Decapolis. Third, the Decapolis and Transjordan: he argues that Jesus preached openly to non-Jews and that his region was thickly urban and multicultural. He also covers a hideout by the Wadi Kerith across the Jordan, which he links to Elijah, John and Jesus, and to the Jerusalem followers' flight to Pella.

The talk ends with a short discussion of a Slavonic version of Josephus that describes John fleeing across the Jordan. Tabor says he thinks Jesus could read and write and may have worked in a family stonemason business, but he stresses that "nobody knows" and presents this as an inference from sources. He also notes that the video description gives the hamlet's population as about 100, while he himself estimates about 200 in the talk.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Mark is the earliest source, and John the Baptist's movement was large and continuous with Jesus's
Tabor insists on reading Mark first, because Matthew and Luke use it. He reads Mark 1 (John baptizing in the desert and in Judea and Jerusalem) and then Mark 6, where Herod hears of Jesus and thinks John has been raised. From this he concludes that "Jesus picks up that preaching effort that campaign that John LED." He points to Luke 3:10-14, a source he thinks differs from Mark, where John's crowds include soldiers and tax collectors. He argues that Jesus's disciples had originally followed John, and that the requirement for replacing Judas (that the replacement had been with the movement "all the way back to John the Baptist") shows that this was "a singular movement."

### 2. Josephus confirms John's popularity and Herod Antipas's fear of him
Tabor reads from Josephus's Antiquities. He quotes the passage saying Herod "feared lest the great influence John had had over the people might put it into his power and inclination to raise a rebellion," and that Herod therefore executed him at Machaerus. Tabor says he does not find the Gospel picture of crowds exaggerated, because Josephus was "quite judicious." He also says that Herod Antipas had reason to fear revolt, given the revolts after Herod the Great's death in 4 BCE. He adds that Josephus's Slavonic version has John fleeing across the Jordan and being opposed by a man named Simon, which Tabor treats as a further independent echo of the flight theme.

### 3. Jesus's preaching was not confined to Jews, and was open to Gentiles from the start
Tabor argues that the Gospels already show Jesus teaching a universal message. He cites Mark 3 (multitudes from Galilee, Judea, Idumea, Transjordan, Tyre and Sidon), Mark 5 (the Gerasene demoniac, who then proclaims Jesus in the Decapolis), and Mark 7 (the trip through Tyre, Sidon and the Decapolis). He reads the centurion's servant story as a Gentile officer whose faith Jesus praises above anything "in Israel." He also reads Luke 4, where Jesus cites the Sidonian widow and Naaman, as saying faith "does not depend on your pedigree." His conclusion is that "Paul did not initiate this." The universal message, in his view, is already present in the John–Jesus movement.

### 4. The Decapolis and the Galilee were a thickly urban, Greco-Roman world
Tabor describes the Decapolis as ten Greek-style city-states, with theaters, colonnaded streets, markets and public baths, a Roman-era political status that gave them their own coins and forces, and an alliance with Rome. He shows photographs of Jerash and argues that Jesus's route through these cities made him "extremely Cosmopolitan." He describes Sepphoris, which he calls "the Jewel of the Galilee," as a Jewish city with a theater, a cardo, mosaics and wealthy Roman-style villas, and says it is "not a g[entile] City" but a Jewish one. He argues that Jesus's sayings about cities, lamps on a hill and royal palaces reflect this urban environment, and that Sepphoris was a natural place for Jesus's family to work.

### 5. Jesus, his family, and the building trade
Tabor argues that the Greek word *tekton* means "builder" rather than "carpenter," so that Jesus's father Joseph and his brothers James, Joseph, Jude and Simon "would have worked in the city of Sepphoris probably as stonemasons." He notes that buildings in this period were mostly stone, and says the gospel examples of foundations and building illustrate this trade. He says that rebuilding under Antipas would have given such a business work. This is one of the claims he most explicitly labels as his own inference ("I think... it's very likely").

### 6. The Transjordan hideout: Wadi Kerith, Elijah, and the flight of the church
Tabor argues that John 3:22-23 (John baptizing at Aenon near Salim, Jesus in the Judean countryside), John 10 (Jesus crossing the Jordan and staying there), and Mark 10 (the crossing "beyond the Jordan") point to a Transjordan retreat near Pella in the Wadi Kerith, where Elijah hid according to 1 Kings 17. He describes a visit with students, the caves on both sides of the valley, and the springs. He cites the fourth-century historian Eusebius, who says the Jerusalem followers fled before the revolt of 66-73 to Pella, and Revelation 12, where the woman flees to a wilderness. He cites Mark 13 ("flee to the mountains") and the fourth-century pilgrim Egeria (named "Aira" in the transcript), who visited Salim and the springs. He adds that the church's flight to Pella and the Kerith region are linked to these traditions.

### 7. Apocalyptic and nonviolent instructions in Luke's material
Tabor reads Luke 3:10-14 as John's instructions to crowds, soldiers and tax collectors: share your coats and food, do not extort, do not rob by violence, be content with your wages. He says "it sounds like this is a kind of a pacifist movement," but argues that the reason for these instructions is that "the Judgment of God and the kingdom of God is right at hand," which makes the movement apocalyptic. He identifies this as a source that appears in Luke only, which he thinks may come from the hypothetical Q document. He notes that Matthew and Mark do not have this passage in parallel.

### 8. Sources in layers, not a harmony
Tabor's method is to separate the Gospels by source (Mark as earliest; Matthew and Luke as dependent on Mark plus Q; John as another stratum) and to argue that the independent strata all report the same big picture of popular crowds and urban Galilee. He says these strata "are independent sources" that "all report this same phenomena," which for him is stronger evidence than any one text.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus was a poor, illiterate peasant from a tiny village.** Tabor argues Nazareth was a suburb of Japhia (Yafa) and about four and a half miles from Sepphoris, and that the evidence points to a literate, urban, cosmopolitan Jesus who may have worked in a family building business.
- **Paul first took the gospel to the Gentiles.** Tabor says that Jesus and John already preached to non-Jews, including Romans and Gentiles in the Decapolis and Tyre and Sidon, so "Paul did not initiate this."
- **Jesus's ministry was confined to the Galilean countryside.** Tabor argues the Gospels show crowds from Judea, Idumea, Transjordan, Tyre, Sidon and the Decapolis, and that Jesus worked across the Jordan.
- **Jesus films show a rural Galilee of hills and rocks.** Tabor says no film shows the urban settings that were actually there.
- **Nazareth is an isolated village.** Tabor says it sat within a dense web of roads and cities, and that Sepphoris, the regional capital, was four to four and a half miles away.
