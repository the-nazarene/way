## Summary

Derek Lambert opens by asking whether Paul and Mark believed in a physical resurrection, meaning Jesus rising in the same flesh-and-bone body. Tabor frames the question as a historical-critical reading of the earliest sources rather than a proof or disproof of the resurrection. He says that "critical means careful," and that the New Testament texts are often read as an interchangeable "deck of cards" when they should be read separately, each author on his own terms.

Tabor's central claim is that the earliest witnesses, Mark's ending, the Gospel of Peter's retelling of it, 2 Peter's account of the Transfiguration, and Paul's letters, describe a glorified, heavenly Jesus, with the tradition of the risen Jesus arising in Galilee. In his reading, Mark 16:1-8 points the disciples to Galilee (Mark 14:28), where Mark 9:1 foreshadows an experience of the glorified Jesus on a mountain. Paul's "spiritual body" teaching in 1 Corinthians 15 and 2 Corinthians 5 is presented as the early view. Tabor argues that the bodily appearances in Matthew, Luke, John, Acts and the longer ending of Mark came later, partly as apologetic responses to critics such as Celsus.

Tabor's account of the evidence is argued and not neutral, and he presents his position as one that "Michael Heiser" found worth taking seriously. Much of the talk is his own argument. Lambert mainly asks questions and adds a few points, such as his reading of the Transfiguration as a "Proto Ascension narrative." The talk ends with Tabor's conclusion that Mark intended a dead, crucified Messiah as the last view of Jesus, and that the later endings "Cobble" Mark's text into something Mark did not write.

## Key Teachings

### Mark's ending is deliberately unfinished (Mark 16:1-8)
Tabor reads the ending in the two dominant early manuscripts as intentional. The women go to the tomb, find a young man in white who says Jesus "is risen" and is not there, and tell the disciples to go to Galilee. They "went out and fled from the tomb," and "they said nothing to anyone for they were afraid." He argues that Mark contains no appearances: "they don't see the body they don't touch it they don't eat with him." He reads Mark 14:28 ("I will go before you to Galilee") as the promise that the appearance will happen there. He says the reader is meant to puzzle over "what happened and how."

### The verb "raised" can mean "lifted up" (Mark 2 and Romans 1)
Tabor notes that the Greek verb used for Jesus's resurrection is the same verb used when the paralyzed man in Mark 2 "rose and took up his pallet." He reads it as "he has been lifted up," a passive form that can suggest an ascension, and says "resurrect" (anastasis, "to stand up") was used for the dead in general. He raises the question of what kind of body the dead "stand" in, which he then takes up with Paul. He also cites Romans 1 ("raised him in power from the dead") as Paul using the same language.

### The Gospel of Peter as an early reading of Mark
Tabor describes the Gospel of Peter (found in 19th-century Egypt, probably second century, breaking off in the middle) as "pretty early" and as a retelling of Mark. It has the young man say Jesus "is risen and gone away from whence he was sent," which Tabor reads as heavenward ascent. It then has the women flee and the disciples go home for the week of Unleavened Bread, "weeping and sorrowful," and Simon Peter and Andrew return to fishing. Tabor argues this is incompatible with the week of meals and touching that appears in Luke and John. He says the text is "not a rewriting of Mark" but has Mark's ending.

### The Transfiguration as a proleptic glimpse of the glorified Jesus (Mark 9:1; 2 Peter 1)
Tabor argues that Mark 9:1 ("some of you are not going to die before you see the kingdom come with power") sets up the Transfiguration on the mountain, where Peter, James and John see a glorified Jesus with Moses and Elijah. He calls this Mark's resurrection "ahead of time," citing his former teacher Norman Perrin as having said so. He reads 2 Peter 1 as the earliest interpretation: the "power and coming of our Lord Jesus" seen as eyewitness "majesty." He says this shows the Galilee experience was about heavenly glory, not a fleshly Jesus.

### Paul's spiritual body (1 Corinthians 9 and 15; 2 Corinthians 5; Philippians 3)
Tabor says Paul is the earliest firsthand witness: he claims to have "seen the Lord" in 1 Corinthians 9, comparing his experience with those of James, Peter and the brothers of the Lord. In 1 Corinthians 15 Paul expects the transformation "in the moment in the blink of an eye," and Tabor says the living will become a "spirit body" like Jesus, the "second Adam" and "lifegiving spirit." He cites 2 Corinthians 5 for the image of the body as old clothing, with the soul "naked" until the new body is put on, and Philippians 3:20-21 for the glorious body. He also says "Flesh and Blood" will not inherit the kingdom, which he takes as opposed to a resuscitated body.

### Corruption of the body and the question of bones
Tabor argues that Paul's view does not depend on the survival of the body: "people go back to the dust given enough time." He says Revelation 20's general resurrection of those who died at sea, and the appearance of Moses and Elijah at the Transfiguration, both show that resurrection in the New Testament did not require a surviving corpse. He therefore says the discovery of bones or a tomb would not disprove the earliest resurrection faith, and he criticizes a colleague's objection on that point.

### Later bodily appearances as apologetics
Tabor dates the bodily appearances in Matthew, Luke, John and Acts to after 70, perhaps into the early second century. He says Celsus argued the appearances might have been ghosts, hallucinations or apparitions, and that Christians responded with stories of the physical Jesus eating and being touched. He points to Acts 1:4 ("don't leave Jerusalem") against Mark's Galilee tradition, and notes that Matthew still keeps some of the Galilee tradition. He calls the longer ending of Mark (including the verse Irenaeus quotes, which Tabor notes is in the Latin) a "bogus ending" composed from Matthew and Luke, with Mary Magdalene's seven demons from Luke. He concludes that Mark wants the final view of Jesus to be "a dead Messiah on the cross."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The earliest Christians believed Jesus rose in the same fleshly body and appeared for days to eat and be touched.** Tabor argues that Mark, the Gospel of Peter and Paul describe a glorified, heavenly Jesus, and that bodily appearances are a later development.
- **The longer ending of Mark is original and part of the gospel.** Tabor calls it a later addition, compiled from Matthew and Luke, that "changes Mark."
- **Resurrection faith requires the body to survive.** Tabor argues that Paul's view did not depend on a surviving body, and that bones or a tomb would not settle the question.
- **Mark's ending is a failure to tell the story.** Tabor argues the unfinished ending is intentional and points the reader back to Galilee and Mark 9:1.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Paul taught vs. the earliest Jesus tradition:** Tabor presents Paul's "spiritual body" in 1 Corinthians 15 and 2 Corinthians 5 as consistent with the earliest Galilee tradition in Mark, and as the earliest view of resurrection.
- **Galilee and the Kingdom:** Tabor links Mark 9:1 ("kingdom come with power") and the Transfiguration with a Galilee origin of resurrection faith, and says the kingdom has "nothing to do with bodies" in that reading.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and the body:** Tabor reads 1 Corinthians 15, 2 Corinthians 5 and Revelation 20 as describing transformation or reconstitution rather than resuscitation, and says the soul is "naked" between death and the new body.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor's central claim is that the longer ending of Mark, Matthew's and Luke's bodily appearances, and John's Galilee chapter were later additions made in response to critics such as Celsus.
- **Who Jesus was:** The talk's Transfiguration and Mark 14:28 material bears on the question of how the first followers understood Jesus after death, as glorified, but Tabor does not develop a separate christology.
