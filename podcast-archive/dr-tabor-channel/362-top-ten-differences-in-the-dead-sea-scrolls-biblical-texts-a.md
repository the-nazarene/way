## Summary

Tabor opens a new series by responding to a viral debate. Scholar Wes Huff, on Billy Carson's channel and then on Joe Rogan's podcast, said that Dead Sea Scrolls copies of the Hebrew Bible match the traditional Masoretic Text (MT) "word for word." Tabor says this is wrong and that the differences matter, even if many are only spelling. He points viewers to Dan McClellan's short video and to the critical edition of the Isaiah scroll (DJD) as the way to check the claim. The question he sets is how the Dead Sea copies compare with the MT, which is represented by the Leningrad Codex from about 1000 CE, and what the differences show. He is careful to say that he is not yet making a final judgment.

His method in this introductory video is to recommend resources, then discuss a few example passages. These are Deuteronomy 5:12 (the Sabbath reason), Deuteronomy 32:8 and 32:43 (the "sons of God" language), Isaiah 53 (the Servant passage, including "his soul will see light"), and Malachi 3 (which he reads as describing two figures). He presents the Dead Sea group as an apocalyptic, messianic, New Covenant community, and he says that Josephus's account of the Essenes should be checked against the group's own writings. He argues that the Dead Sea group expected two or three messianic figures and that their disappointed expectations of an end around 40 years after their teacher's death led to a reinterpretation of Daniel's time periods. He also contrasts their strict Sabbath rules with the practice of Jesus's disciples.

The talk ends with a closing greeting, not the countdown. Tabor says the "top ten" list (counting down to number one) will come in later videos. Parts of the talk are a re-edited older video recorded at a desk during a thunderstorm, which he says has about 800,000 views. The talk is mostly Tabor speaking, with no guest.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The "word for word" claim is wrong, and the variants matter
Tabor says Huff's claim that the texts are "word for word the same" [04:40] does not hold. He grants that many differences are orthographic (spelling). He says the substantive variants, which are real readings and not spelling, are significant "and they do make a difference in the text" [07:48]. He recommends the DJD edition of the Isaiah scroll and Abegg, Flint and Ulrich's *The Dead Sea Scrolls Bible* as the tools for checking this.

### 2. Variation is normal and useful, not a threat
Tabor argues that multiple textual traditions are a benefit. He lists the MT, the Septuagint (which he dates to the 2nd century BCE), the Samaritan Pentateuch, the Dead Sea copies, and the Greek New Testament, which also has many variants [29:54 to 31:26]. He says: "we shouldn't wish for something that we don't have..." [31:26]. He also cites Bart Ehrman's early unease about New Testament variants as a parallel [28:19].

### 3. Deuteronomy 5:12 and the reason for the Sabbath
In the Dead Sea fragment (4QDeut) the Sabbath commandment is tied to creation in Genesis 1 and 2, as in Exodus 20. The MT's Deuteronomy version gives the Exodus deliverance as the reason [33:02 to 34:35]. Tabor says this could be a harmonization, or it could be an older tradition that the Dead Sea group preserved. He notes that the Moses Scroll has the same creation link [34:35].

### 4. Deuteronomy 32: "sons of God" and the divine council
In the Dead Sea text of Deuteronomy 32:8 the phrase is "according to the sons of Elohim," not "according to the number of the children of Israel" [36:11]. Tabor reads verse 43 as calling on heavenly beings, "all you gods," to worship. He connects this to Psalm 82 and to the idea of a divine council, citing Michael Heiser's *The Unseen Realm* [37:45 to 39:18].

### 5. Isaiah 53: "we despised him," "his people," and "his soul will see light"
Tabor reads through the Dead Sea Isaiah 53. In verse 3 the scroll has "we despised him," where the MT has "he was despised" [40:52]. Verse 8 reads "his people" where the MT has "on me" [42:28]. Verse 9 uses a plural tomb reading that Tabor says has been used to argue Jesus survived the cross. He says this is not about Jesus, and he cautions against that reading [44:02]. In verse 11 the scroll reads "out of his suffering his soul will see light." Tabor says this is absent from the MT and that the Dead Sea group might have read it as resurrection [44:02 to 45:37]. He notes that the Scrolls treat their teacher as the Servant figure in their own interpretation.

### 6. Malachi 3: two figures, and the expectation of multiple messiahs
Tabor reads Malachi 3 in the Dead Sea version. He says it appears to name two figures, a messenger who prepares the way and a "Lord" who comes to the Temple, and that "the messenger of the Covenant" is a separate figure [47:11]. He argues the group expected a priestly and a Davidic messiah, and perhaps a third, a prophet like Moses, and that they believed their teacher of righteousness was one of these figures [47:11 to 50:19]. He also suggests the plural in Malachi 3 may be the earlier reading, comparing Zechariah's two anointed figures. He says he has a blog article called "one two three messiah's question mark" on this topic [48:47].

### 7. Calendar, the Wicked Priest, and the failed end-date
Tabor argues the Wicked Priest could not have come on Yom Kippur if the group used a different (solar) calendar [51:52 to 53:26]. He says the group calculated the end in about 40 years after the teacher's death, using Daniel 9 and a cycle of ten jubilees. When the end did not come, they reinterpreted it as God prolonging the time, which he compares to 2 Peter [54:58]. He says the movement likely continued into the first century and may have overlapped with followers of John the Baptist and Jesus [56:31].

### 8. Sabbath rules: the group and Jesus
Tabor describes the Scrolls' Sabbath rules: an animal in a pit may not be helped out, people may not leave the camp on the Sabbath, and meals must be prepared in advance [56:31 to 59:36]. He cites Josephus's praise of the Essenes for this strictness. He contrasts the Gospel stories of disciples picking grain (Mark 2 and Matthew 12) and Jesus's response that the Sabbath is made for people, and he says the group would have been "appalled" [59:36]. He suggests the Jesus movement and John the Baptist were less rigid than the Scrolls, and that John's accepting attitude toward soldiers and tax collectors shows this [1:01:10]. He also says he and colleagues believe they found latrines and a cemetery near Qumran, though he notes some disputes about the women's graves [58:04].

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That the Dead Sea Isaiah matches the MT "word for word."** Tabor says this is not so. Many differences are spelling, but the substantive variants change readings and raise questions [04:40, 07:48].
- **That the Masoretic text has no variants and that Bible codes are reliable.** Tabor says the MT also has variants in its footnotes, and that a single variant would shift any letter-count code [28:19].
- **That Josephus gives the authoritative account of the Essenes.** Tabor says Josephus wrote for the emperor and presents the group as quietist and pacifist. He says to read the group's own writings instead [23:38].
- **That the Dead Sea group expected a single messiah.** Tabor argues the Scrolls point to two or three figures [47:11 to 50:19].

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Essenes/Dead Sea Scrolls:** Tabor's core focus. He argues for reading the group's own texts and calendar (Jubilees, solar calendar) over Josephus.
- **Torah, Sabbath:** The Deuteronomy 5:12 variant and the Sabbath rules contrast the Dead Sea group with Jesus's disciples and with the Pharisees, whom he says allowed more flexibility.
- **Who Jesus thought he was / messianic expectations:** Tabor reads Isaiah 53 and Malachi 3 as evidence for multiple messiahs and for a suffering servant in the Dead Sea reading, but he is cautious about linking these to Jesus.
- **John the Baptist and James's community:** Tabor suggests followers of John, Jesus and James may have had ties to the broader Messianic movement of the land, and that John was less rigid than the Dead Sea group.
- **Resurrection:** The "soul will see light" reading in Isaiah 53:11 may have been read as resurrection by the Dead Sea group, per Tabor.
- **How the texts were transmitted (gospels/canon):** Tabor argues that New Testament writers may have quoted Septuagint or Dead Sea forms of Old Testament texts, so the readings differ.
