## Summary

Tabor opens Part 1 of a three-part countdown asking how accurately the Hebrew Bible has been transmitted. He compares the standard English Bible (he uses the Revised Standard Version) and the Masoretic Text, which he dates to about 1000 AD through the Leningrad Codex, with the biblical manuscripts from the Dead Sea Scrolls, some of which he dates to around 100 BC or earlier. He notes that the Scrolls contain up to about 40% biblical texts, that the group also held books such as Enoch and Jubilees, and that he is working from an English volume he calls "the Dead Sea Scroll Bible," which lists the variants. He says he has picked ten differences arbitrarily and will cover five here (numbers 10 to 6), with the top five in Part 2.

Tabor argues that the differences are not trivial. He reads the Dead Sea evidence in Genesis 1:9, Exodus 1:5, 1 Samuel 17:4, 1 Samuel 10:27–11:1 and Psalm 22:16 as showing readings that differ in meaning or in content, and he backs several of them with the Septuagint and with Josephus. He holds that some Scrolls readings are probably original, and he says the Scrolls should influence English translations, though he does not claim certainty about every case.

He closes by recommending the Scrolls volume for personal study, says some readings may be better than the traditional text and some worse, and promises that in the next part he will discuss whether Jesus knew any of these readings.

## Key Teachings

### Scale of the variants and his method
Tabor says the Dead Sea Scrolls contain hundreds of texts, roughly 40% of which are biblical, though the set is incomplete (he notes the Book of Esther is not represented). He says the differences range from spelling and vowel pointing to whole words and phrases. He compares the Masoretic Text (Leningrad Codex, about 1000 AD) with the Isaiah Scroll from Cave 1, found in 1947 and dated by "many" to 100 BC or slightly earlier. He also says the Septuagint is around 200 BC and that Josephus knew a Greek translation and some Hebrew version. He presents the Dead Sea Scroll Bible as a tool readers can use to check variants themselves.

### Number 10: Genesis 1:9 and alliteration
Tabor argues that the Scrolls preserve a more original rhythm in the creation hymn. The standard text reads "one place," while the Scroll reads "one Gathering," which alliterates with the verb "gathered together." He says the Scroll also has an extra verse, "the waters under the heavens were gathered to their Gatherings and the dry land appeared," which he thinks was dropped by a copyist. He admits the point is "interesting" and "important on a certain level for the style and Rhythm of the text," rather than theologically decisive. He says he follows the Masoretic Text in his own translation of Genesis but adds notes on the Scroll reading.

### Number 9: Exodus 1:5 and 70 versus 75
Tabor says the Masoretic Text gives 70 persons in Jacob's family who went to Egypt, while the Scroll reading (as he gives it) and the Septuagint give 75. He notes that Stephen's speech in Acts uses the 75 figure, so he thinks Stephen followed the Greek tradition or a Hebrew text that read 75. He mentions that scholars propose ways to account for the extra five, but he sets that discussion aside and treats the numerical difference itself as the point.

### Number 8: 1 Samuel 17:4 and Goliath's height
Tabor contrasts the Masoretic height of Goliath, "six cubits and a span" (which he converts to about 9½ feet), with the Scroll reading "four cubits and a span" (about 6½ feet). He says the Septuagint and Josephus also give the shorter figure, and that the Saul comparison ("a head higher than most men") fits the shorter height better. He argues that the longer figure may be a copying error, and he uses the example to challenge readers who hold that "every letter is inspired."

### Number 7: 1 Samuel 10:27–11:1, the Nahash addition
Tabor calls this the largest block of extra material he discusses. The Scroll (4QSam, which he cites as "4Q") adds a passage about King Nahash of the Ammonites (he says "Amorite" in places) who had "grievously oppressed" the Gadites and Reubenites by gouging out right eyes, followed by the siege of Jabesh Gilead. He notes that the modern RSV has added the passage as 27b, which he calls "a major step in the right direction." He says Josephus (Antiquities 6.68–71, as he cites it) knows this tradition, which he uses to argue the passage was in circulation in the first century. He uses the case to dismiss "Bible codes," arguing that changing even a word or two undermines such methods.

### Number 6: Psalm 22 and the piercing of hands and feet
Tabor says the Masoretic Text reads "like lions" (*ka'ari*) with no verb, which translators such as the JPS have completed as "they maul." The Scroll reads "dogs are all around me" and a verb meaning "they have pierced" (*karu*), so that "my hands and feet" are pierced. He argues the Masoretic wording (*ka'ari*) looks like an alteration and suggests Christians may have used the reading as a prophecy of Jesus' hands and feet being pierced. He says the Septuagint's "they dug" is close to the Scroll sense. He also notes that the slide shows the Hebrew verse as 22:17 but the English verse as 22:16, and says he will correct the slide in the final PDF.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The differences between Bible manuscripts are minor and meaningless.** Tabor says the apologetic claim is "actually not the case," citing 70 versus 75, Goliath's height and the Nahash passage as significant.
- **Every letter and word of the Bible is inspired, so the text as printed is reliable.** He suggests that anyone who holds this view must reckon with variants such as the Goliath height, and he says there is no definitive text.
- **Bible codes reveal hidden messages.** He argues that changing a word or two throws off such methods, and that they are "a pretty foolish Enterprise" when the text is not fixed.
