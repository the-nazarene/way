## Summary

This is the second of two videos in which Tabor counts down what he calls the top ten textual differences between the Dead Sea Scrolls' biblical manuscripts and the traditional Hebrew Bible. This part covers numbers 5 through 1. He presents them as ordered by importance and says the last three are the most significant. The question is how much the scroll copies of Hebrew scripture differ from the medieval Masoretic text that standard English Bibles are translated from. He notes that the Leningrad Codex dates to about 1000 CE, while the Dead Sea Isaiah scroll is usually dated to about 100 BCE, and that Greek translations (the Septuagint) date to about 200 BCE.

Tabor argues the differences are substantive, not trivial. His evidence is side-by-side readings: a Psalm 151 that is not in the Leningrad Codex but appears in two Dead Sea copies, a Deuteronomy 32:8 that reads "sons of God" where the standard text has "children of Israel," a Deuteronomy Sabbath commandment that grounds the day in creation as well as the Exodus, and a Malachi 3:1 that in the scroll copy seems to name two figures rather than one. For the top item he argues the scroll reading expects two anointed figures, a priestly and a royal one, which he connects to the messenger John the Baptist and early followers of Jesus.

The talk ends with a warning against claims that the scrolls are "almost identical" to the traditional text, which Tabor says is not the case. He points viewers to his course, his translation, and a book on the Shapira Moses scroll, and he closes by encouraging readers to compare the texts themselves. He does not name the scholar whose remarks he is responding to.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Malachi 3:1 and two anointed figures (number 1)
Tabor says the standard Malachi 3:1 announces one messenger who prepares the way before the Lord, and Mark 1:2 applies this to John the Baptist. In the Dead Sea copy he reads, the text has "they will suddenly come to God's Temple," which he takes as two figures: a messenger who prepares the way and a "messenger of the Covenant" who comes after. He describes these as two anointed ones, "one is more of a priest the other is more of a king," and cites Zechariah 6 for a future king with a priest beside him. He also says other Dead Sea texts that speak in their own voice describe a Davidic messiah and a priestly messiah from Aaron's line. His claim is that this reading fits better with the expectations of the scroll community and of early Christians, and that people reading it in Jesus's time might ask whether John was "the one" or whether another was to come.

### 2. The Sabbath reason in Deuteronomy and the Shapira scroll (number 2)
In the standard Deuteronomy 5:15 (cited by Tabor as verse 15), the reason for keeping the Sabbath is the Exodus from Egypt. In the Dead Sea copy he cites, the reason includes creation: "in six days the Lord made Heaven and Earth... and rested the seventh day." Tabor says this makes the Sabbath a more universal day "for all humankind," and he links it to Jesus's saying in Mark 2:27 that the Sabbath was made for humankind. He notes Exodus has a creation rationale but Deuteronomy does not in the standard text. He then brings in the Shapira Moses scroll, a manuscript sold in 1878 and dismissed as a forgery, which he says contains a similar creation-based Sabbath reason, and which was apparently lost. He describes an article co-written with another scholar and a book by Ross Nichols, and argues the scroll was probably authentic. He does not claim it has been proven.

### 3. Deuteronomy 32:8 and the division of the nations (number 3)
Tabor says the standard Deuteronomy 32:8 says God divided the nations according to "the number of the children of Israel," while the Dead Sea copy reads "the number of the sons of God" (bnei Elohim), which he says probably refers to angels. He says the Septuagint agrees with the scroll here. He also notes that the Dead Sea copy has a call to the nations and to the gods, and a phrase about the Lord avenging the blood of his sons, that is missing from the standard text. He presents these as a substantially different reading, not a minor variant.

### 4. Isaiah 53:11 and "light" after the travail (number 4)
Tabor argues that Isaiah 53:11 in the standard text reads, roughly, "he will see from the travail of his soul" with no object. The Dead Sea copy reads "he will see light" after the travail. He says this seems to imply the servant suffers, is buried, and then sees light, which he says "would hint at something like Resurrection." He calls this a notable difference, and he lists smaller variants nearby such as "bore the sin of many" and the wording about "my people" and the rich man's tomb. He leaves the interpretation of the light open.

### 5. Psalm 151 and the David and Goliath story (number 5)
Tabor says a Psalm about David's youth and his fight with Goliath appears in the Septuagint but not in the Leningrad Codex. Two Dead Sea copies contain a Psalm 151, but Tabor says the scroll version is much longer and is about Samuel anointing David, not about Goliath, though a later part of it does seem to describe the giant's slaying. He calls the scroll version "a completely different text" and asks whether it should be added to Bibles. He also recalls Psalm 22 (discussed in the recap of Part 1), where the scroll reading "they have pierced my hands and feet" appears, which Christians later read as prophecy of Jesus.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That the Dead Sea Scrolls' biblical texts are essentially identical to the medieval Hebrew Bible.** Tabor says there are significant differences, and he lists ten. He notes the claim he is responding to came from an unnamed scholar on a podcast.
- **That the Sabbath is only a Jewish institution.** Tabor argues the Dead Sea Deuteronomy grounds the Sabbath in creation, making it a universal day for humankind.
- **That Malachi 3:1 announces only one forerunner.** Tabor argues the Dead Sea reading expects two anointed figures, and that early readers may have read it this way.
