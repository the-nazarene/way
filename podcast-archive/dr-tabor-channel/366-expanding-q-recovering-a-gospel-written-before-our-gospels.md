## Summary

Tabor asks whether a Gospel of Jesus's sayings can be recovered from the material Matthew and Luke share but Mark lacks, which is the Q source of the two-source hypothesis. He first explains the standard model: Q was a collection of about 50 pericopes, Mark came next, and Matthew and Luke each used Mark and Q. He argues that Luke usually preserves Q's wording better than Matthew, and he gives examples: the "hate your father and mother" saying (Luke 14:26), the sword and division saying, and the "fear Him who can destroy... hell" saying. His main method is to use Luke as the base text, mark material shared with Matthew and Mark, and then add Luke-only material that he judges to fit the Q topics. He calls the result a "Proto gospel," a Gospel before the canonical ones. He marks the additions in yellow (probable) and blue (speculative).

He reports that the expanded Q has "a shred of Pauline theology" nowhere in it and that it "reminds you of the Book of James" and the Didache. Tabor frames this as a hypothesis still in development, presented from a Patreon study segment and offered as speculation. The talk ends with his proposed outline of the reconstructed document: John the Baptist's preaching, the baptism and temptation, Jesus's teaching in Capernaum, healings and the raising of the dead, a mission of the seventy, a series of teachings (Good Samaritan, Mary and Martha, the Lord's Prayer, "ask, seek, knock"), parables of the kingdom, and a closing that promises the disciples thrones judging "the 12 tribes of Israel," tied to the return of the northern tribes.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Q as a pre-Pauline, Jewish-sounding sayings source
Tabor says the material shared by Matthew and Luke but absent from Mark is mostly sayings, and that Q came early, "maybe around midcentury" and a few decades after Jesus's death. He claims it "doesn't really even reflect Paul hardly at all if at all." He treats this as significant because the Mark narrative is, in his words, "very Paul lean in its theology." He also describes Matthew as "very Jewish and Orthodox," in contrast to his reading of Luke. He says he does not agree that Matthew was written first, which he describes as "the traditional view of the church."

### 2. Luke as the more original form of Q
Tabor's principle is that Luke more often keeps Q intact while Matthew condenses and edits it, just as Matthew condenses Mark. He gives three examples:
- **Hating family (Luke 14:26):** Tabor says the Sunday school answer, that "hate" means "love God more," is Matthew's paraphrase ("he who loves father and mother more than me is not worthy of me"). He argues Luke's list (father, mother, wife, children, brothers, sisters) is rhetorical and original.
- **Sword and division:** Luke's "I've come to bring division" is set against Matthew's shorter summary.
- **Fear (Luke 12:4-5):** Luke's longer, rhetorical version, with the warning about the one who "has power to cast into hell," is preferred to Matthew's three-verse version.

He presents Matthew's editing as a consistent habit, "get to the point," and he uses Mark's paralytic story as a parallel example of Matthew shortening a narrative.

### 3. Expanding Q with Luke's special material
Tabor's method is to go through Luke verse by verse after the insertion at Luke 9:51 (chapters 10-18, which he calls "Luke's great insertion"). He sorts the text into parallel material (Matthew and Luke, not Mark), Luke-only material, and Mark material he can ignore. He then asks whether Matthew would have omitted a Luke-only passage. He answers by weighing editorial tendencies rather than certainty: "whenever a student says could the answer is yes usually could unless it's just completely crazy." His probable additions include the friend at midnight (Luke 11:5-8, with "ask, seek, knock"), the rich man and barns (Luke 12:16-21), and the lost sheep, lost coin and prodigal son (Luke 15). He argues the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25-37) and Mary and Martha (Luke 10:38-42) are "not even in Q," but that Matthew may have dropped them for subject-matter reasons.

### 4. Narrative framing and the John the Baptist opening
Tabor argues Q needed narrative hinges to make sense. He notes that Q's sayings need a speaker and context: "who's talking" and "did Q say he said to the multitudes." He proposes Luke 3 (John the Baptist's preaching and the crowd asking "what do we do," including the two-coats teaching) as Q's opening, and he says that without the framing the devil's speech in the temptation "couldn't have read like that." He argues that if you read the sayings without the framing, "then it's just like crazy." He suggests the John material may belong to Q, though Matthew omits it. He also notes that the two-coats saying "sounds like John the Baptist" to him, and asks listeners whether it "sounds like Jesus to me."

### 5. The lawyer, the Torah, and the Good Samaritan
Tabor discusses the lawyer's question about inheriting eternal life (Luke 10:25-28). He says Jesus answers with the Torah, "you will love the Lord your God... and your neighbor as yourself," and that the lawyer then asks "who's my neighbor," which sets up the parable. He presents this as a case where Jesus teaches the Torah, with the Good Samaritan as an addition that Matthew did not include. He suggests Matthew omitted the parable because of its subject: a Samaritan helping a Jew while a priest and Levite do not. He notes that Matthew's name is Levi, which he mentions in passing as a biographical note.

### 6. The reconstructed document and its ending
Tabor sketches his outline: John the Baptist preaching judgment, Jesus's baptism and temptation, the first lecture in Capernaum, healing and the raising of the dead, the mission of the seventy, teachings on neighborly love, prayer, watchfulness, wealth and the coming judgment, and a closing parable of the man going into a far country. He ends by linking the disciples' thrones "judging the 12 tribes of Israel" with the Good Samaritan theme of welcoming "those brothers and sisters from the northern tribes" home, which he ties to the prodigal son and the "lost tribes" tradition. He frames the sequence as a hypothesis, not a finished text. He says it "has a narrative" and is "not just sayings."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Matthew was written first.** Tabor disagrees with the traditional church view and argues that Luke's form is closer to Q and that Matthew reshapes it.
- **Jesus told people to "hate" their families and that means "love God more."** Tabor argues the "love more" reading is Matthew's editorial paraphrase, and that Luke's rhetorical list is closer to the original sayings.
- **The Gospel sayings and narratives are the full picture of Q.** Tabor argues that Q needed narrative framing (John the Baptist, the temptation, the Capernaum teaching) and that the sayings alone do not make sense.
