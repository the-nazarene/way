## Summary

Tabor asks whether Paul proclaimed a message in harmony with that of the historical Jesus, and he answers "no" at the outset, while noting that the question first requires defining the historical Jesus's message. He sets a working boundary: the seven letters most scholars regard as authentically Pauline (Galatians, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Philippians, Romans, 1 Thessalonians, and Philemon, which he names as the list runs), with the caveat that other Pauline letters or portions may be included. He then sets out five questions: what Paul meant by "my gospel" and whether the Jerusalem apostles shared it; how Paul understood his own calling and mission; whether his views on the imminent return of Christ changed; and whether he finally broke with the Jerusalem leaders and died in Rome under Nero.

Tabor's main evidence is Paul's own letters. He reads Romans 1-3 as a single argument: God will judge all humanity impartially, the "doers of the law" would be justified, but no one is righteous, so justification comes by grace through faith in Christ's death. He reads Galatians 1-2 as showing that Paul received his gospel by revelation and was independent of Jerusalem, meeting Peter and James only briefly, and that the Jerusalem "pillars" accepted his mission to the Gentiles, though a later clash at Antioch occurred. He argues that Paul saw himself as a new Jeremiah and as the servant of Isaiah 49, sent to bring the scattered tribes of Israel back. On the end-times, he argues that Paul expected to live to see Christ's return, as the 50s letters show, and that Paul's ethical instructions in Romans 14 and 1 Corinthians reflect that expectation.

Tabor is candid that these are his views and that some are uncertain. On Paul's death, he says he once assumed Paul died in Rome but now thinks "we don't know where he died," citing Clement of Rome's (96) silence about a second arrest. The talk ends with an overview of Paul's "big idea," a summary of his article on the quest for the historical Paul, and promotion of his books, courses and community. The transcript contains only the presentation; the "Ask Me Anything" session described in the video notes is not included.

## Key Teachings

### Paul's gospel in Romans 1-3

Tabor reads Romans 1-3 as the clearest statement of what Paul meant by "my gospel." Paul begins with God as Creator who will "judge justly all humankind both living and dead," and opens with sin and wrath against "all ungodliness and unrighteousness," which Tabor takes to describe non-Jews. Chapter 2 is, in his reading, a setup: judgment is impartial, based on "the doers" rather than "the hearers" of the law. Tabor says the law here means universal moral principles (lying, stealing, adultery, idolatry), not ritual observance, which Paul does not require of non-Jews. Paul's idea of a "Torah of the heart" lets Gentiles who lack the written Torah be judged by it. Chapter 3 then turns the argument: "there are none righteous no not one," quoting the Psalms, so all are condemned. The gospel is justification "by grace through faith based upon the death of Jesus and his blood as an atonement." Tabor compares this to the Evangelical message of forgiveness through Christ's death, and notes Romans 4 extends it through Abraham (Genesis 15:6) and Habakkuk 2:4, which Paul reads as "the one who through faith is righteous shall live."

### Independence and the Jerusalem leaders

Tabor argues Paul insists his gospel came by revelation, not from men. He quotes Galatians 1 ("I did not receive it from any man") and says Paul describes being "taught it through a revelation of Jesus Christ," not a Damascus-road conversion alone. Paul's time in Jerusalem was brief: about two weeks with Peter (Cephas) and a short meeting with James the Lord's brother. Tabor reads this as Paul making an argument: "I couldn't have got it from them." After fourteen years, Paul went up to Jerusalem to lay out his gospel to the leaders "reputed to be pillars." Tabor says they agreed that Gentile converts did not have to be circumcised, and gave Paul "the right hand of fellowship." He then describes a later clash at Antioch (Galatians 2), where Peter appears to expect Jewish observance of kosher laws and separation from Gentile converts, and Paul rebukes him. Tabor treats this as evidence that the Jerusalem agreement did not end the tension.

### Paul's self-understanding: new Jeremiah and Isaiah's servant

Tabor says Paul read his own calling into Scripture. Galatians 1 echoes Jeremiah 1:5 ("before you were born I called you... a prophet to the Nations"), and Tabor suggests Paul saw himself as a new Jeremiah. Paul also reads himself into Isaiah 49, the "Lord called me from the womb" passage. Tabor argues that Paul took the servant as himself and his people, and that his mission was to "raise up the tribes of Jacob" and bring the scattered tribes back, while Gentiles also come in, which would make Israel jealous. He credits this reading to Jason (Jason Staples in the transcript) and mentions Romans 9-11 as the relevant passage. Tabor says that Paul does not think the servant is Jesus, but rather himself and his people. He cites 2 Corinthians 12 (the "third heaven" and "abundance of Revelations") as evidence that Paul believed his apostleship was given directly by Christ.

### The imminent end and "already not yet"

Tabor argues Paul expected Christ's return within his own lifetime. The earliest letters, from the 50s, use phrases like "we who are alive and remain" (1 Thessalonians 4) and say "you will be my Crown of Glory" (1 Corinthians 15, as Tabor frames it). He ties this to events under Caligula, who tried to place his statue in the Jerusalem temple in 41, which Tabor says would have seemed a fulfillment of Daniel. He notes that 2 Thessalonians 2 warns about a "man of sin" who sits in the temple. Tabor says Nero came to power in 54, and that Paul's expectation persisted. Tabor's view is that Paul's expectation of nearness did not change within the seven letters. He allows that Philippians, written from prison, may show a slight shift ("I am persuaded that I will remain," Philippians 1; "our Commonwealth is in heaven," Philippians 3:20). He says the later letters (2 Timothy's "I fought a good fight... finished the course") show a longer horizon, but these are outside his seven-letter core.

### Ethics shaped by the expected end

Tabor argues that Paul's practical instructions make sense only if the end is near. He says Paul tells the Romans that food, drink and holy days are matters of conscience (Romans 14), that marriage and the social order should be kept as they are (including advice against divorce and against women teaching in the assembly), and that gender distinctions will vanish when Christ comes. He reads Paul's stance as "can't we just get along" while waiting for the end. Tabor compares this to Jesus's statement in Luke that in the age to come people "won't marry," and says Paul "has that view finally."

### Paul's death and break with Jerusalem

Tabor says he once believed Paul died in Rome under Nero, but has reconsidered. He points to Clement of Rome writing around 96, who (in Tabor's reading) says Paul was released and went to Spain, but gives no account of a second arrest and execution. Tabor reads Acts as depicting Roman officials (Felix, Festus, and the procurators at Caesarea) finding Paul not guilty, and suggests Paul's release is plausible. On a break with Jerusalem, Tabor says "everybody says no" but he holds open the possibility that Paul broke with the apostles late in life, and places that possibility in 2 Corinthians 10-13 (within the Corinthian correspondence he counts as two letters, with 2 Corinthians 10-13 the section he cites).

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Jerusalem apostles and Paul as one "big happy family":** Tabor says the popular picture (Jesus chooses twelve apostles, who preach, then Paul goes to the Gentiles, "all one big happy family") does not survive a close reading of Galatians 1-2, and that the Antioch clash shows continuing tension.
- **Paul learned his gospel from the Jerusalem leaders:** Tabor argues the common assumption that Paul learned from Peter and James is contradicted by Paul's own account: a brief visit, then fourteen years before returning, and a claim of direct revelation.
- **Paul's conversion as a sudden decision on the road to Damascus:** Tabor says Paul describes being "taught it through a revelation of Jesus Christ," which he argues opens a much broader message than simply recognizing Jesus as the Christ.
- **Paul's death in Rome under Nero as settled:** Tabor says he once assumed this and now thinks "we don't know where he died."

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor says directly that Paul's message was not in harmony with the historical Jesus's, but the talk does not develop Jesus's teaching in detail. Tabor defers that comparison to his other courses.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor treats James the Lord's brother and the "pillars" (James, Peter, John) as having accepted Paul's mission to the Gentiles, while noting that Paul claims he met James only briefly and that the Antioch conflict followed.
- **Torah:** Tabor argues Paul did not require ritual observance (circumcision, dietary laws, festivals) of Gentile converts, and that the Torah of the heart and "doers of the law" frame universal judgment.
- **The Kingdom:** Tabor says Paul's "big idea" is glorified children of God and the redemption of creation, and that Paul's ethics treat the kingdom as near.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** Tabor's talk touches on Paul's expectation of the dead rising first and of the living being transformed at Christ's return, but does not discuss hell.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Not directly addressed; Tabor notes that Paul does not identify the Isaiah 49 servant as Jesus.
- **Gospels and canon:** Tabor uses Acts as historical evidence for Paul's trial and treats Acts with more or less skepticism depending on scholars, but does not give a full account of canon formation.
