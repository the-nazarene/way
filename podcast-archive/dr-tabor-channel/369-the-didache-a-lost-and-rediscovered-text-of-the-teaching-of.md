## Summary

Tabor asks what the Didache, a short Greek church manual titled "The Teaching of the Twelve Apostles," can tell us about the teaching of Jesus and his first followers. He presents it as a non-Pauline witness to the Jesus movement that may preserve material earlier than some New Testament Gospel sources. He argues that its opening chapters bridge the sayings source scholars call Q and a later, expanded body of sayings, and that its moral teaching, Two Ways framework, communal ethics, baptismal practice and Eucharist prayer reflect the movement of John the Baptist, Jesus and James. He is careful to say he cannot prove the document predates the Gospels, and he calls the claim that it reflects a first-century origin plausible rather than established.

Tabor's evidence is mostly internal comparison. He lists parallels with the Sermon on the Mount and Q material, with the Letter of James, with the Qumran Community Rule's "two ways" and communal rules, and with Acts 15 and Acts 20:7. He notes that the Didache's version of the golden rule is stated negatively, as in Hillel's saying, and that its Eucharist prayers bless the cup and bread without describing Jesus's body and blood as a sacrifice. He also argues that the Trinitarian baptismal formula is a later insertion, pointing to the manuscript's later date and to the Jewish-sounding baptism instructions around it. On the manuscript side, he recounts the 1873 discovery of the Jerusalem Codex by Philotheus Bryennios, its 1056 CE colophon, and its later move to the Jerusalem patriarchate.

The talk is Part 1 of a shortened lecture given to his Patreon community. It covers the background, the manuscript history and the first six chapters, including the moral instruction and the opening baptism and Eucharist material. Tabor says he has covered only a little more than half of the document and promises a Part 2 later in the month.

## Key Teachings

### The Two Ways and the Qumran connection
Tabor opens the text itself with the "two ways," a way of life and a way of death. He links this framework to the first four columns of the Community Rule (1QS, which he calls the Serekh ha-Yahad) at Qumran, which likewise presents a way of light and a way of darkness. He also cites the Epistle of Barnabas, chapters 18 through 20, as having a similar two-paths scheme. He does not claim the Didache and Qumran are identical. He argues that the framework is an inherited Jewish pattern, and he suggests the Jesus movement, the Baptist and James stood in a related "way" tradition. He cautions against comparing a first-century CE movement to a group that began around the turn of the first century BCE, noting that movements change over 100 to 150 years.

### The golden rule in negative form, Hillel and Q
Tabor stresses that the Didache states the principle negatively: "Do not do to another what you would not want done to you." He says this is the Jewish form associated with Hillel, not the positive form in the Gospels. He sets it beside Matthew and Luke, using Bart Ehrman's edition, which prints parallel Matthew and Luke passages, to show overlap with the Q material. He argues that the author may have drawn on a version of Q that we do not have, and that his own "expanding Q" project is unfinished. He also argues that the negative form is less intrusive, because it does not impose the giver's taste on others.

### Acts 15 and the negative golden rule
Tabor reports that some manuscripts of Acts 15 contain the negative golden rule as part of James's instruction to Gentiles. He suggests that James's four necessary things (abstaining from blood, what is strangled, fornication, and idolatry) could have been supplemented by a rule about everyday conduct, consistent with Hillel's teaching. He presents this as a speculative reconstruction. He reads the Didache's rule against idolatry, against cruelty to animals and against sexual immorality as the kind of guidance he thinks the Jerusalem leadership would give Gentiles.

### The Eucharist without sacrifice
Tabor's most emphasized point concerns the Eucharist prayers (Didache chapters 9 and 10, which he reads in his talk). He says the bread and wine are blessed in a Jewish manner, with the cup called "the holy vine of David, your servant," and that Jesus is called "your servant," not Lord. He says the prayer does not describe the bread as Jesus's body or the wine as his blood, and he calls this non-Pauline. He compares it to Messianic banquet texts from the Dead Sea Scrolls and to Acts 10's description of Jesus as "a man approved of God." He also notes that the prayer ends with Maranatha and a warning that only the baptized may eat, and he suggests it reflects ordinary communal meals rather than a special ritual, with Acts 20:7 as a possible parallel.

### Baptism and the Trinitarian formula
Tabor argues the Trinitarian baptismal formula is an interpolation. He notes that the surrounding instructions favor living water, cold stream water, and a fast before baptism, which he reads as Jewish-sounding and close to the Community Rule's account of baptism into the new covenant community. He reasons that a later pious copyist could have inserted the formula, and he suggests Matthew's version may have been altered in the same way. He notes that Didaskalia and the Apostolic Constitutions later revised the text to align it with Catholic liturgy, including the body-and-blood language, which he takes as evidence of later change.

### Atonement through good deeds and the community's care for the needy
Tabor highlights the line that one should give "ransom for your sins" and explains that the Greek word lutrosis means ransom, payment or recompense. He states that the text teaches atonement through good deeds rather than animal sacrifice or the death of another, and compares this to the Dead Sea Scrolls claim that atonement comes through prayer and good deeds. He reads the teaching on giving and taking as a communal arrangement in which those who take without need will be held accountable, and he compares it to the communal life described in Acts 1 through 6 and at Qumran.

### Manuscript history and dating
Tabor summarizes the manuscript story. The text survives in one main Greek manuscript, the Jerusalem Codex (number 54), found in 1873 by Philotheus Bryennios in the library of the Jerusalem Holy Sepulchre in Constantinople. It was signed by a scribe named Leon and dated 1056 CE. Tabor notes that this is a copy of a copy, and that the original may have been first century. He cites the early testimony of Eusebius, Athanasius and others, and the publication history of 1883 to 1891. He says the dating and transmission are uncertain, but notes that the Eucharist material is hard to explain as an invention, because later editors added the body-and-blood language.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Didache is a secret or gnostic text like the Gospel of Thomas.** Tabor says it is neither; it presents itself as the teaching of the twelve apostles and is a plain catechetical manual.
- **The Didache's Eucharist describes the bread and wine as Jesus's body and blood sacrifice.** Tabor says the prayers use Jewish messianic blessing language, calling Jesus "your servant," with no sacrificial description.
- **The Trinitarian baptismal formula is original to the text.** Tabor argues it is a later insertion, citing the surrounding instructions and the manuscript's later date.
- **The "nobody's perfect, except Jesus" idea.** Tabor says the Greek telos in the Gospel command to "be perfect" means "complete," and that the instruction is to complete love, including toward enemies.
- **The positive golden rule is the only Jesus version.** Tabor says the Didache, and a manuscript of Acts 15, give the negative form, which he ties to Hillel.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor presents the Didache as a non-Pauline witness with a Eucharist, moral code and baptism shaped by Jewish practice rather than Pauline atonement theology, and he links the Didache's "way" to Acts and to Paul's own vocabulary only in passing.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor connects the Didache's moral instruction to the apostolic decree of Acts 15 and to the letter of James, and he presents the John the Baptist, Jesus and James movement as the likely background.
- **Essenes / Dead Sea Scrolls:** Tabor compares the two-ways framework, communal sharing, baptism, and atonement by good deeds with Qumran's Community Rule and other scrolls, while stressing differences in movement history.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet, nonviolence:** Tabor discusses the Didache's Torah-shaped ethics, its atonement-by-good-deeds language, its instruction on food sacrificed to idols and on not killing animals, and its ethic of turning the other cheek, with a student question about self-defense left open.
- **Gospels and canon formation:** Tabor argues the Didache preserves sayings close to a Q-type source, that it was excluded from the New Testament, and that its material may predate some Gospel sources. He places it within the reception history of Eusebius, Athanasius, Didaskalia and the Apostolic Constitutions.
