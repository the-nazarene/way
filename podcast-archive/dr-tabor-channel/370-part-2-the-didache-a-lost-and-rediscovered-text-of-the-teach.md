## Summary

This is Part 2 of James D. Tabor's lecture on the Didache (Greek for "the teaching"), a short Greek Christian manuscript whose full title he gives as "the teaching of the Lord through the Twelve Apostles to the Nations." Tabor, speaking alone, continues his walk through the text, moving from the two ways (chapters 1-6) into the sections on baptism, fasting, prayer, the Eucharist, itinerant teachers and prophets, community leadership, and the last days (chapters 7-16). His central question, carried over from Part 1, is whether this document preserves teachings of Jesus that are older than, and independent of, the Pauline theology that shaped the New Testament Gospels. He asks, in his words, "could this really come from the Jesus that the church wants us to remember or is this more likely a document that did not get copied and passed on."

Tabor's answer is that the Didache preserves a "non-Pauline" form of the Jesus movement, which he links to John the Baptist, Jesus, and James the brother of Jesus. His evidence is largely internal and comparative: parallels with the Sermon on the Mount material shared by Matthew and Luke (which he thinks reflects a hypothetical source he calls Q), the Dead Sea Scrolls (especially the Community Rule, 1QS), Acts 15, Paul's earliest letter (1 Thessalonians), and Daniel and Zechariah. He treats the Trinitarian baptism formula, the "Sunday" reading of the Lord's day, and the blood language in the Eucharist as later additions or reinterpretations. He dates the text "maybe around 100 CE" but says he personally thinks it is earlier, possibly before 70 CE, and he calls the question of whether it is pre-Pauline open.

The talk ends with Tabor answering comments from Part 1. He explains why many viewers had never heard of the text, suggests that Pauline readings of the Gospels obscure earlier material, and presents his phrase for the document's world: "when Christians were Jews." He closes by promising more on what he calls "judeo Christianity" and "the original way of Jesus."

## Key Teachings

### The two ways and the "Way" of Torah
Tabor sees the opening six chapters, the "two ways" (the way of life and the way of death), as rooted in Moses' setting before the people of life and death and in the blessings and curses of Leviticus and the exposition in Deuteronomy. He ties the document's "Way" to the name of the early movement in Acts and to Dead Sea Scrolls language about "people of the way" and "preparing the way in the wilderness," which he connects to John the Baptist's appearance in the opening of Mark, Matthew, and Luke. He reads the "yoke" as the Torah, so that the Didache offers a "Christian Torah," and he compares the minimum requirements with the James position in Acts 15 (abstaining from meat sacrificed to idols and from idolatry and fornication). He also cites a saying he calls "the Ring of Jesus": "let your alms sweat in your hands until you know to whom you should give," which he says has a double attestation with Jesus' teaching on secret giving in the Sermon on the Mount.

### Baptism, fasting, and prayer as later-edited material
Tabor argues that the Trinitarian formula in chapter 7 ("baptized in the name of the father Son and Holy Spirit") is "probably an interpolation." He says the text reads well without it, that the later part of the chapter refers to baptism "in the name of the Lord" or "in the name of Jesus," and that the formula occurs in the Gospel of Matthew (Matthew 28) but not in Acts or in Matthew quoted by Irenaeus, and that early copies of Matthew lack it. He notes the Jewish character of the running-water requirement, which he compares to the ritual purity practices of the Dead Sea Scrolls. On fasting, the Didache says to fast on Wednesday and Friday rather than the "Hypocrites" days, which Tabor identifies with the Pharisees; he reads Friday as a "preparation" day, suggesting a Sabbath-keeping group. The Lord's Prayer is said three times a day, and Tabor suggests its form reflects a collection of Jesus' sayings such as Q, not necessarily Matthew's version.

### The Eucharist: a non-Pauline meal
Tabor argues this is the most surprising part of the text. The thanksgiving begins with the cup, which "is to represent the Messianic lineage of Jesus the holy Vine of David," and then the broken bread, which stands for the church gathered "from the ends of the Earth into one Kingdom." There is no reference to the shed blood of Jesus. He compares this with the Messianic banquet of the Dead Sea Scrolls, and with 1 Corinthians 11, where Paul says he received the tradition from the Lord. He notes that Luke 22 has two cups and that some manuscripts of Luke lack the second cup, which he reads as a trace of an earlier form overwritten by Paul's influence. He also observes that the meal is a fellowship meal, the "love Feast," which Paul's instructions in 1 Corinthians 11 would change. Tabor thinks the James community would not have spoken of drinking the blood of Jesus, and he cites the fragment in the Hebrew Gospel of Matthew in which Jesus refuses to eat meat with the disciples at Passover, which he takes as a turn away from the Passover lamb.

### Teachers, prophets, and support: the Torah's tithe extended to the Nations
Tabor reads chapters 11-13 as a sociological picture of wandering teachers, prophets, and self-described apostles, and as a discernment problem. He reads the rule that a true apostle stays no more than a day or two, and that a prophet who asks for money or a meal in the Spirit is to be rejected, as a test. He flags the "unpardonable" blasphemy against the Holy Spirit as a warning against rejecting a true prophet who speaks in the Spirit. Itinerants who do not work for a living are "Christ-mongers," which he links to Paul's warning against idleness in 1 Thessalonians. He argues that the first fruits, offered to the teachers "they are your high priests," extend the Torah's tithing to Gentile believers "according to the Commandment." He notes that the Didache's "day belonging to the Lord" should not be read as Sunday and says the Greek word means "belonging to the Lord," which he connects to the quotation of Malachi-type language that ends the chapter about the Lord's name among the Nations. He reads this as "the Nations learning to do sacrifices," in which shared food and goods replace the Temple sacrifices, a pattern he compares with the Community Rule's prayers and good works as offerings.

### Community order and the last days
Tabor describes chapter 15's offices, overseers and deacons, as "worthy of the Lord," and says some Pauline and Pastoral Epistles share this vocabulary. Community discipline is to be by reproof "in peace," with shunning of anyone who acts against another until he repents, and almsgiving is to be done "not to be seen of men." Chapter 16 is for Tabor the clearest evidence of an early, intense expectation. He says "they were very apocalyptic" and that the believers "thought it was going to come very soon." He reads the world-deceiver passage as drawing on Daniel 11 (from about verse 40 on), links it with the Dead Sea Scrolls figure called "son of the most high" (which he reads with David Flusser as an evil figure, not the Messiah), and draws on Daniel 12 and Zechariah 14. He compares the "third resurrection of the dead yet not of all" with 1 Thessalonians' "dead in Christ will rise first" and with the great white throne of Revelation 20. He says the document's "this generation" horizon and the Synoptic apocalypse (Mark 13, Luke 21, Matthew 24) point to a date before or near 70 CE.

### The shape of the early Jesus movement and Paul's "non-Pauline" message
Tabor's program is set out in his remarks about the Gospels and Paul. He says that the Gospels should not be read first because they "have very heavily" been shaped by Paul, and that the Didache, the Book of James, and a reconstructed Q can give "a picture of the Christianity before Christianity." His example is the parable of the two men at the Temple (the Pharisee and the tax collector, which he gives without a verse), where the one "Justified" is justified by humility, not by a sacrifice or by Christ's blood. He sets this against what he calls Paul's "gospel" in "the first let's say four chapters of Romans." He cites Paula Fredriksen's book *When Christians Were Jews* and says the Didache "reflects when Christians were Jews." He argues that after 70 CE, once James and the Apostles were gone and "there are no Gatekeepers left," the Gentile church "moves down its own track."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Trinitarian baptism formula is original to the Didache's teaching.** Tabor says chapter 7's formula is "probably an interpolation." He argues the chapter reads well without it, that early copies of Matthew lack it, and that the text elsewhere refers to baptism "in the name of the Lord."
- **The "day belonging to the Lord" means Sunday.** Tabor says the Greek word means "belonging to the Lord" and that some reading it as Sunday may be mistaken. He links the phrase to the Lord's name and to the Nations' sacrifice.
- **The Eucharist centers on Jesus' shed blood.** Tabor says the Didache's thanksgiving says nothing about shed blood. The cup represents the Messianic lineage and the bread the gathering of the church from the ends of the earth.
- **The Didache is entirely unknown.** Tabor says scholars know it, that it was known to the early Church, and that a copy appears in a Loeb volume, though it is "not upfront" for most people.
- **Paul's theology is the earliest Christianity.** Tabor argues that Paul's Romans 1-4 presents a teaching different from the early teachings of Jesus, and that the Gospels were shaped by Paul.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor's main thesis. He contrasts the Didache's teachings (the two ways, the Temple-parable humility, and the cup and bread) with Romans 1-4 and the Eucharist of 1 Corinthians 11, and he treats the difference as a clue to earlier teaching. He also states that the Didache is "non-Pauline" and that its teaching is not the Pauline gospel.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor ties the Didache to "the James community," reads Acts 15 as the James minimum, and says the document "reflects ... what I think was going on in Jerusalem in the days of James the brother of Jesus." He also says that once Jerusalem fell and "James and all the apostles are gone," there were no gatekeepers left.
- **The Nazarenes, Ebionites, and Essenes:** Only indirectly. Tabor mentions the Dead Sea Scrolls (the Community Rule and the Messianic banquet) and the Hebrew Gospel of Matthew fragments, but he does not name the Nazarenes or Ebionites in this talk.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet, and nonviolence:** Torah is central: the two ways, the "yoke," and the tithe. Sacrifice is discussed as the Nations' "pure sacrifice" and the replacement of the Temple by shared food and good works. Diet appears through the Passover-meat fragment and the first-fruits rule. Nonviolence is not discussed.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Tabor reads the Eucharist bread as the church "gathered together ... into one Kingdom" and the Hebrew prophets' vision of the Nations coming to Jerusalem (Isaiah 2, Micah 4, Zechariah 14).
- **Resurrection, afterlife, and hell:** Tabor reads the last-days passage as describing a "third resurrection of the dead yet not of all," compares it with 1 Thessalonians and Revelation 20, and notes the "great white Throne judgment." He mentions the blasphemy against the Holy Spirit as the "unpardonable sin," not as a hell teaching.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Only briefly. Tabor says the Lord in the Didache is "probably Jesus," while other places refer to the Lord God of Israel, and he reads the Messianic lineage in the cup as centered on Jesus as Messiah.
- **How the Gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor argues that the Didache was not included in the New Testament, that the Trinitarian formula was added to Matthew, that the Gospels are heavily shaped by Paul, and that a reconstructed Q can recover earlier teaching.
