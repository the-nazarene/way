## Summary

Tabor asks whether a set of verses in the Gospels, mostly in Luke 24, are later additions (interpolations) that crept into the mainstream Greek text, and whether readers should remove them. He frames the video as a test of "sensing incongruity" in the text. His main evidence is the nine readings that Westcott and Hort (1896 edition, per Tabor) printed in brackets as "Western non-interpolations." These readings are absent from Codex Bezae (D) and the wider Western text tradition, but present in Vaticanus, Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus and the Byzantine tradition. Tabor walks through seven Luke 24 readings: the "Lord Jesus" and "he is not here but has risen" phrases in verses 3 and 6, Peter's run to the tomb in verse 12, the "Peace to you" greeting in verse 36, the showing of hands and feet in verse 40, the ascension phrase "was carried up to heaven" in verse 51, and the worship and "great joy" wording in verse 52. He argues most of these look like pious expansions, often harmonizations with the Gospel of John. He also mentions Matthew 27:49 (the spear and blood-and-water verse) as a ninth-category example of harmonization, and says the Luke 22 Last Supper variant, which he thinks may align with the Didache, is the major case he will take up in a later video.

The talk ends with Tabor's account of how the Westcott and Hort brackets were handled. The Bruce Metzger textual commentary records a committee divided over these passages, and the majority of the committee retained the longer readings. The RSV removed them to footnotes, and some modern translations keep them with notes. Tabor says he still prefers the RSV for academic use. He presents his own verdicts as judgments ("ding ding double ding"), and the transcript shows he is making a case, not reporting a consensus.

Note on the count: the title and opening say "seven" and "nine." In the talk, the seven Luke 24 readings are the bulk of the case, Matthew 27:49 is a further example, and Luke 22 is deferred to the next video.

## Key Teachings

### The "Western non-interpolations" as a test case
Tabor explains that Westcott and Hort identified nine readings that appear in the Byzantine and Alexandrian lines but not in the Western text (represented by Codex Bezae). Westcott and Hort, as Tabor reads them, judged the Western omissions to be the original and the longer readings to be additions, so they called the Western text "non-interpolated" in these places. Tabor says the label is confusing and that he is treating the longer readings as interpolations while the Western text lacks them.

### Luke 24:3 and 24:6: "the body of the Lord Jesus" and "he has risen"
Tabor argues that "of the Lord Jesus" in verse 3 is the kind of pious expansion a worshipful scribe would add, since a plain "they did not find the body" reads better. In verse 6 he argues that "he is not here but has risen" reads like an Easter proclamation and that "the body's not there" is the more original sense.

### Luke 24:12, 24:34: Peter's run to the tomb and the Emmaus/Simon verse
Tabor says verse 12 (Peter running to the tomb) was probably added to match the Gospel of John 20:1-10, where Peter and the "Beloved disciple" run to the tomb. He notes that Luke never mentions the Beloved disciple, which he takes as evidence against the addition being Lukan. He also flags verse 34 (the disciples' report that the Lord "appeared to Simon") as missing from the Western text, and places the judgment with Westcott and Hort that it was added to conform to the Gospel traditions.

### Luke 24:36, 24:40: the greeting and the hands and feet
Tabor reads "Peace to you" in verse 36 as a later liturgical flourish, since the greeting is routine in churches. For verse 40 (showing hands and feet) he argues it was added to echo the Thomas episode in John 20:20-27 ("touch me," "put your finger in the wounds"), and that the Western text's simpler wording avoids that harmonization.

### Luke 24:51: the ascension and the "Gospel of Luke" question
Tabor argues that "was carried up to heaven" in verse 51 is probably a later addition. He says a reader without the phrase would wonder where Jesus went (he mentions India, Egypt, and the survival-of-the-cross legends). He acknowledges the ascension in Acts 1 and says whether Acts and Luke share an author is still debated among scholars. He calls this "a significant one" and says it is hard to imagine a believer removing it, but easy to imagine a scribe inserting it.

### Luke 24:52 and Matthew 27:49: worship and the spear
Verse 52's "worshiped him" and "returned to Jerusalem with great joy" is, for Tabor, the seventh reading and a likely pious addition. He concludes "without much doubt" the original text of Luke lacked these seven. For Matthew 27:49 (the spear and the blood and water), he says the verse appears in Vaticanus and Sinaiticus and is absent from the Western text, and he places it in the same category of harmonization with John.

### The textual witnesses and the committee dispute
Tabor describes the manuscript base: Vaticanus (B), Alexandrinus (A), Sinaiticus (aleph), all fourth century, versus Codex Bezae (D), which he dates to the late fourth or early fifth century (he says he has seen a sixth-century date). He ties the Western text to Marcion, Tatian, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Cyprian and Augustine, while stating it is "not necessarily the most accurate." He reports that the Metzger commentary's committee split, with a minority favoring brackets and a majority retaining the longer readings, and he shows this as an example of how textual decisions are contested.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Popular belief:** The longer resurrection readings in Luke 24 are part of the original Gospel. **Tabor's correction:** He argues they are later additions, mostly harmonizations with John and expansions of worship language, and that the Western text (Codex Bezae) preserves a shorter, likely earlier form.
- **Popular belief:** Omitted verses in modern translations are simply the result of recent liberal scholarship. **Tabor's correction:** He says Westcott and Hort (1896) first bracketed these readings, and the RSV (1946) moved them to footnotes, so the textual question is older than the RSV controversy.
- **Popular belief:** Criticism of the RSV's omissions is about the Bible's protection from corruption. **Tabor's correction:** He describes the reaction to the RSV (including public burnings) as an overreaction, and says Revelation 22:18-19 is misapplied in this context.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Gospel formation and the canon:** Tabor's central claim is that the longer Luke 24 and Matthew 27:49 readings were harmonized with John and expanded by pious scribes. This bears directly on how the Gospel texts were transmitted and how the canonical text took shape.
- **Resurrection accounts:** Several of the disputed verses concern the appearances, the empty tomb, and the ascension. Tabor's view is that the shorter Western readings leave the resurrection story less developed than the standard text.
- **Luke-Acts:** Tabor notes that whether the ascension in Luke 24:51 belongs with Acts depends on whether the two share an author, a question he says scholars are still debating.
