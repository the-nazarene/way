## Summary

Tabor asks whether the words of the Last Supper that Christians know, "this is my body" and "this is my blood," go back to the historical Jesus or came from Paul. He argues they do not. His alternative reading is that Jesus's final meal was a prophetic enactment (he calls it a "prolepsis," transcribed as "prpic") of a future Messianic banquet, when the Kingdom of God would come and the tribes of Israel would be regathered. Tabor's main evidence comes from four sources: Luke 22 with its disputed verses removed, the Didache's Eucharist prayers, Jeremiah 16 and Isaiah 11 on the regathering, and a Dead Sea Scroll fragment describing a Messianic banquet. He sets 1 Corinthians 11:23-26 against these as the source of the sacramental, body-and-blood version.

The talk is delivered almost entirely by Tabor, who reads and comments on the texts himself. He says that the Codex Bezae reading of Luke omits the words of Luke 22:19b-20, and that the Didache's order (cup first, then bread, with no reference to body or blood) reflects an earlier tradition. He reads Jeremiah 16 and the Scroll's description of a banquet at which the priest blesses the bread and wine before the Messiah, and he presents these as the frame in which Jesus would have understood the meal. He is explicit that his conclusions are a reconstruction and that he does not regard the Didache's text as the exact words Jesus spoke.

The video ends with Tabor's invitation to a blog post containing the full documentation and the Greek text, and a closing statement that the historical Jesus's Messianic hope, rooted in Isaiah 11 and Jeremiah 30-31, was later overlaid by what he calls Paul's version of Christianity. He presents this as his own view and says he would not credit Paul with the whole of later Christianity.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Luke 22 as a prophetic enactment, once the interpolations are removed
Tabor reads Luke 22 as Jesus saying he has "earnestly desired to eat this Passover" with the disciples before his suffering, but will not eat it again "until it's fulfilled in the Kingdom of God." He argues that the cup-then-bread order fits Jewish table practice, where wine is blessed before bread. He says Codex Bezae (which he dates "probably fifth century CE" while noting it "might be correct" as early as the 2nd century) omits Luke 22:19b-20, and he treats the body-and-blood words as theological interpolations. With them removed, he says, the passage reads as "just a reenactment a prpic acting out of what's going to happen when the kingdom of God comes." He ties this to Luke 22:30, where the disciples are to "sit on Thrones judging the 12 tribes of Israel."

### 2. The Didache's Eucharist prayers as an earlier pattern
Tabor argues that the Didache, which he dates "very early 100 CE" and notes that "some put it even before the year 70," gives a Eucharist in which the cup is blessed first and the prayers mention no body or blood. The cup prayer thanks God "for the holy Vine of your son David," and the bread prayer speaks of "life and knowledge" made known through Jesus. The bread, "scattered upon the mountains and being gathered together and it became one," is read as the regathering of Israel and the nations. He suggests this reflects a Q-like or proto-Lukan tradition that later writers overwrote, and he says he cannot be sure the Didache preserves Jesus's exact words.

### 3. Regathering the tribes: Jeremiah 16 and Isaiah 11
Tabor reads Jeremiah 16 as a "new Exodus" text in which Yahweh will be remembered as the one "who brought up the people of Israel out of the North country," and not only out of Egypt. He says Isaiah 11 makes regathering the tribes and drawing the nations the central task of the Messiah. On this reading, the Passover Jesus anticipated was a future gathering of all Israel, which he presents as the setting in which the bread and wine make sense.

### 4. The Dead Sea Scroll Messianic banquet
Tabor cites a fragment he says was found in Cave 1, "in that jar with the Isaiah scroll and a copy of What's called the community rule," with columns 1 and 2 describing a Messianic banquet. In the text, "no one shall extend his hand over the first portion of the bread or the wine before the priest," and the priest blesses the bread and new wine before "the Messiah Israel" extends his hand. Tabor draws parallels with the Scroll community: both call themselves the "people of the New Covenant," both practise a ritual baptism, both share goods in common, and both anticipate two Messiahs (a priestly one and a Davidic one). He frames the Jesus movement as close to these expectations, while stressing that the groups were separate and separated by time.

### 5. Paul's account as a visionary source
Tabor reads 1 Corinthians 11:23-26 as Paul saying he "received from the Lord" what he passes on, and he takes this as a claim to a vision or revelation rather than a link to the apostles. He says the Corinthian meal was a "love Feast," an Agape meal opened by blessing bread and wine, and that Paul wanted it reduced to a sacramental memorial of Jesus's death because some "are eating and drinking too much." In Tabor's account, this is the origin of the body-and-blood ceremony that Mark, Matthew and Luke later follow.

### 6. Plausibility: the blood prohibition and James
Tabor says that within Judaism, eating blood is forbidden, citing Genesis 9 and Acts 15 for Gentiles as well as Jews. He says that drinking blood, even symbolically, is "really kind of Unthinkable" for a Jew, and that it is "very hard to imagine" Jesus or his brother James, who led the Jerusalem community, using such a ceremony. He presents the historical plausibility argument as a reason to prefer the Didache and Luke reconstruction.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The words "this is my body" and "this is my blood" come from Jesus at the Last Supper.** Tabor argues they come from Paul's visionary account in 1 Corinthians 11, and that the Luke version containing them is an interpolation absent from Codex Bezae.
- **Eating and drinking blood, even symbolically, would have been acceptable to Jesus and his Jewish followers.** Tabor says the blood prohibition (Genesis 9, Acts 15) makes this "really kind of Unthinkable" for a Jew.
- **Christian Eucharist practice reflects the original meal.** Tabor says the Last Supper's original meaning was a prophetic enactment of the future Messianic banquet, which later Christianity moved toward a memorial of Jesus's death.
- **Christianity and Jesus are the same thing.** Tabor says "Christianity is not Jesus," and that Paul's version, not Jesus's own message, is the origin of the sacramental practice.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** This is the talk's core question. Tabor contrasts Paul's 1 Corinthians 11 account, which he ties to a revelation, with the Luke (minus interpolations) and Didache versions, which he links to a Messianic banquet expectation.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor uses James's role as leader in Jerusalem as a reason to doubt that the body-and-blood ceremony came from Jesus's Jewish circle.
- **The Essenes / Dead Sea Scrolls:** The Cave 1 banquet text is the talk's main non-Christian parallel. Tabor says the Scroll group is "not the same group" as the Jesus movement, but shares its eschatological meal and dual-Messiah expectations.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet:** Genesis 9 and Acts 15 on the blood prohibition are used as the main argument about diet and plausibility.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Tabor's thesis that the meal anticipates the Kingdom, the regathering of the tribes, and the nations' inclusion (Isaiah 11, Jeremiah 16) is the central reading.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Codex Bezae's omission of Luke 22:19b-20 and the claim of later interpolations are presented as evidence about how the texts developed. Tabor offers these as his reading, not as settled.
