## Summary

Tabor asks what the post-crucifixion appearance accounts are meant to convey, using the playful question "Was Jesus naked at his resurrection?" He says the question matters because it exposes a gap between Paul's account of a spiritual resurrection body and the more physical "touch me" appearances in Luke and John. He notes that if the Gospel accounts are read literally, a body leaving the tomb would be naked, since John 20 leaves the burial shrouds behind. He draws on the way artists have handled the nude or partly clothed risen Christ (a Titian painting, Michelangelo, and two films) to show how much this question has been treated as a point of Christian imagery.

Tabor's main evidence is textual. He sets Matthew 28 and John 20, where Jesus is physically encountered outside the tomb, against Mark 16:8, which he treats as the original ending with no appearances, and Luke, which he says limits such witness to the men. He then points to 2 Corinthians 5, where Paul uses the image of clothing for the resurrection body and speaks of a "naked" soul being re-clothed, and to 1 Corinthians 15, where Paul describes the resurrection as the raising of a spiritual body. He also cites Jesus's answer in Luke 20 about those raised being "equal to angels." Tabor argues that Paul's 1 Corinthians 15 affirmation of burial implies a tomb, but that Paul does not rely on a missing body as proof, and that for Paul resurrection is not the resuscitation of an intact corpse.

Tabor closes by arguing that Paul's letters are the only first-person account and predate the Gospels by "at least two or three decades," so Paul is more likely to represent the original faith of Jesus's first followers in Galilee and Judea. He also notes that Paul puts his own sightings of Jesus on a par with those of Peter, James, and the other apostles. The talk is brief and ends on that claim, with the fuller argument pointed to his blog posts.

## Key Teachings

### The face-value reading produces a naked Jesus
Tabor's opening move is a reductio. If the appearance stories are taken "literally, at face value," the shrouds that wrapped the corpse were left in the tomb (John 20), so "we can only assume that the author intended to portray Jesus coming out of the tomb naked." He presents this tongue-in-cheek, but uses it to raise the broader question of what the appearance stories are meant to convey.

### Paul's clothing imagery for the resurrection body
In 2 Corinthians 5, Tabor says Paul uses clothing as an image for the new body. On his reading, Jesus has shed the physical body "like old clothing left behind," and his soul has been re-clothed with a spiritual body. Paul contrasts this "life-giving spirit" with the flesh-and-blood, corruptible body of dust that humans have now. Tabor says Paul makes this "even more explicit" in 1 Corinthians 15.

### Jesus's own statement in Luke 20
Tabor cites Jesus's reply to skeptical opponents about resurrection in Luke 20. He reads it as saying that those who attain the resurrection neither marry nor are given in marriage, being "equal to angels and are sons of God." He treats this as implying a transformed, immortal state. On his account, Paul would answer the question of the risen Jesus's body differently from Matthew, John, and Luke: Jesus was "fully re-clothed in a new spiritual body."

### Burial without a proof-from-the-empty-tomb
Tabor says Paul's statement in 1 Corinthians 15 that Jesus "died, was buried, and was raised" does imply a tomb burial. He argues, however, that Paul does not treat the missing body as the "traditional Gospel" proof. For Paul, the resurrection of the dead "is not the resuscitation of Jesus's intact corpse."

### Priority of Paul as first-person evidence
Tabor's central historical claim is that "Paul's view is our only first-person account," and that his account "predates that of our Gospel writers by at least two or three decades." He therefore argues it is "more likely representative of the original faith of Jesus's first followers in Galilee and Judea."

### Paul's sightings equated with the apostles'
Tabor notes that Paul "equates his own sightings of Jesus with that Peter, James, and the other apostles." He presents this as part of the case that the earliest view of the appearances was not a physical body walking out of a tomb, though the talk does not develop the argument beyond this point.

### Mark's ending and the spread of appearance accounts
Tabor summarizes the Gospels' appearance stories: Matthew and John have physical encounters outside the tomb, Mark has none (16:8 as the original ending), and Luke limits apostolic witness to men. He directs viewers to his blog post on the strange ending of Mark for the fuller argument. The talk does not develop this point further.
