## Key Teachings

### The temple trade was legitimate; the issue was value and profit
Tabor states that "the popular idea that Jesus objected to money changing in the temple is incorrect." He explains that Jews from around the world needed to convert coinage for offerings, and pilgrims needed to buy sacrificial animals at the temple, especially at Passover. He says the only coins accepted were silver Tyrian shekels and half shekels, which he describes as guaranteed to be 95% pure silver. He argues that the objection was not to pagan images but to the Sadducean priests' justification that the purity of the offering outweighed the images. He also notes that the Jerusalem temple had "the most lucrative system of Temple commerce in the entire Roman world," with added fees and surcharges. He presents this as a "temple state" economy in which "one needs only to follow the money."

### Monday's action as symbolic prophetic protest
Tabor reads Zechariah's end-times statement that there will "no longer be traders in the house of Yahweh of hosts," along with Jeremiah's "den of robbers" line and Isaiah's vision of a "house of prayer for all nations." He argues Jesus's overturning of tables and blocking of gates with Galilean men was meant to signal that "the imminent overthrow of the corrupt Temple system was at hand," not to spark a revolution. He cites Mark for the detail that Jesus would not let anyone carry anything through the temple. He says the crowd was immense and supportive, and that the priests did not dare call in the Romans, since Pilate was known for brutality toward temple crowds.

### Tuesday: the Shema and love over sacrifice
Tabor describes Jesus's Tuesday debates with the Sadducean priests, leading Pharisees and Herodians, including the Roman tax question and the Caesar coin reply. He presents the greatest-commandment exchange as central to Jesus's view of true religion. Jesus quotes the Shema, and then names the love of one's neighbor as the second commandment. Tabor reports that the man agreed love of God and neighbor "would be much more than all whole burnt offerings and sacrifices," which Tabor uses to show a spiritual priority over the temple system.

### The kingdom of God: political overthrow and inner insight
Tabor reads Jesus's reply "You are not far from the kingdom of God" as showing that Jesus's kingdom "involved not only the revolutionary overthrow of the kingdoms of the world, but also a certain spiritual insight into what God most desires from human beings." He concludes that "One would not be complete without the other."

### The plot: fear of the crowd and the need to act fast
Tabor argues that the Gospels report repeatedly that the enemies wanted to arrest Jesus but feared the crowds. The officials' hope, in his reading, was to confront Jesus when he was alone, perhaps at night with few followers. With Passover two days away and an unknown sense of what Jesus "might be capable" of, they decided they had to act quickly.
