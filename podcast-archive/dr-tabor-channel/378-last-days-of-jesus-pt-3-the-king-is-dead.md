## Summary

Tabor asks what can be reliably reconstructed of Jesus's final week (the last meal, the arrest, the "trials," and the crucifixion) once later theological overlay is set aside. He presents a sequence in which the last meal was on Wednesday night, Jesus was crucified on Thursday, and the Gospel accounts of the Passover timing reflect a misreading. He argues that the "eat my body, drink my blood" words of the Last Supper, which first appear in Paul's letter to Corinth, are unlikely to go back to Jesus. His main evidence is that the Gospel of John has no such words, that the idea is alien to Judaism, and that the Didache, an early and apparently independent Christian text, mentions neither blood nor flesh in its account of the Eucharist. He also argues that the trials were a largely Roman political operation, that Pilate is portrayed in the Gospels inaccurately, and that the crucifixion details can be grounded in Josephus and in two archaeological finds (the Yehohanan ossuary and the Caiaphas family tomb).

The talk ends with Jesus's death: after the crucifixion and the spear thrust, Tabor concludes, "The king was dead." Throughout, Tabor treats some of his identifications as his own, such as taking the "other disciple" and the disciple whom Jesus loved to be his brother James, and presents the Last Supper chronology as his reading of John.

## Key Teachings

### The chronology: Wednesday supper, Thursday crucifixion
Tabor argues that Jesus's last meal was on Wednesday night and the crucifixion on Thursday, one day earlier than the traditional Thursday/Friday sequence. He says that in 30 AD the 15th of Nisan fell on a Friday and was itself a Sabbath, so the Gospels' references to a rush before the Sabbath refer to two back-to-back Sabbaths (Friday and Saturday). He cites Matthew's plural "Sabbaths" for the women's visit to the tomb, and John's statement that the accusers would not enter Pilate's courtyard so as not to be defiled before eating Passover. He concludes that Jesus never ate the Passover meal.

### The Eucharist words and their likely origin
Tabor's earliest witness for the last meal is Paul's letter to Corinth, written around 54 AD, which he says passes on a tradition Paul received from Jesus. He argues that the words "This is my body..." and "This cup is the new covenant in my blood" are unlikely to be Jesus's, because eating flesh and drinking blood "is completely alien to Judaism." He suggests the closest parallels are Greco-Roman magical rites. He notes that Paul grew up in Tarsus outside the land of Israel and claimed his link to Jesus was visionary. He adds that Mark, writing about a decade after Paul, probably inserted the tradition under Paul's influence, and that Matthew and Luke depend on Mark.

### John's supper and the Didache as an independent witness
Tabor points out that John's account of the Wednesday meal has no bread-and-wine words, but instead describes an ordinary Jewish supper followed by Jesus washing the disciples' feet and Judas leaving. To find a Christian source independent of Paul, he turns to the Didache, which was found in 1873 in a library at Constantinople and which he dates to the early 2nd century. He says it contains baptism and Eucharist instructions with "no mention of the wine representing blood or the bread representing flesh."

### A Roman operation and an illegal night trial
Tabor argues that Jesus's arrest was an operation authorized by Pilate, with leading priests and Temple police accompanying a Roman cohort (he cites John's figure of 600 men). He says Jesus was first taken to a private house, likely that of Annas, and that the Jewish leaders were motivated by threats to temple trade. He calls the night hearing clandestine and illegal, since the full Sanhedrin would have met in daylight in the Temple compound. He reads Jesus's answer about the Son of Man as an admission that he was Israel's king, which he says was all his accusers needed. He presents the Annas house and the Caiaphas tomb as archaeological support for the trial's setting.

### Pilate's portrait versus the Gospels' portrait
Tabor states that "Scholars are agreed" the Gospel trial scenes before Pilate are historically unreliable, shaped by a later tradition that blamed the Jews and excused Rome. Citing Philo, he describes Pilate as inflexible, vindictive, and prone to handling arrests without trial. He reconstructs the political charges (threat to stability, opposing Roman taxes, claiming to be king), a referral to Herod Antipas, a flogging, and a placard reading "the king of the Jews." He says the two men crucified alongside Jesus were likely *lestai* (a term Josephus uses for Zealot brigands) from a recent uprising.

### Crucifixion details and the bone evidence
Tabor draws on Josephus's description of crucifixion and his report of 500 crucifixions a day during the 70 AD siege. He then uses the 1968 Yehohanan ossuary to argue that nails went through the forearms and heel bones, that the legs were broken to hasten death, and that the Jewish law of same-day burial shaped the timing. He presents this as physical evidence, noting that the Gospel word for "hands" can include forearms.

### Jesus's expectation of the kingdom
Tabor argues that Jesus expected a sudden, dramatic manifestation of God's kingdom, perhaps an earthquake destroying the Herodian Temple, the sun darkening, the dead being raised, and heavenly armies appearing, and that he expected this on "the prophetic third day." He says that until the last minutes Jesus may have believed God would intervene. He takes Jesus's cry of Psalm 22 as an expression of this expectation, not as despair alone.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus was crucified on a Friday after a Thursday Last Supper that was the Passover meal.** Tabor says the last meal was Wednesday, the crucifixion Thursday, and Jesus never ate the Passover meal; the Friday date came from assuming the Sabbath mentioned was Saturday.
- **The Eucharist words of "body and blood" came from Jesus at the Last Supper.** Tabor argues they first appear in Paul, are alien to Judaism, are absent from John and the Didache, and probably derive from Greco-Roman sources or Paul's visionary claims.
- **The Gospels' Pilate, who found Jesus innocent and tried to release him, is historically accurate.** Tabor says the portrait is inaccurate and that Pilate was known for brutality and disregard for legal procedure.
- **The Jews were chiefly responsible for Jesus's death.** Tabor argues the result "was Roman through and through," with the Jewish leaders' role limited to accusation and the arrest being a Roman operation.
- **Jesus resisted arrest with the sword.** Tabor says "we have no evidence that Jesus resisted arrest," though all four Gospels report a scuffle.

The hapless victims of crucifixion, left on the crosses for days, were a common sight to the Jewish population. Josephus reported that during the Roman siege of Jerusalem in the summer of 70 AD, the number of captives crucified reached 500 a day, so many that there was no wood left in the area as all the trees had been cut down. We know quite a bit about the methods the Romans used in crucifying their victims.

**[23:11]** In 1968, the skeletal remains of a Jewish male victim were discovered in a tomb just north of Jerusalem off the Nablus Road. He was in his 20s and his name, Yehohanan, was inscribed on his ossuary. His remains have given us an amazing glimpse into the details involved in Roman crucifixion as it was practiced in 1st century Roman Jerusalem. We know that the nails were put through the forearms and not the hands. Physiologists have shown that nails through the hands will not hold the weight of a body and nails through the wrists would have ruptured blood vessels. The science of crucifixion required that nails be affixed in a way to minimize bleeding. Otherwise, the victim would quickly pass out and die in a matter of minutes.

**[24:01]** The references in the Gospels to Jesus' hands being pierced use a Greek word that can be understood as including the forearms. The feet were nailed through the heel bone. It is the largest bone in the foot and as with the forearm, puncturing this bone will not cause profuse bleeding. In the case of Yehohanan, the nail is still intact through his heel bone. When he was removed from the cross, the nail had bent on a knot in the wood and whoever removed him simply cut the wood away leaving it attached to his foot. Death by crucifixion was a slow process. It could take as long as two or three days. The victims were stripped naked exposed to the scorching Mediterranean sun.

**[24:47]** Death resulted from a combination of shock, exhaustion, muscle cramps, dehydration, loss of blood, and finally suffocation or heart failure. Depending on the angle in which the arms and legs were nailed, death could be brought on more quickly or extended. The buttocks were supported by a piece of wood called a sedecula that offered some support of the body. Over time, as fatigue set in, breathing became acutely difficult. If there was a reason to hasten death, the legs of the victim could be broken, causing the body to slump and making breathing impossible after just a short time. The Romans often left the corpses to rot on crosses, but the Jews had a law requiring those hung on a tree to be buried the same day as they were crucified.

**[25:39]** When allowed, Jews removed the bodies before sundown and buried them. Since Jehohanan's legs were broken, his death was likely hastened to allow for burial the same day as his crucifixion. Jesus and the other two victims were put on their crosses by 9:00 a.m. Thursday. Whether Jesus expected God would rescue him before things went that far is impossible to say. What Jesus likely expected was a sudden, dramatic, and overwhelming manifestation of the kingdom of God. Perhaps a great earthquake that would destroy the Herodian temple, with the sun darkening, the moon turning blood red, the dead being raised, and the appearance of legions of heavenly armies in the sky.

**[26:26]** For Jesus, the prophetic third day had arrived, and the coming of the Son of Man in the clouds of heaven was imminent. The Gospels report that the chief priests and others who had supported them taunted the victims, aiming particular scorn at Jesus. "Let the king of Israel come down from the cross that we may believe." Standing at a distance was Jesus' mother, Mary, as well as Mary Magdalene and the other women who'd followed him from Galilee on this last journey to Jerusalem. According to the Gospel of John, the disciple whom Jesus loved was also present with Jesus' mother. Late in the day when Jesus began to think he might die after all, he officially put his mother in the care of this disciple, whom I have identified as his brother James, now to be the eldest of the family.

**[27:20]** According to Mark, Jesus was on the cross from the 6th to the 9th hour. That is 9:00 a.m. to 3:00 p.m. Toward the end, he began to sense his life slipping away. He cried out with a loud voice in his native Aramaic tongue, "Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani." Those are the opening words of Psalm 22, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" At that point, he bowed his head and breathed his last. Up until the last minutes, perhaps, Jesus believed that God would intervene, save his life, and openly manifest his kingdom.

**[28:02]** Since the Jewish Passover meal or Seder was to be eaten just after sundown that evening, the high priests had asked the Romans to break the legs of the victims to hasten death. The Gospel of John remarks, "They did not want the bodies to remain on the cross during the Sabbath, especially since that Sabbath was a high day." When they came to Jesus, he appeared to be completely lifeless. One of the soldiers thrust a spear into Jesus' side just to be sure. He did not flinch. The king was dead.
