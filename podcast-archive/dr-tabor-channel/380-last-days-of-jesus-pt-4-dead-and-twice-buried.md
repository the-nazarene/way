## Summary

Tabor asks what happened to Jesus's body after the crucifixion and how the belief in his resurrection began. He is the only speaker. The talk is part 4 of a Holy Week series, and it ends by previewing part 5 on how resurrection faith moved from a glorified spiritual body to a revived physical corpse.

He argues that three facts are "indisputable": Jesus was truly dead, he was hastily buried in an unknown tomb, and the movement he began continued under the leadership of his brother James. He rejects the swoon theory and the more exotic alternatives (India, France, Masada) as lacking any basis in reliable sources. He also argues that the earliest Gospel, Mark, originally ended at the empty tomb with no appearances, and that the appearance tradition is first attested in Paul's letter that he calls First Corinthians, dated by Tabor to around 54 AD. He places the Gospels of Matthew, Luke and John 40 to 70 years after Jesus's death, written by non-witnesses who were removed in time and place from Jerusalem.

Tabor's guess about the empty tomb is that Jesus's mother Mary, his sister Salome, and perhaps Mary and Martha moved the body from the temporary tomb after the Sabbath, for a proper burial. He ends by suggesting that the family later had a tomb in Jerusalem, pointing to traditions about James's burial in the Kidron Valley and about Mary's burial near the crucifixion site, with the Mount of Olives area and Talpiot as his best-evidence locations.

## Key Teachings

### The burial was hasty and temporary

Tabor argues that Jesus's death on Thursday afternoon left no time for proper preparation. Jesus's family was from Galilee and owned no burial cave in Jerusalem. With the Passover approaching, Joseph of Arimathea and Nicodemus, both described as Sanhedrin members, got Pilate's permission and placed the body in a tomb. Tabor stresses that this was not Joseph's own tomb, which he calls a common assumption. He draws on John's detail of a new tomb in a garden. The body was wrapped in linen and laid on a stone slab without the usual washing and anointing, meant only as a temporary resting place until after the holidays.

### Site of the crucifixion and burial

Tabor treats the traditional sites as doubtful. He says the Church of the Holy Sepulchre tradition dates from the 4th century, about 300 years after the crucifixion, and that the Protestant Garden Tomb has been dated to the Iron Age or 5th century BC, too early for a newly hewn tomb. He proposes instead the Mount of Olives east of the city, reasoning from the phrase "outside the camp" as a ritual boundary of about 2,000 cubits, and from Roman preference for crucifixion on roads and hills.

### The empty tomb and the question of who moved the body

Tabor compares the four Gospels' accounts of the Sunday visit. In John, Mary Magdalene finds the tomb already open and runs for Peter and "the disciple whom Jesus loved"; they find only the linen. Tabor notes that at this stage "No one leaped to any conclusion about Jesus being raised from the dead. At that point, it was a matter of a missing corpse." He proposes that Jesus's mother and sister, perhaps with Mary and Martha, moved the body after the Sabbath to a permanent tomb and prepared it properly. He labels this a guess ("One is left to guess here") and notes that the proposal depends on trusting John's account.

### Mark's original ending had no appearances

Tabor's central textual claim is that the original text of Mark has no resurrection appearances and ends with the women fleeing the tomb and saying nothing. He says the longer ending was added by copyists "sometime in the 4th century AD," more than 300 years after composition, and that Mark was written around 70 AD. He takes this to mean appearance reports were not considered essential to the gospel story at that date.

### Paul's list is the earliest account of sightings

Tabor says the earliest witness to sightings is Paul in First Corinthians, around 54 AD. Paul reports receiving the tradition that Jesus "died, was buried, and rose on the third day," and lists appearances to Cephas, the Twelve, 500 disciples, James, all the apostles, and finally Paul. Tabor argues that Paul puts his own visionary experience on the same level as those of the founders, and that Paul "seems to be willing to use the term resurrection to refer to something akin to an apparition or vision." The Gospels, by contrast, describe a revived body "wounds and all."

### The Gospels proclaim rather than only report

Tabor says the Gospels of Matthew, Luke and John were written by authors who were not original witnesses and did not live in Roman Palestine. By then the resurrection was "the fundamental cornerstone" and Paul's version had "largely triumphed." He says this does not make the Gospels worthless historically, but that their purpose in reporting the empty tomb and sightings is to proclaim the resurrection and Jesus's death "for the salvation of all humankind." He adds that by then "James was dead and on the way to being forgotten," and Peter and Paul were remembered as founders.

### Family tombs and James

In the closing section Tabor suggests that the Jesus movement under James "eventually reestablished itself" in Jerusalem, and that the family eventually had a tomb there. He cites a tradition that James was buried in the Kidron Valley below the Mount of Olives, and a tradition that Jesus's brothers later buried Mary near the crucifixion place. He says this area, around the Mount of Olives, the Kidron and Hinnom junction, or further south at Talpiot, "seems to fit our best evidence."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Joseph of Arimathea's tomb was where Jesus was first laid.** Tabor says this is commonly assumed but was not the case; the tomb was a new one in a garden, not the family's or Joseph's own.
- **The Church of the Holy Sepulchre and the Garden Tomb are authentic burial sites.** Tabor argues both are doubtful: the first rests on a 4th-century tradition, and the second has been dated too early.
- **Jesus survived the cross and went on to India, France, or Masada.** Tabor says the swoon theory and these variants have no basis in reliable historical sources.
- **Mark originally contained resurrection appearances.** Tabor says the original ended at the empty tomb; the appearance material was added in the 4th century.
- **The Gospels are eyewitness accounts of the resurrection.** Tabor says Matthew, Luke and John were written by non-witnesses decades later, with the purpose of proclaiming the resurrection.
