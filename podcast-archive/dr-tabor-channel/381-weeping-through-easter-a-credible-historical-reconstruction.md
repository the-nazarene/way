## Summary

Tabor asks whether the New Testament sources allow a credible historical reconstruction of what happened on the Sunday after Jesus's crucifixion, and he frames the talk as neither faith-building nor faith-destroying. His answer rests on the claim that Jesus's body was never permanently buried. Tabor argues that Joseph of Arimathea placed Jesus in a nearby new tomb only temporarily, because of the Passover and Sabbath rush, and that Joseph meant to complete the burial after the holidays. On this reading the empty tomb has a mundane explanation, and the women arrived too late to find Joseph. ## Key Teachings

### 1. A temporary burial forced by the Passover and Sabbath
Tabor argues that Jesus died late on the day before the Sabbath and Passover, so his body had to be removed before sundown. He cites Deuteronomy 21:22-23 as the basis of the Jewish rule against leaving executed criminals hanging overnight, and he quotes Josephus on taking down the condemned before sunset. Mark 15:42-46 supplies Joseph of Arimathea, a member of the Sanhedrin, who obtained the body from Pilate. Because there was no time for full burial rites (washing, anointing), Tabor says the women of Jesus's circle planned to finish them after the holidays. John 19:41-42 supplies the detail that the tomb was new, unused, and close at hand, which Tabor reads as an emergency resting place, not Joseph's family tomb. He suggests that the blocking stone also protected the body from predators over the holidays.

### 2. The empty tomb as Joseph's practical next step
Tabor's reasoning is that Joseph intended to return after the Sabbath to move the body to a permanent grave. The women came at dawn expecting to meet him, but, in Tabor's words, "they arrived 12 hours too late," since Joseph had already returned at sundown. On this view the empty tomb is a logistical outcome, not a miracle, and Tabor says the rumor of theft in Matthew is a much later account. He also argues that Mark's "he has risen" and the instruction to go to Galilee are theological additions to what is otherwise historically plausible.

### 3. Mark and Paul as the earliest witnesses, pointing to Galilee
Tabor holds that Mark's original ending (16:8, the women said nothing because they were afraid) is the oldest text, and that the longer endings are later additions. He says the young man in the tomb names Peter, which he connects with Paul's list in 1 Corinthians 15:5. He argues that Mark, by including no Jerusalem appearances, implies a Galilee-first tradition. Tabor treats Matthew 28, with its mountain appearance in Galilee where "some doubted," as a visionary, Galilean tradition. He reads the Matthean and Markan transfiguration (Matthew 17; Mark 9) as possibly a misplaced resurrection story, while noting that this is a suggestion many scholars have made.

### 4. John 20:1-10 as a possible independent early story
Tabor reads the first ten verses of John 20 as a separate, less theological tradition, possibly older than Mark. In it Mary Magdalene reports that "they've taken the Lord out of the tomb," and Peter and the other disciple find the linen cloths. Tabor says there is no angel or interpreter, and that "they" most naturally refers to Joseph and his helpers. He notes that the disciples then "went back to their homes," which he says were in Galilee. He claims the later John 20 appearances and John 21 are added on top of this story.

### 5. The Gospel of Peter's week of mourning and return to Galilee
Tabor treats the fragmentary Gospel of Peter (found in 1886 in an Egyptian monk's grave; an 8th-century copy) as a major but neglected witness. He says it has two empty-tomb accounts: a legendary one and a sober one closely resembling Mark's. Its ending, which breaks off, says that after the feast of unleavened bread the disciples "wept and mourned" and returned home, and that Peter and Andrew went back to their nets on the Sea of Galilee. Tabor argues that this shows a week of despair in Jerusalem with no visions. He dates the text to the late 2nd century but says he would put its substance earlier. Scholars, he notes, disagree on whether it depends on the canonical gospels.

### 6. Paul's resurrection as re-embodiment, not resuscitation
Tabor disputes his unnamed colleague's objection that the body's location would have made resurrection belief impossible. He argues that for Paul and Jesus, resurrection meant transformation into a new body, not a corpse walking out of a tomb. Tabor cites 1 Corinthians 15 for the spiritual body and 2 Corinthians 5 for the image of the old body as a tent or discarded clothing. He also says Jesus's own teaching on resurrection, in Mark's account, describes people becoming like angels with no marriage. He says the Lukan and Johannine insistence on flesh, bones, eating and wounds contradicts Paul and reflects later development.

### 7. The Jerusalem appearances as apologetic development
Tabor argues that Luke and John moved the appearances to Jerusalem, and presented them as bodily and tangible, because of later critics of Christianity. He names Lucian, Trypho (transcribed "Trifo") and Celsus as the critics whose charge was that the faith rested on the foolish tales of deluded women and hallucinations. He also brings in rival divine-man traditions: Apollonius of Tyana, whose followers claimed Zeus as his father and who was said to have ascended bodily, along with a stone inscription from Asia Minor near Tarsus describing his tomb and his heavenly reception. Tabor says the debate among Apollonius's followers over whether his body remained in the tomb is a parallel to the Jesus debates. He concludes that the Galilean origins of faith left the movement open to a charge of mass hallucination, which he thinks explains the later emphasis on tangible proof.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That the tomb was Joseph's family tomb, or that the body was stolen or Jesus's followers started a rumor.** Tabor says the tomb was a new, temporary resting place, and that Joseph simply intended to move the body after the holidays.
- **That resurrection meant a corpse coming back to life.** Tabor argues that for Paul and Jesus it meant transformation into a new body, and that the old body was left behind.
- **That the disciples proclaimed resurrection in Jerusalem immediately after the discovery.** Tabor argues that the earliest sources place the first sightings in Galilee, weeks later, and that the Gospel of Peter shows the disciples mourning in Jerusalem.
- **That the bodily, touchable, eating Jesus of Luke and John is original.** Tabor says this is a later development that contradicts Paul's spiritual-body teaching and reflects apologetic concerns.
- **That the longer endings of Mark are original.** Tabor says Mark ends at 16:8 and that the added appearance stories are later additions by scribes.
- **That resurrection belief depended on an immortal soul.** Tabor says the early followers did not believe in a surviving soul in the sense his colleague's email implies; they believed in bodily resurrection.
