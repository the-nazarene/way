## Summary

Tabor asks whether the gospel resurrection accounts can be read historically if they are treated as separate sources rather than accumulated into one harmonized story. He contrasts this with what he calls the "non-critical" approach of apologists such as William Lane Craig, Gary Habermas and Jeremiah L. Johnson, whom he describes as evangelical believers who defend a bodily, physical resurrection. He says he does not debate them because differing presuppositions make the debate unproductive.

His method is to start with Mark as the earliest gospel and treat the later additions in Matthew, Luke and John as embellishments or theological reshaping. On his reading, Mark originally ended at 16:8 with no appearance narrative, only a message that the disciples would see Jesus in Galilee, and this reflects a glorified, transfiguration-like vision rather than a resuscitated body. Paul's letters, especially 1 Corinthians 15, are the earliest eyewitness testimony, and Tabor treats Paul's "glorious, immortal" body and the "astral" transformation of the dead as the early Christian view. He argues that John 19–21 preserves the most historically detailed material about the burial, including that Joseph of Arimathea's tomb was a temporary burial place near the crucifixion site, and that the disciples at first believed only that the tomb was empty.

The talk closes with Tabor's tentative reconstruction: the sightings took place in Galilee after the feast of Unleavened Bread, the body was later not sought at a known tomb, and the tomb of Jesus would have been a memorial at most. He ends with a short aside about his forthcoming book on Mary, The Lost Mary. ## Key Teachings

### 1. Treat the gospels as separate sources, not one accumulated story
Tabor says apologists take the four gospels and "simply accumulate" details, so that an angel at the tomb (Matthew), a young man (Mark), and two seated figures (John) are all combined into one account. He rejects this as "not really a historical task" and proposes instead to work critically with the sources, comparing them as he would with the accounts of Alexander the Great's death. He notes the sources overlap and depend on one another, but says John shows some independence from Mark, Matthew and Luke.

### 2. Mark's original ending and the Galilee sightings
Tabor argues Mark 16:1-8 is the original ending. The women find the tomb empty, a young man tells them Jesus "has been lifted up" and will be seen in Galilee, and they flee, saying nothing to anyone. He calls the longer endings "forged" and says the manuscripts of the endings are fourth and fifth century, while earlier copies end at verse eight. He links the Galilee promise to Mark 9:1 and the transfiguration, which he reads as the basis of the earliest resurrection faith: a visionary experience of a glorified figure. He suggests Mark ends abruptly on purpose, to direct the reader to the dead Jesus on the cross and to the call to take up one's cross (Mark 8:34).

### 3. Matthew and Luke embellish; John preserves detail
Matthew adds an earthquake, an angel, and guards who "fell like dead men," and moves the appearances to a mountain in Galilee (Matthew 28). Luke relocates the appearances to Jerusalem, drops Galilee, and adds a 40-day period of bodily appearances in which Jesus eats and says "touch me." Tabor reads this as apologetic, meant to answer critics such as Celsus. He treats John as containing reliable detail about the trial (Annas, Caiaphas, a two-stage trial, the festival timing) and argues that John 19:41 describes a temporary tomb "close at hand" because of the Sabbath, not Joseph's family tomb. He says only Matthew calls it Joseph's tomb, to fulfill Isaiah 53.

### 4. Paul as the earliest eyewitness and the "glorified body"
Tabor calls Paul the only eyewitness whose testimony we have ("I've seen the Lord," 1 Corinthians 9 and 15). He reads Paul's answer to "what kind of body?" as dismissive: the risen body is glorious, immortal and incorruptible, a "divine body." He describes this as "astral immortality," comparing the risen to stars, and argues it reflects a view that Jesus was the firstborn of many exalted beings rather than Hellenistic piety. He points to Paul's statement that flesh and blood will not inherit the kingdom (1 Corinthians 15 is cited for the body contrast, and the phrase is quoted in the talk). Tabor argues that bones are irrelevant to this view, citing "the sea gives up the dead" and Paul's contrast of "old clothing" and heavenly garments.

### 5. The burial, the empty tomb and a plausible reconstruction
Tabor argues that John 20:1-10 shows Mary Magdalene finding the stone moved and the tomb empty, Peter and the other disciple running to check, and the disciples believing only that the tomb was empty because they "did not yet know" the Scripture about the resurrection. He proposes that the burial was an emergency one before the Passover and that the body was later moved, though he admits this is inference. He suggests the tomb near the summit of the Mount of Olives (associated with the Carmelites and the Pater Noster church) as a plausible burial site, while calling its identity uncertain and noting the lack of evidence before the fourth century. He also suggests that a tomb of Jesus would have been a memorial place and that early followers would not have treated bones as central, comparing this to the tombs of their own loved ones.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Harmonizing the gospels into one true story:** Tabor says the apologists treat all four gospels as simply additive, and he argues that Matthew and Luke rewrite Mark and that each adds its own material for its own reasons.
- **Joseph of Arimathea's tomb as a known family tomb:** Tabor argues that only Matthew calls it Joseph's, that John 19:41 describes a temporary burial because of the Sabbath, and that the claim that Joseph's family tomb was near the crucifixion site is unlikely.
- **The disciples believed in resurrection when they saw the empty tomb:** Tabor says John 20 says they believed only that the tomb was empty, since they did not yet understand the resurrection.
- **Resurrection requires a physical corpse or bones:** Tabor argues Paul's view has a glorified, non-corruptible body, so bones are irrelevant, and the tomb would be a memorial rather than proof against resurrection.
- **Mark's original ending includes appearances:** Tabor argues Mark ends at 16:8 and that the longer endings are later additions.
