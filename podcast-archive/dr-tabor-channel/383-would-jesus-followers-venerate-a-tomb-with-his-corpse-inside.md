## Summary

Tabor asks whether followers of Jesus would have venerated the tomb where his corpse was laid, and answers yes. His main premise is that the earliest belief about resurrection was not a revived corpse leaving a tomb empty but a glorified, spiritual body, with the bodily remains left behind. If that is right, the tomb would have been a place of memory and pilgrimage, not a threat to the resurrection claim. He speaks largely as a lecturer to a class, with occasional exchanges with students and a remembered email from the scholar Jerome Murphy-O'Connor.

Tabor reasons from the Gospel burial account, which he reads as placing the body in a nearby, unused tomb because of the Passover timing. He proposes that this tomb lay on the Mount of Olives near the place of crucifixion, close to the Paternoster (Our Father) church site, and says he is going "by the only records we have." He is candid that he cannot confirm the details, and he argues that the tradition behind the Church of the Holy Sepulchre has no evidence earlier than the fourth century.

He then turns to the New Testament texts: Paul's comments on the dead in 1 Corinthians 15, Stephen's vision in Acts 7, and Paul's "caught up" experience in 2 Corinthians 12. He argues that none of these treats an empty or occupied tomb as decisive. The talk ends with a short paraphrase of Paul's contrast between the "last Adam" as a life-giving spirit and flesh and blood not inheriting the kingdom, with no further conclusion drawn.

## Key Teachings

### The burial place was a temporary, nearby tomb
Tabor finds it odd that John, who in his view believes in a bodily resurrection, stresses that the tomb "just happened to be near." He reads the account as a rushed burial before the Passover meal: the body is laid in an unused tomb close at hand, and the women later follow to see where he was put. He says he does not know whose tomb it was, only that the account says it was temporary and near the crucifixion site.

### The traditional sites are weakly supported
Tabor says Helena, Constantine's mother, was told of a tomb on the Mount of Olives and built a basilica there, while the Church of the Holy Sepulchre already marked the site. He states that the Holy Sepulchre has no evidence earlier than the fourth century, and that its tradition was lost in the Byzantine period until the twentieth century. He links the Mount of Olives tomb to the Carmelites' rebuilding of the Paternoster church, where a cave tradition about the Lord's Prayer was discovered alongside a first-century tomb. He admits he "[doesn't] know that he would go in a tomb" and that these identifications are uncertain.

### Veneration is memory, not belief that the body is still there
Tabor argues that people visit tombs to remember important figures, citing Lubavitchers at the Rebbe's tomb, who, he says, do not think he is the Messiah. Quoting Murphy-O'Connor's email, he suggests the tomb would have been talked about and visited, and that followers who believed Jesus was a glorified Son of God would not "obsess about his bones." He describes a pilgrimage spot with a porch and benches where those who cared about Jesus would sit and reflect.

### The earliest resurrection view was a spiritual body
This is Tabor's central claim. He says the question of whether a tomb would disprove resurrection only arises if one imagines "a resuscitated corpse." He cites Paul's 1 Corinthians 15 (the dead in Corinth who have "fallen asleep" and will be raised first at Jesus' return), Stephen's vision of Jesus at God's right hand in Acts 7, and Paul's account in 2 Corinthians 12 of being "caught into heaven." He reads Paul's line that the last Adam is a "life-giving spirit" and that flesh and blood will not inherit the kingdom as the framework for this view.

### The thirty-to-seventy-year gap is largely unknown
Tabor stresses how little is known of the years from about 30 to 70. He says James seems to be in charge, Peter is doing mission work, and Paul visits Jerusalem only occasionally. Since nothing reports on the tomb, he argues the absence of evidence does not show the followers ignored it. He says he has "nothing" about what they were doing, and that they likely cared about the tomb "but not as proof of resurrection."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Belief that an identified tomb would disprove the resurrection.** Tabor argues this only holds if resurrection means a revived corpse. On his reading of Paul and Acts, the risen Jesus was a glorified spiritual body, so the tomb is not evidence against resurrection.
- **Belief that the Church of the Holy Sepulchre marks the verified tomb.** Tabor says the tradition has no evidence earlier than the fourth century, and he proposes the Mount of Olives area instead, while noting it is uncertain.
- **Belief that the earliest followers could not have venerated Jesus' tomb.** Tabor argues that people honor the remains of important figures as a form of memory, and that followers who believed in a glorified Jesus would still have visited the tomb.
