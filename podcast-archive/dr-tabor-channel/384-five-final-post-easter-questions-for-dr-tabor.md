## Key Teachings

### Joseph of Arimathea and the tomb
Tabor argues that Joseph of Arimathea is probably a real figure, though everything said about him has "clustered towards embellishment." He says that only Matthew explicitly calls the tomb Joseph's, and that Matthew's wording fits his theological purpose: "according to Matthew, who's trying to fulfill Isaiah 53, he was buried with a rich man." He says he "heard that growing up" as one of the proofs that Jesus was the Christ. He also says that Matthew uses other prophetic texts loosely in the infancy narrative (Jeremiah for the slaughter of the innocents, Isaiah 7 for the virgin birth, and a Nazarene link that has "nothing to do with the town"). He treats these as signs that Matthew is building a scriptural case. He reads John's account of "a tomb nearby that nobody had used ever" as a practical choice made during the festival, which "makes perfect sense to me."

### Which account is earliest, and what is embellishment
Tabor treats Mark as the earliest and most basic account of the empty tomb, with "several women after sunrise." He reads John's version, in which Mary goes alone "early, not after sun," as a surviving piece of an older tradition. He says, "John knows he's contradicting Mark and he's he said, 'I've got another story.'" He calls the Gospel of Peter a useful check because "he doesn't know anything about any of this. See, he only knows Mark." In his view, a late author who knows only Mark is not aware of the burial details that Matthew adds. He also argues that the Jewish night trial and the early morning hearing before Pilate are implausible: "This is not how we proceed with Jewish prudence if any of that is true. It's as we say a kangaroo court." He suggests that Nicodemus is a cautious figure and that Joseph is "a true believer."

### The grieving disciples and the Emmaus story
Tabor imagines the disciples' state after the crucifixion. He argues that they would have had to stay in Jerusalem through the feast, since pilgrims did not leave early and "the final day is a Sabbath day." He describes a scene of mourning and ordinary routine and draws on Emily Dickinson's "the bustle in a house the morning after death." He calls the Emmaus story "my favorite," because the disciples "don't even recognize him at first and then they recognize him in the breaking of bread." He describes how he would film it, with a James who looks like the other disciples and who denies being the Lord. He presents this as a creative idea for a film, not as historical reconstruction. He also describes his wider hope for such a film: "Not magic tricks, but like real stuff. Take care of poverty."

### What happened to the body
Tabor admits that the question of the body's fate is unanswered in the sources. He says that "Bart kind of gets close to that," but he does not know what Bart thinks happened. He proposes that the body was placed in an ossuary after a year and that it "probably became a family tomb of the family of Jesus." He adds that the believing community scattered after 70, with some going "to Msada" and some "across the Jordan." He presents this as a plausible story, not a documented one. He also says that a body would have "gone back to the dust," and that "there does seem to be a kind of a shadowy life in the tomb," which he links to beliefs common in ancient cultures.

### Resurrection versus an immortal soul
This is the longest argument. Tabor says that in Homer, Achilles tells Odysseus (captioned "Sessus") that he would "rather be a servant and a plowman in the world above than ruler of all the dead." He presents this as evidence that "life is life and death is death" in the ancient world until the Hellenistic period. He cites Genesis 1 for the goodness of the world and the line "Dust you are and to dust you return." He argues that the Jewish sources show a developing idea over time: Psalm 139 ("If I make my bed in sheol, thou art there") shows that God fills the realm of the dead, and Daniel's "those who sleep in the dust" shows an early resurrection hope. He describes earlier texts in which the dead "know nothing," and he says that "by the time of Jesus, they're already developing."

### Jesus on resurrection in Mark 12
Tabor reads the Sadducee question about the woman with seven husbands as Jesus' only recorded teaching on resurrection. He says Jesus answers that the Sadducees do not know the Scriptures, and then argues from Exodus 3 that God calls himself "the God of Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, not I was," so that "the dead still live to God." He says the reasoning is about God's relationship to the dead, not about an immortal soul. He adds that resurrection is "a reward it's a thing you obtain," and that the risen life is a new "glorified body," which he compares to Paul's language in 1 Corinthians 15 about the first person made of dust and the second made "a life-giving spirit."

### Against an interim state
Tabor argues that popular funeral preaching assumes the departed loved one is "with the Lord right now." He says this assumption reverses the biblical view. He cites Ecclesiastes ("the spirit goes back to God that gave it") and says that the dead are not already fully alive. He sets this against Revelation 14:13 (quoted without citation): "blessed are the dead who die in the Lord... at the end they come forth from being dead." He concludes that "I don't think you find that [an interim state] in the New Testament." He also refers to a book he says Coleman wrote, "resurrection of the dead and immortality of the soul are not the same thing," as support.

- **The early Jewish hope was an immortal soul that goes to heaven at death.** Tabor argues that the earlier texts describe death as death and that the soul idea came from Greek and Hellenistic thought.
- **The body is a prison from which the soul escapes.** Tabor says "the body is you," and that resurrection means the whole person being raised in a new body.
- **The dead are already with the Lord in an interim state.** Tabor says he does not find this in the New Testament, which he reads as saying the dead come out of death at the end.
