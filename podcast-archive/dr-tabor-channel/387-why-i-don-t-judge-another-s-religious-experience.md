## Summary

Tabor asks how a historian should respond to people who report religious experiences, especially encounters with Jesus. His answer is a personal rule: he does not judge anyone's spiritual experience, only his own, though he will form an opinion when a claim is "really crazy." He begins with Paul, whom he says reported encountering a heavenly, glorified Jesus, a man Paul may have seen in Jerusalem or at festivals. Tabor treats Paul's accounts in Acts and his first-person report in 2 Corinthians 12 as evidence that Paul experienced Jesus as present and speaking, but he says he has "no idea" how to account for that. He then gives examples: a televised healing claim by Pat Robertson, an elephant that supposedly flew across the Atlantic (which he would reject), and listeners' stories of seeing Jesus. He calls himself a naturalist, argues that the real wonder is the human person, and says that even if Jesus turned water into wine, "so what?" The talk ends on a practical note: he wants to teach Christian origins, ancient Judaism, and the Dead Sea Scrolls to a diverse class with different presuppositions, and he asks for charity in such discussions.

## Key Teachings

### Paul's encounter with a glorified Jesus
Tabor reads Paul's experience as a vision of a man he had known. He suggests Paul "had seen around" a Galilean who was probably thought to be one of the "Messiah types," and that after the crucifixion Paul became convinced he had met that same person in heavenly glory. Tabor says he does not know whether Acts 9 and Acts 20-22 describe the event, but he holds that Paul reports a direct, first-person encounter. He notes that Robin Faith Walsh, Bart, and he himself had all discussed this "heavenly glorification and ascent" theme, and that the difference lies in the "historicizing" of it.

### Paul's prayer and the thorn (2 Corinthians 12)
Tabor cites Paul's report that he asked the Lord three times to remove "the messenger of Satan" afflicting him, and was told the grace was enough and that strength is made perfect in weakness. He presents this as evidence that Paul reported talking to Jesus in an intimate, direct way. He says, "How do you account for that? I have no idea."

### A rule about judging others' experiences
Tabor says his rule of thumb is that he never judges another person's spiritual experience, only his own, unless the claim is "really crazy." He uses the Pat Robertson prayer-request example, in which a woman reported new teeth and a gold cross, to show a claim he would consider beyond the bounds. He contrasts this with his students and his teaching: he treats listeners' stories sincerely, saying that people who tell such stories "pretty sure it happened unless they're just doing it for some other reason."

### Limits of what we know about the cosmos
Using the elephant analogy (an elephant flying from JFK to Heathrow, tracked by satellites, which he would reject outright), Tabor asks whether "we know enough about the cosmos" to rule out encounters with the dead, revelation beyond the material world, or the claim that minds are just computers. He answers, "I don't feel I do."

### The natural world and the "miracle" of the person
Tabor calls himself "more of a naturalist" and says the natural world is wonderful enough. A mouse flying around his head, he jokes, would make him the Messiah, but he finds more wonder in another person, "only made out of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen." He calls magic tricks such as the water into wine story a distraction, and says he doesn't "probably think that happened," though "even if it did, so what?"

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Miracles are the main proof of who Jesus was.** Tabor says the "miracle" that matters is the human person; tricks such as turning water into wine are beside the point, and even if they happened, "so what?"
- **Extraordinary religious claims should be settled by the same standard as ordinary factual claims.** Tabor separates the elephant example, which he would reject, from religious experience, which he will not judge for others. He says we do not know enough about the cosmos to rule out encounters with the dead or revelation beyond the material world.
