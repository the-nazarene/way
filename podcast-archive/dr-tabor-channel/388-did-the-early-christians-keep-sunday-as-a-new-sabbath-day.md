## Summary

Tabor asks whether early Christians treated Sunday as a new Sabbath. His answer is no. In his telling, the first Jewish-Christian communities kept the Sabbath, and the early Christians who met on Sunday honored it as the "Lord's Day," a day of new creation and rejoicing, not as a replacement Sabbath. He supports this with the Didache, Acts 20:7, the Pliny the Younger letter to Trajan, and a Leviticus-based argument that Sunday's link to the resurrection came through the wave sheaf offering after Passover. He presents the wave-sheaf point as tentative and notes that the narrative may have been shaped to fit the festival, not the reverse.

The talk ends with a later development. Tabor says that by the time of the Council of Nicaea the Sunday observance was "pretty well nailed down," and that the church eventually had to take over the fourth commandment and claim Sunday for it. He says the Eastern Church was still keeping both days, and that curses were directed at those who kept the Jewish Sabbath. He closes by pointing to Seventh-day Adventists as people who know the argument well.

## Key Teachings

### Sabbath kept alongside Sunday in the earliest stages
Tabor says that in the first stages "people kept both days." He describes Judeo-Christians as continuing to keep the Sabbath, which he calls "the holy day" and one of the Ten Commandments. He argues that Sunday was celebrated as the Lord's Day but "it's not a Sabbath."

### Didache, Acts 20:7 and the Sunday gathering
Tabor calls the Didache "our earliest reference" for meeting on the Lord's Day to break bread. He reads Acts 20:7 as a Sunday meeting that came after a seven-day Sabbath week, with Paul leaving the next day. He says the Sunday gathering may have commemorated the day Jesus was exalted to heaven, though he frames this with "I think."

### The wave sheaf offering and Sunday after Passover
Tabor links Paul's image of the first fruits of resurrection to the wave sheaf offering in Leviticus. He says that offering was made on the Sunday within the seven days of Unleavened Bread, and that "there's always a Sunday if you have a 7-day period." He describes Jesus as seen by early Christians as "the first of the harvest." He then raises a counterpoint he attributes to Bart Ehrman: the narrative may have been constructed to fit the festival, and Jesus "needs to be the Passover." Tabor says this "entirely possible" reversal is also a live option.

### Pliny the Younger and an early morning assembly
Tabor cites Pliny the Younger's letter to Trajan describing an assembly that "assemble[s] early the next day and take[s] harmless food." He reads this as a gathering on the day after a Saturday-type meeting, held early before work because Sunday was a workday. He says the "Easter" remembrance was early but still "not the Sabbath day yet."

### Later church legislation and the fourth commandment
Tabor argues that the church later had to "take over the fourth commandment" and assign Sunday to it. He says curses were pronounced on those who kept the Jewish Sabbath, that the Eastern Church kept both days, and that by Nicaea the Sunday practice was "pretty well nailed down." He says Jews would object that "the seventh day is the day."
