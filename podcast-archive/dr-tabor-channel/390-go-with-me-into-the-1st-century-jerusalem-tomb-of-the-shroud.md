## Summary

This is a home-video tour, narrated by James Tabor, of a first-century tomb south of Jerusalem's Old City, in the Hinnom and Kidron valley junction near Akeldama. The tomb is where a burial shroud was reportedly found in a robbed loculus. Tabor's stated question is what a first-century Jerusalem burial shroud was like and whether this find is the only one from the period. ## Key Teachings

### The discovery and the claim of uniqueness
Tabor recounts that the find came during a Friday-evening hike in the Hinnom Valley before Shabbat, when Gibson and students found a freshly robbed tomb. He says he noticed "clean breaks" on ossuary fragments outside the entrance, and that a textured, fabric-like material in a loculus led him to think of a Second Temple period shroud (06:44). He argues that earlier finds were not comparable. A fragment from "Jason's tomb" was about 1 cm by 1 cm and was lost after being sent to the Department of Antiquities. A woolen fragment with gold thread from de Saucy's excavation of the Tombs of the Kings is, in Tabor's view, "probably Byzantine" (07:35). By his account, the Jerusalem find is the first near-complete carbonized shroud, not a fragment.

### Radiocarbon dating (archival clip)
An unnamed lab staff member says the sample was processed like the lab's other samples, which he describes as about 5,000 per year. He reports the result first by telephone and then by letter, and says the cloth was first century (04:16). Tabor quotes the closing line of the letter: "Our friends from the Shroud of Turin days would certainly have appreciated a result like that one." The clip does not give the calibrated date range, the sample size, or the lab's method notes, so the dating can only be taken as reported in the talk.

### Tomb architecture and burial practice
Gibson and Tabor describe the tomb as a Second Temple period tomb with "clean" ossuary breaks, elongated loculi, and what Tabor calls a "dual loculus" with a central channel (14:43). Tabor says that tombs in this cemetery are unusually decorated for the period, defying the local geology. He also describes a "nefesh," a tower-like monument above the tomb, and the steps he thinks were its substructure (11:02). The cut-out zigzag marks and a shaft tomb are shown on camera. Where Gibson or Tabor reference a burial with hair and bone, the transcript is too garbled to tell which tomb or which claim is meant.

### The rolling stone
Tabor argues that scholars have been "hunting for a round stone," but the Greek of the Gospels "just says a stone which was rolled" and "doesn't mean that it was round" (09:13). He says round stones were used only for large monumental tombs. Small tombs needed only a small stone that could be rolled. The argument is brief and not tied to a verse, but it links the excavation to the Gospel burial account.

### The James Ossuary
Tabor notes that "some people claimed that the James Ossuary could not have come out of this tomb," on the grounds that the opening was too narrow (09:13). His response is partly inaudible in the captions. He reportedly says that ossuaries of different sizes could be removed from this tomb and that the location is the key point. Because the reply is unclear in the captions, the reader should check the video before relying on his reasoning.
