## Summary

Tabor asks what "Abrahamic faith" actually was before it was divided into Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, and he argues that none of the three later traditions represents it. He presents this as a lecture on the forthcoming fourth edition of his book *Restoring Abrahamic Faith*, first published in 1991 as a pamphlet and later as a spiral-bound and then a published edition. He frames the task as a historian's, not a scientist's or moral philosopher's, and begins with René Descartes' "I think, therefore I am" as the starting point of individual conviction. He also says sincerity and passion do not by themselves establish truth.

His central claim is that the three traditions share a "wrong turn": they teach that the created world is not our home, that humans are immortal souls destined for heaven, and that a redeemer must rescue us from a fallen lower world. Tabor argues that the oldest text, Genesis, teaches the opposite. Creation is repeatedly declared "good," humans are made from "soil" (dust) and are living breathers like other animals, and the human vocation is to reflect God and "walk before" him in the world. He supports this with readings of Genesis 1, 2, 12, and 18, Deuteronomy 32:4, Numbers 16:22, and Acts 17:27. He attributes the immortal-soul idea to Greek influence, citing a "golden tablet" he dates to about 400 BC.

The talk ends mid-sentence in a closing quotation attributed to Moses ("Be strong and courageous. Do not fear..."), so the final argument is incomplete in this transcript. Tabor leaves the Christian and Islamic claims largely unexamined in detail; his concrete textual work is almost entirely on Hebrew Bible passages and one Pauline speech in Acts.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Creation is good, and the world is not a place to escape

Tabor says Abrahamic faith "begins in the very first chapter of the Hebrew Bible" (Genesis 1), where creation is affirmed as good in a sequence of seven declarations, the last being "very good," followed by the Sabbath. For him this means the created world and human history are not a temporary prison. He contrasts this with the view that "this created world is not our home," which he calls the "basic long wrong wrong turn" shared by all three traditions.

### 2. Humans are dust creatures made in God's image

Drawing on Genesis 1:27 and the second creation account (Genesis 2), Tabor argues that humans are both "in the image of Elohim" and made from "soil" (he prefers this to "dirt" in his own translation). In Genesis 2 the man becomes "a living breather," the same term used for animals and fish, so human life is biological. The line "you are dust and to dust you will return" is, for Tabor, part of the original creation and "not a result of any fall." He presents this as a challenge to the claim that humans are essentially divine beings with immortal souls. He acknowledges that the "image of God" reflects self-awareness and reason, which he says give humans a "god-like capability."

### 3. The immortal-soul and heaven-as-home idea is a later, Greek-influenced import

Tabor says most religions, including the three Abrahamic ones, teach that humans are "divine beings" with "immortal souls" trapped in bodies. He cites an ancient Greek "golden tablet," which he dates to about 400 BC, "even before Plato," as the earliest source of this dualism he knows of. He says its confession ("I am a child of earth and starry heaven, but heaven alone is my home") "essentially" became the Hellenistic view that "took over Abrahamic faith." He compares this confession to the Christian, Muslim, and Jewish creedal statements, treating them as parallel in form. This is his interpretive claim; the talk does not give the tablet's text in full or a manuscript reference.

### 4. The call of Abraham: purpose, blessing, and "walking before" God

Tabor reads Genesis 12, where God, identified as "El Shadai," tells Abraham to leave and to "walk before me and be perfect" (he prefers "blameless," in the sense of having no blemishes, not flawless). He links this to Deuteronomy 32:4, where God's work is "perfect" and "without iniquity," to argue that the human goal is to be "like that God." He then cites Genesis 18:19, reading the Hebrew *yada* ("I have known him") as intimate knowledge rather than "chosen," and emphasizes that Abraham is to teach his household "the way of Jehovah or Yahweh" by "doing righteousness and justice." He says the plan has three parts ("who is God, what is the way, and what is the plan"), that the promised blessing extends to "all the nations of the earth," and that this makes the vision "suprahistorical" and "universal."

### 5. The "way" is the Sinai/Horeb Decalogue, read through the prophets and Israel's history

Tabor says the "way of justice and righteousness" that Abraham transmits is laid out at Sinai/Horeb in "the 10 words, the decalogue," which he calls "faith for all humankind." He adds the prophets as an amplification and includes "the thousand-year history of ancient Israel" as the family story to meditate on. He describes the Hebrew Bible as "the story of one man's family," noting that Genesis has 50 chapters before the call (in Genesis 12) is reached in its narrative structure. He treats the text as the product of "several versions and editing and writing by many authors," an acknowledgment that he frames as compatible with its theological core.

### 6. Abrahamic faith as personal identity, contrasted with "four spiritual laws" evangelism

Tabor says his own confession is "I am a Hebrew," taken from Jonah, and he reads Acts 17:27 as a "pretty good Hebraic message," noting that Paul is quoting two Greek poets. He says God "does not live in shrines made with hands," so temples and sacrifice are not the locus of faith, and that people "live and move and have our being" in God and are his "offspring." He contrasts this with an evangelical "four spiritual laws" conversation, in which a Christian student asks whether he is certain of heaven: "That's not Abrahamic faith. That's not what Abraham was told." The Acts 17 quotation is the only direct Pauline text in the talk, and Tabor does not argue Paul's relation to Jesus; he simply presents Acts as a summary of Hebraic faith.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That Judaism, Christianity, and Islam are the "Abrahamic faiths" in their current forms.** Tabor argues that none of the three "are representative of Abrahamic faith," and says this is not a condemnation of the people who adhere to them.
- **That the created world is not our home and humans are immortal souls bound for heaven.** Tabor says Genesis presents a good creation and mortal, biologically living humans; he attributes the heaven-as-home idea to Greek dualism.
- **That human mortality ("dust to dust") results from a fall.** He says the dust/return statement is part of the original creation in Genesis 2.
- **That "perfect" in Genesis 12 means flawless.** He says the Hebrew word means "blameless" or unblemished, and cites Deuteronomy 32:4 to show it refers to moral integrity.
- **That sincerity and conviction guarantee truth.** He opens by saying sincerity and passion "do not necessarily equal truth," though committed seekers will have them.
