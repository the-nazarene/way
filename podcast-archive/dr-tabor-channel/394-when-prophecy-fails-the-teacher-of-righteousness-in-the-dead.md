## Summary

Tabor opens by saying he wants to discuss the Teacher of Righteousness in the Dead Sea Scrolls, a figure he says is often translated "the Teacher of Righteousness" but "probably should be translated the true teacher or the right teacher." He frames the clip as part of a larger subject he calls "when prophecy fails, or to be more dramatic, dead messiahs who don't return": what a messianic group does when its leader dies, or the end fails to arrive as predicted. The interviewers raise the idea, which Tabor says several scholars have put forward, that the Teacher was an office passed down like a papacy. Tabor says he tends to think the two cases we know of, the original Teacher and the figure the scrolls describe, point to a real individual, and he suggests that John the Baptist "may have been" the Essene Teacher of Righteousness, succeeded by Jesus, who then appointed James and Simon. He presents this as a possibility, not a conclusion.

His main evidence for a historical Teacher is the scrolls' expectation that the end would come within about 40 years of the Teacher's death, a timeframe he says the community believed "this generation will not pass" would see fulfilled. He calls apocalyptic movements a "100% failure rate" and argues the Jesus movement faced the same problem. He answers the objection "so you're saying Jesus was a false prophet" by pointing to Acts 1 (the disciples' question about restoring the kingdom to Israel, and Jesus's reply that it is not for them to know the times and seasons) and to the Q source's promise of twelve thrones. He notes that 2 Peter 3:8 offers a reckoning of time that stretches prophecy, and that people still produce detailed schemes from Daniel. The clip ends with Tabor arguing that the community's eschatological hope should still produce a present kingdom-shaped life, citing Isaiah 11 and Isaiah 65 on peace, including peace with animals, as the vision that should shape how believers live now.

## Key Teachings

### The Teacher of Righteousness as a historical figure, not only an office
Tabor says the Scrolls mention the Teacher roughly 25 or 26 times, and that he sent the interviewers a document listing these references. He is "convinced that that he as described here is the real guy" because the text ties the end to a period about 40 years after his death or killing. He grants that "there can't be other teachers" and that the title may later have been applied to others, but he holds that the original figure is identifiable.

### The 40-year timeframe and failed prediction
Tabor reads the scrolls as setting a clock: "from the time that the teacher dies until all the men of the lie and the men of wickedness are wiped out will be about 40 years." He compares this to the first-century Jesus movement, which he says expected the kingdom and the twelve-tribe restoration within its own generation. He describes the usual responses to failure as either reinterpreting the fulfillment ("predtoist," meaning preterist, and the "spiritualist" move, "Jesus did return when we said he would, but it was just invisible") or recalculating the dates.

### John the Baptist as possible Teacher and the succession idea
Tabor speculates that "for all intents and purposes that John the Baptist may have been the aene [Essene] teacher of righteousness in the early first century succeeded by Jesus." He imagines John with "his own 12 disciples and three appointed you know priests," passing the role to Jesus, who appointed his brother James and then Simon. He presents this as a model of office-like succession and asks the interviewers "What do you think?"

### Jesus and the question of the times and seasons
Tabor says people ask whether he is calling Jesus a false prophet. His reply: Jesus is recorded as saying "As for the times and seasons, no one knows." He points to Acts, where the disciples ask whether Jesus will "at this time restore the kingdom to Israel," and to the Q source's final verse about sitting on thrones "judging the 12 tribes." He says whether or not Jesus said the Acts words, Luke's recording "shows that the movement is recognizing that the dating can be different."

### Dating schemes and 2 Peter
Tabor cites 2 Peter's line that "a day with the Lord is a thousand years and 1,000 years is a day," and asks how many ways people have counted the 1335, 1260 and 1290 figures in Daniel 11 and 12. He observes that "people have it all figured out" online and says "maybe they do and maybe they don't."

### Living as if the kingdom has arrived
Tabor argues that whatever the timetable, believers "should live a kind of escatological life," which he glosses as "the millennial thing." He ties this to the prayer "Let your will be done on earth as it is in heaven," and asks: "Wouldn't you live as if we're already in the kingdom?"

### Isaiah's vision of peace
He directs listeners to Isaiah 11, which "describes the messianic era," and Isaiah 65, which "describes the new creation." He quotes the line "They will not hurt or destroy on all my holy mountain" and adds that nations come to Jerusalem "learning the way of peace," including peace with animals. He connects this to "the Kingdom way" the interviewers have promoted.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That the Teacher of Righteousness must be translated as a fixed title.** Tabor says it "probably should be translated the true teacher or the right teacher."
- **That a failed prophecy means the prophet was false.** Tabor raises this objection himself ("So you're saying Jesus was a false prophet then?") and answers that Jesus's own reply in Acts allowed for uncertain dating.
- **That apocalyptic groups recover from failure by simply reinterpreting it.** He describes the predestinarian and spiritualist moves as ways of rescuing a failed prediction, implying he finds them unpersuasive.
