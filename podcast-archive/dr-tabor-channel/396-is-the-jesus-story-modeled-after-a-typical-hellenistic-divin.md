## Summary

Tabor asks whether the Gospels, especially Mark and Luke, are shaped like the "divine man" (*theios anēr*) narratives of the Greco-Roman world, in which an extraordinary human has a wondrous birth, performs miracles, teaches, dies or disappears, and ascends to become a god. He frames the question around the genre of the *aretalogy* (a work praising a figure's wonders) and asks whether the Gospels belong to it, and if so, whether any historical Jesus can still be recovered. He also raises the older debate running since the 1830s.

His main guide is Morton Smith's 1971 JBL article "Prolegomena to a Discussion of Aretalogies, Divine Men, the Gospels and Jesus," which Tabor reads and summarizes. Smith's central claim, as Tabor presents it, is that the Gospels are more similar to these divine-man accounts than to any other non-Christian literature. Tabor walks through ancient parallels: the Hellenistic and Roman figures Asclepius, Apollonius of Tyana, Empedocles, Plato, Romulus and Hercules; Justin Martyr's and Origen's responses to Celsus; and imperial texts such as the Augustus inscription and an Ephesian decree for Caligula. He also reads a Roman soldier's letter from around 150 for flavor.

Tabor accepts many of the parallels but resists the conclusion that Jesus is purely literary. He argues that the teachings and the geographic and archaeological itinerary of Jesus (he cites the Gabbatha pavement mentioned in John) are different from the Apollonius material, and he says he would test the parallels against his expanded reconstruction of Q. The segment ends with Tabor saying he is still "tracking Jesus," that he will reread Philostratus's *Life of Apollonius* this month, and that he is considering whether a similar "tracking" approach could work for Apollonius. The talk is a segment of a longer Zoom session for his Patreon community, so the closing is informal and does not settle the question.

## Key Teachings

### The divine-man pattern and the aretalogy

Tabor describes the *theios anēr* matrix as a recurring story shape: a miraculous or divinely arranged birth, early wisdom, wonders and miracles (healing, exorcism, walking on water, raising the dead), confrontation with rulers, persecution or death or escape, and then ascent to heaven or divinization. He lists figures including Osiris, Hercules, Asclepius, Pythagoras, Empedocles, Apollonius of Tyana, Alexander, Plato and Roman emperors. He argues the Gospels, Luke especially, fit this pattern more closely than a simple list of miracles would. Tabor says the Smith article distinguishes this kind of narrative ("let me tell you the story of my hero") from a bare catalogue of wonders.

### Smith's two claims, as Tabor presents them

Tabor quotes Smith's framing of the aretalogy as "praise and propaganda" and then gives Smith's two stated facts: many accounts of ancient divine men are variants of a recognizable aretalogical form, and the Gospels are more similar to these accounts than to other non-Christian works. Smith, Tabor says, judges the question of direct influence "comparatively unimportant" and does not want the comparison thrown out. Tabor presents this as a reason to engage the material, not to be defensive about the Gospels' uniqueness.

### Early Christian responses: Justin and Origen

Tabor describes Justin Martyr (c. 150) as facing the problem that Jesus' deeds closely resemble those of other wonder-workers. Justin, as Tabor reports him, says these parallels were demons inspiring pagan writers to imitate Christianity. Tabor then cites Celsus, the second-century critic whose work survives only through Origen's reply (early third century). Celsus' sketch of wandering self-proclaimed saviors and sons of God (quoted in the talk from Origen) is, for Tabor, a useful picture of the cultural milieu. Origen, Tabor says, does not deny such figures existed but argues they have nothing to do with the messianic ideology of the Hebrew Bible.

### Birth and ascension parallels

Tabor reads a set of ancient stories: Plato's birth, in which Ariston abstains after a vision of Apollo, and the day of birth is linked to the Delians' tradition about Apollo; Hercules' conception, when Zeus makes a night last three times as long; the disappearance of Romulus in a flash of light; and the vanishing of Empedocles, after which his followers find no body and assume he has gone to heaven. Tabor compares the empty tomb in Mark 16 ("He is not here. He has been lifted up") to the Empedocles story, noting that Mark has no appearances there. He extends the pattern to Judaism through Josephus's account of Enoch, Elijah and Moses, a theme he discusses in an article on his site, and he cites the Hebrew Bible's account of Moses' death (Deuteronomy 34) as the base story Josephus embellishes.

### Imperial language and the "gospel" of Augustus

Tabor reads the Augustus inscription in which Providence sends Augustus "as a savior," who has "put an end to war," who is "visible" and who has made "the birthday of the god" the "beginning of the gospel" (*euangelion*). He also cites an Ephesian decree for Caligula calling him "the god visible" and "the shared savior of human life," and a Roman sailor's letter of around 150 that Tabor uses to show how religious language had spread to ordinary people. He argues these texts show that the language of savior, incarnation and gospel circulated before Jesus and so is part of the cultural background the Gospels drew on.

### Tabor's counter-argument: teachings and archaeology

Tabor does not accept that everything is borrowed. He argues that an underlying historical itinerary of Jesus, one that can be checked archaeologically, comes through the Gospels, and he gives the pavement called Gabbatha, mentioned in John as the place before Pilate, as an example. He says Apollonius has nothing comparable. On teachings, he says the test is whether the expanded Q reconstruction contains only common second-temple wisdom, in which case the argument for Jesus' distinctiveness would fail. He says he would not accept the claim that the teachings are simply borrowed without checking specific parallels, and he regards the teachings as a separate question from the miracle matrix.

### Marcus Vincent's claim (as Tabor reports it)

Tabor cites an interview in which Derek Lambert asked Marcus Vincent (name as transcribed; Tabor's reference is garbled, and the associated claim about Marcion or "Marcian" is also unclear in the captions) about the Gospels' sources. Tabor quotes Vincent as saying that every element of the Gospels can be shown to derive from a Hebrew Bible legend or parallel. Tabor says he disagrees and offers the Elijah raising of a boy and the axe-head story as examples of the kind of parallels Vincent might have in mind, while saying general parallels are always available. He treats this as a weaker version of the argument than Smith's.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Gospels are uniquely Jewish and so cannot be compared to pagan divine men.** Tabor says this view was strong in the 1950s and 60s and is "still with us," but he argues that comparison need not be defensive; the parallels can be studied without denying Jesus' Jewishness.
- **Every element of the Gospels is borrowed from the Hebrew Bible.** Tabor disputes the strong form of Marcus Vincent's claim (as he reports it) and says the broader matrix (miracles, ascent) is shared with the Hellenistic world, while the teachings and itinerary need separate testing.
- **Parallels to divine men prove that Jesus is purely legendary.** Tabor rejects this inference and says a historical core can still be investigated, archaeologically and through the teachings.
- **Christian apologists could simply dismiss the parallels.** Tabor reports Justin's demonic explanation and Origen's reply as ancient precedents, and he notes that Justin's own response was to accept the parallels and explain them away.
