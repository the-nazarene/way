## Summary

Tabor's question is what the newest dating work on the Dead Sea Scrolls, combining carbon-14 (C14) tests with AI analysis, actually shows, and whether press coverage has overstated it. He focuses on fragment 4Q114 from Cave 4, which preserves parts of Daniel 10-11, the visionary section he calls the longest prophecy in the Bible. He says scholars had already dated these chapters on literary-historical grounds to the 160s BCE. The updated C14 results give a two-sigma calibrated range of 230 to 160 BCE, which he says overlaps the period when the final part of Daniel was presumably written.

Tabor argues that some news reports wrongly took the result to confirm that Daniel's vision dates to the 6th century BCE, the time of Daniel himself. He calls that reading "entirely incorrect." What the dating does, in his account, is push the possible date of these chapters back into the early 3rd century BCE. He describes the underlying study as led by Mladen Popović of the University of Groningen, which used two data sets: C14 dates for a large group of Judean Desert manuscripts, and multispectral images the Israel Antiquities Authority has taken over about fifteen years. The imaging, which Tabor says was developed with NASA technology, brings out carbon-based ink. Dated scrolls were then used to anchor the handwriting analysis across the whole collection.

The talk ends with the claim that one manuscript dated to the 3rd century BCE is a copy of Daniel, making it one of the earliest copies of that book. From this, Tabor suggests the 3rd century BCE should be seen as a period of intense scribal writing and composition, more intense than previously thought. He warns that sensational reporting tends to outrun the published facts, and he points viewers to the PLOS One article for what is actually established.

## Key Teachings

### The dating does not settle the date of Daniel's vision

Tabor's central correction is that a date for a manuscript is not a date for the content's origin. He says the 230 to 160 BCE range fits the period when the last part of Daniel was "presumably authored," but he does not treat that as proof of the author or of when the prophecy was first given. He calls the 6th-century reading, which assumes Daniel himself wrote the vision, "entirely incorrect." The argument rests on the difference between when a text was composed and when a surviving copy was made.

### Two dating methods are converging

Tabor describes two lines of evidence. First, the literary-historical dating of Daniel 10-11 to the 160s BCE, which he attributes to scholars generally, and which he says was already set out in the Discoveries in the Judean Desert series and a 2020 BASOR article by Eugene Ulrich, his former Notre Dame colleague. Second, the new C14 result with a two-sigma range of 230 to 160 BCE. He says the two ranges overlap, which is why the result is significant but not a new proof of a 6th-century origin. He also notes that other Dead Sea Scrolls were already dated to this period on paleographic (handwriting) grounds.

### The method: C14, multispectral imaging, and AI handwriting analysis

According to Tabor, the Popović study used C14 results from many Judean Desert manuscripts as anchor points. It then combined them with multispectral images, made by the Israel Antiquities Authority over about fifteen years, that make carbon-based ink more legible by separating layers of the surface. Tabor says the handwriting from the scrolls that could be precisely dated was then used to extend the dating across the whole collection, which he presents as the new "frontier." He cautions that the method is new and that the claims should be read against the published article.

### A 3rd-century BCE copy of Daniel

Tabor's most specific claim is that one of the manuscripts this study dates to the 3rd century BCE is a copy of the book of Daniel. He says this makes it not just the earliest known copy of Daniel but one of the earliest copies that ever existed. He presents this as direct evidence for the circulation of Daniel's text in that century, though the transcript does not give the manuscript's number or its link to the 4Q114 fragment.

### Scribal activity in the 3rd century BCE

From the dating, Tabor draws a broader conclusion: that scholars should think of the 3rd century BCE as a period when writing, composition, and the work of scribes was "quite intense, more intense than what was thought previously." He offers this as what the research "suggests," not as a settled finding.

### Sensational reporting versus published results

A recurring theme is that the press has run ahead of the science. Tabor says the sensational tends to "rule over the factual," and he points viewers to the PLOS One article and an early Guardian story as the basis for what is actually known. He also cites a quotation from Joan Taylor in the Guardian piece, though the transcript does not give her words.
