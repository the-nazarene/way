## Summary

Tabor asks what a genuine first-century Jewish burial shroud looked like, and what the 2000 discovery of one in a robbed rock-cut tomb on the southern lip of the Hinnom Valley (Akeldama) tells us about the Turin Shroud. He argues that the Gospels' burial accounts (Mark, Matthew, Luke, John) describe ordinary Jewish cave burial, but that no shroud had ever been recovered from Jerusalem because cloth rarely survives there. His evidence is the tomb itself, the surviving cloth and headpiece fragments, a double wool-and-linen layer, a radiocarbon result from the University of Arizona that he reads as first century, and DNA and pathogen work that found leprosy and tuberculosis in the remains.

He also covers the looting: he suggests the robbers wanted ossuaries with inscriptions such as "Yeshua," that a dealer he approached did not recall the matter, and that two names from the tomb (Maria and Salomi) were recovered. He mentions the James ossuary and the Israel Antiquities Authority's charge against Oded Golan, but says it cannot be connected to this tomb.

The talk turns to the Turin Shroud in its last section. Tabor says he is "not an expert on the shroud" but agrees with Nicholas Allen's argument that it is a medieval (14th-century) production tied to changing art of Christ's suffering. He says the Akeldama shroud is single-layered in the way the Turin cloth is not, that the Masada cloth reports do not match, and that the Turin Shroud cannot be shown to prove bodily resurrection. The talk ends with Tabor asking viewers for information about the ossuary names and with reflections on accidental discoveries. Jacob Berman's questions near the end prompt the Turin discussion.

## Key Teachings

### The Gospel burial account and the Jerusalem tomb evidence
Tabor says Mark, Matthew and Luke describe Joseph of Arimathea buying a shroud and wrapping Jesus hastily in an emergency tomb before the Sabbath and Passover. He notes that Matthew calls it a "clean" shroud and that John adds a separate head covering. He argues that the Gospels reflect a broadly accepted picture of Jewish cave burial, but that the material details had never been confirmed archaeologically in Jerusalem because textiles do not survive there.

### The shroud tomb and its robbery
Tabor describes a tomb with three levels, a porch and a blocking-stone slot, found freshly robbed on a Friday in summer 2000 while he was hiking with Shimon Gibson and students. Tomb robbers had broken ossuaries with metal mallets and scattered pieces down the hillside. Tabor says the team collected and reconstructed the fragments, which the Israel Antiquities Authority supervised, and that the niche had been sealed with plaster, which he links to contagion from the disease the remains carried. He argues that the robbers were after inscribed ossuaries, which can be sold illegally, because graves in this tradition contain no valuable goods.

### What the cloth and headpiece show
Tabor argues that the Akeldama cloth, unlike the Turin Shroud, was not one long sheet laid over the whole body. In his account, the head was wrapped separately, which is why the preserved headpiece matters and why no face image appears on it. He also points to a double-layered cloth of wool over linen, which he says was unexpected. He links the wool to high status and notes that the Torah prohibits mixing linen and wool (he gives no verse). He says that a body would not have been moved after wrapping because it would smear, so the Turin image is not what a hasty burial would leave.

### Dating, disease and DNA
Tabor reports that a blind radiocarbon test at the University of Arizona (Doug Donahue and T. Lang, as he names them) placed the cloth in the first century. He reads the summary as late second century into early first century, and he describes the tomb's remains as showing leprosy (Hansen's disease) and tuberculosis. He says the individual was male and that the leprosy case is claimed as the earliest with a confirmed date. He reports that mitochondrial DNA tests in the 2000s were limited, and he describes a planned new-generation sequencing project for Jerusalem tombs, including a proposed test of Caiaphas's ossuary. He also says the Akeldama individuals share a maternal connection with people from the Talpiot tomb.

### The ossuary inscriptions and the James ossuary
Tabor says that about a quarter to a third of Jerusalem ossuaries carry inscriptions and that the robbers likely wanted those. He says the team later recovered names including Maria and Salomi (which he calls common names). He recounts approaching a dealer, offering a payment per name, and being told later that the dealer remembered nothing. He says the James ossuary surfaced in November 2002 and that the IAA charged Oded Golan with possessing a piece from a recently robbed tomb. Tabor says he does not necessarily accept that charge and that the Akeldama tomb has never been named in the case.

### Why the Turin Shroud is not this shroud
Near the end, Tabor says he prefers "religious relic" to "fraud" and that Allen's video shows how art changed in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries to show Christ's suffering more graphically. He says Allen explains the blood, the image formation and the sequence of its appearance. Tabor argues that apologists making claims about the Turin Shroud as Jesus' burial cloth "are going way beyond the evidence." He addresses the Masada cloth claims: he says the Masada textiles were carbon-dated but were mats and clothing, not burial shrouds, and that the published data is in the Israel Exploration Society volumes. He calls on readers to check the sources before accepting claims that a Masada shroud matches the Turin cloth.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Turin Shroud is a first-century burial cloth of Jesus that proves the resurrection.** Tabor says the Turin Shroud is a medieval production per Allen's argument and that the evidence does not support the resurrection conclusion.
- **Jewish burial involved laying the body on one long cloth and wrapping it without moving it.** Tabor argues the Akeldama remains and headpiece show otherwise, and that moving a wrapped body would smear it.
- **Jerusalem burials would have left a cloth record.** Tabor says textiles rarely survive in Jerusalem, which is why the Akeldama find is rare.
- **A "Yeshua" ossuary means Jesus' ossuary.** Tabor says the inscription does not identify Jesus and that such names were common.
- **The Masada burial shroud matches the Turin Shroud.** Tabor says the Masada carbon dates were for mats and clothing, not burial shrouds, and asks people to check the sources.
