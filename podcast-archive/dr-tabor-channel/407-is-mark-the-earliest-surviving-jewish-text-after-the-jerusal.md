## Summary

Tabor asks the reader to set aside the traditional title "Gospel of Mark" and read the text as a Jewish document written shortly after the Roman destruction of Jerusalem and the Temple in summer 70 CE. His claim rests on dating and genre. He argues that if Mark dates to shortly after 70, it is the earliest surviving Jewish text responding to that catastrophe, and that Josephus's *Jewish War* (which Tabor dates to 73) is a history, not a proposal for how to "follow the God of Israel" in a post-Temple world. He presents Mark as exactly that kind of proposal. He accepts Markan priority as the standard view and cautions that the best manuscripts should be used, noting the disputed opening phrase and the shorter ending, which he calls the authentic ending.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Mark as the first post-70 Jewish text, and the dating argument
Tabor asks listeners to "pull it out of the New Testament mentally" and drop the title. He argues that if Mark is dated "shortly after the destruction of Jerusalem in the summer of 70 CE," it is "the oldest Jewish text emerging uh after the destruction." He notes Josephus's *Jewish War* at 73 as a possible rival, but says Josephus is writing history, not proposing a way to live. He admits the dating is a shared working assumption among "many of us who work in the field." Tabor's chronology places Jesus' crucifixion in 30 CE, Paul's seven undisputed letters in the 50s, and the Temple's destruction in 70. He describes the period from 30 to 70 as the church of James the Just, which "disappeared."

### 2. The Jerusalem catastrophe and the problem of following the God of Israel without the Temple
Tabor frames the 70 CE destruction as a crisis for all forms of Judaism, including the followers of Jesus, "because they're Jews." He cites Brandon's question about the "dark age" between Paul's letters and 70, and the Temple's scale (Herod's expansion; Tabor's estimate of "145 acres"). He argues that diaspora synagogues (Corinth, Ostia) existed, but that Jerusalem remained the administrative and pilgrimage focus, with Josephus claiming pilgrim numbers "up to 3 million." Synagogues therefore did not fully answer the question of how to live Torah after the Temple. He draws a rough parallel to Catholics losing the Vatican.

### 3. The seven points of Mark's proposal
Tabor's central method is to read seven Markan passages as a Jewish program:
- **Sabbath** (Mark 2:23-28): the disciples' grain-picking, "the Sabbath is made for people," and David's bread as an example of need overriding law.
- **Ritual purity** (Mark 7:1-23): he reads the passage as a teaching that defilement comes from the heart, not food, and says the translation "thus he declared all foods clean" is misleading because the Greek does not say that.
- **Shema and commandments** (Mark 12:28-34): the scribe's answer, which Tabor says is "more than all the burnt offerings and sacrifices," and which he links to Hillel and Shammai.
- **The widow's gift** (Mark 12:41-44): she gives more than the festival donors, so Temple attendance is not required.
- **Temple destruction and persecution** (Mark 13): the Temple's fall is an event, not "the end of the age," and the gospel must be preached to all nations first.
- **The son of man** (Mark 13:26; Mark 14:61-62): read through Daniel 7:13-14 as the exaltation of "the people of the most high," which Tabor reads as collective and as the Messiah's power, not a second coming of Jesus in the clouds.
- Mark's "Christology" is messianic but not divine: Jesus refuses the title "good" ("no one is good but God alone," Mark 10:18), which Tabor says is a solidly Jewish point.

### 4. Rabbinic parallels and "Judaisms"
Tabor compares Mark with the rabbis after 70. He cites Yohanan ben Zakkai's escape from Jerusalem in a coffin and his academy at Yavneh, and the Mishnah's *Pirkei Avot* chain of tradition. He notes that the rabbinic documents are later than the events they describe. He argues that rabbis like Yohanan ben Zakkai and Akiba also linked the Temple's fall with messianic expectation, and that Akiba identified Bar Kosiba as the Messiah. So messianic identity alone does not make a text "Christian." He proposes replacing fixed labels with "Judaisms" and treating apocalyptic movements as responses within late Second Temple Judaism. He also argues that the "oral Torah" was not only a Pharisaic property, since the Nazarenes too had to interpret purity and Sabbath.

### 5. Resurrection as exaltation, not resuscitation
Tabor reads Mark's empty tomb (Mark 16) with its instruction to go to Galilee as a point of origin for visions of Jesus in glory. He presents the Transfiguration (Mark 9:2-8) as an anticipation of that glory, set on Mount Hermon rather than Tabor. He attributes this reading to Norman Perrin and agrees with Crossan and Ehrman about the Gospel of Peter's ending ("We wept and cried for seven days"). He concludes that Mark "doesn't care about revived corpses" and is concerned with Jesus' exaltation to God's right hand, which he links to Paul's vision of transformed believers in 1 Thessalonians.

### 6. Mark's community and the delay of the parousia
Tabor says Paul expected the return "in his own lifetime" (1 Thessalonians 4:15-17), and that by the 70s the community faced "the delay of the paraca." Mark, he argues, answers that the Temple's fall is the first sign and that the community is living in "the time of the end," with waiting, suffering, and preaching to the nations. He describes Mark 13 as written "with the sense of sorrow," not triumph. He contrasts this with 4th-century Christians who mocked Jerusalem's Jews from the Mount of Olives.

### 7. Later Christianizing of Mark
Tabor argues that Matthew and Luke rewrite Mark and alter the wise scribe's praise (Matthew removes the scribe), the "good teacher" exchange, and the disciples' failure. He says the disciples in Mark "never understand anything ever." He suggests Mark is calling readers to take up the cross (Mark 8:34) rather than treating Jesus' death as an exemption for followers. He also says Mark was rarely quoted by the church fathers and was overlooked in favour of Matthew and Luke.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mark is an early Christian gospel.** Tabor argues it is a Jewish text whose traditions concern Jews, and that "Christians were Jews" (Fredriksen).
- **Mark's Jesus is God.** Tabor says Mark's Messiah points to God; Jesus' "good" exchange (Mark 10:18) shows a non-divine Messiah.
- **Jesus' death exempts followers from the cross.** Tabor reads Mark 8:34 as calling followers to take up the cross.
- **Replacing the Temple with Jesus is Christian.** Tabor says the scribe's answer (Mark 12) is a Jewish proposal, not a Christian one, and that rabbis also worked out how to live without the Temple.
- **Pharisees alone had oral Torah.** Tabor says the Nazarenes also interpreted the law orally, since purity and Sabbath needed interpretation.
- **Judaism was rigid and legalistic.** He warns against this caricature, citing kavanah (intentionality) in prayer.
- **Matthew was written first.** Tabor accepts Markan priority and says Matthew softens Mark's difficult material.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor reads Mark's ethics as close to Paul's seven undisputed letters, including moral living and the turn to the one God, while Mark keeps Jewish purity concerns in a non-Temple frame.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor places the James church in the 30-70 period, says it "disappeared," and notes James's Temple prayer and Paul's meeting with James in Galatians.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet:** The Sabbath, purity (Mark 7) and the scribe's "more than burnt offerings" passage form the centre of his case for a post-Temple Jewish ethic. He argues that Mark replaces ritual purity with a heart-based ethics.
- **Kingdom on earth:** Tabor says Jesus' "You're not far from the kingdom" means the kingdom is an understanding of God's rule, not only the apocalypse.
- **Resurrection and afterlife:** Mark's resurrection is exaltation, linked by Tabor to Paul's transformed believers and to ascent traditions (his own book on Paul's ascent is mentioned).
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor's Messiah is non-divine and points to God, and the son of man is collective. He also argues the Messiah label alone does not mark a text as Christian.
- **Gospels and canon formation:** Tabor argues Mark was neglected by the church fathers and rewritten by Matthew and Luke, who altered the scribe, the "good" exchange and the disciples' failure.
