## Summary

Tabor, a New Testament scholar, sets out his own faith statement in an interview with Aaron Abke and Kameron Waters. He calls it "Abrahamic" or "Hebrew" faith, not Judaism or Christianity, and presents it as the subject of his revised book *Restoring Abrahamic Faith*. The central question is what this faith was at its source in the Hebrew Bible, and whether Jesus of Nazareth taught it. Tabor argues that it rests on four pillars, which the book's chapters follow: (1) who God is, a nameless "force of all forces" called Yhovah; (2) the Way, the Torah as justice and righteousness (mishpat and tzedaqah), summarized in Mark 12; (3) the divine plan, Israel's mission to be a light to the nations; and (4) the Messiah, a figure whom Tabor says God never wanted and who is not the center of the faith. He claims Jesus held all four, with the Shema and the love commandments as the summary. His evidence is mainly the Hebrew Bible (Genesis 18, Isaiah 51, Isaiah 40 onward, Genesis 14), Mark 12 and other Gospel sayings, the Dead Sea Scrolls (Damascus Document, the Two Ways, a fragment on a messianic banquet), and his reading of Paul's seven undisputed letters, which he says never identify the Messiah with the creator God.

The interview moves from definitions (why "Hebrew," the meaning of God's name, the Melchizedek prayer) to comparisons with Jesus and then to broader claims about the "project" of repairing the world, the limits of eschatology, the rejection of eternal hell, and the contrast between Freud's "projection" and Tabor's preferred idea of human beings "reflecting" the divine. Tabor carries nearly all of the substantive argument. Abke and Waters frame questions, offer comments (for example on the Two Ways and the Gospel of the Hebrews), and draw connections, but Tabor's positions are his own and the talk is an interview, not a formal lecture. Tabor closes by saying Abrahamic faith is a mission that people can join now, and that the ideas are "within your human heart." The talk ends with a short sign-off from Abke.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Abrahamic faith is older and more universal than the three religions
Tabor argues that Judaism, Christianity, and Islam are "derivative" faiths that have departed from an original Hebrew faith found in the Hebrew Bible, which he says is "the quarry from which you were digged" (Isaiah 51). He points to Genesis 1 as addressed to all humanity and says Abraham "wasn't even an Israelite" (08:50). He frames the three "confessions" (Shahada, Christian confession, Jewish practice) as later forms and says Abraham did not make any of them. He acknowledges he is not "bashing" Christians or Muslims, but he calls the conversion-and-exclusivity model of these faiths a departure. He also notes that Judaism officially accepts non-Jews who "begin to follow the way of justice and righteousness" (15:40).

### 2. The name of God and the concept of "will be / is / was"
Tabor says God cannot be named because names are for things and God is not a thing. He reads Exodus 3 (Exodus 6:3 in his citation, "I will be who I will be") and explains that the four-letter name (Yhovah) is a concept, roughly "will is was," and not a proper noun. He calls God "the force of all forces" and says "Elohim," though plural, takes a singular verb. He argues that the Canaanite associations of Yahweh and El were redefined in the Torah and prophets as the creator, and that "L" (El) means power. He describes the divine nature repeated in the Hebrew Bible as merciful, gracious, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness (30:29 onward), and says humans are to imitate this nature because they are made in God's image.

### 3. Torah as "the way": mishpat, tzedaqah and the Shema
Tabor says the "way" (derek) of Yhovah is the content of the Torah: the Ten Words and broader ethical commands, including care for people with disabilities. He names five key words (mercy/love, justice, righteousness, truth, faithfulness) and says these are "the five big words." He says Jesus affirmed this way in Mark 12, when asked for the greatest commandments: the Shema and "love your neighbor as yourself." He also cites Isaiah's prophetic statement that God desires mercy and not sacrifice, and says God "never wanted a temple." He says the Torah passages that accommodate sacrifice and kingship are historical concessions, not God's desire (34:42 onward).

### 4. The Messiah is not central, and God never wanted one
Tabor argues that popular beliefs about the Messiah are wrong in two ways. First, he says the Hebrew Bible contains only about ten Messiah texts, and the word "Messiah" is never used for the figure in those texts (38:26). Second, he says none of the ten has been fulfilled. He cites 1 Samuel for the people's demand for a king, which he treats as a concession, and says the Messiah belongs to a "job description" still unfolding. He frames Jesus's Mark 12 question (Psalm 110 is implied, not named) about how the Messiah can be David's son and David's lord as a rebuke to scribal literalism. He says Jesus is "Lord" only insofar as one follows his teaching (39:33).

### 5. Jesus was not God; the Messiah is distinct from Yhovah
Tabor argues that Jesus "never claimed to be God" and that no critical scholar of the historical Jesus would hold that he did. He cites Mark 10 ("Why do you call me good?") as Jesus refusing the title of "good," and John 17:3 as Jesus praying to "the only true God." He credits James Dunn's *Christology in the Making* with concluding that no New Testament passage confuses the Messiah with Yhovah. He says Paul's seven undisputed letters never make that confusion either (1:00:11), and that later developments (Irenaeus, Origen, Clement of Alexandria) moved toward Hellenistic philosophy and away from the original faith. He presents this as a defensible academic claim while acknowledging that he is speaking more personally in this interview.

### 6. The Ebionite/Nazarene line, the Dead Sea groups, and the "project"
Tabor connects his account to the Ebionites and Nazarenes, to the Dead Sea community, and to the Damascus Document, which he cites for the two ways of life and death. He says the Teacher of Righteousness was raised up "to guide us in the way of his heart" and that this language is personal. He describes "prepare the way in the desert" (Mark 1:2-3) as a call to a moral enterprise, not a set of doctrines about salvation after death. He also describes the "project" (FTP) of bringing justice and righteousness to humans, animals, plants and the whole earth, reading Genesis 2 ("dress and keep the garden") and Isaiah 11 (wolf and lamb; "the earth is full of the knowledge of the Lord") as the vision. In his account, the Melchizedek bread-and-wine scene (Genesis 14) prefigures the Qumran messianic banquet, which he says concerns "the whole world" and not the Eucharist. He says a Melchizedek blessing ("Baruch at Elon," "creator of heaven and earth") is an older prayer he and his household use.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Abraham was a Jew, a Christian, or a Muslim.** Tabor says Abraham was none of these; the only record of him is in the Hebrew Bible, and he is the first "Hebrew" (08:50).
- **The Hebrew Bible is full of prophecies about Jesus, and the Old Testament is "a big prophecy about Jesus."** Tabor says there are about ten Messiah texts, that the Messiah is never named as such in them, and that none has yet been fulfilled (38:26; Abke's "53" and "100" guesses are corrected).
- **God wanted a temple and a Messiah.** Tabor says God never wanted either; both are accommodations to human history and to Israel's wish to be like the nations (34:42; 36:26).
- **Jesus is God, or Jesus is Yhovah.** Tabor says Jesus never claimed to be God and that the Messiah is distinct from the creator; he cites Mark 10 and John 17 (50:53; 57:09).
- **"Hell" with eternal fire is a biblical teaching.** Tabor says the Hebrew Bible contains no Satan with demons awaiting souls in a lake of fire, and that an eternal torment of finite creatures is a "crazy" idea (1:27:38-1:28:07).
- **John the Baptist is only the forerunner.** Tabor says Jesus called John the greatest of those born of women and that the "least in the kingdom" clause is a later addition (52:53-53:25).
- **Eschatology is central to the faith.** Tabor says the Bible does not reveal many details of the afterlife or the new creation, and that Deuteronomy 29:29 limits speculation (55:02).
- **Judaism's oral Torah is necessary.** Tabor describes the Orthodox view of two Torahs and contrasts it with a Protestant-style reading of the written text, which he sees as closer to the original (16:54).

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught.** Tabor says Paul's seven undisputed letters never identify the Messiah with the creator, and he treats Paul as a disciple of "the way" whose visions "took it in other directions." He also says later Pauline and post-Pauline developments (Christianity as an edifice) depart from the original faith.
- **James and the Jerusalem community.** Abke says the Epistle of James lays out the Two Ways and that "the Way" in Acts 24 may refer to Yhovah's way; Tabor agrees the Two Ways framing fits and cites James for "a good overview of the way."
- **The Nazarenes, Ebionites, and Essenes.** Tabor ties the desert "way" and the Melchizedek messianic banquet to these groups, citing the Damascus Document, the Community Rule fragment, and the Teacher of Righteousness. Abke adds the Gospel of the Hebrews and the Two Ways fragment.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet and nonviolence.** Tabor centers the Torah as justice and righteousness and says God desires mercy, not sacrifice; he cites Isaiah 66 and his reading of Isaiah 11 for peace among nations. Diet is not addressed. Nonviolence appears in his "project" and his blue-thread vs. red-thread contrast of violence and blood.
- **The Kingdom on earth.** Tabor insists that "this world is our home" and that the task is to fill the earth with justice and righteousness "as the waters cover the sea," contrasting this with heaven-focused salvation.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell.** Tabor says the Hebrew Bible does not describe eternal hell and that the afterlife is largely unrevealed. He does not discuss resurrection in detail.
- **Who Jesus thought he was.** Tabor says Jesus identified with a Messiah figure but was not God, that he taught the way of Yhovah, and that he "came … to serve." He says an Ebionite view of a messianic spirit resting on Jesus is one possibility.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed.** Tabor refers to layers and redaction, Mark as the earliest Gospel, a "Hebrew Matthew," and later Greek philosophical developments. He adds that a historical-critical reading gets closer to the earliest teaching, though he admits such methods are imperfect.
