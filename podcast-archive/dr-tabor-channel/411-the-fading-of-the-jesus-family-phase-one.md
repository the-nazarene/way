## Summary

Tabor opens a planned miniseries on what he calls "the fading of the family," meaning the Jesus family (Mary, James, Simon, Jude, and the sisters). This first episode asks how the author of Luke-Acts handles the family. Tabor assumes Mark was written first and that Luke used Mark as a source, rewriting it extensively. He notes that the author of Luke-Acts is anonymous and that the traditional name "Luke" is only a tradition. He argues that where Mark names Jesus's mother and brothers, Luke removes or leaves unnamed those references. He walks through the crucifixion, burial, empty tomb, and Acts 1, where "the brothers" appear without names.

The argument builds to Acts 12 and Acts 15. In Acts 12:17, Peter sends word to "James and the brothers," which Tabor says a reader of Luke-Acts alone would not identify. In Acts 15:12-19, at the Jerusalem assembly, Tabor says James speaks and gives the decision, but is introduced without identification. Tabor then points to Galatians, where Paul calls James "the Lord's brother," and to Acts 21, where Paul reports to James and the elders. He concludes that James led the Jerusalem movement until his death in 63, and that Luke-Acts downplays this. These claims rest on Tabor's reading of Luke-Acts compared with Mark and Galatians. He presents them as an argument to be studied, not as settled fact.

The talk ends with a call to subscribe and a preview of later episodes. Tabor carries the whole presentation himself, with no guest or host voice.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Luke rewrites Mark and removes family names

Tabor's starting premise is that Luke used Mark as a base and "overwrit[es] it fairly completely so that it really becomes a new work." His key example is the Nazareth scene. In Mark 6:3, Jesus is called the carpenter (Greek *tekton*, which Tabor says more likely means a stoneworker), and the family is named: Mary's son, James, Joses, Judas, Simon, and sisters. Tabor says Luke's parallel reads "Is this not Joseph's son?" and drops the mother, brothers, and sisters. He says Luke "never names the brothers."

### 2. The women at the cross, the burial, and the tomb

Tabor compares Mark 15:40-41 with Luke's version. Mark names Mary Magdalene, "Mary the mother of James the Younger and of Yose and of Salomi," and Salomi. Luke, he says, keeps "the women who'd followed him from Galilee" but does not name Mary the mother or Mary Magdalene. He repeats the pattern at the burial and at the empty tomb (Mark 16:1-2 and Luke 24). Luke 24:10 names Mary Magdalene, Joanna, and "Mary the mother of James," and Tabor reads this as Luke being willing to name the women but not emphasizing them. He says the pattern is "not the blotting out, but certainly the fading of the family."

### 3. Acts: the brothers appear unnamed

In Acts 1:14, the women, "Mary, the mother of Jesus, and with his brothers" are in the upper room. Tabor says Luke knows the brothers must be present but does not name them. He also notes that James is absent from the list of the Eleven, with Peter at their head. In Acts 12:17, Peter says "Go tell this to James and the brothers," and Tabor says this only works as a clue to a reader who already knows who James is.

### 4. Acts 15: James's decision, unidentified

Tabor's central claim is that at the Jerusalem assembly in Acts 15:12-19, after Peter and Paul speak, "James replied," and Luke does not identify him. Tabor argues Luke "can't take that out," because other sources show James was in charge. He cites Galatians, where Paul calls him "James the Lord's brother," and Acts 21, where Paul goes to James and the elders and gives an account of his work. Tabor says Paul sees himself as accountable to James in Jerusalem, which he calls "the headquarters of the Nazarene movement."

### 5. James's leadership and death

Tabor says James was the leader of the movement for "over 30 years," until his death in 63, and that the Jerusalem community treated him as "the pillar of the community." He says the talk's question is why Luke does not emphasize that the leader was Jesus's brother. He raises the possibility of a "dynasty" or "caliphate" but says he does not want to "get ahead" of the evidence. He presents the question for the series to explore.

### 6. Mary as a historical Jewish woman

Tabor says he is looking at Mary as a Jewish woman of her time, "not as a Catholic saint or a nun or queen of heaven." He describes her as the mother of eight children who "probably ends up a widow." This is a framing point he sets up for his upcoming book, *The Lost Mary*, not a fully argued claim in this episode.

- **James as a minor figure.** Tabor says James was "in charge of the movement," the "pillar of the community," and the leader for over 30 years, which he says Luke obscures.
