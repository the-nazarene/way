## Summary

Tabor asks what "God" meant in the Hebrew Bible, and whether the sense of God in those texts can be taken seriously once the ancient Near Eastern mythology in them (Leviathan, Rahab, Yahweh's possible origins as a mountain god with a consort) is acknowledged. He says he reads that mythology as mythology, but argues that by "rarifying" it, a fairly consistent concept of God emerges across Genesis, the prophets and the Psalms. He presents this as one possible description of reality, not a religion. He grounds the idea in Genesis 1 (his translation of the opening verses as a temporal clause and the claim that humans are made in the image of Elohim), in the divine name revealed to Moses ("I will be who I will be," which he reads as an imperfect verb), in the Exodus 34-style proclamation of God's mercy and justice, and in Second Isaiah's monotheistic claims. He links this to process philosophy (Whitehead, as read through Hartshorne) and to Jesus' emphasis on ethics, citing the Shema, neighbor-love, and the Mark 12 scribe exchange ("You're not far from the kingdom").

The talk ends with Job. Tabor argues that Job's friends and Job share a theodicy problem that the Hebrew faith does not resolve, that God's answer in the book is "not much of an answer," and that the Hebrew view of death ("dust to dust") gives no comforting afterlife. The transcript closes with Job's hope that his "redeemer" will vindicate him, followed by music. The talk is an edited, informal presentation to his research group, so its claims are offered as his working reading rather than settled results.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Genesis 1 as order out of chaos, not a cosmic origin story
Tabor reads Genesis 1 "conceptually." He offers his own translation of Genesis 1:1-2 as a single temporal clause ("At the first of Elohim creating the skies in the land"), then describes the chaotic deep and the hovering spirit as the state of the planet before organized life. He says the waters are chaotic in the sense that a storm at sea is dangerous, and that the dry land is "a place for humans." The "order" he describes is natural: gravity, electromagnetism and the nuclear forces producing structures that build up to living things. He explicitly says these are not "magic tricks." He compares this to the way frozen cells are "dead" in the sense of inactive but not destroyed, using that to make a point about defining death.

### 2. Humans made in the image of Elohim, not as servants of the gods
Contrasting Genesis with Gilgamesh, Enuma Elish and Egyptian creation texts (which he describes through Jonathan Z. Smith's "locative" idea, where humans exist to serve the gods), Tabor argues that Genesis 1:26 presents humans as made male and female in the image of Elohim, with freedom of thought and choice. He reads Psalm 8:5 as "a little less than Elohim," arguing that most translations render it "angels" for theological comfort, though he thinks Elohim is the right reading. He calls the freedom that humans have "the most scary thing I can think of."

### 3. The divine name and the nature of God as "becoming"
Tabor works with Exodus 3 and Exodus 6 (the name revealed to Moses). He reads the Hebrew "I will be who I will be" as an imperfect verb signaling ongoing action, and draws a parallel to Whitehead's idea that God is "becoming." He relates Elohim (grammatically plural but with singular verbs) to a single "force of all forces," and cites the Numbers phrase "God of the spirits of all flesh" as evidence that the author linked human spirit to that force. He sees Paul's Acts 17:28 quotation ("In him we live and move and have our being") as an echo of the same idea, though he attributes it to earlier Greek poets.

### 4. Ethics as the core of God's name
Tabor emphasizes that when Moses asks to see God's glory, the answer proclaimed is a list of ethical attributes ("merciful and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness"), not dominance. He says the phrase about visiting the sins of the fathers "to the third and fourth generation" was added later by editors who thought the list was "getting a little bit too positive." He adds that Deuteronomy 10:17 and Deuteronomy 6:4-5 tie justice, truth and righteousness to the nature of God. He notes that this is a sampling from his chapter on God.

### 5. Second Isaiah, Jeremiah and monotheistic claims
He quotes Isaiah passages ("Besides me, there's no Elohim," "I am the first, the last") and a Jeremiah passage ("Call to me and I will answer you") and states his opinion, which he says is not universally held, that Jeremiah wrote Second Isaiah.

### 6. Jesus as an ethical Jew and the Mark 12 exchange
Tabor argues that Jesus stressed that "the Sabbath is made for humans, not humans for the Sabbath," treating laws as ways of mapping ethical behavior. He presents the Shema and neighbor-love (Mark 12:28-34; he says "Mark 10") as central, and calls the scribe's reply, which is followed by "You're not far from the kingdom," the greatest statement in Mark. He says Matthew and Luke drop it. He suggests that Jesus' affirmations were ethical rather than sacrificial and that "I think Jesus was an advanced guy."

### 7. Acts 15 as a hypothetical James letter
Tabor reads the Acts 15 decree (abstain from idolatry, from blood, and from sexual immorality) as a version of James' ruling. He then imagines James speaking to Gentiles, and says "I wish it were true. I have no idea it was true." He observes that Torah's priestly material (Exodus 25 to Numbers 10) is full of slaughter and atonement, and that some prophets, including Jeremiah, "brush that aside," saying God never commanded it.

### 8. Job, theodicy and the absence of an answer
Tabor reads the oldest layer of Job (without the prologue and epilogue) as a debate about whether suffering proves wickedness. He says God's speech in the whirlwind "is the author's way of saying there is no answer but there is an answer," and that Job's own closing hope (a "redeemer" who will read his written case) is the only hope on offer. He argues that the Hebrew faith as a whole only makes sense if there is future judgment or an afterlife, which the earlier texts do not clearly provide: "dust to dust you shall return."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That the Hebrew Bible's God is just Canaanite mythology and can't be taken seriously.** Tabor grants the mythology and argues that a consistent conceptual picture of God can be distilled from it.
- **That Psalm 8's "a little less than Elohim" means "angels."** He says most translations render it "angels" for theological comfort, and argues for "Elohim."
- **That God is a supernatural being who performs magic.** He describes the order in Genesis 1 as natural forces, not "magic tricks."
- **That the Hebrew Bible promises an afterlife and eternal reward.** He argues that the older texts speak of death as "rotting in the grave," and that Job's ending offers no answer.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor uses Paul only as an echo (Acts 17:28), and contrasts Jesus' ethical emphasis with later doctrinal forms. He does not build an argument about Paul's letters in this talk.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** His Acts 15 reading ("What if James said...") is speculation, which he flags as such ("I have no idea it was true").
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet and nonviolence:** He contrasts the priestly sacrifice material with prophetic rejections of it, and reads Acts 15's blood prohibition as a restriction on shedding blood, including animal sacrifice.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** His only kingdom reference is the Mark 12 "not far from the kingdom" line, which he uses as evidence of Jesus' ethical emphasis.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** This is central to the Job section. Tabor argues the Hebrew texts lack a clear afterlife, and that the faith would need future judgment to make sense of suffering.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** He notes that Matthew and Luke drop the Mark 12:34 line, and that editors added the "visiting sins" clause to Exodus 34.
