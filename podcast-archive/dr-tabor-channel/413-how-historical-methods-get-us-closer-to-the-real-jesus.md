## Summary

The talk is a short Q&A answer by James D. Tabor to Jeremiah Giehl of the Pulling the Thread podcast. The question is whether historical-critical methods for studying Jesus are constructive or destructive. Tabor argues they are constructive. He says "critical" means careful, not "tear down," and that the method aims to strip away "the web of theological overlay" to reach the historical Jesus. ## Key Teachings

### Historical-critical method as construction, not demolition
Tabor's opening argument is definitional. "Critical" means careful, and the method "means build up and construct." He describes its target as the theological layers laid over the figure of Jesus, which he says must be peeled back before one can know "what he was really about and what he said and taught." He frames the alternative, treating the Gospels as direct transcripts, as a position that leaves "nothing to discuss."

### Q and the hard sayings
Tabor says Q is a useful concept regardless of whether one "believe[s] in Q." Material Matthew and Luke share that Mark lacks is, in his view, mostly teaching of Jesus and includes "more primitive, more original" material. His example is a saying that among those "born of men" there is none greater than John the Baptist. He argues that an editor (redactor) probably added the clause "the least in the kingdom is greater than" him, because the original saying was too shocking for later Christian theology. His method: "we go for the hard sayings of Jesus," the ones later followers would have explained away.

### Hebrew Matthew as a control text
Tabor holds up a Hebrew version of Matthew (edited by George Howard, captioned "Evan Bohan" for the rabbi who preserved it) as evidence. He says that version lacks both the "least in the kingdom" clause and the baptismal formula "in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost" of Matthew 28. He also notes that Origen and other church fathers quote Matthew and would have "made a big deal about it" had the trinitarian formula been in their copies, though he adds that Origen "certainly thought Jesus was divine." Tabor's conclusion is that Jesus did not use the trinitarian formula ("Jesus is a trinitarian" is his ironic framing of the Christian reading).

### Mark as the earlier Gospel and Matthew as its rewriter
Tabor says Mark is the earliest Gospel, acknowledging that the church tradition held Matthew to be first. He illustrates with Mark 10: a man calls Jesus "good master," and Jesus replies, "Why are you calling me good? There's no one good but God." He argues Matthew rewrites this, reframing the question so it is not put to Jesus about goodness, because Matthew's readers would find Jesus's reply problematic. The point is that Matthew's changes reveal the theological concerns of a later writer.

### Christ of faith versus Jesus of history
Tabor draws a sharp distinction. The "Christ of faith" developed through church councils, creeds, and "the thoughts of Paul," which he says became dominant. Paul, in his view, wrote "decades" after Jesus, and whether Paul reflects Jesus is a question he says the talk will reach later. He says he wants "the Jesus of history."

### Jesus as proclaiming an original Abrahamic faith and the kingdom of God
Tabor presents his own positive account: he has spent "45 years" on the historical Jesus, and he thinks Jesus was "proclaiming what we might call a original Abrahamic faith," bringing "the good news of the kingdom of God" rather than a new message. He says this is his latest thinking and is developed in a book he holds up but does not name in the captions; he says the book will be discussed later.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Critical" scholarship is destructive and aims to tear down the Gospels.** Tabor says critical means careful and constructive, aiming to build up the layers that go back to Jesus.
- **Everything in the four Gospels is Jesus's own words, so the red letter edition is enough.** Tabor says editors added to the text, and the Gospels must be sorted by layer and compared with earlier texts.
- **Matthew is the earliest Gospel.** Tabor says this is a church tradition and that Mark is earlier, with Matthew rewriting it.
- **Jesus taught the Trinity, as shown by the baptismal formula in Matthew 28.** Tabor argues the formula is a later addition not present in the Hebrew Matthew and not quoted by Origen in the form he would have expected.
- **Jesus brought a new message.** Tabor says Jesus proclaimed an original Abrahamic faith and the good news of the kingdom of God.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor explicitly sets the "Christ of faith," shaped by "the thoughts of Paul," against the "Jesus of history." He defers the question of whether Paul reflects Jesus to later discussion, so the talk raises the question without answering it.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Tabor describes Jesus's message as "the good news of the kingdom of God," an Abrahamic faith, without elaborating on what that kingdom is in this excerpt.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor's trinitarian point and his "good" example bear on Jesus's self-understanding. He argues Jesus did not teach a divine Trinity and did not expect to be called "good" in a way that would identify him with God.
