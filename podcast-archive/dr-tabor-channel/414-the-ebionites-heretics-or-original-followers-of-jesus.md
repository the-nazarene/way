## Summary

Tabor answers the question of whether the Ebionites were heretics or reflected the original followers of Jesus, in a segment from a Q&A with Jeremiah Giehl of the Pulling the Thread podcast. He opens by directing viewers to his blog article on the Ebionites and Nazarenes, which he calls a detailed overview with sources and a numbered summary. He then sketches Ebionite beliefs: Jesus was a human being with a father and mother, an anointed Messiah and a "prophet like Moses"; they were vegetarian and rejected temple animal sacrifice as a later addition; and they held that the Torah had been altered by priests and others, which he compares to modern historical-critical reading.

His main evidence is the fourth-century heresiologist Epiphanius, whose *Panarion* (chapters 29 and 30) treats the Ebionites and Nazarenes as heretical groups. Tabor argues that Epiphanius's own text points to an earlier Nazarene sect of Judaism east of the Jordan that shared these practices, and that the two names describe essentially the same early movement. He compares this with Dead Sea Scrolls material that also questions the temple sacrifices and the authority of the received Torah. Tabor also argues that the Ebionites' main objection to Paul was that Paul was a later figure who claimed a vision, while the Twelve had known Jesus in the flesh. He ties this to Paul's "after the flesh" remark and to the blood prohibition of Acts 15.

The segment ends with Tabor's reconstruction of the Ebionite worldview: a return to God's original ideal (no divorce, a peaceable kingdom), with Isaiah 11 as the picture of the Messiah's rule, and a view of Jesus as the final prophet rather than one to be succeeded by later revelation. Tabor frames this as the Ebionite position and as a plausible one, and he says he has changed his mind on related questions over the years.

## Key Teachings

### Jesus as human Messiah and prophet like Moses
Tabor says the Ebionites held that Jesus was "a flesh and blood human being born of a woman," and that "born of a woman" in Hebrew simply means a human being, not a virgin birth. They saw Jesus as Messiah in the sense of one anointed by the Spirit, citing Isaiah 61 and its echo in Luke 4. They also called him a prophet like Moses, which Tabor ties to Acts 7 (Stephen's speech). He adds that "Messiah" should not be heard as "king" in the usual sense. In his reading, Hebrew "nasi" (prince or ruler) fits better, and God alone is the true king.

### Vegetarianism and rejection of temple sacrifice
Tabor says the Ebionites did not eat meat and held that the temple slaughter of animals was a later addition. He cites Genesis 1:29 ("every green plant") as the original diet, with Genesis 9's permission to eat meat treated as a concession. For sacrifice, he cites Jeremiah 7 ("I did not ever command you to do this"), Amos, and Isaiah 66 ("Heaven is my throne"). He reads the Jeremiah 7 phrase "den of robbers" as a mistranslation of a word meaning "to rip apart," the same word used for a ravenous lion in Isaiah. He also points to the Community Rule from Qumran, which says that prayers will be the offering in place of animal blood and holocausts, as an earlier precursor of the Ebionite position. He reports that the Ebionite "Hebrew gospels" have Jesus warn against shedding innocent blood in the temple.

### Returning to the original Torah
Tabor says the Ebionites believed the Torah had been altered over time, especially by priests, and that some material "that is not really of the Torah" had been added. He gives divorce as an example: Moses permitted it, but Jesus says that "from the beginning it was not" so. He compares this method to modern historical-critical study, and says the Qumran community likewise claimed a better Torah (he gives as an example a claim that David had many wives because he lacked the true "seal book of the Torah"; he gives no source for this).

### Epiphanius, the Nazarenes and the name "Ebionite"
Tabor describes Epiphanius as a Trinitarian bishop writing around 370 CE, after Constantine, who presented Paul as his main authority. The *Panarion* ("medicine chest") lists 80 heresies, and Tabor notes that its first heresy group list includes Jewish sects. In heresy 18 (the Nazarenes) Epiphanius describes a Jewish sect living across the Jordan that practiced circumcision and the Sabbath, honored the patriarchs, rejected a Torah that had been "polluted," and abstained from sacrifice and meat. Tabor argues that Epiphanius treats the later Ebionites and these Nazarenes as separate, but that in the early period they were basically one movement, with "Nazarene" a name the followers used themselves and which Jews still apply to Christians. He links this to Isaiah 11:1 and the "branch" of David. He also explains that "Ebionite" means "poor ones," connected to Jesus' beatitude on the poor.

### Paul versus the Twelve
Tabor states the Ebionite objection as a simple choice: follow those who were with Jesus and were promised rule over the twelve tribes, or a "13th" figure who claims a vision. He notes that the Ebionites questioned whether Paul had really seen Jesus. He sets out the Eucharist dispute: the Ebionites would not drink blood, even symbolically, and he argues that the Gospel accounts of the cup as Jesus' blood (which he says appear in Mark, probably derived from Paul) would not come naturally to a Jew from a blood-abstaining tradition. He points to the Acts 15 prohibition on blood and meat sacrificed to idols as a counterpoint.

### "After the flesh" and the danger of later revelation
Tabor reads Paul's words "We no longer know Jesus after the flesh" as a claim to newer revelation. He argues that the Ebionites would reject this, since they had "touched him and knew him and heard him firsthand." He compares the pattern to Muhammad and to the Mormon Book of Mormon as later revelations that claim to supersede earlier ones. In his telling, the Ebionites would hold that Jesus was the final prophet, and that the ideal of peace found in Isaiah 11 (the wolf with the lamb) is what Jesus was expected to bring.

### Mark as a post-temple text
Briefly, Tabor refers to a blog post and video arguing that Mark is "the earliest surviving Jewish text after the Jerusalem temple was destroyed." He says Mark presents seven ways to follow the God of Israel without a temple, quoting the Shema and the claim that loving God and neighbor is greater than burnt offerings. He says this is a view he has only recently come to and that he finds it "pretty persuasive."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Ebionites were just a random heretical sect.** Tabor argues Epiphanius's label is a polemical frame, and that the Ebionites and Nazarenes were basically one early movement with a continuous set of practices.
- **"Born of a woman" implies a virgin birth.** Tabor says in Hebrew it simply means Jesus was a human being, with a father and mother.
- **Messiah means "king" in the usual sense.** Tabor says "king" is misleading; God is the true king, and the Messiah is better understood as a "nasi" (prince or ruler).
- **The Jeremiah 7 "den of robbers" describes thieves.** Tabor reads the Hebrew as "to rip apart," with the same word used for a ravenous lion.
- **The Torah is fixed and unaltered.** Tabor says the Ebionites (and Qumran) held that priests and others added material to it, a view he likens to historical-critical study.
- **Paul's view of Jesus after the resurrection is simply continuous with the earliest apostles.** Tabor reports that the Ebionites questioned Paul's apostleship and visions.
