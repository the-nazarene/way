## Summary

Tabor argues that the Hebrew Bible, read as the story of Abraham's family, is not a religion in the usual sense. Ordinary religions, he says, are mainly about another world and the afterlife, while this book is about "this world." He presents the talk as a faith statement from his new book *Restoring Abrahamic Faith*, which he says he wrote with his academic hat off, though it still carries full notes and a bibliography. He is careful to say he reads the Bible historically and critically, not literally.

His main evidence is textual. He says the Hebrew Bible gives no clear answers about life after death. He cites Job, Ecclesiastes and Isaiah 26 (the last of which he calls late), and says Daniel is late and contains the book's one resurrection text. He reads Genesis as presenting human freedom and choice outside Eden, with violence following. He then frames his position around three questions: what "the way" of God means, what the divine "plan" is, and what humans should do about it. He points to the Lord's Prayer ("Let your kingdom come") as a summary of his hope for the earth.

The talk ends with Tabor describing Abrahamic faith as a "lonely road" that he does not direct people to, and with his statement that he would label himself "Hebrew" rather than Ebionite. Much of the closing exchange is unlabeled, so it is not always clear whether the short affirmations ("Mhm," "Yeah, I share that") come from the host.

## Key Teachings

### The Hebrew Bible is about this world, not the next
Tabor's central claim is that the Hebrew Bible "doesn't give any answers" to questions about life after death. He says the book's answer is found in "the good earth," and he uses Robert Frost's poem "Birches" to make the point: "The earth's the right place for love." He argues that humans did not "fall" into this world, saying "It's not a mistake. Humans didn't fall. They made choices to leave childhood innocence."

### Afterlife and resurrection are faint or late
He cites several texts to show that resurrection is not a clear theme. He quotes Job's wish for a "redeemer" who could defend him, and Job's images of a cut tree sprouting again and of a man who "lies low and awakes no more." He describes Ecclesiastes as saying the same thing. He calls Daniel "fairly late" with "one text on resurrection," and says Isaiah 26 is "also a late text." He contrasts this with the New Testament, where, he says, resurrection is set out at the end in a defined way.

### Life outside Eden is violent, and God's "way" is open to question
Tabor reads the story of Cain and Abel as the start of a world where "violence fills the earth," which he calls "outside the gates of Eden," citing Bob Dylan. He says he does not read the Bible literally. He then poses the question of "the way" directly: "Is the way going and slaughtering men, women, and children and animals and genocide?" He presents this as a question his book works through, not as a settled answer.

### The organizing structure: God, the way, the plan, and response
He says the book is organized around four questions: what "God" means in Hebrew thought, what "the way" is, what the "plan" is, and what to do about it. He says he added a chapter on the Messiah "mainly because of Christians" and ends with a chapter on practical response.

### Ethics: justice, righteousness and "fixing the world"
Tabor's favorite passage is Genesis, where Abraham "will teach the way of Hashem... justice and righteousness" to his household. He presents the Jewish idea of *tikun* ("fixing the world") as the human task, which he says he does not find in the Hebrew Bible itself. He applies it to pollution, animals and planetary stewardship, and says the five virtues of the Hebrew Bible (which he lists in the book but does not name in the talk) are the core of his ethic.

### The Kingdom as earthly hope
He closes with the Lord's Prayer, "Let your kingdom come, your rule. Let your will be done on earth as it is in heaven," which he treats as a summary of his hope for this world.

### "Hebrew" as a label, not "Ebionite"
Tabor says that if he had a label, it would be "Hebrew," not Ebionite. He glosses "Hebrew" as one who "crosses over" from the culture of Abraham's time, and uses that to describe a move from human greed and selfishness toward a vision of what the world could be. The derivation is his own gloss in this talk; he does not cite a source for it.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Religion is essentially about the afterlife.** Tabor says ordinary religions focus on "another world" and the afterlife, while the Hebrew Bible is "a this worldly book."
- **The Hebrew Bible clearly teaches resurrection.** He says resurrection appears only in late texts (Daniel, Isaiah 26) and that the book gives no clear affirmation about life after death.
- **Humans fell from a perfect state.** He says "Humans didn't fall" and that the Eden story shows choices, not a fall.
- **Abrahamic faith is a tradition with a clear institutional home.** He says it is a "lonely road," that he does not direct people to any group, and that it was historically "a household faith."

- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** Tabor's central argument is that the Hebrew Bible largely lacks an afterlife doctrine, with resurrection appearing only in late texts. - **The Kingdom on earth:** He treats the Lord's Prayer's "Let your kingdom come... on earth as it is in heaven" as the heart of his earthly hope. He presents this as the vision of what the world could be, without tying it to a historical reconstruction of Jesus.
