## Summary

Tabor asks why the Gospel of John never names Jesus's mother, why its main source is an unnamed "disciple whom Jesus loved," and what this says about the fading of the Jesus family from the early tradition. This is the second part of his "Fading of the Jesus Family" series, following a video on Luke and Acts. He presents it as a close reading of John, with the texts shared in a document.

His central claim is that the beloved disciple is James, the brother of the Lord, the leader of the Jerusalem Nazarene community. He argues this from John 19:26-27, where the dying Jesus hands his mother to the beloved disciple. Tabor reads this as a handover of family responsibility to the next brother in line. He supports the identification with several other points: the beloved disciple's connections to the high priest (John 18), the way the Gospel names no disciple who appears in the later chapters, the rumor in John 21 that this disciple would not die before Jesus returned, and parallel traditions about James in the Gospel of Thomas, the Gospel of the Hebrews (as quoted by Jerome), and Hegesippus. He contrasts this with the tradition that the beloved disciple was John son of Zebedee, which he traces to Irenaeus around 200 CE and calls late and derivative. Tabor says he is making an assertion rather than presenting settled fact, and he notes that the identification is "not something most people have heard."

The talk ends with the observation that Mary is never named in John, and a teaser for Phase Three, which will turn to what the rest of the New Testament says about James. Tabor says he will move beyond the Gospels to Paul and other New Testament texts.

## Key Teachings

### The beloved disciple as James, from the cross (John 19:26-27)
Tabor reads John 19:26-27 as the key verse. Jesus, dying, sees his mother and "the disciple whom he loved" and gives them to each other, and "from that hour the disciple took her to his own home." He calls this "the eldest son, handing on the responsibilities of the mother and the children to the next in line," and argues that the next in line is James, whom Paul calls the brother of the Lord. He says the reasoning is that Jesus would not give his mother's care to a non-family member while a brother was leading the movement. He concludes that "the beloved disciple is in fact James," and he adds that the verse shows this.

### Connections to the high priest (John 18)
In John 18, the beloved disciple "was known to the high priest," and enters the courtyard where Peter waits. Tabor says the Gospel first takes Jesus to the house of Annas, whom he calls the leader of a family of high priests. He argues that an intimate follower with these ties must have had a priestly connection. He links this to Hegesippus's account, which Tabor says preserves that James wore the priestly miter, and that he could enter the priestly areas of the temple. He also says Mary, in his forthcoming book *The Lost Mary*, descends from priestly and Davidic lines, though he does not develop that here.

### Reading John 20-21 and the beginnings of the empty tomb tradition
Tabor argues that John 20 is the earliest empty-tomb account and reflects what "probably happened," with no angels or earthquake, and with the body moved Saturday night to a tomb supplied by Joseph of Arimathea. He says the beloved disciple "beats Peter" to the tomb and "believed" only in the sense that he saw the tomb was empty, not that he believed in resurrection. He notes parallel material in the Gospel of Peter (disciples mourn seven days, then return to Galilee) and treats John 21 as the Galilee appendix, based on the beloved disciple's testimony. He points to the sentence "Anyone who's named isn't the beloved disciple" as a way to narrow the candidates in John 21:2.

### The rumor that the beloved disciple would not die (John 21:20-23)
Tabor reads the rumor that this disciple would not die before Jesus returned as a circulating story that the Gospel refutes. He suggests the story arose after James's death (which he dates to 63 CE), when the early community expected the coming of Jesus within the generation. In his reading, Jesus had "turned things over to James," and James would be the last before the return.

### Late origins of the John son of Zebedee identification
Tabor argues that the popular idea of the beloved disciple as John the fisherman, son of Zebedee, who lived in Ephesus and cared for Mary, has no early basis. He says the earliest reference is Irenaeus, around 200 CE, and that Eusebius (the transcript gives "Eczeius") noted two Johns, an elder and the son of Zebedee, and suggested Irenaeus confused them. He presents this as the source of the later identification. He also argues that the Zebedee brothers are poor candidates on other grounds: Luke 9:51-55 and Mark 10:35-40 depict them as vindictive and ambitious, which he thinks makes them unlikely to be the figure of intimate care.

### Other traditions about James and the "Jerusalem" line
Tabor cites the Gospel of Thomas, where disciples ask who will lead them after Jesus departs, and Jesus says to go to "James the righteous." He also quotes Jerome's citation of the Gospel of the Hebrews, which he describes as a text used by the Jerusalem community: after the Last Supper, James swears not to eat until he sees the risen Jesus, and Jesus later gives him bread. Tabor says he does not claim that this account is historical, only that the tradition is "being passed on among the Jewish followers of Jesus connected to the Nazarene movement." He adds that Paul's Jerusalem visits show James in charge.

### Mary and the family in John
Tabor says Mary is never named in John. He reads the Cana episode (John 2:1-5) as showing Jesus in charge of the family, with Joseph absent. He takes "Woman, what have you to do with me?" as not a rebuke, and reads "my hour has not yet come" as tied to the Gospel's drama. He notes that Jesus is called "son of Joseph" in John 6:42, and that John 7:3-5 presents the brothers as not yet believing. He presents Joseph's disappearance and the absence of any birth narrative as part of the Gospel's fading of the family.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The beloved disciple is John, son of Zebedee, the fisherman.** Tabor says this identification comes from a late tradition, first attested by Irenaeus around 200 CE, and is contradicted by the textual evidence that points to James.
- **John the son of Zebedee lived in Ephesus and cared for Mary there.** Tabor calls this "a completely unreliable tradition" and "very late and very derivative."
- **The Zebedee brothers as the intimate disciples.** Tabor argues from Luke 9:51-55 and Mark 10:35-40 that the sons of thunder were vindictive and ambitious, which does not fit the beloved disciple.
- **Jesus's brothers were not part of his circle.** Tabor argues that the family, including the brothers, was once central, and that John's silence about them reflects a later fading rather than the original picture.

- **James and the Jerusalem community:** This is the talk's core argument. Tabor ties the beloved disciple, Acts 15, Paul's testimony, Hegesippus, and the Gospel of Thomas into a picture of James as leader of the Nazarene movement.
- **Jesus's family:** Tabor's main theme is the progressive removal of Mary, Joseph, and the brothers from the story, which he says runs from Luke and Acts through John.
