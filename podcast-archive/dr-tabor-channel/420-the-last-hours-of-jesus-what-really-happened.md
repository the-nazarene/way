The talk's stated question is what really happened in Jesus's last hours, given that the four Gospels give differing accounts of the trial, Pilate, Barabbas and the burial. ## Key Teachings

### Method first: the Gospels as literary texts
Bond argues that the four Gospels must be read first as "skillfully put together literary texts with particular purposes in mind," and that the reader should ask what each witness is trying to achieve. She describes her talk as starting by examining the four accounts on their own terms before attempting to "piece together" a picture of what happened. Tabor adds that "critical" in this setting means "careful, not sloppy," and that Matthew, Mark and Luke should not be assumed to say the same thing.

### Pilate: four Gospels, Josephus, Philo and material evidence
Bond's doctoral work was on Pontius Pilate, and she treats him as a literary character as well as a historical figure. Her method was to examine how each source projects ideas about Rome onto the governor. She names the differences between Josephus's *Jewish War* and *Antiquities* (Josephus is more negative in the *Antiquities*) and Philo of Alexandria's strongly hostile description. She also says that the material record for Pilate has grown, citing the Pilate stone with "prefect of Judea," some coins, a small ring bearing the name Pilatus, and a ceremonial pavement in Jerusalem with coins of Pilate underneath, which she reads as evidence the pavement was built during his time. She notes that he was in Judea for about ten or eleven years.

### Caiaphas and the mechanics of a Passover execution
Bond says Caiaphas was a political appointment, put in place by Pilate's predecessor Valerius Gratus (the speaker's own name given in the transcript is "Gratus"), and that he had to balance keeping the people and the prefect content. On whether a trial was needed, Bond says she thinks not: "human life is really cheap" and crucifixion could be applied without one. She argues that a figure with a following might still have been examined, with the authorities choosing between killing a leader quickly, as Herod Antipas did with John the Baptist, or rounding up followers more widely. She adds that most messianic pretenders of the first century, as Josephus lists them, ended up dead.

### Barabbas: the name, and the "uprising" prisoners
Tabor and Bond both find Barabbas interesting. Bond notes that Barabbas appears in all four Gospels, and that each uses him slightly differently. In Mark, she observes, Barabbas is in prison with "the prisoners from the uprising" (Mark 15), which she reads as possibly ambiguous about whether he was himself one of the rebels. Tabor notes the variant name "Jesus son of the father" (Jesus Barabbas), suggesting the choice Pilate offers may be a play on names. Bond says she has written on Barabbas and that she thinks there may be a historical core, though he has "somehow got into the tradition."

### Burial and the reliability of the tomb tradition
Bond lists burial as one of the questions her talk will address, and says it is contested whether Jesus was buried, whether he was not buried, and, if buried, whether it was the kind of burial the Gospels describe or something less glamorous. The transcript gives no further argument on this point.

### Tiberius, Sejanus and the reporting chain
Tabor asks whether Tiberius, then on Capri, would have known of Jesus's death. Bond says she thinks it "unlikely" that Tiberius knew much, while noting that non-canonical texts have letters from Pilate and emperors reporting the affair. She reasons that Pilate's position was shaped by Sejanus (whom she says had been in power around 26 CE when Pilate was appointed, and who fell in 31 CE), and by the absence of a Syrian legate in Antioch during most of Pilate's time in office, since the three legates of the period served their time in Rome. She suggests that this absence may have made it harder for Pilate to call on a legion in a crisis. She also discusses the incident in Philo where Pilate's shields in Jerusalem caused complaints, reading it as an attempt to look loyal to Tiberius during an uncertain time.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Pilate is a figure with no independent record.** Bond says the material evidence has grown, citing the stone, coins, a ring and the pavement, and that Josephus and Philo give more than the Gospels alone.
- **The scenes familiar from films, such as Pilate washing his hands, come from the Gospel record.** Tabor says these are cultural images that are not necessarily tied to the sources, and that people often do not know where they come from.
- **Mark is a repeat of Matthew and less important.** Tabor says he once read Mark as a repetition of Matthew, and later came to see Mark as the foundation for the other Gospels.
- **Tiberius would have known about Jesus's execution.** Bond says this is unlikely, while noting that later texts claim otherwise.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Jesus vs. the gospel writers:** Bond's and Tabor's central question, whether the Gospels' passion narrative reflects events or the writers' theological and literary aims, bears directly on how the Gospels were shaped (Tabor's own talk is titled on this theme).
- **Historical Jesus and his context:** The talk spends most of its time on the Roman provincial setting (Pilate, Caiaphas, Sejanus, the Syrian legate), which is relevant to understanding the political pressures around a Jewish leader executed at Passover.
- **Gospels and canon formation:** Bond's method of reading each Gospel on its own terms, and Tabor's remark that Mark came first, bear on how the four accounts relate to one another.
- **Who Jesus was:** Tabor raises the possibility that Jesus was one of several messianic figures whom Rome treated as a threat, and Bond agrees that such figures generally ended in execution, but neither speaker reaches a conclusion about Jesus's self-understanding.
- **Matthew's crowd statement:** Tabor notes that Matthew 27's statement about the crowd's blood has been widely quoted and says students should not simply accept all four Gospels as one account, though neither speaker expands on its later use.
