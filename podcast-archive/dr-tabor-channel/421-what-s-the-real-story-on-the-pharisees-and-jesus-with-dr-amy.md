## Summary

Tabor hosts this video, which is mainly a promotional interview for the "New Investigations of the New Testament" virtual conference (September 26-28), in which Levine is slated to present "Jesus and the Pharisees: Correcting Stereotypes." Most of the substantive content comes from Levine, with Tabor asking questions and adding examples. The conference blurb Tabor reads states the talk's premise: Christians have portrayed Pharisees as xenophobic, legalistic hypocrites and Jesus as the egalitarian reformer, while the primary sources (Paul, Josephus, the Dead Sea Scrolls, rabbinic literature and the Gospels) supposedly show the Pharisees as popular, pragmatic liberalizers and Jesus as comparatively more conservative and strict.

Levine's case rests on three lines of argument. First, the Pharisee stereotype is a flexible projection of whatever Christians dislike, and it is dangerous because the Pharisees are the forerunners of rabbinic Judaism, so maligning them reaches Jewish ears as antisemitic. Second, Levine argues that Jesus's own sayings ("you have to be more righteous than the Pharisees," the Sermon on the Mount's demand for perfection) and his practice (wearing fringes, debating Torah application, speaking with authority) place him inside a Pharisaic style of Torah debate and adaptation. She points to Pharisaic-style accommodation (the divorce allowance, Paul's pragmatic counsel) and to material evidence such as stone vessels, Sabbath lamps and handwashing as broadly Jewish practice rather than Pharisee-specific. Third, she argues that Jewish argument is a sign of engagement with Torah, which Christian church culture discourages, so reading Jesus within Jewish debate places him "in the heart of Judaism."

Tabor and Levine end by praising the conference lineup and cost ($79 before the 13th, with recordings kept by attendees), and by wishing for films that would portray a more realistic Pharisaic and Galilean Jesus. Levine says she would prefer people learn about Jesus from the Gospels read alongside scholarship rather than from Hollywood. ### Key Teachings

### The Pharisee stereotype is a flexible projection
Levine argues that in the Christian imagination Pharisees "can be anything you want," serving as a stand-in for whatever Christians dislike. She says books have used them to represent wokeness, super-liberalism, or conservative retrenchment. Her stated goal is to stop this and "do the history" instead. Tabor's framing reinforces the point, saying the Pharisees are "usable" in popular imagination.

### Pharisees as liberalizers and the lineage argument
Tabor reads the conference premise that the primary sources show Pharisees as popular, egalitarian, pragmatic liberalizers who helped Jews unite and resist Roman assimilation. Levine says the Pharisees "make things a little bit too easy" in some cases, and offers the Pharisaic habit of allowance (divorce permitted "because of your hardness of heart," Moses allowing divorce) as compassionate rather than lax. She contrasts this with the Qumran writers' criticism of the "Seekers after smooth things," who demanded stricter, more exact observance. She also points to Pharisaic adaptation to Roman rule and to the temple, and to the rabbinic lineage: the sages who compiled the Mishnah and Talmud belonged to the school of Hillel, which Levine notes gives those sources their own bias, as with a history written only by one party.

### Jesus's high bar and Pharisaic accommodation
Levine argues that Jesus sets a very high bar ("you have to be more righteous than the Pharisees," Matthew's Sermon on the Mount demand for perfection), which she compares to her mother's demanding grading. She contrasts this with the Pharisee approach of allowances ("do your best, but if you can't manage, try to give it 90%") while Jesus asks for 100 percent. Tabor adds that Jesus's sayings on divorce and other matters raise questions about circumstances, which is itself a Torah-updating process.

### Jesus within Torah debate and practice
Tabor and Levine argue that Jesus, like the Pharisees, is out among the people, is a popular teacher, interested in asking how biblical law applies today, and speaks with authority. Levine notes that the Pharisees' authority rested on knowing their texts and "walking the walk," so Jesus's charge of hypocrisy presupposes that they did practice what they taught. She argues that "hypocrite" in Greek was a theatre term for pretending, and that English usage now defines the word circularly by the Pharisees, which obscures the original sense.

### Minor and major commandments, and the fringes
Levine argues that Jesus wore fringes (tzitzit, per the Synoptics according to Tabor's question) and that this is not a minor matter but "literally wearing Torah." She reads Matthew 23 as Jesus endorsing tithing mint, dill and cumin while also urging attention to "the weightier matters of the law," a both-and rather than either-or position. She says Jews routinely rank commandments (ten, then three, then one), and that Jesus's practice fits that pattern.

### Handwashing, chalk vessels and Sabbath lamps as broad Jewish practice
Levine argues that practices often associated with Pharisees (Mark 7's handwashing, stone vessels, Sabbath lamps) are part of wider Jewish life, and that they were also means of resisting Roman colonialism and marking identity. Tabor agrees, citing archaeology. Levine contrasts the Qumran community, which was "its own thing," with the broader Jewish population, comparing it to the Amish. She argues that the Qumran rules about Sabbath walking and latrines (which Tabor notes made the Qumran latrines far off) were not typical of general Jewish life.

### Argument as a Jewish practice and why Christian churches differ
Levine says Jesus's disputes with the Pharisees look antagonistic from a New Testament perspective but would have been ordinary in a first-century Jewish context, comparable to the argumentative style of the yeshiva. She says Christians argue less because "if you argue too much, you're into heresy." Tabor and Levine both describe Jewish argument as a sharpening process, with the question "what about?" answered by "you're right, but I'm right, too."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Pharisees as legalistic, self-righteous, hypocritical villains.** Levine argues this is a stereotype built from whatever Christians dislike, and that the primary sources and Jewish tradition present the Pharisees as popular, pragmatic and compassionate teachers.
- **Jesus as the liberal reformer opposed to conservative Pharisees.** The conference premise, read by Tabor, argues that Jesus emerges as more conservative and strict than the Pharisees, who were the liberalizers.
- **"Hypocrite" simply means the Pharisees.** Levine says the Greek word was a theatre term for acting or pretending, and that English usage has become circular.
- **Fringes and other minor observances are unimportant.** Levine argues that Jesus wearing fringes and the Matthew 23 call to tithe mint, dill and cumin while attending to weightier matters show that observance mattered to him.
- **Handwashing, chalk vessels and Sabbath lamps are specifically Pharisaic.** Levine argues they were part of general Jewish practice and a form of resistance to Roman colonialism.
- **Only Christians are "believers."** Levine objects to the category, saying that non-believers simply believe other things.
