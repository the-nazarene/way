Tabor argues that Jesus began as a disciple of John the Baptist, that the two shared an apocalyptic expectation rooted in Daniel and related texts, and that Jesus' movement took its shape after John's arrest and death. ## Key Teachings

### Jesus as a disciple of John the Baptist, and the "greater than John" saying
Tabor treats John as the founder of the movement and Jesus as his follower until John's arrest. He cites a saying in which Jesus says that among those born of women none is greater than John, which Tabor says appears in Matthew and Luke through the shared Q material. He explains that Christian copyists added a qualifier about the kingdom, and he argues that the saying is a "harder saying" that probably predates later church attitudes. He also says Jesus called John "more than a prophet," which Tabor links to Deuteronomy's description of a prophet who speaks face to face with the Lord. Tabor adds that John "is Jesus's teacher." He reads John's Elijah role as the reason John moved his baptizing to a location near the Elijah stories, and he says Jesus sent word to John asking whether he was the one to come (Q, per Tabor).

### Apocalyptic expectation, Daniel, and the failed timetable
Tabor's central claim is that Jesus must be understood as an apocalyptic figure. He directs students to the book of Daniel and to Josephus, who says that an oracle about a coming savior provoked the war (Jewish War, book 6). Tabor says Josephus is pointing to Daniel 9 and the seventy-weeks prophecy. He describes Dead Sea Scrolls texts that place their own teacher within the final Jubilee, and says the Melchizedek scroll (cave 11) locates the rise of the teacher in the final 49-year period. Tabor says Jesus' statements that "this generation will not pass" (Mark 13) and that some listeners "will not taste of death until you see the kingdom of God come with power" (Mark 9) show that Jesus expected the kingdom within his own generation. He uses the Luke, Matthew, and Mark versions of the Olivet discourse to argue that Luke "fixed" the timetable by moving the end to an indeterminate future after the destruction of Jerusalem, while Matthew adds "delay" parables. Tabor's conclusion is that the community lived "on the edge of their seats" and that the expectation failed, with Rome growing stronger, which he says was "what they least expected."

### Messianic suffering and the Son of Man
Tabor argues that Jesus anticipated suffering, not only his own death. He reads the Son of Man sayings in Mark 8, 9, and 10 as collective, so that disciples are also called to suffer. He ties "take up a cross" to martyrdom and to Daniel 7, where the saints of the Most High receive the kingdom. Tabor says Jesus' statements on the suffering Son of Man draw on the Servant songs of Isaiah and, after John's death, on Zechariah. He proposes that Jesus read Zechariah 13 ("strike the shepherd and the sheep will be scattered") onto John's execution, and that Jesus' statement that Elijah "did to him what was written of him" in Mark 9 reflects that reading. Tabor says that Jesus' mother Mary, and the Essene texts, share this sense of suffering as the cost of a community's vindication.

### John's death and the shift in Jesus' message
Tabor proposes that John's execution "shook the movement to its roots" and sent Jesus away to "a lonely place" (Mark, per Tabor). He suggests that Jesus' message became more radical afterward, with commands to love enemies and turn the other cheek, although he says he is not sure this is the right description because so little of John's ethical teaching survives. Tabor's account is that Jesus, after John's arrest, took up John's messianic role and added the expectation of suffering. Sledge proposes that Jesus' background as a Galilean working-class man may have pushed his teaching toward greater radicalism; Tabor answers that the question is difficult and gives his tentative view only. Tabor also uses Luke's account of John's preaching (sharing a second cloak and food with the hungry) to show John's ethics.

### Jesus as exorcist, and the miracle-worker context
Tabor and Sledge agree that exorcism was central to Jesus' work. Tabor cites the statement that "if I by the finger of God cast out demons, then the kingdom of God has come upon you" as a Q saying and says it shows exorcism as evidence of the messianic age. He describes Jesus' vision of Satan falling "like a lightning bolt" as one of the few first-person mystical experiences in the gospels, and notes that Luke preserves it. Tabor also notes that other Galilean holy men (he names Hanina ben Dosa and Honi, though the captions garble the names) are rarely placed next to Jesus in scholarship. He says Jesus' miracle-working must be placed in a Jewish Galilean context and that gospel supernatural material may have been layered on later. Tabor points out that the Hanina ben Dosa tomb or ossuary is an area of Jewish-Christian dispute, and he says a rabbi he consulted in Jerusalem (Rabbi Yehuda Getz, per the captions) asked that such discussions not be publicized because of the risk of hatred toward Jews.

### Dead Sea Scrolls parallels and the "scripture prophet"
Tabor argues that Jesus and his contemporaries reinterpreted scripture about themselves, and he says the Dead Sea Scrolls provide earlier examples. He describes the Habakkuk Pesher and the Thanksgiving Hymns, especially the middle section, which he says is autobiographical and draws on Isaiah 53. He cites Michael Fishbane and Israel Knohl (captioned "Israel Canel") for this reading. He calls the process a "scripture prophet" (a term he credits to Michael Wise) and gives the example of David Koresh, who read Psalms about a sinful Messiah as describing himself. Tabor's point is that Jesus' messianic self-understanding fits a known pattern of messianic reinterpretation. He also says the Dead Sea Scrolls contain a text (4Q521, which he says he published) in which the Messiah raises the dead, and that Jesus' reply to John's disciples echoes it.

### Two messiahs and Jesus as a priest-king pairing
Tabor argues that Jewish expectation included more than one anointed figure, a priestly Messiah and a Davidic Messiah, citing Zechariah 6 and Zechariah 3 and Zechariah's "two anointed ones." He says John may have been the priestly figure and Jesus the Davidic one. He describes an interview with a journalist (Martin Bashir, per the captions) about his book The Jesus Dynasty, in which he said the idea of two messiahs was the shock. Tabor's claim is that John held the priestly role and Jesus the royal role, and that Jesus' statement "John is greater than me" is the evidence he needs.

### Against Jesus as a founder, and against an armed revolt
Tabor says Jesus was not a "founder" in the sense of starting a new religion. He compares the Buddha, whose earliest teachings he says bear "very little correspondence" to later Buddhism, and says that Jesus would not have recognized "messianology" as his aim. He also argues that Jesus was not a Zealot who planned an armed revolt, because "he did not gather arms." He qualifies this by suggesting Jesus "was also sort of hedging his bets" and that the War Scroll shows the Qumran community expected God to do the decisive work. Tabor says Jesus' mother, having watched crucifixions, may have taught him not to take up arms (Tabor's reconstruction, not a claim with a source given).

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus as founder of Christianity.** Tabor says Jesus was not a founder who set out to start the religion later called Christianity; the figure in the sources is an apocalyptic Jewish teacher whose expectations failed.
- **Jesus and Christianity's creeds are continuous.** Tabor argues that the fourth-century creeds and their claims (pre-existent deity, abolished law, new covenant) are not compatible with what the earliest sources suggest.
- **John the Baptist as a wild, half-naked screamer.** Tabor says this is Hollywood and popular imagination, not the sources; he treats John as a serious prophet and teacher.
- **Jesus as an armed Zealot.** Tabor says he did not gather arms and that the sources do not show a plan for an armed revolt, though he acknowledges that some of Jesus' sayings and his disciples' actions point toward a more militant reading.
- **Jesus and John as Essenes.** Tabor says this is frequently proposed, but he thinks the Essenes would have rejected Jesus and John on ritual purity and halakha.
- **Jesus as a mystic in the way Paul was.** Tabor says apocalypticism does not make someone a mystic. He agrees with Sledge that Jesus had one clear mystical experience (the vision of Satan falling) but says Paul is the clearer mystic.
- **Jesus as only a Christian figure.** Tabor says Jesus was a first-century Jew and that his miracle-working belongs in a Galilean Jewish context, not only in the Hellenistic setting in which later gospel material may have been shaped.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught.** Tabor and Sledge say Paul is probably the figure who most shaped the Christianity that developed. Tabor says Part II will cover Paul, so the talk leaves this question open.
- **James and the Jerusalem community.** Tabor reads the Hegesippus account of James's answer to the high priest as an echo of Jesus' apocalyptic answer. He says the Gospel of John may preserve a witness that he identifies as James, and he presents James as the head of the family and movement after Jesus. Part II is supposed to cover James.
- **The Nazarenes, Ebionites, Essenes.** Tabor says the Ebionites, whom he calls "the poor," may preserve an authentic echo of the teachings of Jesus, James, and John the Baptist, and that they were condemned as heretics by the fourth century. He treats the Qumran community as close but not identical to Jesus and John, and says the Essenes would have rejected them.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet, nonviolence.** Tabor discusses the Shema and Leviticus 19 as the great commandment, Jeremiah 7 on sacrifice, and the Sabbath animal-in-a-pit rule as a point of agreement between Jesus and the Pharisees. He says Jesus' message became more radical after John's arrest, including love of enemies and turning the other cheek, and says Jesus did not gather arms.
- **The Kingdom on earth.** This is central. Tabor says Jesus expected the kingdom of God to arrive on earth within his generation, that Daniel and Zechariah frame it as a military and political victory, and that the failed expectation shaped later Christianity.
- **Resurrection, afterlife, hell.** Tabor touches on the raising of the dead in 4Q521 and on Jesus' statement that the Messiah will raise the dead, but he does not develop a view of the afterlife.
- **Who Jesus thought he was.** Tabor argues that Jesus understood himself in a messianic role alongside John, in a priestly and Davidic pairing, and that Jesus read Isaiah 53 and Zechariah onto his own fate. He says the church made Jesus into the "opposite" of what he would have wanted.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed.** Tabor treats Mark as earliest, Q as a shared source of Matthew and Luke, and John as containing genuine geographical material. He says Luke revised Mark's timetable and Matthew added delay parables. He also says the Didache may preserve a non-Pauline version of the faith.
