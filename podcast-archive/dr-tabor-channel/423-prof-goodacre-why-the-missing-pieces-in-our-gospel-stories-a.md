## Summary

This is a pre-conference interview, promoting the third annual "New Investigations of the New Testament" virtual conference on the historical Jesus. Tabor carries much of the opening, explaining the conference format, the speaker lineup, and the price, and he also reads the conference blurb. Goodacre's main argument comes in the middle section, where he explains the title of his talk, "Missing Pieces."

Goodacre's question is how much of Jesus's life the surviving sources actually preserve. He argues that the common image of the historical Jesus quest as a jigsaw puzzle with some pieces missing understates the problem. In his view, nearly all of Jesus's words and actions are lost, and the scholarly habit of re-reading the same few texts can lead scholars to assume that everything important is somewhere in them. As evidence he points to the gap between the childhood story in Luke 2 and the public ministry, the silence of Paul and Mark on Mary, Joseph and Bethlehem, and the thin record of Jesus's family and formative influences. He does not deny that much can be known, and he says Ed Sanders was right that we can know a great deal about Jesus's environment. He presents the balance as a matter of method and humility rather than skepticism.

The interview ends with Tabor's closing question, which Goodacre answers in part with the point that uncertainty about Jesus is hard to refute, and that scholars should be willing to say "I don't know." Tabor and Goodacre then close with plans for future conversations.

## Key Teachings

### The jigsaw puzzle is the wrong picture
Goodacre says the phrase "jigsaw puzzle with missing pieces" is a cliché of historical Jesus scholarship. He turns the image around to ask how many pieces are missing, and his answer is nearly all of them. He argues that the surviving material (the three Synoptic Gospels, John, Paul, and a few other early sources) is a tiny deposit, even if taken as entirely accurate. He adds a joke: a thrift-store puzzle with missing pieces still shows the picture on the box, while the Jesus puzzle does not show a clear picture at all. He also says there is "almost a dishonesty" in implying that everything important survives, though he states that he does not accuse colleagues of dishonesty.

### Ancient biography with almost nothing before the ministry
Goodacre contrasts the Gospels with modern biography. He notes that a class on Jesus found that the only story students could recall about Jesus's youth was the account of Jesus at twelve in Luke 2, after which the sources jump to his adulthood at about thirty. He says a modern biography of Barack Obama built this way would be "rubbish." He also points out that Paul, whom he treats as the earliest extant material, says only that Jesus was "born of a woman" (Galatians 4), with no mention of Mary, Joseph or Bethlehem. Mark has no Bethlehem either. In his account, the sources are far too few to support a full portrait.

### Family, Nazareth and occupation
Goodacre says the family is a "forgotten story" that he spends a couple of hours on when teaching. He notes that in John's Gospel Jesus's mother is never named, Joseph appears only as "son of Joseph," and the brothers appear in John 7 without names, while Matthew and Mark name them. On occupation, he says "carpenter" is a single-verse detail, and the Greek word could mean woodworker or stonemason. He and Tabor discuss the possibility that Jesus worked in Sepphoris, the nearby urban center, which was being rebuilt in his lifetime, and Goodacre says this is a hypothesis rather than a conclusion. He mentions a Caesarea inscription listing Nazareth as a priestly town, which he suggests may fit Jesus's own time, and he also notes that the traditions naming Jesus's grandparents (Joachim and Anna in the Protevangelium of James) are later and should be treated with caution.

### Formative influences and John the Baptist
Goodacre argues that the usual answer to "who most influenced Jesus?" is John the Baptist, and that the evidence for this is secure. He qualifies the claim: the Gospels mention John because he was famous, and Josephus treats John as a major figure around the same time the Gospels were written. In his reading, the Gospel writers felt a need to subordinate John to Jesus, which suggests John's prominence was already a known fact. He then asks who else shaped Jesus, and points to unnamed women and men of his youth. He cites Sarah (name garbled in the captions) on the male-female pairing in the double tradition, suggesting a possible female mentor behind Jesus's pairs of parables (mustard seed and leaven). He names Mary Magdalene, Joanna and Susanna in Luke as likely real figures, and says that the origins of Mary Magdalene (Magdala or elsewhere) are debated.

### The method: reading the sources for their environment
Goodacre's constructive proposal is that historians should move outward from the sources to the surrounding world. He credits Ed Sanders with the view that we can know a great deal about Jesus's environment, and cites the work of Eric and Carol Meyers on Sepphoris as an example of this approach. He argues that the illegitimate response to scarce sources is to treat the surviving texts as if they were all that existed, which warps the picture of Jesus. He also argues that this has an ethical side: if scholars read only the Gospels on the Pharisees, they will produce a distorted image that has fed antisemitic stereotypes. Josephus and other sources fill in a more balanced picture. He says that "people have died because of these stereotypes."

### Tabor's questions and what the sources cannot settle
In the final exchange, Tabor asks what Goodacre would ask Jesus if he had one hour with him. Goodacre answers that he would ask about his family, specifically James, and whether James was older or younger. Tabor gives a related view (not a claim Goodacre endorses) that James is portrayed as older in the Protevangelium of James. Goodacre adds questions about the meaning of "kingdom of God" (whether it was political), the "Son of Man" (whether Daniel 7 or Ezekiel is in view), and how Jesus responded to John's execution, and whether he expected martyrdom. Goodacre's answer on the wider issue is that some claims about Jesus are hard to refute (for example, a claim that he lived into his fifties), and that this makes the field "kind of fun" but also requires humility, including a willingness to say "I don't know."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That the sources contain everything important about Jesus's life.** Goodacre argues that most of Jesus's words and actions are lost, and that the surviving material is a small fraction of what existed.
- **That the Gospels reveal Jesus's childhood and youth.** Goodacre says the only childhood story is Luke 2, followed by a jump to Jesus at thirty, which he calls a poor biographical structure.
- **That Jesus's trade was simply "carpenter" in a village workshop.** He argues the Greek term could mean woodworker or stonemason, and that the village picture may be too narrow given Sepphoris nearby.
- **That the Gospels' portrayal of the Pharisees is accurate.** Goodacre says reading only the Gospels produces a warped picture that has fed antisemitic stereotypes, and that Josephus and other sources give a fuller view.
- **That God would have ensured all important data made it into the New Testament.** He calls this a theological assumption that historians should not carry into historical work.
