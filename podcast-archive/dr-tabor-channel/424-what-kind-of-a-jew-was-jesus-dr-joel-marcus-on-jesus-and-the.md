## Summary

Tabor's interview with Joel Marcus previews Marcus's lecture "Jesus and the Law" for a virtual conference on the historical Jesus. The question is what Jesus thought about the Torah, and how far the Gospels, which are our only access to him, have been shaped by their authors' positions, especially Paul's. Marcus frames the problem as a chicken-and-egg question: did Jesus shape the Gospels, or did the Gospels shape our picture of Jesus? He says the only workable starting point is the Gospels themselves, read critically and worked backwards.

Marcus's main evidence is comparative. He points to Mark 7, where a handwashing dispute ends with a saying that "nothing from outside can defile you," which Mark reads as cleansing all foods. Matthew keeps a less radical version and drops the food conclusion, which Marcus takes as a sign that Matthew comes from a more Torah-observant stream than Mark. He also argues that Matthew 5 is ambivalent: it affirms the eternity of the law and then sets out a series of antitheses. Marcus also weighs the Q saying that "the law and the prophets were until John," the apocalyptic urgency of early sayings, and the wider Jewish and early Christian debate over whether the Torah would change in the new age. He notes that the evidence for such Jewish expectations is thin, and that W.D. Davies's older study relies largely on late rabbinic sources. Tabor adds Paul's statements in 1 Corinthians 7 about the shortened time and the Acts 21 episode, and frames the whole discussion as an invitation to his conference. Marcus and Tabor both stress that the differences among the Gospels are signals about the communities that wrote them, not simple contradictions.

The talk moves to John the Baptist, whom Marcus treats as fragmentary in the sources, and then to Bob Dylan's biblical imagery. It ends with an upbeat promotion of the conference and of Tabor's Patreon community. Marcus's positions are offered as provisional ("I think," "maybe"), and the two speakers repeatedly say they are still working through the questions.

## Key Teachings

### The Torah and the new age (Paul vs. the early sayings)

Marcus argues that Paul clearly believed the Torah had changed because the new world was already breaking in. What Marcus says he would most like to know is whether Jesus shared that view. He notes that the Qumran community held that the law changes with each age of the world, which could suggest a changed law in a messianic age, but he treats this as a possibility, not a finding. He credits W.D. Davies's older study of the Torah in the messianic age, but calls its evidence "very spare" and says most of it comes from rabbinic sources that are "all pretty late." Marcus's open question is whether Paul "just made it up" or whether the idea existed in Second Temple Judaism without surfacing explicitly until later.

### Mark's Pauline affinity and the handwashing pericope

Marcus says he wrote an article roughly thirty years ago reviving the older hypothesis that Mark comes from a Pauline sphere of theology. On his reading, Mark shares Paul's belief that the new age has arrived and also Paul's sense that something essential about the Torah has changed. The key text is Mark 7: a question about handwashing, which Marcus notes is not a biblical commandment, leads to Jesus's pronouncement that nothing from outside defiles. Marcus says Mark explicitly reads this as cleansing all foods, so the saying permits pork and shellfish. He treats this as Mark's interpretation of the saying, not necessarily what Jesus meant.

### Matthew as a Torah-observant retelling

Matthew has a less radical version of the saying and omits Mark's food conclusion. Marcus attributes this to Matthew's position in "a more Torah observant stream" of Jewish-Christian groups who continued to observe the law, and he links this to the James tradition ("your namesake James"). Tabor also points to a Sabbath grain episode that Matthew retells with a different justification from Mark's. Both speakers present these differences as evidence of competing communities, not contradictions to be harmonized away.

### Matthew 5: the eternity of the law and the antitheses

Marcus says Matthew 5 is revealing. It opens the Sermon on the Mount, which Matthew arranges as a new Moses giving authoritative teaching on a mountain. Marcus cites B.W. Bacon's early twentieth-century observation that Matthew's teaching is organized into five discourses. The Sermon begins with Jesus affirming that not an iota will pass from the law, then moves into a series of antitheses ("you have heard that it was said... but I say to you") that Marcus says sometimes intensify and sometimes revoke Torah. He acknowledges the conservative reading that Jesus is always giving the true meaning of the law, but says this does not fit well with lines like the eye-for-an-eye antithesis. Marcus also notes that the ending on a Galilean mountain, where Jesus tells the disciples to teach the nations "to observe everything I have commanded you," makes Jesus look like a rabbi who revises the law.

### Allegory vs. literal observance: Philo, Origen, and Paul

Marcus compares two ways of reading the law's spiritual meaning. Philo of Alexandria, in *On the Migration of Abraham*, says that circumcision points to the removal of desire and the Sabbath to rest, but that these people are right in principle without abandoning literal observance, since observance gives a deeper sense of the spiritual meaning. Origen, Marcus says, concludes from similar premises that literal observance is no longer needed. Marcus says he thinks Paul would side with Origen's conclusion. He ties the "circumcision of the heart" tradition (Deuteronomy, Jeremiah's new covenant, and Paul in Romans 2) to this debate.

### Urgency, the short time, and Paul's accommodation

Tabor argues that Jesus's sayings have a strong sense of urgency, and that the Gospels read against that background differently than Paul's letters. Tabor cites Paul's statements in 1 Corinthians 7 that the appointed time "has grown very short" and that the form of this world is passing away. He reads the Acts 21 episode, in which Paul is said to observe the law in Jerusalem while James says the Gentile believers need not be placed under it, as possibly reflecting Paul's situational accommodation ("I become as a Jew to the non-Jew"). Tabor and Marcus both say they are uncertain whether Acts is reliable here.

### John the Baptist and the Q saying

Marcus says the Q saying that "the law and the prophets were until John" seems to distinguish the old age from the kingdom of God proclaimed since John, and so runs against the eternity-of-law opening of Matthew 5. He is cautious: the sources on John are "so fragmentary," and the tradition about John contains nothing as radical as that saying. He argues that the Christian tradition had "a vested interest in subordinating John to Jesus," and that John may have thought of himself as "the main man." Marcus also says that Mark's description of John's "baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins" suggests John thought his baptism already conveyed forgiveness, so the new age was already breaking in for John. Tabor cites Luke 3 to argue that John's teaching sounds much like Jesus's, and notes that Acts describes followers of John in far-flung cities who had not heard of Jesus. Both speakers point to the Mandaeans and the Pseudo-Clementine literature as evidence of ongoing John-centered groups.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Gospels contradict each other, so they cannot be reconciled.** Marcus and Tabor argue that differences between Mark and Matthew (and Luke) signal different communities answering the same question, and that these differences are more informative than any harmonization.
- **Matthew is simply a Torah-observant Gospel with no tension about the law.** Marcus argues Matthew 5 affirms the law's eternity and then includes antitheses that sometimes intensify and sometimes revoke Torah, so it is ambivalent.
- **Mark 7's saying is just Jesus's own position on food.** Marcus says Mark explicitly reads the saying as cleansing all foods, which is Mark's interpretation, and he treats Jesus's own intent as an open question.
- **Jesus's teaching on the law is simply the literal, "true meaning" of Moses.** Marcus says this conservative reading is possible but does not work well for every antithesis.
- **John the Baptist is a crazy figure who only screams at people.** Tabor describes this as a common stereotype, and cites Luke 3 to suggest John's teaching resembles Jesus's.
- **John was simply subordinate to Jesus.** Marcus argues the Christian tradition had a vested interest in this subordination, and that John may have thought his own baptism already conveyed forgiveness.

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Marcus's central question is whether Paul's view that the Torah changed in the new age came from Jesus or was Paul's own, and he explicitly suggests Mark may share Paul's outlook. He leaves the answer open.
