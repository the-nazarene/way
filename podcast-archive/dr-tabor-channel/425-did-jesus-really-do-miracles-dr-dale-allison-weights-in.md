## Summary

This is a pre-conference interview hosted by James Tabor, who introduces Dale Allison as a previewer of his talk at Bart Ehrman's New Investigations of the New Testament conference. The talk is on whether Jesus really performed miracles, and Tabor frames it with the question of whether the canonical miracle stories are legendary inventions, misreadings of ordinary events (he recalls William Barclay's sandbar explanation of Jesus walking on water), or possibly "genuinely puzzling events." Most of the 39 minutes is Allison speaking, with brief interjections from Tabor. Tabor also mentions his own courses and Patreon community, and some lighter material about the conference and bookstore.

Allison's main claim is that historians have usually taken one of three positions: that the miracles cannot happen (citing David Hume and Rudolf Bultmann), that they happened because Jesus was divine, or that they should be bracketed as beyond historical judgment. He says he occupies none of these and proposes a "fourth way," which he describes as comparative rather than theological. He argues that the traditional definition of "miracle" bundles two claims, an unexplained event and divine agency, and that the first can be assessed separately from the second. He supports this by pointing to miracle and "glowing" claims across religions and to his own sense that "strange things happen outside of religious contexts." He also says the evangelists probably believed their stories were true, and that most miracle stories were likely remembered through scriptural patterns and changed in the telling rather than invented from scratch. He does not work through individual stories in this talk, saying he will go "story by story" in the lecture.

The talk ends with a warm sign-off, a note on the conference dates, and Tabor's remark that he will be listening to the lectures. Allison closes on the theme of humility and open questions. Nothing in the interview settles any specific miracle story; it sets out the method Allison intends to use.

## Key Teachings

### The three standard approaches, and Allison's rejection of them
Allison lists three positions he finds unsatisfactory. The first is that "none of this could ever happen," which he ties to Hume and Bultmann. The second is that "all these miracles happened because Jesus was the son of God or Jesus was divine or the spirit worked through him." The third, which he attributes to some evangelicals, is that miracles should be set aside as questions historians cannot answer. He says he is "in none of these camps" and that his lecture will explain "a fourth way" that is "a comparative approach," not "a theological approach."

### Hume, agreed in method but not in conclusion
Allison says he agrees with Hume's basic method, that "we should judge what happened or didn't happen on the basis of recurrent human experience," but disagrees about what that experience shows. He adds that modern academic treatments of religion have tended to be reductionistic, while the traditions themselves are "full of so-called miracles or supernatural events or wonders," and that scholars may be missing something by not taking those stories seriously.

### Splitting "miracle" into two questions
Allison observes that the traditional definition of miracle has two parts: an event we cannot explain, and God as its agent. He says these are usually fused "just a habit of thought," and that deciding what happened "is way easier than figuring out the question of agency." As a historian, he says, he needs only to argue "that such and such happened whether it fits what I learned in my college science classes or not." In his book *Interpreting Jesus* he says he replaced "supernatural" with "metanormal," while acknowledging that "what is normal?" is also a problem.

### The Neanderthal and the cell phone
Allison uses the image of a cell phone dropped in front of a Neanderthal cave. The Neanderthal would know the object exists but could not reverse-engineer it, which is "not because the Neanderthal is stupid," but because it lies "outside of his usual parameters of thinking." He suggests the same may hold for some phenomena now, and says he will argue from parallels "that some of the stuff in the gospels could go back to Jesus and that we shouldn't dismiss it too quickly."

### Parallels across religions and the "glowing" claim
Allison argues that Christian miracles have parallels in other traditions, and that each tradition uses its miracles to show its own truth (Muslims, Roman Catholics). Standing outside those frameworks, he says, "the same sorts of claims are being made for the same sorts of events," with "Jesus glows, Buddhists glow, Roman Catholic saints glow." He wants to ask "What the heck is going on here?" on the basis of "open-minded exploration of human testimony," not theology.

### Different origins for different stories
Against a one-size-fits-all approach, Allison says "God did everything" and "they're all legends" are both too simple, and that "there are different origins for the different stories." He says that he does not think the evangelists "are just saying, 'Oh, I want him to be a divine man, and here are the five or six things that divine men do.'" He thinks they more often remember events through "scriptural patterns" (so that a story can be written up as Jesus being like Moses), and that stories "evolve right in the telling," like rumor or folklore. He calls wholesale invention "rare." He also suggests that the evangelists "believe that their stories are true or historical even when they're doing things that make us think no that just can't be right."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Historians must either deny miracles or affirm them as God's work.** Allison rejects both and offers a comparative "fourth way" that separates the question of what happened from the question of agency.
- **Miracles are either all true or all legends.** He argues there is "no one-sizefits-all explanation" and that different stories have different origins.
- **The evangelists invented miracle stories to fit a divine-man template.** He says that is not how he thinks it worked; he sees more memory shaped by scriptural patterns, and believes the evangelists thought their stories were true.
- **Rationalist explanations like Barclay's sandbar are sufficient.** Allison describes the older rationalizing approach (Jeremias, Taylor, Dodd) as one that explains away miracle stories while crediting the rest of the tradition, and he implicitly rejects it as the default.
- **"Supernatural" is a clear category.** Allison says he rejects the category, because "we don't understand the so-called natural."

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Allison suggests that many who cared about Jesus did so "because he was famed as a miracle worker," rather than for his teaching. This bears on how the earliest followers related to Jesus, though Allison does not develop the point in this talk.
- **The gospels and canon:** The talk's central question is how the canonical miracle stories were formed, whether by invention, memory shaped by scriptural patterns, or folklore-like evolution in the telling. Allison's view that the evangelists believed their stories were true is relevant to how the gospels were written.
- **Historical method:** The talk is less about any single tradition than about the method historians should use.
