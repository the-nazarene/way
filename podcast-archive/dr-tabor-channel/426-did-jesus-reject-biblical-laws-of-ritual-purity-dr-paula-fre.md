## Summary

The talk is a pre-conference interview between James Tabor and Paula Fredriksen, ahead of her lecture "Turning the Tables on the Purification of the Temple" at the Bart Ehrman-sponsored virtual conference "New Investigations of the New Testament." Tabor frames the question as whether Jesus rejected the biblical laws of ritual purity. Tabor notes that several New Testament scholars have set Jesus against Judaism by focusing on purity, and he links this to the caricature of Judaism as legalism.

Fredriksen's position, as Tabor reports it and as she begins to lay it out, is that Jesus was a normal first-century Jew who cared about purity. She says that "compassion is to purity as fish is to bicycle," meaning that Jesus's compassion does not exclude concern for purity, especially at the temple, where purity mattered most. Her evidence comes from the Gospels: the leper in Mark 1 is told to show himself to the priest and offer what Moses commanded, and Ed Sanders observed that pilgrims going up to Jerusalem for Passover began a seven-day purification process tied to the red heifer ashes. She also points to Sanders's argument that the temple action was a prophetic, apocalyptic gesture rather than a protest against purity.

The interview is a preview and does not give the full argument. Tabor offers his own thoughts on Mark 7, the table-turning, and the Pharisees, and he reads from a recent book (which he links to Dale Allison) that argues Jesus never made the temple gesture or prophesied the temple's destruction. The conversation ends with logistics for the conference and Tabor's thanks.

## Key Teachings

### Purity was a universal ancient concern
Fredriksen argues that purity was a shared concern across the ancient world, not a uniquely Jewish one. She says "Jews had purity rules, Greeks had purity rules, Athenians had pure everybody," and that purity was part of how people related to the divine, involving physical and what we might call spiritual states. Tabor adds examples from Greek temple rules (holy water, sexual abstinence, footwear) and cites Eliade and Douglas's *Purity and Danger* as anthropological background. The point is that a figure who rejected purity would have been, in her phrase, "the first modern man in the ancient world."

### Compassion versus purity is a false choice
Fredriksen says she once wrote an article against the idea that Jesus's compassion put him against purity. Her core claim is that Jesus can be compassionate and still concerned with purity, especially around altars and temples. This is the framing for her whole talk.

### Gospel evidence for an observant Jesus
Fredriksen points to three things from the Gospels:
- In Mark 1, Jesus tells a cleansed leper to show himself to the priest and offer what Moses commanded, which she and Tabor take as purity practice.
- Sanders noticed that Jesus goes up to Jerusalem for Passover with the pilgrim crowds, who go up a week early because of the seven-day red heifer purification.
- Tabor notes John's report that Jesus's enemies avoided defilement before Passover, and he suggests that Jesus being taken to Pilate (who had been arrested) was an involuntary exception.

### The table-turning: Sanders versus a third reading
Tabor explains that Sanders read the temple action as a prophetic gesture about the eschatological destruction and rebuilding of the temple, and that Sanders argued it had nothing to do with disliking purity. Tabor says New Testament scholarship has often kept Sanders's prophetic-gesture reading while assuming Jesus disliked the temple's purity system. Tabor reads aloud from a recent book, which he connects to Dale Allison, that proposes a "third argument": Jesus never made such a gesture and never prophesied the temple's destruction, with appeals to Mark, John, and Paul's letters. Fredriksen's own treatment of the table episode is what the lecture will develop; the interview does not give her conclusion.

### Mark 7 and intentionality
Tabor says he does not read Mark 7 as declaring all foods clean and that the revised standard's added line ("Thus, he declared all foods clean") is not in the text. He reads the chapter as about defilement rather than food laws. Fredriksen agrees that it is not about food. Tabor then describes the rabbinic idea of *kavana* (intentionality): ritual observance without inner intention is condemned, and a good Pharisee would agree. He compares this to modern religious habits.

### Caricatures of Judaism and the Pharisees
Tabor and Fredriksen agree that caricatures of Judaism as legalism, majoring on minors and trivia, persist and shape students' expectations. Tabor reports that A. J. Levine, whom he interviewed, argued that the Pharisees were "fairly loose" rather than strict, which he presents as a corrective to stereotypes.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus rejected purity because he was compassionate.** Fredriksen argues compassion and concern for purity are not opposed, and the Gospels show Jesus observing purity-related practices.
- **Judaism is legalism focused on trivia.** Tabor and Fredriksen say this caricature persists and shapes students' views before they read the texts.
- **Mark 7 declares all foods clean.** Tabor says the revised standard's added line is not in the text and that Mark 7 concerns defilement, not food laws.
- **The Pharisees were strict and rigid.** Tabor reports Levine's view that they were fairly loose.
- **Judaism and Christianity are opposites with purity as Judaism's stand-in.** Tabor says this opposition is a misreading and has led to harmful results.
