## Summary

Tabor asks whether the idea that Mary had no sexual relations and no children besides Jesus, the "ever-virgin" Mary, appears in the historical sources, and how it became a core doctrine of Orthodox Christianity in East and West. This is the third talk in his "Fading of the Jesus Family" series. He presents it as a continuation of the process in which the family of Jesus, especially Mary and James, receives less attention across the New Testament and later tradition.

Tabor's argument runs through the texts in order. He reads Matthew 1:18-25 as the first explicit statement of a conception without a human father, and notes that Matthew 1:24 says Joseph "knew her not" until she bore a son, which he takes to imply later marital relations. He argues that Isaiah 7:14 says "young woman" (Hebrew *alma*), not "virgin," and that Matthew's use of the Greek *parthenos* is a loose fulfillment. He reads Luke's annunciation as a second, different account, and treats John's Logos prologue as having no birth narrative, with Jesus called "son of Joseph" in John 1:45 and 6:42. He notes that Mark and the Pauline material show no virgin birth, and that Mark 6:3 and Matthew 13:55 name Jesus's brothers and sisters as children of Mary.

Tabor says the explicit perpetual-virginity idea does not appear until the third and fourth centuries, although the second-century Protoevangelium of James hints at it by making Joseph an older widower with children. He attributes the doctrine to the influence of Hellenistic dualism, Platonic and quasi-Gnostic ideas that devalued the body, and ascetic ideals such as Origen's, with Jerome as a prominent defender. He describes the Western and Eastern positions on the "brothers" (Joseph's children by a former wife, or cousins), and presents his own view that the texts indicate Mary had other children with Joseph. The talk ends with a promotion of his forthcoming book *The Lost Mary* and a preview of the fourth and final episode of the series.

## Key Teachings

### Matthew's "virgin conception" and the question of Joseph
Tabor argues that Matthew 1:18-23 is the earliest text to present Mary's pregnancy as the work of the Holy Spirit with no human father, and that this idea "is here stated for the first time by Matthew." He reads Matthew 1:24 ("But knew her not") as a sign that the marriage was later consummated: "Now she does know a man. Mary sleeps with Joseph." For Tabor, Matthew therefore holds both a virginal conception and a later normal marriage, and this shows the conception story is not a denial that Mary had children.

### Isaiah 7:14 and the Hebrew word *alma*
Tabor explains the original context of Isaiah 7 as King Ahaz of Judah in the eighth century BCE, facing the Syro-Ephraimite threat, with the sign aimed at "a child of his own day." He argues the Hebrew *alma* means a "young marriageable woman," not a virgin, and that the Greek *parthenos* in the Septuagint-based quotation is what introduces "virgin." He calls Matthew's use "a direct fulfillment" that is "certainly inaccurate," though he says his purpose is to trace the family, not to judge Matthew's exegesis.

### Luke's annunciation and "I haven't known a man"
Luke, in Tabor's reading, tells the story from Mary's side, with the angel Gabriel, a Galilean setting in Nazareth, and Mary described as "a virgin engaged to a man whose name was Joseph of the house of David." He stresses Mary's question in Luke 1, "How shall this be since I have no husband?" and says the Greek means "I haven't known a man," which he thinks a plainer translation would render as "I haven't had sex." He takes "to know a man" to mean sexual intercourse throughout the Bible, and he treats Luke's conception as a different story from Matthew's, with no dream and with different emphases.

### John: the Logos and "son of Joseph"
Tabor says John has no virgin birth narrative. He reads the Logos prologue (John 1:1, 1:14, "the word became flesh and tabernacled among us") as the divine Word becoming flesh, which he compares to Plato's *Timaeus*. He observes that Jesus is called "son of Joseph" only twice in the New Testament, both in John (John 1:45 and 6:42), and that Mary is never named in John. He suggests John may have held Joseph to be the biological father, and that "virgin conception" would be a better label than "virgin birth" for the idea in the other gospels.

### Mark, Matthew 13:55 and Paul: the brothers and sisters
Tabor reads Mark 6:3 and Matthew 13:55 as naming Jesus's mother Mary and his brothers James, Joseph (Joses), Simon, and Judas, with unnamed sisters. He says a "normal reading" makes these children of Mary and Joseph, and notes that many non-Catholics accept this. He also cites Paul's report of a visit to Jerusalem three years after his revelation, where he "saw none of the other apostles ... except James, the Lord's brother." Tabor treats Paul's usage as a further sign that the family was known, and as part of the reason James's authority is central to his wider series.

### The historical development: second to fourth centuries
Tabor describes the Protoevangelium of James as presenting Joseph as an older man with children from an earlier marriage, so that the brothers would not be Mary's. He says the explicit doctrine of Mary's perpetual virginity dates to the third and fourth centuries, and that dating earlier hints is difficult. He names Origen in Alexandria, whom he says castrated himself and whose ascetic views shaped his theology, and Jerome, who translated the Latin Vulgate and "very explicitly defends" Mary's perpetual virginity. He describes two positions: the Western view (the brothers were stepchildren of Joseph, who himself remained celibate) and the Eastern view (the brothers were Clopas's children, i.e., cousins, rendered "Cloffus" in the captions). He says both are attempts to avoid the implication of the text.

### Hellenistic dualism as the driving force
Tabor's central explanation for the change is what he calls "Hellenistic dualism," the idea of a perfect spiritual world above and a corrupt material world below, which he links to Neoplatonism and to a "quasi-Gnostic" devaluation of the body. He says this idea is "not a Hebrew idea," since Genesis presents creation as good and the first human commandment as "be fruitful and multiply." He cites Plato's *Phaedo* and Socrates's death as an example of contempt for the body. He presents this as a current in the air during the second to fourth centuries, not the product of a single "sinister hand," and says the New Testament still preserves traces of the family, "you saw it in Matthew."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The virgin birth is the earliest and most widely attested idea.** Tabor says Mark, Paul, and John show no virgin birth narrative, and that the idea appears first in Matthew, with a second version in Luke.
- **Isaiah 7:14 predicts a virgin birth of the Messiah.** Tabor argues the Hebrew *alma* means "young woman," the sign concerns King Ahaz's own time, and Matthew's use of the Greek *parthenos* is a loose fulfillment.
- **Mary's perpetual virginity is biblical.** Tabor says it is not biblical and was defended and developed by the church in the third and fourth centuries, under the influence of ascetic and dualist currents.
- **The brothers and sisters of Jesus were obviously not Mary's children.** Tabor says the "normal reading" makes them Mary's children, and that the Western and Eastern explanations (stepchildren, cousins) are attempts to avoid the text's plain sense.
- **The idea that Jesus had no human father is the same as denying that Mary had other children.** Tabor argues these are separate claims, and that a virginal conception does not require Mary to have been sexually inactive afterward.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Jesus family and James:** Tabor frames the fading of Mary and the brothers as part of a larger movement that also denies the authority of James, linking the ever-virgin doctrine to the shrinking role of the Jerusalem family in the tradition.
- **Paul vs. Jesus:** Tabor notes that Paul refers to James as "the Lord's brother" and says Paul shows no knowledge of a virgin birth, which bears on how much of the birth narratives belong to the later gospel tradition rather than to the earliest sources.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** The talk's main argument is that Matthew and Luke introduce the virginal conception, while Mark and John do not, and that later church writers (Protoevangelium of James, Origen, Jerome) developed the perpetual-virginity doctrine. This is directly relevant to how the gospel texts were shaped and read.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor's argument does not address Jesus's self-understanding directly, but his point that Joseph is called Jesus's father in John 1:45 and 6:42 bears on how early writers identified Jesus.
