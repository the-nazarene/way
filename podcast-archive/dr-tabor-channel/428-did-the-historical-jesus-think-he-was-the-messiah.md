## Summary

Tabor takes up the question of whether the historical Jesus thought he was the Messiah, and he frames it as a "chicken and egg" problem: did the gospels shape Jesus's self-understanding, or did Jesus shape the gospels? He says the gospels clearly present Jesus as Messiah, including the high-priest trial scene in Mark, but he calls them theological documents and says they need historical-critical handling. He argues that Jesus most likely identified with the Davidic Messiah rather than a priestly one, citing the Dead Sea Scrolls' expectation of two figures, Zechariah 6 and Isaiah 11, and the Davidic genealogies in Matthew and Luke. His main early evidence is Paul's Romans 1:3-4, which he dates to the 50s CE and which he says reflects Paul's contact with James and Peter.

Tabor then argues that Jesus's messianic role was non-military. He reads John's account of Pilate's question ("my kingdom's not of this world") as a rejection of armed revolt, and pairs it with Matthew's arrest scene and an early saying from the source he calls Q ("by the finger of God cast out demons"). He understands the kingdom as the kingdom of Daniel 2, 7 and 9, to be established by God through his Messiah, with Jesus proclaiming repentance and good news that the kingdom is breaking in now. He ends by answering his host's question with "yes," while repeatedly hedging with "I think," "my guess," and "I don't know." He also acknowledges that he is in a minority among scholars, who more often describe Jesus as a prophet or eschatological proclaimer.

## Key Teachings

### The Messiah question as a historical problem
Tabor says the gospels plainly portray Jesus as Messiah, and he gives the trial before the high priest Caiaphas ("Are you the Messiah? I am.") as the most dramatic example, which he attributes to Mark, "I think our earliest source." He insists the question is whether this came from Jesus or was added later. He says history can be pursued "as a science" by dating sources, weighing their goals, and comparing them (he gives the example of Matthew rewriting Mark). He frames the "historical critical approach" as meaning careful, not destructive.

### Davidic rather than priestly messiah
He notes that the Dead Sea Scrolls, about a century before Jesus, anticipate two messiahs: a priestly one from the line of Aaron and a Davidic one. He says it would be "hard to argue that Jesus claimed to be the priestly Messiah," and that the priestly role more likely goes to John the Baptizer, whom Luke 1 introduces as a priest. He reasons that a messiah in this period would normally be the Davidic king, and that Zechariah 6 (a "branch of David" with a priest beside him) and Isaiah 11 are the central texts. He cites the two genealogies in Matthew 1 and Luke 3 as tracing Davidic descent, while admitting they differ. He concludes that Jesus "did in fact identify with one of these figures," which he calls his view.

### Paul's Romans 1 as the earliest witness
Tabor says "a lot of people don't realize Paul is our earliest source," writing "a decade or two" after Jesus's death and in contact with James the brother of Jesus and with Peter. He quotes Romans 1 on Jesus being "descended from David according to the flesh," and he reads "declared to be the son of God with power by his resurrection" as generic, applying to others as well. He dates this letter to the 50s CE and says it correlates with all four gospels and with the later Gospel of Peter.

### A non-military kingdom
Using John's trial account, which he says seems to come from a historically useful source, Tabor reads Jesus's answer to Pilate ("my kingdom's not of this world") as a rejection of armed insurrection, a claim that Jesus would not raise "armies outside Herod's palace." He pairs this with Matthew's arrest scene, where Jesus tells his followers to put away their swords and says he could call "12 legions of angels." He says this does not mean Jesus gave up on an overthrow, only that it would not come by human force.

### Spiritual warfare and the kingdom of Daniel
Tabor draws on an early saying from the source he calls Q ("If I by the finger of God cast out demons, then the kingdom of God has come upon you") and on the image of binding the strong man. He reads these as a "spiritual battle" against Satan, whom he says Paul calls "the god of this age." He says Jesus would be considered "crazy basically today" for believing he had angelic forces behind him. His reading of "the kingdom" is the kingdom of Daniel, in which the God of heaven breaks in pieces the kingdoms of the world and gives rule to his Messiah. He presents the messianic role as the office Jesus identified with and that he likely studied in those passages.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Read the Bible and the answer is there.** Tabor challenges the view that a careful reader needs only the text and more study. He says historical criticism requires comparing sources, such as Matthew's revisions of Mark, rather than treating the gospels as uniform.
- **Mark and Matthew say essentially the same thing.** Tabor recounts his own childhood assumption that Mark was a repeat of Matthew, and then his discovery that Mark was the foundation for Matthew.
- **Jesus as a political revolutionary.** Tabor reads the Pilate scene and the sword-sheathing in Matthew as rejecting armed rebellion, while still holding that Jesus expected an overthrow, though by God's hand.
- **Scholarly consensus that Jesus was a prophet, not a messiah.** Tabor says he is in a minority and that most scholars prefer labels such as "Jesus the prophet" or "eschatological proclaimer of the kingdom."
