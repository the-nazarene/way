## Summary

The talk, the second segment of an interview with Justin Holmes (Deconstruction Zone), asks how closely Jesus's sense of his identity and mission matches the expectations of the Dead Sea Scrolls community, and in particular its "Teacher of Righteousness." Tabor opens by warning against treating either figure as a "carbon copy" of the other. He then describes the scrolls' community as apocalyptic and dates its teacher "around 70 BCE," noting that the teacher's name is unknown. Tabor argues that the community's expectations included two Messiahs and cosmic triumph, and that scholars who say the suffering of the Messiah was added after the fact are taking a position he does not accept.

His main evidence is a set of Hebrew Bible passages, including Psalm 40, Isaiah 48-50, and Isaiah 52-53, which he reads as describing a figure who is not explicitly called the Messiah but who does the Messiah's work and suffers. He proposes that Jesus identified with these texts. He also argues that Jesus's entry into Jerusalem on a donkey (Zechariah 9:9) and the temple action (Jeremiah 7) were deliberate "messianic prophetic actions," following the model of Ed Sanders. Tabor says he is "not dogmatic" and points listeners to his lecture at Bart Ehrman's conference for the full method, and to his course for more detail. He closes by stating that he thinks Jesus "thought he was the Messiah," while acknowledging that the question of how Jesus's followers later shaped the story remains open. The talk ends abruptly, at about 24:41, mid-discussion of the War Scroll and Daniel.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The Teacher of Righteousness as a partial parallel
Tabor describes the scrolls' community as preparing "the way in the wilderness," which he links to John the Baptist's self-description and Isaiah 40:3. He says the community was "gathering a people, a righteous remnant" awaiting two Messiahs, a priestly one and a kingly one. He stresses that he does not think the two figures are identical, and that the parallels are "significant" but must be handled carefully.

### 2. Psalm 40 and the suffering servant texts as self-identification
Tabor's central argument is that Jesus read certain passages as describing himself. He points to Psalm 40 ("Sacrifice and offering you do not desire, but you've given me an open ear"; "I delight to do your will, oh my God") and to Isaiah 49 and 50, where a servant figure is told to "reconstitute" Israel and to bring light to the nations. He says Isaiah 50:4-6 uses language similar to Psalm 40 ("He wakens my ear"; "I gave my back to the smiters"). He cites David Koresh's reading of these texts as a caution about how a messianic mind can read scripture about itself. Tabor acknowledges these are interpretive proposals, not proofs, and that there are "a dozen others" he did not cover.

### 3. Prophetic actions: the donkey and the temple
Tabor asks whether the Zechariah 9:9 donkey entry was historical or was added by a gospel writer to make Jesus fit the prophecy. He argues it was historical, and that the next day's action in the temple (quoting Jeremiah 7 and Isaiah on "a house of prayer") was part of the same enactment. Drawing on Ed Sanders's concept of prophetic actions, he compares this with Isaiah walking naked and Ezekiel's dung fire. He calls the pattern "a messianic panomine": acting out the Messiah's role "mighty and triumphant, but humble and rejected." He notes that the temple trade continued after Jesus's death, so the action did not achieve what Zechariah describes, but he suggests it may have been a foreshadowing.

### 4. Layers of interpretation: sayings vs. deeds, and how the Gospels record them
Tabor sets out three positions: that nothing reliable is known about Jesus's self-understanding; that the historical Jesus is entirely a product of the Gospels; and a fundamentalist position that everything attributed to Jesus is literal. He rejects the first two, and he calls the Gospels a kind of "videotape" in a joking remark. He recommends looking at both what Jesus said (the sayings, which he regards as "less edited") and what he did, especially the final week, which he calls more distinctive than generic preaching and healing.

### 5. The "strike the shepherd" text and the Damascus Document
Tabor presents Zechariah 13 ("strike the shepherd and the sheep will be scattered") as a text the Gospel writers may have added to the Kidron crossing scene. He contrasts this with the scrolls: the Teacher's hymns speak of "my intimate friend who ate bread with me," which he reads as a reference to a real betrayal, and the Damascus Document (versions A and B) applies the same Zechariah text to a teacher who has been "gathered to his fathers." He suggests Jesus might have known this material and applied it to himself.

### 6. Cosmic expectation, not a rebellion against Rome
Tabor argues that the War Scroll's imagined battle is cosmic, with the enemy falling as Assyria's army did, rather than a plan to seize the arsenal at Sepphoris. He links this to Jesus's saying about twelve legions of angels, which he says Jesus probably believed, and to Daniel 7, where the saints are worn down before triumph. He says the "Western powers" in the scrolls are read as Rome and Greece, and that the suffering expectation was present in these texts.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Suffering was added later, and no one expected a suffering Messiah.** Tabor says that most scholars hold this, and he argues instead that the suffering-servant texts were read as applying to a figure who would suffer before triumph.
- **The Dead Sea Scrolls expected only a human battle against Rome.** He argues that the War Scroll's battle is cosmic and that the Teacher's community expected divine victory, not a revolt.
- **The Gospels can be taken as a literal record of everything Jesus said.** Tabor rejects the fundamentalist extreme and asks which elements reflect Jesus and which were added by the writers.
- **Jesus did not exist, or everything comes from the Gospels.** He describes this as a view that would require the whole story to come from the texts, and he rejects it.
- **Mark's Jesus is a glorified figure.** Tabor says Mark has no glorified portrait, and that even the original ending leaves the reader at the cross.
