## Summary

The talk opens with a question put to Tabor: did Jesus understand John as part of a "two messiah" relationship? Tabor answers that he thinks Jesus may have come to see it that way. He treats John as "the main guy, the inaugurator of the movement," and points to a saying shared by Matthew and Luke that "among those born of women, there's none greater than John." He argues this saying, together with Deuteronomy 18:15-18, makes John more than a prophet in Jesus' eyes, a "prophet like Moses." Tabor then argues that the gospels progressively subordinate John to Jesus: Mark presents the baptism without difficulty, Matthew adds an objection from John, Luke does not narrate the baptism, and John's gospel barely mentions it. In his reading, the two movements split their work, with John in the north near the Wadi Abbas (called "Brook Kerth" in the captions) and Jesus in the Judean hill country, and Jesus taking over after John's arrest, "picking up the baton." Tabor says he is unsure what labels to give the two figures, but offers "a priest" and "one of a royal line." ## Key Teachings

### John as the greater figure (the "prophet like Moses" argument)
Tabor cites a double-tradition saying in Matthew and Luke, which he calls "one of our earliest sources," in which Jesus says none born of women is greater than John. He notes a Greek-writer caveat that "the least in the kingdom is greater than he," but says the statement is "stark enough" to keep. He asks what "more than a prophet" means and answers that it is "the prophet like Moses," citing Deuteronomy 18:15-18. He contrasts Moses with Joshua, who must consult the ephod and the priest, while Moses speaks "face to face." On this reading Jesus honors John as the inaugurator of the redemptive movement.

### The gospels progressively subordinate John
Tabor's main evidential claim is a sequence across the gospels. Mark "has no problem" with Jesus coming to be baptized and receiving a revelation. Matthew adds that John objected and should be baptizing Jesus, which Tabor says he "would really doubt if that happened." Luke "doesn't even mention the baptismal scene," and John's gospel has no baptism, only John's reflection. Tabor says the later gospels "really need Jesus to be elevated past John." He proposes going back to Mark: Jesus came, was baptized, and some of John's disciples then associated with Jesus.

### Two figures who split the work
Tabor argues that people reading these texts would have seen "two figures emerging." In John's gospel, John works in the north around the Wadi Abbas in Jordan, while Jesus is in the Judean hill country in the south. He suggests this lasted "months maybe," until John's arrest, after which Mark says Jesus "went forth preaching" the same message John had preached. He says he would "argue for two messiahs" in this sense, while admitting he is unsure what labels to use.

### Different lifestyles: Nazirite austerity versus ordinary contact
Tabor contrasts John, living austerely in the desert and apparently "a Nazerite," with Jesus, who is more urban and does not keep the same vows about eating, drinking and touching. He says Jesus touches a leper and a dead body and is not "so worried about this sort of ritual impurity," especially outside the temple. Tabor says that if Jesus entered the temple he would have observed its rules, since "as a Jew" he sees no reason he would not. He adds that Jesus was prophetically opposed to the commerce in the temple.

### Paul's temple conduct as a comparison
Tabor briefly compares Jesus with Paul, saying that according to Acts Paul observed temple practice in Jerusalem, even though Tabor reads some of Paul's letters as implying the rules are unnecessary. He uses this to argue that a Jew in an established religious setting would not simply "walk in and act like some kind of a fool."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The gospels present John simply as Jesus' forerunner, subordinate from the start.** Tabor argues the gospels progressively downgrade John: Mark shows little difficulty, Matthew adds an objection, Luke omits the baptism narrative, and John mentions only John's reflection. In his view, that movement is a sign of later theological adjustment, not the original picture.
- **Jesus was a lone figure with no relationship to a rival or parallel movement.** Tabor suggests Jesus and John were two figures who split their work, with Jesus taking over the same message after John's arrest.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor's Paul comparison is brief and mostly about temple observance. He notes that Paul's letters seem to imply the rules are unnecessary, while Acts shows Paul observing them in Jerusalem.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet and nonviolence:** Tabor discusses ritual purity and the Nazirite contrast. He argues Jesus touched lepers and dead bodies and was not concerned with ritual impurity outside the temple, but would have observed temple rules inside it.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** The central question. Tabor argues Jesus may have understood John as the "prophet like Moses" and a principal figure, and that Jesus' own role in a two-figure picture is a possibility he does not settle.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** The sequence Mark → Matthew → Luke → John is the core of Tabor's argument that the gospels changed the portrayal of John over time.
