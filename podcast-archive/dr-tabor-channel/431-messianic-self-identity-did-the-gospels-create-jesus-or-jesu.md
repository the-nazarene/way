## Summary

Tabor asks whether Jesus himself believed he was the Davidic Messiah and foresaw his own suffering and death, or whether the gospels added both after the crucifixion. He says most New Testament scholars deny both claims, treating the predictions of the passion in Mark 8-10 as later additions that make Jesus appear to know his fate in advance. Tabor disagrees. He argues for a "soft thesis": Jesus read himself into Hebrew Scripture (the Psalms, Deuteronomy, Isaiah, and especially the Servant Songs of Second Isaiah), and therefore went about consciously fulfilling what he found there. He supports this with Q material, a comparison to the Teacher of Righteousness in the Qumran Hodayot, an argument about the Servant Songs, a reading of the Temple action as a symbolic protest against the sacrificial system, a distinction between real prophecy and the "fulfillment" citations in Matthew and John, and a survey of later messianic claimants (Josephus's list and modern figures). He acknowledges that the argument is a "soft thesis" that could be wrong. The talk ends on the Habakkuk commentary from Qumran, which Tabor uses to explain how a delayed end-time expectation could be absorbed into faith, and which he presents as the background of much of the New Testament.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The scholarly denial and Mark's passion predictions

Tabor says most academic New Testament scholars would deny that Jesus claimed the Davidic Messiahship and would deny that he predicted his suffering. In his account, the passion predictions in Mark 8-10 are "imposed by the evangelists later, particularly Mark," and they make Jesus look as if he knows everything. He quotes Schweitzer's 1906 closing image of Jesus throwing himself on "the wheel of history," and he invokes Bultmann's 1951 view that the suffering Son of Man was read back into Jesus's own outlook. Tabor's position is that the messianic and suffering self-understanding is historical, not a later construction.

### 2. Self-identification through Scripture, and the Qumran Teacher

Tabor's central argument is that Jesus "found himself in texts of the Hebrew Bible" and so acted consciously within them. As a model, he uses the Qumran "Teacher" whose first-person hymns (1QH, the Hodayot, hymns 11-17) and the fragment 4Q491 describe a figure called before birth, rejected, suffering, and exalted. Tabor follows Michael Wise and Israel Knohl (who argued independently for an autobiographical reading) and Martin Abegg (who associated 4Q491 with the Thanksgiving Hymns). He dates the Teacher to the early first century BCE, citing Wise's work on the Teacher's floruit. Tabor's comparison is that Jesus was "a repeat of that phenomena," and he says the Teacher "seems to be a priest figure" rather than a Davidic Messiah. He presents Psalm 40:6-8 ("Here I am... it is written of me in the scroll of the book") as an example of the kind of first-person self-reading he has in mind.

### 3. The Servant Songs: Israel, an individual, and Isaiah 49 and 53

Tabor argues that the "servant" of Isaiah 41-53 is grammatically and contextually Israel, but that the servant songs develop an individual voice within Israel who "inaugurates a kind of revival of Israel." He points to Isaiah 49:1-6 ("Yahweh called me before I was born") and to the "bruised reed" passage as a task of bringing justice to the nations, which he equates with the Messianic task. He notes that Paul applied Isaiah 49 to his own mission, and cites Jason Staples on this point. He says he agrees only "to some degree" with the claim that the Messianic reading of Isaiah 53 was not present in the early tradition, citing Qumran and rabbinic material as evidence that the idea was older than the Christian church. He does not treat Isaiah 53 in full.

### 4. The Triumphal Entry and the Temple action as symbolic acts

Tabor reads Zechariah 9:9 behind the donkey entry down the Mount of Olives as a deliberate signal, in line with E. P. Sanders's view that Jesus performed symbolic actions. He compares this to the prophets' symbolic acts (Isaiah walking naked, Ezekiel's actions), and argues the Temple action was likewise a signal. He says the Greek word translated "robbers" (*lestes*) in the quotation refers to people who "rip apart," not thieves; he credits Paula Fredriksen with this observation. He also argues that Jesus's intent included welcome for Gentiles, citing Isaiah 56:7 and the "house of prayer for all peoples" phrase. Tabor says he thinks Isaiah 56:7 may be a priestly interpolation, and he thinks Psalm 51's closing verses were added later.

### 5. Jesus and the sacrificial system

Tabor states: "I think Jesus spoke against the sacrificial system." He distinguishes the protest against commerce from a protest against the sacrifices themselves. His texts are Jeremiah's statement that God never commanded burnt offerings and Isaiah 65-66, where God rejects sacrifice (Tabor cites the passage on slaughtering a lamb as if slaying a human). He says he is persuaded by Simon Joseph's *Jesus in the Temple*, which he says "changed my mind" about Jesus's relation to the Temple. He presents Jesus as an observant Jew who looked for a renewed, eschatological Temple, and he says the source criticism of the Priestly material (Exodus 25 through Numbers 11) is outside his field.

### 6. "Fulfillment" citations are not prophecies

Tabor argues that the Matthean and Johannine formula citations (Matthew's virgin/Emmanuel passage; the bones passage in the Psalms; John 13:18) are "window dressing" and not genuine predictions. He says Isaiah 7 concerns a marriageable young woman and that the psalm is about an unnamed persecuted person. He contrasts these with the actual messianic texts he has been discussing in Daniel, Isaiah, and Jeremiah, which he says describe a coming figure and what he will do. He also treats the commonly repeated claim of "over 350 prophecies" as the kind of popular belief he was raised with.

### 7. Messianic claimants and the "Jesus dynasty"

Tabor uses Josephus's account of "every knave and impostor" (Josephus lists twelve Messiahs, per Tabor) to argue that messianic claims were common in the period, and he gives examples including the Samaritan at Mount Gerizim, Theudas at the Jordan, and an Egyptian on the Mount of Olives. He identifies Menahem and Judas the Galilean, and Eleazar ben Ya'ir at Masada, as members of messianic families, possibly Davidic, and he names his own book *The Jesus Dynasty* in that context. He also discusses modern messianic figures such as Moses Gabori, and he refers to David Koresh and his own book *Why Waco*. Tabor suggests that a messianic self-identity is a recognizable social type, and that these figures still appear today.

### 8. Delay, faith, and the post-Teacher period

Tabor closes with Habakkuk 2 and the Qumran commentary on Habakkuk (1QpHab), which says that "the vision is for an appointed time" and that "the final period is prolonged beyond what they thought." He argues that Qumran reinterprets the delay as God acting "on time," and that the community responds by living by faith in the teacher. He connects this to 2 Thessalonians 2 and to Paul's use of the Habakkuk and Abraham texts. Tabor's conclusion is that this "post teacher period in which there's delay" underlies "most of the New Testament." He also says that Jesus himself "100%" adopted the imminent apocalyptic expectation.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Scholars' consensus that Jesus never claimed to be the Messiah and never foresaw his death.** Tabor says this is the majority academic view and argues against it, holding that Jesus self-identified as Messiah and suffering servant.
- **Isaiah 53 is purely a later Christian reading.** Tabor says the Messianic interpretation is not purely Christian and that Qumran and rabbinic material suggest an earlier origin. He does not fully develop the point in this segment.
- **Over 350 Old Testament prophecies are fulfilled in Jesus.** Tabor says that many of the cited "fulfillments" are "window dressing," not prophecies. He contrasts them with the actual messianic texts.
- **The Temple action was simply a protest against commerce.** Tabor argues Jesus also opposed the sacrificial system itself, while acknowledging the commercial corruption.
- **Expected end-time delay was never a problem for early believers.** Tabor argues the Habakkuk commentary shows how a delayed expectation was reinterpreted as faith and "God acting on time."
