## Summary

Tabor asks how, when, and why the family of Jesus, and Mary in particular, was played down in the emerging Christian movement while Mary was later exalted as "Mother of God" and "Queen of Heaven." He says the fading was largely accomplished "by the end of the first century CE" and was carried out mainly by the gospel writers and, to a lesser degree, by Paul. He treats the talk as an overview of a four-part series and a summary of his book *The Lost Mary*, not a detailed account of each step.

His evidence is a set of textual "breadcrumbs." Mark 6:3 names Jesus as "the son of Mary" with four named brothers. John never names Mary and hides the identity of the beloved disciple, whom Tabor argues is James. Paul calls James "the Lord's brother" and treats him as the leader. The Gospel of Thomas tells the disciples to "go to James the just." Acts shows James in charge in chapter 15 without calling him Jesus's brother. Tabor also cites Matthew 1:18 on Joseph not "knowing" Mary before the birth, and he argues that lifelong virginity was a later idea. He adds an archaeological reconstruction of a Jerusalem household on Mount Zion. He also argues that the Ephesus tradition for Mary is very late and rests on 19th-century visions.

The second half is a transcript of an interview with Robert Cune of Closer to Truth. In it Tabor describes Mary as a Jewish matriarch with eight or more children, of Davidic and priestly descent, and says she was widowed early and became head of the household. He says the Jesus movement was an apocalyptic messianic movement within Second Temple Judaism, not "Christianity." He frames the project as a historical contextualization that follows the method of the quest for the historical Jesus. The talk ends with Tabor's remarks on the name Miriam versus Mary.

## Key Teachings

### The beloved disciple is James
Tabor says he has argued since *The Jesus Dynasty* (2006) that "the beloved disciple is James," not John son of Zebedee, as later tradition holds. His main argument is the cross scene: Jesus, dying, entrusts his mother to the unnamed disciple, and Tabor reasons that the next in line in a Jewish family would be the brother, James. He says Robert Eisenman is one of the few scholars who agree, and that James Charlesworth wrote a thick book on the question but concluded it was Thomas.

### Mary as Jewish matriarch of a large clan
Tabor describes Mary as a Jewish woman with "seven or eight children," widowed early, who became "in a sense the head of the clan." He argues she came from the Davidic line and also had a strong priestly line, which he calls "doubly royal." He says she grew up in Sepphoris, the largest urban center of Galilee, not in the village of Nazareth four miles away. He also argues the family was in danger from Roman suspicion of Davidic claimants and cites a figure he calls Vespasian (captioned "imperves patient"), who destroyed Jerusalem in 70 and asked about descendants of David. No source for that detail is given in the talk.

### Late development of Mary's perpetual virginity
Tabor argues that "this perpetual virginity is a much later idea, maybe in the late 2nd century into the third century," linked to ideas that holiness requires celibacy. He says Judaism has no ideal of celibacy within marriage. He reads Matthew 1:18 as saying the marriage was not consummated until Jesus's birth, which he thinks Matthew treated as unremarkable. He also notes Martin Luther's view that Mary remained a virgin all her life.

### James as successor and the Jerusalem household
Tabor says James "takes over leadership of the movement" after Jesus's death, and that he led "the entire Jesus movement," not only a "Jerusalem church." He cites Paul's statement that he met "James, the Lord's brother," and says the Jesus movement kept observing the Torah and the temple feasts until the temple's destruction in 70. He places the group on Mount Zion, in a wealthy follower's house where the Last Supper was held and where Mary would have lived in a busy household. He quotes Jonathan Z. Smith, "I'm imagining in the direction of the truth," as his method for the reconstruction.

### The Ephesus tradition is late
Tabor says visitors to Ephesus wanted to see Mary's house there, but that tradition is "very very late," with "nothing about Mary ever going to Ephesus" for 1700 years. He says it arose from confusing John the Elder of Ephesus with John the Apostle, and that a 19th-century nun, Anne Emmerich (captioned "Anne Emerick"), had visions placing Mary's house there. He says early traditions have Mary dying in Jerusalem.

### Fading of John the Baptist and the family in the Gospels
Tabor argues that John the Baptist is "brushed to the side," and that the saying "the least in the kingdom is greater than he was" was added later. He says it is missing from Hebrew Matthew and from the Clementine literature, which quotes the same material. He presents John the Baptist as part of the extended family, since Luke places Mary with her relative Elizabeth at John's birth.

### Method: breadcrumbs and contextualization
Tabor says the book takes every textual source about Mary, about a dozen passages, as "breadcrumbs in the forest," and reads them within a first-century Jewish setting. He calls this "contextualization" rather than speculation. He also argues that a woman being called "son of Mary" was unusual and signals her prominence, comparing it with ossuary inscriptions that usually name fathers.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mary is the best-known figure with a well-known life.** Tabor says the life between her appearances at Jesus's birth and death is unknown, and that the New Testament gives only breadcrumbs.
- **The beloved disciple is John son of Zebedee.** Tabor argues it is James, the brother of Jesus.
- **Mary died at Ephesus.** He says this tradition is very late and comes from confused identities and 19th-century visions. Early traditions have her dying in Jerusalem.
- **Mary was a perpetual virgin from the start.** He dates the idea of perpetual virginity to the late second or third century and says Judaism had no such ideal.
- **The early movement was "Christianity" with Peter and Paul at its head.** Tabor says it was a Jewish apocalyptic messianic movement that kept the Torah and temple until 70, and that James led it.
- **Mary was a sort of nun or queen of heaven in her own time.** He says she was an ordinary Jewish matriarch with a large household.
