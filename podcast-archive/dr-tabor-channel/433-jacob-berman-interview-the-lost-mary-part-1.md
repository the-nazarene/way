## Summary

Tabor's question is how Mary, the mother of Jesus, came to be "lost," and what can be recovered of her as a historical figure rather than the devotional figure of later Christian tradition. He distinguishes her from Mary Magdalene and says the New Testament gives her only about a dozen scenes. Tabor argues that the loss was partly deliberate: "I argue in the book that it is a deliberate eraser." He says that in the church's theology she was needed only to bear Jesus, and that the later doctrines of her perpetual virginity and sinlessness moved her out of ordinary human life. He also says the sources are thin, so the historian has to use outside material, notably the Protevangelium of James, and "breadcrumbs" from elsewhere.

The central evidence Tabor offers concerns the family. He reads Jesus' "brothers and sisters" (Mark 6:3) as children of Mary and Joseph, not cousins or stepchildren. He says Matthew 1:25 ("he knew her not until she had brought forth her firstborn son") implies ordinary marital relations afterward, and he compares the family size to ordinary large families of the period. He argues that James, the Lord's brother, led the Jerusalem movement after Jesus' death, citing Paul's letters, Acts 15 and later church writers. He also argues that the "beloved disciple" in John is James rather than John, and that the Gospels progressively "mute" the family, with Mark still naming "Mary, mother of James" and Luke omitting the brothers.

The talk ends with Tabor saying that Mary was Jewish, not Christian, and that the book is an attempt to restore her to her faith and history. Tabor presents his positions as his research conclusions, and some (such as the beloved disciple identification) he acknowledges are minority views.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Mary is lost through sparse sources and deliberate erasure
Tabor says the New Testament gives Mary only about a dozen scenes, bookended by Christmas and the crucifixion (the transcript's "Mary, the mother of Jesus" framing). He calls her loss "a deliberate eraser," because for the church she needed only to bring Jesus into the world. He says Stephen Shoemaker (Duke) makes a similar point, that her knowledge was "muted and written out," and that this happened because the Jesus family mattered so much to the first followers and then less to later ones. Tabor also says that later Western and Eastern traditions, over roughly 150 to 200 years, made her into a figure set apart: the perpetual virgin, mother of God, sinless, and in some accounts never needing bodily functions.

### 2. The "brothers and sisters" were Mary's children
Tabor's central historical claim is that Mary had several children after Jesus. He names James, Joseph (Joses), Simon and Jude as brothers, and "his sisters" as unnamed. He rejects the cousin and stepchild readings, and notes that the Greek Orthodox view makes them Joseph's children from an earlier marriage while the Roman Catholic view makes them Joseph's brother's children. He cites Matthew 1:25 for ordinary marital relations after Jesus' birth. He compares family sizes with his grandmother's family of eleven and with a birth record he says he found from Egypt (eight children, spaced by years), arguing that Mary likely had five or more sons and up to three daughters. He says a possible name for one daughter is Salome, and "Miriam" is a plausible name for another.

### 3. James led the Jerusalem movement
Tabor argues that James the Lord's brother succeeded Jesus as leader of the movement, which he calls "the sect of the Nazarenes." His evidence includes Paul's report that he "visited James, the Lord's brother" (Galatians 1 is the implied source; the transcript does not give a verse), the claim that "all the church fathers" admit James's leadership (Tabor names Clement of Alexandria), and Acts 15. In Acts 15 Tabor says James decides the dispute over Gentile converts. Tabor says Peter and Paul speak first, James issues the decision, and James writes the letter "from James and all the elders and apostles." He reads this as evidence that James, not Peter, was in charge. He also says that Acts 12 reports James the son of Zebedee killed under Herod, so the James who mattered was the Lord's brother, and that "James who?" is a common blank for students.

### 4. The beloved disciple is James, not John
Tabor says the beloved disciple of John 19:26-27 is James, and that this is a minority view he shares with Robert Eisenman. His argument is that if Jesus was the eldest son of a family with several younger brothers, the care of his widowed mother would pass to a brother, and John's Gospel says Jesus entrusted his mother to the disciple. He says the identification of the beloved disciple with John son of Zebedee traces to Irenaeus, and that the "two Johns" confusion (including John the Elder at Ephesus) lies behind it. He also notes that Mark and Luke give the sons of thunder unflattering stories, which he uses to question the John of tradition.

### 5. Gospel writers progressively mute the family
Tabor argues the Gospels show a pattern. In Mark, Mary is "Mary of Yose" and later "Mary, mother of James," which he reads as Mark assuming readers know who James is. Matthew names the brothers, while Luke omits them and at the cross refers only to "women from Galilee." John, which Tabor treats as the latest Gospel, gives no name to Jesus' mother and presents the family as unbelieving. Tabor notes that Paul's letters never name Mary, though Galatians 4:4 says God sent forth "his son born of a woman, born under the Torah."

### 6. Mary as a Jewish woman, and the Protevangelium of James
Tabor says Mary's name (Miriam) comes from Moses and Aaron's sister, and that the festivals (Passover, Shavuot, Sukkot) and Sabbath preparation are part of her world, so she is Jewish rather than Christian. He treats the Protevangelium of James, which he says was attributed to James the brother, as the "emblem" of later Marian legend: Joachim and Anna, the immaculate conception (which he distinguishes from the virgin birth), the temple upbringing, and the midwife Salome's examination. He calls these "the world of trying to protect her from being a real human being." Tabor gives the Protevangelium a 4th-century context in his remarks on the traditions, but he says the book's own content is what he is analyzing.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mary's brothers and sisters were cousins or stepchildren.** Tabor argues they were Mary's children, citing Mark 6:3 and Matthew 1:25.
- **The immaculate conception means the virgin birth.** Tabor says his students at Notre Dame made this error; the immaculate conception concerns Mary being born without original sin.
- **Mary is a Christian figure, and the "Mary of faith" is the historical Mary.** Tabor says she was Jewish, and that the devotional figure of the blessed virgin and mother of God is a later construct.
- **James was not a major figure, and John led the early movement.** Tabor says James the Lord's brother led the Jerusalem movement (Acts 15), and that the beloved disciple was James rather than John.
