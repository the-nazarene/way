## Summary

Tabor, interviewed by Jacob Berman, asks how to reconstruct the historical Mary, mother of Jesus, from sources beyond the canonical gospels, including apocryphal texts and other material. He points first to the Gospel of Philip, which names three Marys who travelled with Jesus (his mother, a sister, and Mary Magdalene), and links this to John's picture of a group of women at the head of whom stands Jesus' mother (John has her at the cross). He also notes that Joseph seems to vanish from the narrative, which he says some traditions explain by an early death [00:19].

Most of the talk concerns Jesus' paternity. Tabor argues that the gospels only say the father is God or the Holy Spirit, and that Matthew and Luke use phrasing that could be read as a divine begetting without implying that no man was involved. He compares this to Genesis 18, where Sarah conceives a child promised by Yahweh and Abraham is still the father. He says that as a historian he assumes Jesus had a human father, and he weighs Joseph (the most likely, as John twice calls Jesus "son of Joseph") against a rumored alternative name, "Panthera." He traces the rumor through a rabbinic story about a Rabbi Eleazar and a saying attributed to "Yeshua," a Celsus-type late report, and a 19th-century pun on *parthenos* that he says he could not find earlier. He stresses that he has never claimed Jesus' father was a Roman soldier, and he cites Christopher Zugman's view that the dates of Panthera's unit do not fit but do not entirely rule out fatherhood.

The talk closes with archaeology and tradition about Mary's later life: Sepphoris as the urban centre of Galilee, Mount Zion and the dormition traditions placing Mary in Jerusalem, the Acts account of Peter going to "James and the brothers," and the Dead Sea Scrolls as evidence for apocalyptic celibacy as distinct from a Neoplatonic, body-denying celibacy. Tabor says Philo will not help much because of his philosophical outlook. The transcript ends with music and no conclusion; Tabor says he plans a documentary and might skip paternity there.

## Key Teachings

### Mary's wider circle in the sources
Tabor says the Gospel of Philip's list of three Marys (mother, sister, Magdalene) "correlates" with John's band of women around Jesus' mother [00:19]. He treats Joseph's disappearance from the story as a historical clue, though he notes the New Testament does not state his death outright [00:19].

### Language of divine begetting
Tabor argues that the phrase "the Holy Spirit will come upon you and the thing begotten in you will be the son of God" could mean the child is "filled with the spirit from the womb," not that God was physically the father, especially if Matthew and Luke draw on another tradition [01:27]. He draws a parallel to Genesis 18, where one visitor is Yahweh and Sarah conceives, while Abraham is still plainly the father [02:40, 04:27]. He contrasts this with Greco-Roman divine-birth stories (Zeus, Hercules, Plato accounts), which he calls more explicitly mythological [03:25].

### Historian's assumption of a human father
Tabor says he has never claimed Jesus' father was a Roman soldier. His position is that "we don't know who his father is," but as a historian he assumes one existed, since "all humans have fathers" [06:30]. He argues that God-created "fake DNA" is an incoherent option, and he states that Joseph is the most likely father [07:42, 08:39]. He then notes that if Joseph is the father, the Matthew and Luke birth narratives would have "no standing at all" as history [19:14].

### The "Panthera" tradition and rabbinic material
Tabor reports a rabbinic story in which Rabbi Eleazar is quoted on the wages of a harlot brought to the temple, and the tradition remembers a reply attributed to Yeshua [09:35, 11:02]. He says Eleazar was later accused of being a *minim* (heretic) and that Jesus was increasingly seen as leading a movement with "two powers in heaven" [11:02]. He argues that "Yeshua ben Panta" or "Bar Panta" may have been a local designation surviving within decades of Jesus' death, though he admits this rests on "just one story that survives" [14:44]. He says the *parthenos* pun explanation for Panta appears only in 19th-century scholarship, which his graduate student could not trace earlier [14:44, 15:48]. He notes that the church father Epiphanius explains the name as belonging to the clan of Joseph or Mary [15:48, 16:45].

### Christopher Zugman and the Roman army
Tabor reads Zugman's view that Panthera's service years do not fit fathering Jesus, but that this "does not entirely negate" the possibility, since Panthera might have fathered Jesus before enlisting [22:06, 23:22]. Tabor adds that Panthera was a slave impressed into the Roman army, and that Jane Schaberg's suggestion of rape by a soldier is one scenario he does not endorse [20:47, 23:22].

### Archaeology and Mary's later life in Jerusalem
Tabor describes Sepphoris as Galilee's urban capital, four miles from Nazareth [09:35]. He says the traditions of Mary's death and burial on Mount Zion are "pretty solid" and that Mary may have been the matriarch of a household in Jerusalem [24:34]. He ties this to Acts 12 (Peter going to "James and the brothers") and to excavation work on Mount Zion with Shimon Gibson, and to the cenacle research of David Clawson [25:33, 26:41].

### Dead Sea Scrolls and apocalyptic versus Neoplatonic celibacy
Tabor says the scrolls cannot name Mary but show what can be known about women and celibacy. He distinguishes apocalyptic celibacy ("the appointed time of the end has grown very short") from Neoplatonic celibacy, which rejects the flesh [26:41]. He says Philo is "not going to be much help" because of his heavenly, Neoplatonic outlook [26:41].
