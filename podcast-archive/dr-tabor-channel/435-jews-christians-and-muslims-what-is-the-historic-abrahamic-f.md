## Summary

Paul Williams, host of the Blogging Theology channel, interviews James Tabor about the fourth, revised and expanded edition of *Restoring Abrahamic Faith*. The video opens with a short preface by Tabor, who describes the book as a personal "manifesto" rather than an academic history. The main question is what the historic Abrahamic faith looks like when one reads the Hebrew Bible on its own terms, apart from later Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. Tabor says he has "taken off my academic hat" for this book, and that he wrote it largely for Christians who follow Jesus.

Tabor argues that the core of this faith is one creator God who cannot be represented by an image, an ethical Torah (the "ten words," or *devarim*), the prophets' call to justice, and Abraham as the model. He reads Isaiah 40-66 as a vision in which Israel is meant to be "a light to the nations" and nations will come to Jerusalem to learn God's way. He also argues that the Sabbath and the Ten Commandments have a universal scope, grounded in Genesis 1-2. On the Messiah, he says the Hebrew Bible describes a Davidic figure, not a divine one, and that no text there describes a Messiah who dies for sins and is raised. He presents Paul's justification by a blood sacrifice, the later Christian Sabbath-to-Sunday change, and the literal-flesh Eucharist as departures from this older pattern. Much of his evidence is taken from the Hebrew text as he translates it, from Mark's account of the empty tomb, from the Didache, from a Pliny letter he dates to about 110 CE, and from Dead Sea Scrolls material on a messianic meal.

The conversation ends with Williams praising the book and saying he disagreed with parts of it, and with both men promising a later discussion of Tabor's *The Lost Mary*. Tabor also says a fifth book, *Jesus Betrayed*, is in progress. Much of the interview is Tabor speaking at length, with Williams asking pointed questions, especially on the Messiah, Isaiah 53, and Paul.

## Key Teachings

### The Torah as a universal ethic, not a sacrificial system
Tabor says the Ten Commandments are better rendered "the ten words" or "ten matters" (*devarim*), and that they set up a culture meant to bring blessing to everyone. He argues that the prophets, not just the Torah, expand these words by rebuking the people for lying, stealing and adultery while they still come to the temple claiming deliverance. He cites Isaiah 2 and the image of nations going up to Jerusalem to learn "his ways" and "walk in his paths." He presents the Torah as a "mirror" that reveals a person's actual state, and says the problem is not the mirror. In his view, the Hebrew Bible teaches justification by faith but does not tie it to a human being's death as an animal sacrifice. He cites Micah on sacrifice ("Shall I offer my firstborn son for my sins?") as repudiating that idea.

### Universality of the Sabbath and the Decalogue
Williams asks how the Torah, which seems to be Israel's covenant gift, can be "for all mankind." Tabor answers from Genesis 2, where God blesses the Sabbath before Israel exists, and from the two versions of the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5), one of which grounds the Sabbath in creation. He also cites an Isaiah passage that blesses the stranger who "lays hold of the Sabbath." He says Joseph Hertz's commentary on the Decalogue supports the universal reading. On the Sunday question, he argues that the Gospels present Sunday as the day of the empty tomb, that by the second century Christians called Sunday "the Lord's day" and sometimes "the eighth day," and that the change was gradual. He presents Pliny's letter to Trajan as the earliest external reference to Sunday worship. Williams and Tabor both note this is a contested area with a large literature, which Tabor says he covers in the book's footnotes.

### Who the Messiah is in the Hebrew Bible
Tabor says there are ten Hebrew Bible passages that the book treats as messianic, and that the figure is never called "the Messiah" in them but is described as a descendant of David. He says Isaiah 11 is the main chapter, and that the Davidic branch is expected to gather the scattered tribes, bring light to the nations, and draw them to Jerusalem. He stresses that "Messiah" (anointed) also applied to priests and kings, and to Moses and the seventy elders in the Torah. He says the Davidic figure is "absolutely not" God, and cites Micah on ruling in the strength of "his God." He uses John 17:3 and Revelation 11:15 to argue that the New Testament itself distinguishes "the only true God" from the Messiah. He suggests that the capital-M "Messiah" as a category is a later development.

### Isaiah 40-66 and the suffering servant
Williams asks how Paul could claim the scriptures foretold a Messiah who would die for sins (1 Corinthians 15:3-4), when Williams cannot find this in the passages Tabor lists. Tabor says Paul probably drew on Isaiah's servant songs, especially Isaiah 53, and that the Christian reading of this servant as an individual who bore the world's sins is one possible reading. He argues that the servant is more plausibly a collective of faithful Israelites who are rejected and killed. He cites Jeremiah's lament about being "killed all the day long," and Jesus' words about the prophets who were killed. He also reads Isaiah 53 as using plural language ("upon them") and says the chapter never mentions a Messiah. He allows that Jesus may have identified his own movement with the suffering righteous, but says this is far from what the later church established.

### The Eucharist and the Qumran messianic meal
Tabor argues that the early Christian meal, in the Didache (which he says was found in a Greek monastery in the nineteenth century), meant gathering as one body and the "vine of David," not literal flesh and blood. He says the Christian sacramental meaning developed later, and that he knows of only one parallel, in Greek magical papyri that involve drinking blood. He says it is "inconceivable" that James and the Jewish followers would have adopted a ritual of drinking a human's blood, since blood was forbidden in the Torah. He compares the Dead Sea Scrolls messianic banquet, where two messiahs share bread and wine, and notes that Jewish Sabbath meals use bread and wine as symbols of food and joy.

### Abraham, Miriam and the scope of the book
Tabor frames the book as a return to the "ancient paths" of Jeremiah 6, a quest that begins with Abraham leaving Ur. He says the "way" is a term found in Isaiah 40:3 and used by the early Jesus movement, and borrowed from the Dead Sea Scrolls. He says the book is for Christians, and that Jesus, in his view, "stood for Abrahamic faith." He also notes that Matthew changed Mark's "Why do you call me good?" and that Luke kept the original, which he discusses in a footnote. In the closing exchange he says he wants readers to ask how many of the book's "foundation stones" are preserved in Islam.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Torah was abrogated and is obsolete.** Tabor says the Torah was reframed in Christianity as a means to merit, but the Torah is a "mirror" revealing human failure, and it was meant as an ethical standard for all people.
- **The Sabbath is only a Jewish ritual.** Tabor argues that Genesis 2 and the Decalogue's creation grounding make the Sabbath universal, and that the Sabbath is a "sign" between the Creator and his people.
- **Sunday replaced the Sabbath by divine command.** Tabor says the change was gradual, starting with Gospel resurrection accounts and developing into "the Lord's day" and "the eighth day" by the second century.
- **The Messiah was expected to be God and to die for sins.** Tabor says the Hebrew Bible describes a Davidic figure who gathers Israel and brings justice, and that no passage describes a dying or resurrected Messiah.
- **Isaiah 53 is about the Messiah.** Tabor reads it as about a collective of faithful Israelites who are rejected, and notes it never mentions a Messiah.
- **The Eucharist is the literal body and blood of Jesus.** Tabor argues the earliest meal meant gathering and symbolism, and that blood-drinking would have been forbidden to James and the Jewish followers.
- **Yahweh is the one true God, and other gods are rivals.** Tabor says the Hebrew Bible's God is "behind everything," not one more deity, and that "Elohim" points to a force beyond created things. He resists reading the first commandment as simple henotheism, though he acknowledges that academics often take that view.
- **Jesus is God.** Tabor says Jesus prays to God and has a God, citing John 17:3 and Revelation 11:15, and that he "stood for Abrahamic faith."

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor argues that Paul's justification by blood sacrifice and the literal Eucharist depart from the Hebrew Bible and from James's Jewish followers. Williams presses him on where Paul got the idea, and Tabor suggests Isaiah 53.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor places James in charge of a Sabbath-keeping, Torah-observant movement, and says he cannot picture James adopting a blood-drinking Eucharist.
- **Nazarenes, Ebionites, Essenes:** Tabor opens by saying the early followers of Jesus are sometimes called Jewish Christianity or "Ebionite" Christianity, and that they migrated east with parallels to the Dead Sea Scroll group. He also draws on the Qumran messianic meal.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet, nonviolence:** The Ten Commandments as ten words, the rejection of sacrifice as atonement, and the vision of nations learning the Torah and beating swords into plowshares. Diet is not discussed.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Tabor's reading of Isaiah 2 and 40-66 is a vision of nations coming to Jerusalem, peace, and justice, which he sees as the future goal of history.
- **Resurrection, afterlife, hell:** Tabor discusses the empty tomb and the Galilee appearances in Mark and Matthew, but says the Hebrew Bible does not teach a dying-and-rising Messiah. Afterlife and hell are not discussed.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor says Jesus was a "good representation" of Abrahamic faith, a Davidic-style figure who appointed twelve apostles to rule over the twelve tribes, and who did not claim divinity.
- **Gospels and canon:** Tabor says Matthew changed the "Why do you call me good?" saying, while Luke kept the original, and that Mark is the earliest Gospel.
