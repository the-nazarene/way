## Key Teachings

### Method: breadcrumbs and imagined reconstruction
Tabor presents the historical Mary as a "quest" modeled on the historical Jesus quest. He says the sources are thin: he counts twelve passages and treats each as a "crumb" from which to build a picture. He stresses that the result is "an exercise in imagination," grounded in Josephus, Galilean geography and archaeology, and that he has not "just made it all up." He also says plainly that some of his claims, such as her "matriarch" role, "I cannot prove this at all."

### Mary as a married Jewish mother with children
Tabor rejects the later Catholic and Eastern picture of Mary as perpetually virgin. He reads Mark 6:3 as naming Jesus's brothers as full brothers, not half-brothers from a previous marriage of Joseph, and he takes Matthew 1:25 as implying that Joseph and Mary lived as a married couple after Jesus's birth. He calls the later aversion to sexuality a reason for the "robbing" of Mary's life, and he imagines a woman with "seven or more children," some of whom probably died. He also suggests Joseph may have died or disappeared early, leaving Mary a widow, and he tentatively links the Jesus "son of Joseph" tradition and the James ossuary to this household, while saying he will not do the archaeology in this conversation.

### The Panthera question
Tabor addresses the claim, which he says "everyone" repeats, that he argued Jesus's father was a Roman soldier named Pantera. He denies this, saying "anyone who says that has not read what I've written," and says his view is that Joseph was most likely the father. He is cautious that a birth record would say "father unknown," since the Gospel of John's "we were not born of fornication" and the Gospel of Thomas's charges show that the accusation was in circulation. He traces the name to Epiphanius, who, Tabor says, reports that Panthera was a family name, and to rabbinic references to "Yeshua bin Panteras." He argues that the "pun on parthenos" explanation first appears only in the nineteenth century, and he treats the Panthera story as "slander at first" that later became a family-name explanation.

### Mary in Galilee under Roman violence
Tabor places the family in Nazareth, a village near Sepphoris, which he calls the largest town in Galilee and a major urban and regional center, and he describes Sepphoris's burning and the crucifixions along the road as events Mary would have lived through. He also describes the "year of the three messiahs," citing Judas son of Hezekias, the Perean messianic upstart, and the shepherd king, from Josephus. Crossan extends this, arguing that Jesus, growing up in Nazareth, would have heard about the Roman legion's destruction of Sepphoris (which he dates, following Josephus, to 4 BCE) from family and village memory, and he suggests Mary would have had to explain to him "where was God the day the Romans came."

### Mary and James: succession and the Jerusalem line
Tabor argues that James was the most important of Jesus's brothers and the successor of the movement, and that the New Testament and later sources were shaped to reduce the family's role. He cites Acts 1 (Mary and the brothers listed among the believers at Pentecost), Acts 15 (James's decision, which he says "doesn't even call him the brother of the Lord"), and John 19 (the beloved disciple, which he takes as James). He cites Stephen Shoemaker's view that Luke removes the family in order to remove them from the story. Tabor also says the Ebionite tradition, and a James-centered Gospel of Thomas tradition, support a James succession. He presents Mark 3's "who are my mother and brothers" episode as a case where Mark seems to minimize family and where the family can still be read as present. He calls his view that Mary was "the wellspring behind the teachings of Jesus" a claim he cannot prove, and he uses it to frame the five themes of Jesus's teaching in the book's final chapter.

### Nonviolent resistance as a first-century Jewish option
When Derek asks for the "best version" of Jesus's message, Crossan argues that nonviolent resistance to Rome was a specific Jewish option in the period between the revolts of 6 BCE and 66 CE, not something Jesus invented or something Christianity originated. He reads Josephus as hostile to nonviolent resisters ("purer hearts, but impure hands"), and he says that Rome treated such leaders as criminals: "We either crucify you, send you to the beasts, or we send you to an island." He argues that Pilate's execution of Jesus without rounding up his lieutenants shows that Jesus was executed as a leader of nonviolent resistance, and he calls the idea of a "nonviolent Messiah" a plausible Jewish development. He treats Jesus's movement as one model among others and says he wants every religion to answer "are we a sustainable species?"

### Violence, the environment and the limits of the Bible's vision
Crossan argues that "escalatory violence" is the warning of Genesis 4 (Cain and Lamech) and Genesis 6 ("fills the earth"), and that the Bible's concern about war (Isaiah's swords-into-plowshares image) did not foresee agriculture and environmental damage as a threat. He says that even a world with no war would still be on a trajectory to destroy the earth, and he lists human self-destruction in "atomically, biologically, chemically, demographically, ecologically" terms. He is clear that he is not a pacifist and would resist an attack. He also points to the treatment of other species and the environment as part of a single moral concern.

### Heaven on earth, not rescue from earth
Crossan argues that the biblical hope is not that God will destroy the world, since that would be an admission that creation "blew it," and that the hope is for heaven to come down to earth. He reads the Lord's Prayer's "as in heaven so on earth" in Matthew 6 as "on earth now in heaven later" in English, and he uses the river and trees for the healing of the nations in Revelation 22 as an image of earth's perfection. He ties this to Genesis 1 and to Sabbath, Sabbath-year and Jubilee rhythms of justice in time.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus's father as a Roman soldier named Pantera.** Tabor says that anyone attributing this view to him has not read the book; he holds that Joseph was most likely the father, and he traces the Panthera name to later polemic and a family-name explanation.
- **Mary as a perpetual virgin with no sexual or bodily life.** Tabor says this is a later theological development, and he reads Mark 6:3 and Matthew 1:25 as describing a married woman with children.
- **Jesus's family as irrelevant to the movement.** Tabor argues that the New Testament and later tradition minimized James and the family, and that the Jerusalem community was a family succession.
- **Nonviolent resistance as a Christian invention.** Crossan says it was a Jewish option in the first-century setting, and that Jesus was one practitioner of it.
- **God will destroy the world and create a new one.** Crossan argues that this view has no real basis in Scripture, since it would mean that God "blew it," and that the biblical hope is heaven coming down to earth.
- **Salvation as "going to heaven."** Crossan contrasts this with the biblical picture of earth being perfected and with the activities of churches working for justice and poverty relief.
