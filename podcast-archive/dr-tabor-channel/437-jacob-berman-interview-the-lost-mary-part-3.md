## Key Teachings

### Mary as an observant Jewish woman and mother
Tabor's first point is that Mary was Jewish and observant of Torah. He says he wants her "known as the Jewish mother of Jesus," and he argues that ancient Judaism, not modern Judaism in its many forms, is the proper context for her. He also states a concrete estimate: she would have been the mother of "eight or more children, whether they all live to adulthood or not," with an example of five boys and three girls. The talk gives no source for the number. It is an inference from his reconstruction.

### An urban upbringing in Sepphoris and Nazareth
Tabor argues against the picture of a rural village childhood. He says the archaeological tradition places Mary's birth in Sepphoris, which he calls the second-largest city in the Galilee. He dates the destruction of Sepphoris to 4 BC, after Herod's death, during what he calls "the year of the three messiahs." He attributes the revolt in the north to Judas son of Hezekiah, whom he distinguishes from Judas the Galilean, a later figure of 6 CE. He says Roman forces sent down "three legions" and burned the city. From the hilltop of Nazareth, Tabor says, the fire would have been visible. He links this to the saying about a city on a hill that "could not be hidden," which he thinks Jesus is probably alluding to. He describes Nazareth as a suburb of Yafia, about a mile and a half from Sepphoris, with two major roads crossing nearby, one to Egypt and one to Damascus. He says Herod Antipas made Sepphoris his capital and that it minted its own coins.

### Literacy
Tabor says he doubts the "Mediterranean peasant" framing, and he specifically disputes the view that Galileans were illiterate. He says that Bart "always argues" that people of this kind were illiterate, and he does not accept that for Jewish culture. His reasoning is that the Hebrew alphabet has only 22 letters, that synagogue prayers are memorized from hearing them read, and that an alphabetic script is "quite easy to learn on a basic level." He says he thinks Jesus was literate, and he refers to a synagogue at Sepphoris, while noting that none has yet been found at Nazareth.

### Archaeology of first-century Galilee
Tabor cites archaeological work as evidence for an urban Galilee. He says Josephus reports "over 240 villages," and he names James Strange and Jodi Magness as excavating in the region, along with a site the captions render as "U Kaa," which he says Tom McCulla and others have excavated. He also says several first-century ruins have been found in Nazareth, that Ken Dark has done the most work on this, and that the remains near the Church of the Annunciation are ordinary houses: two- and three-room houses with sub-levels and roofs, not wealthy mansions.

### Mary as "godmother of the movement"
Tabor argues that Mary is not an insignificant figure who disappears once the movement begins. He says she is "the godmother of the movement," and he moves from the title "mother of God" to this one. As evidence he points to the image search for "Mary, mother of Jesus, day of Pentecost," where Mary is usually at the center of the scene, with Mary Magdalene and sometimes James nearby, the apostles gathered, and tongues of fire above. He says that the early representations put her "front and center," and he wants her returned to the Jewish people. He admits that this will be hard, and says "probably" when asked whether they will take her back.

### Longevity and the averages
Tabor responds to the objection that Mary could not have lived to see James die. He says that ancient life expectancy figures are averages skewed by infant mortality, and that a person who got past 12, 13, or 14 "could easily live into your 50s, 60s, 70s." He says there are records of people living into their 80s and 90s, not only in the New Testament, and he proposes prosopography of known figures of the Roman world as a method. He adds a comment about physicians, and says Jewish practice may have included handwashing before procedures, linking this to Semmelweis in Vienna in the 1800s. The handwashing point is an analogy he offers, not a claim about Mary's own care.

### Suffering: crucifixion and the death of John the Baptist
Tabor describes the grief Mary would have felt at the crucifixion of her son, calling it "unimaginable." He says that he has written a chapter on what crucifixion involved, and he quotes Josephus describing it as "the most wretched of deaths." He then turns to John the Baptist's beheading, and says John was part of the same family, because Mary and Elizabeth were related. He calls John "the only source we have now," and he links this to the family's losses.

- **Nazareth did not exist.** Tabor says that claim is "just incorrect," and that first-century remains have been found there.
- **Mary disappears from the story once the movement begins.** Tabor argues that she was its "godmother," and that early Pentecost imagery puts her at the center.
- **Ancient people did not live long.** Tabor argues that short reported lifespans reflect infant mortality, not the lifespan of adults, and that people in the 80s and 90s are attested.
