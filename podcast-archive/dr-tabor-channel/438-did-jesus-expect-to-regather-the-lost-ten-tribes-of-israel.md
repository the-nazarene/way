## Summary

Tabor asks whether Jesus expected to regather all Twelve Tribes of Israel, including the northern tribes often called "lost," and whether the choice of twelve apostles reflects that expectation. He opens with Luke 22:28-30, which he identifies as a Q passage from the Last Supper, where the disciples are promised thrones to "judge the 12 tribes of Israel." He argues that the Hebrew prophets repeatedly promise that God will restore all twelve tribes, and he prefers the term "scattered tribes" to "lost tribes."

His main line of evidence is a survey of prophetic texts, especially Isaiah 11, Isaiah 2, Isaiah 61, and Hosea, which he says his book *Restoring Abrahamic Faith* outlines in full. He reads Peter's and James's letters as addressed to the scattered tribes, and he uses Jason Staples's work on Hosea in Romans to suggest that Paul may also have understood his Gentile mission as gathering dispersed Israelites. Tabor says the Hebrew Bible's Davidic figure was expected to gather the nations and the tribes, and that none of these prophecies was fulfilled in Jesus's lifetime. He suggests that Jesus believed he had been anointed to do this work, that James, Peter, and Paul shared the expectation, and that the kingdom was thought to be imminent.

The talk ends as an open-ended lecture rather than a conclusion. Tabor notes modern messianic groups who claim to be the lost tribes, says he does not know whether the twelve apostles were ever meant to sit on thrones in the way the New Testament describes, and leaves the question for listeners to consider.

## Key Teachings

### The twelve apostles and the twelve tribes
Tabor argues that the Q saying at the Last Supper (Luke 22:28-30) explains why Jesus chose twelve apostles: "One apostle for each tribe." He says this is rarely discussed in popular Christianity. He treats the twelve as a sign that Jesus expected the tribes to be gathered, and he reads the sayings about sitting on thrones as concrete expectations rather than symbols.

### The "scattered" tribes and the Hebrew prophets
Tabor says the Hebrew prophets consistently promise restoration of all twelve tribes, and he points to the prodigal son as a parable that "has been understood anciently" as a picture of the tribes returning. He cites Josephus as placing the tribes "up around the Black Sea" in his own time, though Tabor notes they have since moved. He says the question of whether the tribes were actually restored is less important to him than what the texts say.

### Peter and James writing to the tribes
Tabor reads the opening of James (addressed to the twelve tribes in the dispersion) and of 1 Peter (addressed to exiles in Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia) as evidence that these leaders saw their mission as reaching Israel. He proposes a division: "Peter going to the Jews... and Paul to the nations," and says James stayed in Jerusalem as far as we know.

### The Davidic figure in Isaiah 11
Tabor says the Hebrew Bible has about ten passages about a Davidic figure, who is never called "the Messiah" in them. He focuses on Isaiah 11:1-10: a shoot from the stump of Jesse, the spirit of the Lord resting on him, and a banner (*nes*) that draws the nations to Jerusalem. He compares the "rest upon him" language to the Ebionite texts, which he says describe the spirit resting on the final anointed figure, and to Jesus's baptism, where the spirit descends like a dove and "nests." He says the figure's main task is to gather the world back to God, ending with the wolf and lamb and the earth full of the knowledge of the Lord.

### Paul, Hosea, and the gathering of the nations
Tabor credits Jason Staples (a student of Bart Ehrman, per the caption) with taking Hosea's "not my people" passages seriously in Romans. He quotes Romans 9 (Hosea's "those who are not my people I will call my people") and asks whether Paul applied this to scattered Israelites or to Gentiles. He says Paul believed some people were "being elected and chosen and others are not," and he reads Romans 11:25-26 ("when the full number of the Gentiles come in, all Israel will be saved") as possibly meaning the tribes gathered together with the nations. He says most New Testament scholars he knows read Romans as only using Hosea's language for Gentiles.

### Jesus's self-understanding and fulfillment
Tabor says Jesus fulfilled none of the Hebrew messianic prophecies in his own lifetime. He reads Isaiah 61 ("the Lord has anointed me to preach good news to the poor") as showing that Jesus believed he had been anointed by the Spirit and was "the guy" who would gather the tribes. He says Jesus's expectation may have depended on a response from the people, and he notes the suffering servant motif as a possible contingency. He cites the anointing at Bethany as an early sign of Jesus's anointed role and says he "has no problem" with Jesus being anointed. He says he follows Schweitzer on this point of Jesus's sense of calling.

### The kingdom and the earliest movement
Tabor concludes that the earliest vision of Jesus's movement, shared by James, Peter, John, and Paul, was that "the time is fulfilled" and "the kingdom is at hand." He argues that choosing twelve leaders for twelve tribes was a step toward gathering Israel before the kingdom arrived. He says how this translates to later Christianity is "up for grabs," and he notes that Zechariah's promise to save Judah first and then Ephraim fits this pattern.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The tribes are simply "lost."** Tabor prefers "scattered tribes," arguing that the texts describe them as dispersed and expected to be gathered, not permanently gone.
- **The twelve apostles are a symbolic detail without a clear purpose.** Tabor argues they are tied to the expectation of restoring all twelve tribes, as Luke 22:28-30 suggests.
- **Paul's mission is only to Gentiles, with no link to Israel's restoration.** Tabor argues that Paul's use of Hosea and his "all Israel will be saved" language may point to gathering scattered Israelites along with the nations.
- **The new heavens and new earth are another cosmos (the Christian revelation picture).** Tabor says Isaiah 11 describes a new creation "right here" on earth, not a different cosmos.
- **Jesus fulfilled the Hebrew messianic prophecies.** Tabor says none of the prophecies were fulfilled in Jesus's lifetime, and that his expectation depended partly on a response from the people.
