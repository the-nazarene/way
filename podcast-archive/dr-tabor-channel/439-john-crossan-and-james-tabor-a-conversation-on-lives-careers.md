## Summary

This is a nearly two-hour informal conversation, recorded in Orlando, Florida, between James Tabor and John Dominic Crossan, with an unnamed moderator who the description identifies as Derek Lambert of the MythVision Podcast. The stated aim is not a debate but a look at "who these guys are": how each came to faith, how each became a historian, and what that history says about Jesus. Roughly the first half covers autobiography. Crossan describes entering a Roman Catholic monastery at sixteen, taking a religious name, and leaving in 1969 after a dispute over his public criticism of Humanae Vitae. Tabor describes a Churches of Christ upbringing, a childhood visit to Jerusalem at sixteen that shaped his interest in history, and his baptism at ten and again at seventeen.

The second half turns to scholarship. Crossan credits his parable work and his time with Norman Perrin at the University of Chicago, and describes how a reading of the Good Samaritan as a provocation shaped his thinking. Both speakers say they once leaned toward mythicism (Crossan says he interviewed Robert Price and Richard Carrier) and now hold that a historical Jesus stands behind the gospels. Crossan argues that miracle stories such as the walking on water and the virginal conception are read better as parables or metaphors than as literal events. Tabor argues that apocalypticism, the expectation that God would act decisively and soon, was a failed hope that Jesus shared in a changed form, perhaps with a conversion after John the Baptist's death. He grounds this in Qumran material, Daniel, Isaiah, Zechariah, and the Thanksgiving Hymns. The two disagree in places: Tabor is more willing to reconstruct Jesus's self-understanding as a messianic figure and to read a historical kernel into Mark's last week, while Crossan leans toward treating the gospel material as a literary construction whose meaning matters more than its literal history. The talk ends with a discussion of Josephus and the Herod banquet, and with Tabor announcing a next episode on "the lost Mary."

## Key Teachings

### Miracles as parable: the walking on water and healing
Crossan's central method is to read the text before asking whether an event happened. Of Mark's walking-on-water scene he says, "Somebody said Jesus walked on water. He didn't. Now, when in doubt, read the text." He argues the story is about the disciples rowing without Jesus, going nowhere, until he rejoins them, and that the community already knew "that the boat could take off without Jesus." He adds that the point is the message, not the feat: "keep Jesus in the boat, because we ain't in the boat, you're going nowhere." He also argues from the gospel temptation scenes that if Jesus could do anything he wanted, the texts show him choosing not to display power, and that his deeds are mostly healing rather than spectacle.

### Virginal conception as metaphor
Crossan distinguishes the virginal conception from the Catholic doctrine of the Immaculate Conception, which he says concerns original sin, and from the Thomistic image of light passing through glass. He argues that in the Jewish tradition extraordinary children were born to aged, infertile parents, while in Roman tradition a god comes down; Luke's version, he argues, claims that Jesus is superior to Caesar's sons of god. He says of Mary's "let it be" response that "she said yes." His conclusion is blunt: "I don't believe it happened for a second," and he says denying it literally would make Jesus "just one more guy." He also argues that the literal-versus-nonliteral framing is itself a modern question the authors would not have asked.

### Apocalypticism as a failed narrative
Tabor, who calls himself "Mr. Apocalyptic" in his own work, traces an apocalyptic stream from the Qumran community's reading of Daniel through John the Baptist and Jesus to the letter of James. He states the definition he is using: "apocalypticism is the delusion" in the sense that it is the belief that God will come soon to do it for you. Crossan adds that "apocalypticism is the belief that God's going to come soon to do it for you. Magic." Tabor argues that the prophets never promised that God would act without human cooperation, citing the covenant as a two-way relationship, and that the apocalyptic hope amounted to a "failure of nerve." He says, "the one enterprise in human history, thought enterprise that has failed 100% is predicting the end of the age and when it would come."

### Jesus after John the Baptist
Tabor reconstructs John's program as a second exodus: going into the desert, crossing the Jordan, and repenting in preparation for God. When John was executed, Tabor argues, Jesus faced a choice to pick up John's mantle as a renewed prophet, and that "the only way I can explain what happened to Jesus is a massive conversion." Crossan adds the alternative of martyrdom, but both agree Jesus was baptized by John and accepted his theology for that moment. Tabor says Jesus's apocalyptic message was reshaped into a call to cooperation, which Crossan links to nonviolent resistance: "He came and came up with this idea of nonviolent revolution."

### Messianic self-identity
Tabor argues, drawing on Michael Weise's book *The First Messiah* and his own article on the dynamics of messianic self-identity, that Jesus may have read himself into the servant material of Isaiah 49 and the branch of David in Isaiah 11, without calling himself "Messiah" in the later sense. He proposes that Jesus saw himself as the Davidic figure beside a priestly figure, a pairing he links to Zechariah 4, and that John's death forced him to reconsider. He hedges: "I don't go to the bank with it." Crossan says he is not sure how Paul would have viewed the matter but that Jesus was a Jew, and does not dwell on the question.

### Mark's last week and the sightings in Galilee
Tabor reads Mark's account of the final week as a story of a man trying to stay alive, moving between the crowd in the temple by day and his family at Bethany by night, and being arrested at Gethsemane. He argues that Jesus's death was not a foreordained plan but something he feared. He offers the Gospel of Peter's "for eight days we wept and mourned" as evidence of griefstricken disciples rather than triumphant ones, and says "the sightings, I like to call it sightings of Jesus were in the Galilee," pointing to John 21. He also raises the Talpiot tombs as a possible family tomb, while noting that "who cares about bones" in the sense that the resurrection question is not a bones question. Crossan's view is that the resurrection stories are literary and that "nobody's singing and clapping at the empty tomb" in Mark's world.

### Josephus, the banquet and why invention matters
In a closing section, Tabor and Crossan discuss the Herod banquet in Mark 6 and Josephus's account of John the Baptist's death. Tabor cites Louis Feldman's advice to take Josephus's broad framework seriously while discounting inflated numbers, and Tabor's own article on Moses, Elijah and Josephus. Crossan says a Herod banquet "never happened" is "utterly correct but inadequate," because the real question is why Mark invented a scene that vilifies Herod. He suggests the banquet and daughter motif may draw on Ovid's *Metamorphoses* (through Bruce Louden's comparative work) and that Mark's scene reflects the Roman banquet culture.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus literally walked on water.** Crossan argues the text is a parable about the disciples' need for Jesus, not a report of a feat.
- **The virginal conception is the same as the Immaculate Conception.** Crossan corrects this, saying the latter concerns original sin and the former is a claim about Jesus's status.
- **Apocalyptic expectation was a sound prediction.** Tabor argues it failed and that Jesus likely revised it, toward cooperation and nonviolent resistance.
- **Mythicism is the obvious conclusion.** Both speakers say they once leaned that way and now think a historical Jesus stands behind the texts, though the degree of historical reliability is still contested between them.
- **Mark's account of the last week shows Jesus seeking death.** Tabor reads Mark as showing Jesus trying not to die and moving carefully between crowd and family.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor describes a "beating heart" of Paul that he feels in the seven undisputed letters, and says the Paul he knows is not the later orthodox Paul. He also notes Paul's contrast between a natural and a spiritual body, which Crossan's resurrection account implies is different from the gospel stories.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor places the letter of James in the apocalyptic stream and sees the "scattered 12 tribes" as part of its world, while noting that James was not a Pauline document.
- **Nazarenes, Ebionites, Essenes:** Tabor's Qumran and Daniel material is the main connection, with the Teacher of Righteousness at the start of the stream.
- **Torah, nonviolence and the Kingdom:** Crossan's reading of Jesus's "nonviolent revolution" and Tabor's Genesis 1 reply to apocalyptic passivity bear directly on these questions.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** The talk discusses the empty tomb, Galilean sightings, the Gospel of Peter's grief, and Revelation 20's sea of the dead, though no clear position on the afterlife is stated.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor's messianic self-identity argument, drawing on Isaiah and Zechariah, is the talk's most direct treatment of this.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Crossan's treatment of Mark as a literary construction, and the Gospel of Peter as a non-canonical witness, bear on this.
