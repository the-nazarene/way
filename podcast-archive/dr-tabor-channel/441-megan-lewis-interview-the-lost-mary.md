## Summary

Megan Lewis interviews James D. Tabor about *The Lost Mary: Rediscovering the Mother of Jesus*. The question is whether Mary can be recovered as a real, flesh-and-blood Jewish woman, separate from the theological figure of later Christian devotion. Tabor says the sources are thin and describes his method as following "breadcrumbs": giving more weight to earlier sources, weighing each author's biases, and setting texts against archaeology and the political history of Galilee and Judea. He says Sepphoris, Nazareth, Mount Zion in Jerusalem, and caves in the Judean hill country all help him place her in a believable setting, and he cites Mount Zion co-director Shimon Gibson as having found no blatant errors.

The central claims are these. Mark 6:3 names Jesus's brothers and mentions sisters, and Tabor takes this at face value. Luke's account drops the names, which he calls "the fading of the family." James the brother of the Lord, whom Tabor takes to be the figure who speaks in Acts 15, succeeded Jesus as leader. Tabor argues that the "beloved disciple" of John's Gospel was James rather than John. He attributes the later removal of Mary's other children, and the growing emphasis on her sexual purity, mainly to a Platonizing dualism that treated the body and the earth as lower. Tabor presents these as his own readings and says he expects scholars to disagree. He ends by describing a planned set of studies on five figures (Mary, John the Baptist, Jesus, James, and Mary Magdalene) and hopes the book will start a wider conversation. Most of the talk is Tabor speaking. Lewis asks questions, offers a comparison to the Mesopotamian priestess Enheduanna, and summarizes his points.

## Key Teachings

### Mary as a historical woman, not a theological persona
Tabor says the "Marian devotion" of Catholic and Orthodox tradition, including the titles "mother of God" and "ever virgin," stands apart from the historical woman. He compares this to the quest for the historical Jesus and proposes separating "the heaven from the earth," so that the "confessional" Jesus and Mary are not confused with the people who lived in Galilee. He wants Mary to be remembered "as she was in her own time," a married Jewish woman with children.

### Mark 6:3 and the siblings of Jesus
Tabor takes Mark 6:3 at face value and names the brothers James, Joseph, Jude, and Simon, along with unnamed sisters. He says later traditions, which he describes as the Eastern view (Joseph an old man with children from an earlier marriage) and the Western view (the brothers are cousins or stepchildren because Joseph was kept celibate), both work to remove the children from Mary. He says he "just go[es] right down the middle" and treats them as her children.

### Luke, Acts, and "the fading of the family"
Tabor argues that Luke omits the names of the brothers that Mark gave, and that in Acts the brothers appear only as a clause ("his mother Mary and brothers were there"). He contrasts this with Paul, who in Galatians (he refers to Paul's Jerusalem visit, where Paul meets "James, the brother of the Lord") clearly knows of James. In Acts 15, a "James" speaks at the Jerusalem council after Peter and Paul, and Tabor identifies him as the Lord's brother, not James son of Alphaeus. He argues that Acts withholds the title "brother of the Lord" in order to keep the focus on Peter.

### Jerusalem leadership and the sources on James
Tabor argues that James led the Jerusalem community after Jesus. He cites the Armenian quarter's claim to James as patron, Paul's reference to James as a "pillar," the Gospel of Thomas (saying 14, "go to James the just"), and Hegesippus, preserved in Eusebius, for James's death and reputation. He adds Josephus's account of James's death in the *Antiquities* and a passage from Clement of Rome that describes James as holding the "chair." He says multiple attestation makes the tradition strong despite its legendary elements.

### The beloved disciple was James, not John
Tabor says the "beloved disciple" in John's Gospel is James, and he locates the John identification in Irenaeus, around 180 CE. He argues that the beloved disciple leaning on Jesus's breast, and the scene at the cross ("Son, behold your mother"), fit a son who would care for a widowed mother, whereas John the son of Zebedee is shown in the Synoptics as asking for places at Jesus's right and left and wanting to burn a village. He says the Ephesus house of Mary has a late and weak tradition behind it.

### Dualism and the desexualization of Mary
Tabor links the later emphasis on Mary's virginity and purity to the influence of Middle Platonism and Neoplatonism, with Philo as an example, and to a dualism in which the earth is a prison and the body is lower. He adds a comparison with Greek gold-foil funerary texts, which he dates to the third century BCE, in which the dead claim a heavenly origin. He argues that the mainstream Jewish and ancient Near Eastern view, expressed in Gilgamesh and in Jesus's own kingdom sayings, valued earthly life. He takes Matthew 1:25 as evidence that Joseph and Mary lived as a married couple after Jesus's birth. He also notes the later traditions that Mary did not use the toilet and that the church fathers distrusted women and sexuality.

### Kingdom on earth and "Jerusalem above"
Tabor relies on the Q material shared by Matthew and Luke, including the prayer petition "As your will is done in heaven, so be it on earth" and the saying that the kingdom is "right here before you." He argues that Jesus taught a kingdom on earth, and he sets this against Paul's phrase "Jerusalem above is the mother of us all," which he uses to show how far later Christianity moved toward heavenly focus.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mary had no children besides Jesus.** Tabor argues that Mark 6:3 names real brothers and sisters, and that the perpetual-virginity tradition is a later theological development.
- **The beloved disciple was John, son of Zebedee.** Tabor argues that the beloved disciple was James, the brother of Jesus, and that the John identification comes from Irenaeus around 180 CE.
- **Joseph was an old man who had no sexual relations with Mary.** Tabor reads Matthew 1:25 as implying marital relations after Jesus's birth, and he rejects the Eastern and Western workarounds that make the brothers stepchildren or cousins.
- **Peter was the sole leader of the early church.** Tabor argues that James led the Jerusalem community, and that Acts downplays him.
- **Mary's sanctity separated her from ordinary bodily life.** Tabor says later church writers' claims that she did not use the toilet or that she lived like an angel are not history.
