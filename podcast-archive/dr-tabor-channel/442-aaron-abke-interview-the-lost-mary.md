## Summary

Aaron Abke interviews James Tabor about Tabor's book *The Lost Mary: Rediscovering the Mother of Jesus*. The question is why the historical Mary has nearly disappeared from view, though she is the most venerated woman in Christian history. Tabor frames her as "the best known, least known woman in history" and says he wants "to return her to her Jewish people." Most of the interview is Tabor speaking. Abke mainly asks questions, reads passages from the book, and adds commentary, including a point that Jewish homes were literate and an agreement that ancient people were often more capable than modern scholars assume.

Tabor's central argument is that Mary was a Davidic, Torah-observing mother of a large family, probably raised in Sepphoris, and that later doctrines of perpetual virginity and the "erasure" of James the brother of Jesus served a dualistic, Hellenistic view that devalued the body and sexuality. He reads Matthew 1:25 and Mark 6:3 as evidence for Mary's other children. He treats James as Jesus's successor and as the leader in Acts 15. He proposes that Luke's genealogy is Mary's, that Jesus carried a double Davidic and priestly lineage (drawing on Psalm 110 and Hebrews), and that "the beloved disciple" was James rather than John. He presents these as a hypothesis to be discussed, not settled fact, and he says he is "not writing anything in stone."

The talk closes with Tabor's proposal that scholars begin a "quest for the historical Mary," Abke's comparison to Schweitzer's quest for the historical Jesus, and the book's final chapter. In that chapter Tabor argues that the "wellspring" of the Jesus movement's spiritual force "is most likely Mary herself."

## Key Teachings

### 1. Mary as a Jewish, urban, Davidic woman
Tabor argues that Mary's identity has been lost to Jewish life. He asks what a Jewish home in Galilee would have looked like, including Sabbath preparation and the festivals. He places the family in Sepphoris, the Galilean capital, and not simply in the village of Nazareth, four miles away. His sources are Josephus, who called Sepphoris "the jewel of the Galilee," and his own excavations there with the late Jim Strange. He says Sepphoris was destroyed in 4 BC, in the revolts after Herod the Great died, and rebuilt under Herod Antipas. He says Jesus's references to royal palaces may reflect that childhood experience. He calls Joseph a *tekton* (builder or architect, not simply "carpenter") and says the family was "distinguished," Davidic and priestly, not "backward illiterate peasants."

### 2. Literacy and the Jewish home
Tabor argues that Jewish homes were literate because of Torah reading, prayer and synagogue life. He says Hebrew has few letters and can be learned quickly. He cites a story in which Rabbi Eleazar, a second-century rabbi who spoke positively of Yeshua, records a legal discussion about a harlot's wages from Deuteronomy, answered with a Micah-based saying about "filth" and toilets. Tabor treats the Luke 2 story of the boy Jesus in the Temple as "a kind of trope," but says Jesus's engagement with texts is plausible. He points to the Dead Sea Scrolls Teacher of Righteousness as a parallel for a figure reading prophecy about himself. He argues that minimalist scholars wrongly deny Jesus any knowledge of Scripture. He also cites synagogue archaeology from Magdala and Masada, against an older claim that there were no first-century synagogues.

### 3. Perpetual virginity and Hellenistic dualism
Tabor says Matthew 1:25 ("And he knew her not until she had brought forth her firstborn son") implies normal marriage afterward. He reads the brothers in Mark 6:3 (James, Joseph, Jude, Simon) and the unnamed sisters as Mary's children. He argues that the doctrine of perpetual virginity came from Hellenistic dualism, which devalued the earthly and the bodily. He contrasts this with Genesis 1, where creation is "good," and with a Jewish view in which women are honored as matriarchs. He criticizes Tertullian and the church fathers for their views of women. He also says that Josephus's statements about women are shaped by his own experience. He notes that the theological debates about whether Mary went to the toilet reflect the same dualism.

### 4. James as successor, and the erasure of James
Tabor argues that James the Just succeeded Jesus as leader of the Jerusalem movement and that his legitimacy depended on being Jesus's full brother, which is why later tradition had to erase him. He treats Gospel of Thomas saying 12 ("go to James the just, for whom heaven and earth came to be") as evidence of James's standing, linking it to the Jewish idea of righteous ones who sustain the world. He reads Acts 15 as James's decision, which Tabor says Luke's narrative obscures ("And James stood up"). He says the decision required Gentiles to abstain from idols, fornication and blood. He reads James's letter as a Davidic leader writing to "the 12 tribes scattered abroad," and reads Luke's final Last Supper saying about twelve thrones as the setup of a provisional government. He cites Hegesippus, quoted by Eusebius (transcribed "Andius"), on James's death and his entry into the sanctuary. He also cites Clement of Rome on James's "chair" and the Nazarene and Ebionite sources on the high priest's garment. He says these are arguments to weigh, not proofs. He says he does not accept Mary Magdalene as the beloved disciple. He argues instead for James, citing Jesus's words from the cross ("Son, behold your mother") as the moment James became head of the family.

### 5. Two genealogies, Davidic and priestly
Tabor argues that Luke 3's genealogy is Mary's, not Joseph's. He points out that Luke names Nathan where Matthew names Solomon, and that the Bible records a Davidic son named Nathan through Bathsheba. He identifies Luke's "Eli" with the Joachim of the Proto-Evangelium of James, Mary's father. He says the Luke list includes priestly names, such as Levi and Matthat. He says he gets "in trouble" for this view among colleagues. He cites Richard Bauckham's work on the family of Jesus, though he says Bauckham does not agree that Luke is Mary's genealogy. He reports that Eastern church sources preserve a double (Davidic and priestly) lineage for Jesus. He argues this would explain how James could enter the sanctuary. Abke comments that this might explain why the Nazarenes and Ebionites claimed Yeshua as Messiah.

### 6. Psalm 110 and a priestly Messiah
In a late segment, Tabor argues against the view that Hebrews makes Jesus a priest only by a heavenly order. He reads Psalm 110 in Hebrew as "You are a priest forever according to my word, O righteous king," and argues that the Hebrew text does not support "order of Melchizedek" as a name. He ties this to Jesus's question to the Pharisees about Psalm 110 and to the Qumran Melchizedek figure. He says this supports a priestly-Davidic successor line and that James fits that pattern. He presents this as a point he did not include in the book. Abke calls it "a zinger" and suggests a new book.

### 7. The "five categories" and Mary as the source
Tabor says that in the final chapter he assembles Q-type material common to Matthew and Luke, not in Mark, and organizes it into five categories. He says this is the teaching package of the Jesus-James movement and that it comes from his mother. He says he cannot prove it, but that "we don't know anything from the ancient world" is a limit that applies to all such claims. He also addresses Abke's question about the final chapter's thesis and says he would not change a word.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mary is the perpetual virgin.** Tabor argues Matthew 1:25 and Mark 6:3 indicate normal marriage and Mary's other children; he attributes the doctrine to Hellenistic dualism.
- **Jesus and his family were illiterate, backward peasants.** Tabor argues Jewish homes were literate and the family was Davidic and priestly, living near the Galilean capital Sepphoris.
- **There were no first-century synagogues.** Tabor says that claim was common in his graduate school days, and that synagogue remains at Magdala and Masada have since been found.
- **Jesus's brothers were cousins.** Tabor argues the brothers were Mary's children and that "cousin" readings in later sources reflect the desire to protect Mary's virginity.
- **Peter, Paul, or John led the early church.** Tabor argues James led the Jerusalem movement and that Acts 15 shows his authority.
- **The beloved disciple was John.** Tabor argues it was James, citing the traditional attribution's late origin (Irenaeus c. 180) and John's own text.
- **Luke's genealogy is Joseph's.** Tabor argues it is Mary's, with Eli as Joachim.
- **Jesus was not Jewish.** Tabor says some readers think he was "Christian," which he rejects; he treats messianism as a Jewish phenomenon.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Jesus vs. Paul:** Tabor cites Romans 1:4 for Paul's own acknowledgment that Jesus was Davidic "according to the flesh," and contrasts Paul's Hellenistic mysticism with the Jewish Jerusalem movement under James.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** The central thread: James as successor, Acts 15 as his ruling, Hegesippus on his death, the Gospel of Thomas saying, the "chair," the Nazarene and Ebionite sources on the priestly garment, and the James letter as a Davidic leader's letter.
- **Nazarenes and Ebionites:** Tabor draws on Ebionite sources for the high priest's garment and on Nazarene claims about James. He also suggests the Nazarene and Ebionite movement east of the Jordan may have had family records supporting Jesus's Davidic claim.
- **Torah:** Tabor describes Torah-based Jewish home life, Sabbath, festivals, and the Acts 15 requirements for Gentiles (abstaining from idols, fornication, blood).
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor argues Jesus saw himself in Davidic-priestly terms, citing Psalm 110, Hebrews, and the Q-type sayings about twelve thrones.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor argues that Luke had sources (including a travel-diary-like "we" section) and that the later tradition erased James from the record. He also notes that Matthias's election in Acts 1 shows the family's role.
