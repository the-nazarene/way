## Summary

Host Robert L. Kuhn interviews James Tabor about Tabor's book *The Lost Mary: Rediscovering the Mother of Jesus*. Kuhn asks what the book's core claims are and why they are radical, then sets Tabor's work in the context of his earlier books on Jesus, John the Baptist, Mary Magdalene, James and Paul. Tabor carries most of the interview, and Kuhn's questions mainly prompt him.

Tabor's central claim is that Mary is "the best known least known woman in history." He argues she should be placed back in her own Second Temple Jewish setting: a Jewish matriarch, a widow at the head of a large household of five sons and at least three daughters, and a member of the Davidic line with a priestly strand mixed in. He presents the "breadcrumbs" approach as his method. He takes every textual source, mainly the New Testament, and reads each passing reference as evidence (Mark 6:3 "son of Mary," the absence of Joseph after Jesus is twelve, Matthew 1:18, Paul's silence about her). He adds archaeological context from the Mount Zion excavation and the priestly houses on that hill. He says the reconstruction is imaginative but disciplined, "imagining in the direction of the truth."

The talk ends with Tabor's observation that the name Mary is the Greek form of Miriam, a Jewish name carried by a sister of Moses and Aaron. He presents this as one more piece of evidence for her Jewish identity. The interview ends with closing music at about 27:33. The talk is a persuasive overview rather than a technical argument, and most of the claims rest on Tabor's own inferences from the sources he cites.

## Key Teachings

### Mary as a Jewish matriarch in a Jewish household

Tabor argues that Mary's identity has been lost behind her role in Catholic and Protestant doctrine. He describes her as "a Jewish woman, a Jewish matriarch" with "eight or more children," early widowed and so "in a sense the head of the clan." He places the Jesus movement inside "an apocalyptic messianic movement within second temple or ancient Judaism before the fall of the temple in 70," and says "the Jesus movement is not Christianity." He describes her as "aristocratic," probably growing up in Sepphoris, which he calls the largest urban center of Galilee, rather than in Nazareth. He also argues that she belonged to a family that was "doubly royal," combining the Davidic and priestly lines. He says this point is "almost never pointed out."

### Reading the breadcrumbs: "son of Mary" and the missing Joseph

Tabor uses Mark 6:3, which he calls "our earliest gospel" record of Jesus's life, to argue that Jesus is identified as "son of Mary." He says that in the ossuary inscriptions women are named as parents only when they are prominent, and that this phrasing signals her importance. He argues that Joseph is absent from the story after Jesus is twelve and "probably dead at that time," since he would otherwise have been present at the crucifixion. He concludes that Mary became "the matriarch of a household" that included "five boys, four brothers and Jesus," along with sisters.

### Perpetual virginity as a later development

Tabor says Matthew 1:18 shows that Joseph "knew her not until she brought forth her firstborn son," which in ordinary Jewish usage implies marriage and later children. He argues that "this perpetual virginity is a much later idea, maybe in the late 2n century into the third century," tied to a philosophical ideal that holiness means asexuality. He states that "there's no ideal within Judaism of being celibate and living in companionship with a man or a woman." He ties the doctrine to Martin Luther's view that Mary "remained a virgin her whole life," and to Roman and Greek Catholic exaltation of her as "queen of heaven."

### James as successor and the Jerusalem household

Tabor argues that when Jesus died, James "takes over leadership of the movement," and that James should be seen as "a successor of Jesus" leading "the entire Jesus movement," not merely "the leader of the Jerusalem church." He says the movement was waiting for the end and observed Torah and the festivals (Passover, Pentecost, Feast of Tabernacles, Day of Atonement) and went to the temple from about 30 to 70 CE. He says the Vatican's arrangement of Peter and Paul would be better if James were in the middle. He locates the household on Mount Zion, a hill southwest of the Temple Mount, where the priestly mansions stood. He suggests that a wealthy follower's house there hosted the Last Supper and the group under James, and that Mary would have been at the center of a busy household.

### Paul's silence about Mary

Kuhn raises the point that Paul never mentions Mary. Tabor responds that Paul describes Jesus as "born of a woman, born under the law," which he reads as affirming a real human being rather than a figure who dropped from heaven. Tabor notes that Romans says Jesus "is descended according to the flesh from the lineage of David," which for him confirms the family's Davidic claim without naming Joseph or Mary. He notes that Paul "met James, the Lord's brother" on a Jerusalem visit after his change of view, and that Paul still regarded himself as Jewish and as observing Torah.

### The name Miriam

Tabor argues that Mary's name should be understood as Miriam, the Hebrew name, which "just comes across in Greek" as Maria. He says the name was borne by Moses' and Aaron's sister and was common among Jewish women, and that very few non-Jewish women bore it. He presents this as another piece of evidence for her Jewish identity.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mary as a perpetual virgin from the start.** Tabor argues this is a later development (late second to third century) and that Matthew 1:18 implies a normal marriage afterward.
- **Mary as mainly a theological figure, "queen of heaven."** Tabor says she should first be understood as a first-century Jewish woman in a Jewish household.
- **The Jesus movement as Christianity.** Tabor argues it was a Jewish apocalyptic messianic movement before 70 CE, observing Torah and the festivals.
- **Peter and Paul as the central founders.** Tabor argues James led the movement after Jesus and should be placed in the center of the Vatican image.
- **Jesus's brothers as cousins or half-brothers.** Tabor implies the four named brothers were full siblings in the Jewish household, and that the later "don't worry" explanations are wrong. He presents this as a point his students raise, so it is his argument rather than a settled fact.
- **Mary as a passive figure in a back room.** Tabor argues she would have been at the center of a busy household that hosted visitors and shaped its business.
