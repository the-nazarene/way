## Summary

This is a recorded slideshow lecture by James Tabor, given at live events while promoting his book *The Lost Mary: Rediscovering the Mother of Jesus*. The audio is rougher than the professionally recorded audiobook, which Tabor mentions at the start. The talk's question is the one the book opens with: Mary is "the most famous but least known woman in history" (06:32), and Tabor asks how she can be so revered and yet so forgotten. He treats the New Testament references as the "breadcrumbs" for a quest for the historical Mary, comparable to the historical quest for Jesus.

Tabor's answer has three parts. First, he reads the Gospel and Acts passages about Mary and the brothers of Jesus as evidence of a large, active family, with Mary as its head and James as her son and leader of the Jerusalem movement. Second, he uses archaeology and tradition to place Mary's early life in Sepphoris and Nazareth and her later life in Jerusalem, especially at the traditional upper room on Mount Zion. Third, he argues that Mary was pushed to the margins in the Gospel tradition, beginning with Luke-Acts, and that the church's later emphasis on her purity and sanctity muted her human and leadership role. Tabor presents these as his reconstruction, and he says more than once that some points are speculation.

The talk ends with Tabor's suggestion that Mary was a spiritual teacher to Jesus and James, and that the Sermon on the Mount and the letter of James carry her voice. He thanks the audience and stops there. The talk is almost entirely Tabor speaking, with no guest or host.

## Key Teachings

### Mary's role in the New Testament is small in quantity but significant in content
Tabor says the New Testament has "basically nine main passages" about Mary, "nine main scenes," and that some of them repeat. He says the scenes are the birth and genealogy accounts in Matthew and Luke, the twelve-year-old in Jerusalem, the wedding at Cana, the Capernaum and Nazareth family scenes, the crucifixion, and the upper room in Acts. He says John never names Mary and that Paul mentions a woman bearing the Son but not Mary by name. Tabor's method is to treat each scene as a clue and to join the clues into a larger picture.

### The Capernaum scene and the family of Jesus
Tabor reads Jesus' statement "Who is my mother and my brothers and sisters? Those who hear and do the word of God" as positive toward Mary, not dismissive. He says Jesus is inviting the crowd into the family and that "mikasa sucasa" captures the sense that the family can be widened. He calls the scene "usually taken as negative" and says it is "incorrect to take it as negative." He then uses the Nazareth passage (Mark 6:3) to build the family: Jesus, four named brothers (James, Joseph, Judas, Simon), and three sisters, which would make eight children. He says the sister names Mary, Salome and Anna are traditional and not certain. He also says that if Joseph died early, as some ancient church tradition held, Mary was a single mother raising a large family. Tabor identifies Joseph as the father, following "the standard view of most scholars," and says he will not address the question of the father in this lecture.

### Mary's origins in Sepphoris and the Davidic-priestly claim
Tabor argues that Mary grew up in Sepphoris, the urban capital of Galilee under Herod Antipas, rather than in the Jerusalem Temple as later legend says. He draws on a fourth- or fifth-century Byzantine tradition that places Mary's family house on the southern slope of Sepphoris, and he says he finds the Protoevangelium of James's stories about the Temple unhistorical. He describes a small first-century mosaic and house excavation there, but he cautions that the Sepphoris finds are not Mary's house. He also says he thinks Mary was Davidic and that she was priestly as well as royal, and that the book will give the evidence, though this lecture does not.

### Nazareth as a Davidic village and the Ken Dark excavations
Tabor says Nazareth may have been a village of about 200 people, and that the name may reflect a Davidic lineage among its families. He argues that the Davidic families of Nazareth included Mary and Joseph. He describes first-century remains under the Sisters of Nazareth convent that Ken Dark has excavated, and he refers to Dark's 2015 article and his Palestine Exploration Fund book. He says he is "more interested in what's underground" than in the reconstructed village.

### Mary in Jerusalem and the upper room
Tabor argues that Mary lived out her life in Jerusalem and was the matriarch of the early movement. He contrasts this with the view that she went to Asia Minor with John, which he calls "doesn't even make sense to me." He says that because he identifies James as the beloved disciple, Mary's home would have been with James and the brothers. He discusses the Dormition Abbey and the upper room on Mount Zion. He says the Byzantine upper room and the lower Davidic room are probably medieval or later in their present form, but that the space may be valid. He says the Madaba map (sixth century) and Bargil Pixner's work suggest that a Byzantine structure preserves the upper room. He also refers to David Clawson's book and to Steven Shoemaker's work on the Dormition traditions. He says that the upper room gathering in Acts 1 and the Pentecost scene in Acts 2 place Mary with the Twelve in Jerusalem.

### How Mary was marginalized: Luke-Acts and the family
Tabor compares Mark's crucifixion and burial accounts with Luke's. He says that where Mark names Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Mary the mother of Joses, Luke replaces the names with "all those who knew him and the women who followed him from Galilee." He says Luke also changes the Nazareth question from "the carpenter, the son of Mary, the brother of James" to "Is this not Joseph's son?" He argues that Acts cannot remove James, and that James appears in Acts as the leader who makes decisions, which means Acts implicitly keeps the family in view. He also gives the Galatians 1 mention of James as the only other independent witness to James' role. Tabor's conclusion is that the marginalization was gradual: the family's role faded as sexuality, celibacy and the sanctity of Mary and Jesus took over the picture, and as Paul's emphasis on the "Christ of the Spirit" displaced interest in the earthly family.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mary is prominent throughout the New Testament.** Tabor says the New Testament has very few references to her, "basically nine main passages," and that "if you've never really tallied the references, you will be shocked to know that there are very few."
- **The Capernaum scene is a rejection of Mary and her family.** Tabor argues that Jesus is widening the family to include the crowd, not disowning his mother and brothers.
- **Mary grew up in the Jerusalem Temple as a vestal-like virgin.** Tabor says he finds the Protoevangelium of James's Temple story unhistorical and places Mary's youth in Sepphoris.
- **Mary went to Asia Minor with the apostle John.** Tabor says this "doesn't even make sense to me" and argues she stayed in Jerusalem.
- **James was not a significant figure apart from Jesus.** Tabor argues that James is "the great James who becomes leader of the movement when Jesus dies," supported by Acts and Galatians 1.
