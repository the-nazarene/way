## Summary

Megan Lewis interviews James Tabor, a retired religious studies professor, about what historians mean by the "historical Jesus" and how they try to separate it from later belief. Tabor frames the interview around a question he calls "the making of a Messiah": did the Gospels create Jesus, or did Jesus's own activity and self-understanding shape the Gospels? He says he cannot prove his answer and presents his views as tentative.

Tabor's main argument is that Jesus "well might have had" a messianic self-consciousness. He supports this by reading Psalm 40 and the Isaiah servant passages as texts a pious first-century Jew could read as addressed to himself, and by listing messianic "categories and candidates." He also points to Zechariah 9:9 and the donkey entry into Jerusalem as a possible case where an action might have been performed deliberately. He admits these are not proofs and that some of this reasoning is speculative.

On method, Tabor describes a positive process: compare the Gospels without harmonizing them, identify the earliest layers (including the material shared by Matthew and Luke that is often called Q), read each evangelist's distinctive themes, check the texts against Josephus, Philo, the Mishnah and archaeology, and assemble a core of things historians consider most likely. He also draws on non-canonical sources: the Didache, which he says does not interpret the Eucharist sacrificially, and the Gospel of Thomas, which he reads as pointing toward James the Just as a leader. He ends by saying historians can make claims about Jesus's final week but not about miracles, and he stresses humility about how much is unknown.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The historical question versus the Christ of faith
Tabor distinguishes the "Jesus of history" (the human Jesus in Galilee and Judea) from the "Christ of faith" (the glorified heavenly figure). He argues that historians cannot examine miracles or the resurrection as such, so claims like Jesus walking on water are not historical questions in his view. What a historian can say, in his words, is that in Jesus's final week "Jesus went to Jerusalem, confronted the authorities, and was crucified by the Romans, and reported to be alive on the third day." He says he knows no major historian who would disagree with that statement. He also argues that creedal formulations developed over centuries and were disputed for about 300 years.

### 2. Method: sources, differences and layers
Tabor uses Mark 2 and Matthew 12 (the disciples picking grain on the Sabbath) as an example of why the Gospels should not be harmonized. He says the differences in reasons and answers are useful evidence. He describes the shared Matthew–Luke material, which he calls "Q" (while noting that scholars use other labels and that Q is debated), and says that reading it separately gives "a different voice of Jesus," drawing on Bart Ehrman's work. He suggests studying Luke's special material (L) and each evangelist's themes. As an example of Luke's emphases he cites Jesus's "Father, forgive them" on the cross and the disciples "sleeping for sorrow" in Luke's Gethsemane account, contrasted with Mark's portrayal.

### 3. Messianic self-consciousness: Psalm 40, Isaiah and Zechariah
Tabor asks the listener to imagine a pious Jew reading Psalm 40:6-8, with its lines about sacrifice not being desired and "in the volume of the book it is written of me," and then the individual-sounding "servant" passages in Isaiah, especially Isaiah 50:4-6. His proposed model is "categories and candidates": a expected category (priestly or Davidic Messiah) plus a possible candidate who comes to think the text applies to him. He compares this to the "Jerusalem syndrome," which he says usually affects Christians and sometimes leads to dangerous acts, and says he is not accusing Jesus of anything like that. He lists five possible "messianic pantomimes" he will discuss in his lecture, and offers the ride into Jerusalem on a donkey (Zechariah 9:9) as one example. He notes that all four Gospels report it, that Mark is probably the earliest, and that the scene may have been shaped by the prophecy.

### 4. Extra-canonical sources: the Didache and the Last Supper
Tabor describes the Didache, which Eusebius mentions, and says it was discovered in the late nineteenth century. He cites Ehrman's Loeb translation. He points to sayings attributed to Jesus that appear there but not in the canonical Gospels, such as the instruction to "let your gift sweat in your hands," and to the opening "two ways" material. On the Eucharist, he says the Didache's bread and wine are not "the body and blood of Jesus" and that the text "doesn't interpret them sacrificially." He suggests that Mark's Last Supper account may be less historical because it could derive from Paul's account in 1 Corinthians 11. He notes that John's Last Supper has only the foot-washing and no bread and wine.

### 5. The Gospel of Thomas and James the Just
Tabor cites Gospel of Thomas saying 14, in which the disciples ask who will guide them after Jesus leaves and are told, "Wherever you go, go to James the Just, the brother of Jesus." He says that church records he calls "other church records" support the idea that the earliest followers looked to James. He reads Paul's accounts (Paul's visit to Cephas and James, and the list of resurrection appearances including to James in 1 Corinthians 15) and Acts 15 (the Jerusalem council in which James speaks and issues a decision) as evidence that James held authority. He says Mark mentions the brothers of Jesus without making James the successor, and he describes the leadership as "almost like a caliphate."

### 6. Archaeology: Sepphoris and Nazareth
Tabor says archaeology gives context the texts lack. He notes that Sepphoris, the urban center of Galilee, is never mentioned in the New Testament, while Nazareth lies about four miles south of it. He says excavations there show the urban background of Jesus's home region. He mentions his own 33 years of archaeological work.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"The Gospels are self-evident; just read what they say."** Tabor says the four Gospels differ and that interpretation is unavoidable, so historical work begins by asking what the sources say and how they relate.
- **"Historians deny any supernatural event happened."** Tabor says historians do not take that position, but miracle stories such as walking on water are not something historical method can investigate.
- **"Historical study is destructive and minimizing."** Tabor argues it is a positive and creative enterprise that tries to find the most likely core of the material.
- **"The creeds reflect a settled early Christian consensus."** Tabor argues that the questions about Jesus's divinity and humanity were disputed for about 300 years.
- **"The Gospels should be harmonized."** Tabor argues that the differences between the evangelists are evidence to be studied, not errors to be reconciled.
