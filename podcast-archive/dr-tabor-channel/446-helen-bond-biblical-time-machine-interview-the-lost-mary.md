## Summary

This is an interview on the podcast *Biblical Time Machine*, posted to James Tabor's channel. Helen Bond and Lloyd Llewellyn-Jones host, and Tabor, who is discussing his book *The Lost Mary*, carries most of the substance. The hosts' questions move through Mary's royal ancestry, the paternity rumors, the perpetual virginity doctrine, the size of Jesus's family, the Assumption and her burial, and finally a closing "time machine" question. The interview was recorded as the main part of the episode, with an archaeology segment reserved for the Time Travelers Club.

Tabor's central proposal is that Mary was "doubly royal," descended from both the Davidic line and the priestly (Hasmonean/Levitical) line. He argues that Luke's genealogy (Luke 3) is in fact Mary's, and that some eastern church fathers said the same. He supports this with names he reads as Levitical or Hasmonean in the genealogy, and with the claim that the family of Jesus was keen on genealogies. He describes this as a possibility he judges plausible, not a settled result.

On paternity, Tabor says he accepts that Jesus had a human father and takes the question historically. He reviews the options: Joseph, an unnamed other man, or a soldier called Panthera, whose name appears in a second-century polemic by Celsus. He reports three rabbinic references from Sepphoris to "Jesus son of Panteras" and a Roman soldier, Abdes/Pantera, buried in Germany, but he admits this is inconclusive. He rejects the Greek *parthenos* ("virgin") pun explanation because he traces it only to a 19th-century German scholar. Perpetual virginity, he argues, grows from later ascetic ideals and from ignoring Matthew 1:25. He ends by saying he would fill in a birth certificate as "father unknown," while leaning toward Joseph. The interview closes with his proposal that the beloved disciple was James, and with his wish to visit Acts 1 and 15 in the time machine.

## Key Teachings

### Mary as doubly royal (Davidic and priestly)
- Tabor argues that the genealogy in Luke 3 is Mary's, not Joseph's. Early Christians "uniformly said" this, he says, and modern scholars rejected it as harmonization.
- He points to a name in Luke's list, Nathan, which he identifies with a son of David and Bathsheba mentioned in Chronicles, not the prophet Nathan of Samuel. He calls this name "too strange" to be invented.
- Further names he reads as Levitical or Hasmonean: Matthat (compared with Josephus's father, Matthias), Levi, and Jannaeus (known from the Hasmonean king Alexander Jannaeus). He argues the Judean line in Luke mixes with a Levitical line, and cites Torah marriage patterns in support.
- He says eastern church fathers called Mary "doubly royal," with priestly and Judean or Davidic lineage, and that a reference to James wearing the priestly miter fits this. He calls these "breadcrumbs."
- He notes that Hadrian arrested two grandsons of Jude, "the brother of Jesus," and that the *despsyni* ("relatives of the Lord") were interested in genealogies. He says genealogical records were kept in Sepphoris, which he treats as a chapter of the book.
- Bond raises the women named in Matthew's genealogy (Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, Bathsheba). Tabor agrees these women are often noted for sexual irregularity, and says the reader should not judge them before reading the story of Mary.

### The paternity question and the Panthera tradition
- Tabor says he accepts the "wow proposition" that humans have fathers, and treats the birth narratives as legendary or mythological on that point.
- He says the earliest hint of rumor is in John, where Jesus's opponents say "We weren't born of fornication," and that Matthew 1 implies Joseph was thought not to be the father.
- He reports that Celsus, a second-century critic, claimed Mary slept with a Roman soldier named Panthera. Origen's quotations of Celsus are the source.
- Tabor says that the church fathers who answered Celsus did not deny that a person named Panthera existed, but said the name was in the family, a point he finds significant.
- He cites three rabbinic references from Sepphoris, dated by him to around the turn of the first century, quoting "Jesus son of Panteras" favorably (including a remark about a prostitute's wages and toilets).
- He mentions a Roman soldier, Abdes/Pantera, a freed slave from Sidon, buried on the Rhine in Germany, whom he visited. He says this is one possibility, not proof.
- He concludes that his preferred answer would be "father unknown," while leaning toward Joseph, and that Matthew and Luke make Joseph's paternity uncertain.

### The Greek *parthenos* pun theory
- Bond suggests a possible mistranslation of the Greek *parthenos* (young woman, maiden, or virgin) behind "Panthera."
- Tabor rejects this. He says it is a "kneejerk response" among scholars, but he traced it: it first appears in the 19th century, suggested by a German scholar, and not in early church writers.
- He notes that the Greek words "Parthenos" and "Pantera" share only the initial P.

### Perpetual virginity and its origins
- Tabor argues that the doctrine of perpetual virginity comes from ignoring Matthew 1:25 ("knew her not until she had brought forth a firstborn son"), and from Matthew 13:55, which names the brothers James, Joseph, Jude, and Simon, and sisters.
- He links it to later Hellenistic dualism, which describes holiness in ascetic terms, and to Augustine's account in the *Confessions* of sleeping without blankets and eating simple food.
- He describes church fathers who said negative things about women, and says Mary was then removed from her Jewishness, her children, her sexuality, and her motherhood, to be "above the world."
- Bond raises the Protoevangelium of James (Anna and Joachim) and Joseph's status in the Latin tradition. Tabor notes the Protoevangelium says Mary was "intact" at Jesus's birth.

### The size of the family
- Bond observes that the Gospels name five brothers and several sisters, and that Tabor suggests three sisters, for eight children. Tabor says that eight children was not unusual in the 19th and early 20th century, but eight living children in first-century Galilee would be exceptional.
- He says the names of the brothers are given right after Mary, which he takes as evidence they are her sons. He admits the question is one of the things "we just don't know."

### The Assumption, the death of Mary, and the tomb
- Tabor relies on Stephen Shoemaker's work on Mary devotion. He reports a traditional death on Mount Zion, under the Dormition Abbey, and a tomb at the foot of the Mount of Olives that is empty.
- He says the traditional date of death (49, as he calls it) "seems to be just pulled out of a hat," and that Mary could have lived long enough to see James die.
- He argues the Assumption is very late, arising in a climate where her decay and body were seen as unfitting for the vessel of the Son of God.
- He rejects the tradition that John took Mary to Ephesus. He says it rests on late visions of a nun, and that Irenaeus's name for John of Ephesus is a confusion with "John the Elder," not John son of Zebedee.

### James as the beloved disciple and successor
- Tabor argues that the beloved disciple in John is James, and that James succeeded Jesus in leading the movement. He says the Galatians order (James, Peter, John) supports James's role and that John son of Zebedee's New Testament reputation does not fit the beloved disciple.
- He reads the "passed over" pattern in Jewish succession to explain why Mary is not presented as taking over the movement.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mary had no children besides Jesus / perpetual virginity was always the belief.** Tabor says Matthew 1:25 and 13:55 point to Joseph and Mary living together and having children, and that perpetual virginity developed from later ascetic ideals.
- **Mary's bodily life was ideal and free of human functions.** Tabor says later church writers described her as never using the toilet and eating perfectly balanced food, a view he attributes to a removal of her from the earth.
- **Immaculate conception and virgin birth are the same thing.** Bond notes that students at Notre Dame often confused them. Tabor explains that the Immaculate Conception concerns Mary being born without original sin.
- **Mary was taken to Ephesus by John son of Zebedee.** Tabor says this has little early support, the earliest reference is Irenaeus, and the Ephesus house tradition rests on late visions.
- **Mary's Assumption has ancient roots.** Tabor argues it is very late, and reflects an aversion to her decay and body.
- **The Greek *parthenos* explains the Panthera tradition.** Tabor rejects this because it appears only in the 19th century.
- **Luke's genealogy is simply Joseph's.** Tabor argues it may be Mary's, while acknowledging that scholars reject this.
