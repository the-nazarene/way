## Summary

John Batchelor's CBS "Eye on the World" interview with James D. Tabor, about Tabor's book *The Lost Mary*, asks who the historical Mary, mother of Jesus, was, and why the New Testament and later church tradition say so little about her. Tabor argues that Mary was a Jewish woman from the Sepphoris area who married Joseph, a builder, and bore a large family of which Jesus was not the firstborn. He presents the Protoevangelium of James, the perpetual-virginity tradition, and the later Latin and Eastern explanations of Jesus's "brothers" as attempts to move Mary away from ordinary Jewish family life. He reads the Talpiot tomb and the James ossuary as possible evidence for the Jesus family, and he says Mary likely died in Jerusalem before 70 CE.

Most of the interview is Tabor narrating his reconstruction: Sepphoris as Mary's birthplace, the "Pantera" name and the Roman soldier tradition, John the Baptist's connection to a cave in the hill country, James's leadership after Jesus, the Jerusalem house on Mount Zion, and the "Q" sayings shared by Matthew and Luke. Tabor often states these as his own readings ("I think," "I would argue"), and he says that he is presenting history, not theology.

The interview ends with Batchelor recapping the book's subtitle and his own sign-off. Tabor's last substantive points are that Mary's teaching is reflected in the sayings attributed to Jesus, John, and James, and that the book's closing chapter presents "Mary's message then and now." He admits he cannot prove that Mary shaped her children's teaching.

## Key Teachings

### Sepphoris, Joseph the builder, and Mary's home region
Tabor says that Mary was born in Sepphoris, the Roman-era urban capital of Galilee, and that Nazareth was a small village within walking distance of it (he says about 45 minutes). He points to Josephus calling Sepphoris "the jewel of all the Galilee," to its destruction around 4 BCE after Herod the Great's death, and to the crucifixions that followed the revolt of Judas son of Hezekiah. He argues that Joseph was a *tekton*, which he renders as a builder or probably a stonemason rather than a carpenter, and he connects this to Jesus's building images and to the rebuilding of Sepphoris during Jesus's youth. He also links the "city set on a hill" saying (Matthew 5:14) to Sepphoris, and he links Mary's parents, Joachim and Anna, to a wealthy landed household in that city. Tabor presents the wealth claim as an argument from the Protoevangelium's content, which he says a second-century writer would be unlikely to invent, given the later idea of the poverty of Jesus's family.

### "Jesus son of Pantera" and the Roman soldier tradition
Tabor says rabbis in Sepphoris in the early second century called Jesus "Yeshua ben Pantera" (Jesus son of Pantera). He describes Eleazar as a rabbi who spoke positively of Jesus's teaching. Later, the critic Celsus is reported through Origen as spreading a story that Mary was seduced by a Roman soldier named Pantera. Epiphanius, by Tabor's account, treats Pantera as a family name in Joseph's line. Tabor then presents a Latin tombstone found at Bingerbrück (Julius Abdes Pantera, from Tyre/Sidon) and suggests the soldier could have been a Jewish man conscripted into the Roman army. He insists he does not claim Joseph or the father of Jesus was a Roman soldier, and says the man was more likely a young Jewish relative who was later conscripted.

### The Protoevangelium of James and the perpetual virginity of Mary
Tabor summarizes the Protoevangelium of James: Mary is raised in the Jerusalem temple, becomes pregnant, is physically examined, and is found intact. He argues that the tradition that Mary remained a perpetual virgin, with Joseph as an old widower and the brothers as his children or cousins, is a later theological development that began in the second through fifth centuries and then took over. He reads Matthew 1:25 ("he knew her not until she had brought forth her firstborn son") as implying normal marital relations afterward, and says the siblings of Jesus are Mary's children. He says that "many Catholic theologians" and historians would agree, while the Latin church later made the brothers stepchildren or cousins. His stated aim in the book is to "give her back to her people" as a Jewish mother of seven or eight children, with Joseph dying early (he is not mentioned after Jesus is 12).

### John the Baptist, the hill country, and a cave
Tabor says Mary fled to the hill country of Judea to Elizabeth, as Luke 1:39 describes. Working from the chronology, he argues that Mary was about three months pregnant when John the Baptist was born, so she may have been present at John's birth. He describes excavating, with the late James Strange, a cave in the valley he calls Anaram, which he associates with the cave in the Protoevangelium of James. He reports wall art from the later Christian period showing John's head on a platter, a sword, and three crosses, and a water reservoir for baptism. He calls it a cave "of legend at least." He also says the Baptist is best called "the dipper," and he distinguishes him from the Baptist church.

### James the brother as successor and the Jerusalem movement
Tabor argues that James, not the fisherman John, is the disciple to whom Jesus entrusted his mother on the cross, and that the tradition identifying the Beloved Disciple with John the fisherman does not begin until Irenaeus in 180. He cites Gospel of Thomas saying 14 (from the Nag Hammadi library, discovered 1946), where the disciples are told to go to "James the just," and Acts 15, where a James speaks last at the Jerusalem meeting about Gentiles. He cites Galatians 2 for Paul calling James "the brother of the Lord" and one of the so-called "pillars." He reads Paul's remark about "so-called pillars" as a sign of tension between the Jerusalem group and Paul's own visionary authority. He says James led until 62 CE, when he was stoned and beaten to death by the same priestly family that had Jesus killed.

### The Mount Zion house, Mary's death, and the flight to Pella
Tabor describes the Upper Room site on Mount Zion, which he says is built over a first-century foundation and was once marked as the "church of the apostles" in Byzantine-era mosaics. He describes an excavated wealthy courtyard house he has dug for over ten years, which he thinks may have served as the headquarters of the movement, and he imagines Paul visiting James there, with Mary greeting visitors. He says the Armenians revere James more than Peter. Tabor says the group fled Jerusalem under Simon, who he argues succeeded James, and that Simon went across the Jordan to the region of Pella. He ties this to Revelation 12 (the woman, the son taken to heaven, and the remnant who flee into the wilderness). He argues Mary did not go with them, that the New Testament's last reference to her is Acts 1 (with the brothers at Pentecost), and that she likely died in Jerusalem before 70 CE, with the Dormition tradition on Mount Zion reflecting the place of her death. He says Simon was later crucified under Trajan, at over one hundred years old, which he presents as from a later source.

### The Talpiot tomb, the ossuaries, and the James ossuary
Tabor describes the 1980 discovery of the Talpiot tomb south of Jerusalem, with ossuary inscriptions including Jesus son of Joseph, Maria, Mariamne, Matthew, Judah son of Jesus, and Yose. He stresses the nickname "Yose," which occurs in Mark 6:3 and on only one other ossuary, and he argues that Joseph (the brother) is missing because he died early. He describes a tenth ossuary that disappeared and a later ossuary, the James ossuary, which appeared in 2002 with the inscription "James son of Joseph, brother of Jesus." He reports a seven-year trial over whether that inscription was forged and says the owner was acquitted, but he presents the evidence as in dispute. He says DNA testing is being planned for the ossuaries, and that a 2005 test on one ossuary (Antigonus, the Hasmonean king) produced only maternal DNA, with male Y-chromosome testing being more difficult. He says tests have been done on two of the Talpiot individuals, Mary and Jesus, and that mitochondrial DNA would be used to test sibling relationships. He argues the tomb does not disprove resurrection, citing 1 Corinthians 15 on "spiritual body" and saying a bodily resurrection in the sense of a resuscitated corpse is not what Paul describes.

### Q, the sayings, and "Christianity before Paul"
Tabor says Matthew and Luke share about 50 sayings of Jesus, not in Mark, which German scholars in the 1830s to 1850s extracted as a separate source (Q). He argues Q is an external source, probably earlier than or contemporary with Mark, and resembles a compendium of teaching rather than narrative. He compares the Sermon on the Mount material and says six or eight Q-type sayings appear in the book of James (for example, the injunction not to swear). He attributes some Q-type sayings to John the Baptist, such as sharing coats and food, and says the teaching of John, Jesus, and James is continuous. He says the five figures he has spent his career recovering are Mary, Mary Magdalene, John the Baptist, Jesus, and James, and he calls their teaching "Christianity before Paul." He presents Mary as the probable source of these teachings, while acknowledging that he cannot prove it.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mary was a perpetual virgin, and Jesus's brothers and sisters were cousins or stepchildren.** Tabor argues that Matthew 1:25 and the normal Jewish family context make Mary the mother of the siblings, and that the perpetual-virginity tradition is a later development.
- **Mary was from Nazareth, a poor village.** Tabor argues that Mary's family lived in or near Sepphoris, the urban capital, and that the Protoevangelium's wealth details are evidence for a landed family.
- **John the fisherman is the Beloved Disciple.** Tabor says the identification begins with Irenaeus in 180 and that Jesus would more plausibly have entrusted his mother to James, a blood brother.
- **Jesus's father was a Roman soldier.** Tabor says he does not hold this. He presents the Pantera tradition as a rumor reported by Origen, and he suggests that a Jewish relative named Pantera may have been later conscripted.
- **Mary went to Ephesus or traveled widely.** Tabor says the later traditions placing her in many locations are late, and that her death is best located in Jerusalem.
- **Finding the Jesus family tomb would disprove the resurrection.** Tabor argues that Paul's account of a spiritual body does not require the bones to remain, so the ossuary evidence would not disprove resurrection as a belief.
