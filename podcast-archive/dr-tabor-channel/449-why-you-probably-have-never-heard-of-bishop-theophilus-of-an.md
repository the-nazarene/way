## Summary

Tabor asks what kind of Christianity Theophilus, sixth bishop of Antioch, actually defended in his only surviving work, the *Ad Autolycum* addressed to a friend named Autolycus, which he dates to around 180 CE. He frames the lecture as a sample from his non-credit online course "Christianity Before Paul," and he presents it as a case study in "what sometimes is called Jewish Christianity," a stream he says largely disappeared as Christianity developed in the second through fourth centuries.

His answer, which he offers as his reading rather than settled fact, is that Theophilus's defense reads much closer to Hellenistic Judaism than to the Pauline gospel. His evidence includes: Theophilus's emphasis on the creator God and on turning to God through repentance and obedience, with no mention of redemption through Christ's blood (a point he attributes to Robert M. Grant's unpublished commentary); a passage on the Ten Commandments and a "negative golden rule"; the use of the word "synagogue" for the church, which Tabor says the standard translation wrongly renders as "holy churches"; and a baptism passage that he links to John the Baptist's message of repentance. He also draws parallels to the speech attributed to Paul in Acts 17 and to Romans 1–2, which he argues are themselves largely Hellenistic-Jewish in content, and to the book of James.

The talk ends mid-lecture, around 49:08, while Tabor is working through Theophilus's quotation of the Ten Commandments and noting the one Sabbath reference. He closes the discussion of the sections he has covered by urging viewers to read the whole work and to sign up for the course.

## Key Teachings

### Repentance, obedience, and turning to the creator God
Tabor's central question is "How do you get right with God?" He reads Theophilus as offering a path of turning from sin toward the one creator God. Using the metaphor of a mirror clouded by rust and eyes covered by cataracts, Theophilus tells Autolycus that sin blinds the soul and that the "physician" who heals is God, "who heals and makes alive through his word and wisdom." Tabor notes that Theophilus does not mention redemption through Christ, quoting Grant: "Theopilus does not mention redemption through Christ but he mentions redemption all the way through getting saved." Tabor adds that this silence may relate to Theophilus's doctrine that salvation comes through "repentance and obedience," and he sets it against John the Baptist's call to repent and be baptized.

### Parallels to Acts 17 and Romans 1–2
Tabor compares Theophilus to the Areopagus speech in Acts 17, which he says contains God as creator who "doesn't live in shrines made by man," the quoted Greek poets, and only a final mention of Jesus at Acts 17:31. He argues Theophilus's style and content are similar: most of the work "could be read in an ancient synagogue or a modern synagogue." On Romans, Tabor says Romans 1:18 introduces "the wrath of God," but in Romans 2 Paul presents a universal standard in which those who do good receive glory and peace "the Jew first and also the Greek." Tabor says students read Romans 2 and assume it is Paul, but "It's not Paul. It's Henistic Judaism." Romans 3, he says, returns to the Pauline claim that "There's nobody righteous."

### "Synagogue" and the book of James
Tabor argues that Theophilus uses the word "synagogue" for the church, which Grant's translation brings out, while older standard translations render it as "holy churches." Tabor calls that rendering "inexcusable," since in 180 CE "synagogue" would signal Jews to readers. He ties this to James 2, where the poor man is put at the back of the "synagogue," and says Theophilus's imagery of harbors and rocky islands for churches and heresies is "very similar to the book of James." He also notes that Theophilus cites no gospels, no New Testament writings, and no quotations from Jesus or Paul.

### Baptism, water, and regeneration
Discussing Theophilus's reading of Genesis 1 on the fifth day, when creatures from the waters were blessed, Tabor says Theophilus links this to "receive repentance and remission of sins through the water and the labor of regeneration." Tabor compares the basin to a mikvah and calls it "Baptism for repentance and remission of sins," which he says is "what John preached." He notes the Greek term *palingenesia* as used in Titus 3, and cites Acts 2:38 as the baptism formula he grew up with. He admits uncertainty about whether Theophilus means Christian baptism in the later sense, since "It's not like the dedicay" (his caption rendering of the Didache), and he leaves the question open.

### Being "anointed" and the name Christian
Tabor reads Theophilus's wordplay on *Christos* ("anointed") as a claim that Christians are so named because they are "anointed with the oil of God," a claim he says would have been treated lightly in a late-second-century setting where persecution was a real danger. He suggests this may refer to a baptismal ceremony rather than literal oil, and he links it to James 5:14 (anointing with oil for the sick) and to unnamed baptismal liturgies. He admits, "I don't know what he means" by Theophilus's comparison to a castle or ship.

### The Torah, the Ten Commandments, and the golden rule
Tabor argues that Theophilus's ethics rest on the Torah's commands and on a universal moral sense. Theophilus, as Tabor reads him, says the Gentiles "show what the law requires is written on their hearts," and he quotes the negative golden rule in its Jewish form ("whatever a man would not wish to be done to himself, he should not do it another"). Tabor says the Christian version is the positive "golden rule," and he calls the Jewish form the "silver" or "platinum rule," because it "covers more than the positive." Tabor also cites the Torah command "Don't trip a blind man" as an example of moral knowledge that everyone already has.

### Resurrection and immortality
Tabor reads Theophilus as preaching resurrection in a form he says James likely shared: the one who obtains resurrection will be "above angels" and "won't be physical beings marrying, giving in marriage" (citing Luke 20). Tabor notes Theophilus's language of "eyes of the soul" and says the human being is "by nature mortal and immortal," which he calls Platonic. He says immortality is not guaranteed until the resurrection, "Otherwise, you perish."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Theophilus is just another apologist like Justin Martyr.** Tabor says he is "lumped" with the second-century apologists but is "so different," and that his defense does not present the standard Christian gospel.
- **Theophilus's gospel is salvation through the blood of Christ.** Tabor argues Theophilus describes salvation through repentance, obedience, and turning to the creator God, with no mention of redemption through Christ.
- **Acts 17 and Romans 2 express Paul's theology.** Tabor says students read these as Paul, but in his view they reflect Hellenistic Judaism. He adds that he doesn't know whether Paul actually said the Acts 17 speech.
- **"Synagogue" in Theophilus means assembly, so "holy churches" is an acceptable translation.** Tabor says the translation is inexcusable because in 180 CE the word would signal Jews to readers.
- **The golden rule is a Christian teaching.** Tabor says the negative form is Jewish, found in the Didache and rabbinic sources, and that the positive form is the one often called "golden."
