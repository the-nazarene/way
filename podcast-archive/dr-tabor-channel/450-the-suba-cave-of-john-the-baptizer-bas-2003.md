## Summary

Tabor, a historian of early Christianity, gives a illustrated dig report on the Suba cave near Ein Karem, west of Jerusalem, which he and Shimon Gibson excavated starting in spring 2000. The question is whether the cave has a real connection to John the Baptist. Tabor frames it in two parts: first, he reads Luke 1 and 3 as a historian's account that gives family, place and chronology clues about John's family and early life; second, he walks through the cave's finds. These include a carved figure with a staff, a raised hand and a tunic, which Gibson reads as John; a plastered reservoir with steps; and a stratigraphy of Byzantine material over about two meters of early Roman material, including thousands of broken one-handled water jugs. Tabor argues that the finds show repeated ritual activity in the first century, and he suggests John or his followers as the likeliest group behind it, while stating that he cannot prove this.

Tabor is careful about the limits of the evidence. He says the drawings date to the Byzantine period, not the first century, and he presents the John identification and the ritual explanation as hypotheses. He reports that technical consultant Egon Loss would not link the finds to John, and he criticizes press coverage that set him against Gibson.

The talk ends, mid-sentence in the captions, with Tabor's account of Jesus and John's relationship. He draws on John 3 and 4 and Mark 1 to suggest that Jesus baptized in the Judean countryside in the same period, then withdrew to Galilee when the Pharisees took notice.

## Key Teachings

### Luke as a historian's source for John's family and early life
Tabor says Luke "is not like" Mark: he calls Luke "the plotting historian" who gives a year marker (the 14th year of Tiberius, which Tabor dates loosely to 26 or 27 CE) and family details. He reads Luke 1 as placing Mary's visit to Elizabeth in a Judean town in the hill country, with Mary staying about three months. Tabor notes that Elizabeth is called Mary's "kinswoman," which he renders as a close relative. He points to the Levitical names in Luke's genealogy of Mary (Luke 3) as evidence of a mixed Judahite and priestly family, since Elizabeth and Zechariah are priests. He also treats Luke's line that John was "in the desolate places" (Luke 1:80) as describing a rural, solitary life rather than a Essene upbringing. Tabor says this is a "nice speculation" he does not accept. Here he presents the "lost years" of Jesus and John as a pattern of urban trade for Jesus and rural solitude for John.

### The Suba cave: discovery, layout and stratigraphy
Tabor recounts that Gibson phoned him in December 1999 asking whether his university would sponsor an excavation. Gibson had found drawings on the cave walls that a local kibbutz had long ignored. The cave is roughly 70 feet long and 12 to 15 feet across, with plastered walls and a natural ceiling. Tabor argues that the cave is a water installation with a plastered surface, and that the finds are not the usual silt-fill of a reservoir. He says the excavation found an archaeologically stratified sequence: modern wash, Turkish and Ottoman layers, a half meter of Byzantine material, and then about two meters of early Roman (Herodian) material. Tabor quotes Egon Loss calling the cave "the most unusual site that has ever been found in Israel in terms of stratification," and says Loss had dug over a hundred caves without seeing anything similar.

### The drawings and the identification of John
Tabor says the figure on the left wall, with a raised hand, a staff and a tunic marked by many small hack marks, is the central piece of the case for John. He argues the tunic marks suggest a hair garment, which he links to John's Nazirite-like ascetic image. He says the figure's eyes have been defaced, probably in a later Islamic period. Other figures include an arm and a cross (which he dates to around the fourth century), a head, and three crosses on a hill. Tabor and Gibson disagree about the head and the banner: Gibson reads a banner on a post, while Tabor wonders whether it is a sword on a platter. Tabor says the carved relic niche helps date the drawings to a range from the late Byzantine through Crusader periods. He admits this is disappointing, since he had hoped for first- or second-century Christian art.

### The broken jugs, the foot basin and the ritual reading
Tabor describes roughly 250,000 sherds from the cave, mostly small one-handled water jugs, which were not cooking vessels and show signs of having been smashed forcefully. He says Loss counted a minimum of 43 of these jugs in one meter of floor nine in the early Roman layers, and that the activity spans about 150 years, from the Herodian period to about 150 CE. The cave shows no evidence of habitation, cooking or sleeping. Tabor reads a carved foot basin in the early Roman layer as a possible ritual foot-washing or anointing step, and cites the Damascus Document's language about setting one's foot "on the path of righteousness" as a speculative parallel to pre-baptismal ritual. He compares the practice with the Mandaeans, whom he describes as a conservative group still practicing baptism in Iraq and Iran, and says their books date to the fourth century.

### Jesus and John: baptism in the Judean countryside
Tabor argues from John 3 that Jesus was baptizing in the Judean countryside while John was at Aenon near Salim, north of the area he calls the Jordan's Judean side. He describes the springs of the region as sites where large-scale baptism could happen, and he says the cave could fit that setting, though he says it is unproven that Jesus knew it. He reads John 4:1-2 as the Pharisees learning that Jesus was baptizing more disciples than John. He reads Mark 1 as the "pickup" of the fishermen after a summer, fall and winter of joint baptizing, which he dates to 26 or 27 CE, a sabbatical year.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That the drawings prove John the Baptist was in the cave.** Tabor says he cannot prove it, and he says the AP wire story wrongly set him against Gibson by juxtaposing "can you prove" with a quote from Gibson, without any of the evidence or pottery argument.
- **That the cave is a mikvah.** Tabor rejects the mikvah reading (Ronnie Reich's view) because the structure is about 70 feet long and is a water reservoir cut in the Iron Age.
- **That the drawings are first-century art.** Tabor says the drawings are Byzantine, with a relic niche helping to date them to the late Byzantine through Crusader periods. The first-century layer is the pottery beneath, not the art.
- **That a Nazirite is the same as a Nazarene.** Tabor says the "Nazirite" is a vow-taker who does not cut the hair, not the same as a "Nazarene." He draws on a Nazirite he knew in Israel.
- **That Jesus was about thirty when baptized, and that this is a settled fact.** Tabor says we have been "conditioned" to assume the age, and he jokes that Jesus may have been older.
