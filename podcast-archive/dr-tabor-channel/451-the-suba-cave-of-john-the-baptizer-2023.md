## Summary

Tabor asks where Jesus and John the Baptizer actually baptized, and whether a little-known cave west of Jerusalem could have been one of those places. He argues from three kinds of evidence read side by side: the Gospels of Mark and John, the Q sayings source (as preserved in Luke and Matthew), and the physical remains of the cave itself. He places John in the Jordan valley in the north at Aenon near Salim, Jesus in Judea in the south (citing John 3:22), and says the hill country west of Jerusalem, near Ein Karem, had the water needed for large-scale baptism.

The main evidence is the Suba cave, excavated by Tabor and Shimon Gibson with UNC Charlotte students in 2000 and continued through the following years. Tabor describes a plastered, water-fed Iron Age reservoir with steps and a Byzantine-era John image on the wall. The first-century layer, roughly two meters of fill, yielded thousands of fragments of small juglets, a footprint-shaped anointing basin, and no sign of habitation. Tabor reads this as a ritual baptism site and attributes the cave's later Byzantine medallions and the infancy-gospel story of Elizabeth's flight to the same local tradition. He is candid that he "cannot prove" the cave was where Jesus baptized.

The talk ends with a pointer to the forthcoming Israel Exploration Society volume (he says 2024), to Gibson's book on the cave and Tabor's *The Jesus Dynasty*, and to Tabor's blog, where he argues for reading Mark together with the narrative portions of John.

### Key Teachings

### John as more than a forerunner
Tabor argues the usual picture of John as a brief figure who "points the way to Jesus" understates the sources. He cites the Q passage in Luke 7 and Matthew 11, where Jesus asks what the crowd went out to see, "A prophet maybe," and answers that John is "more than a prophet." Tabor links this to Malachi, the last prophet in the Hebrew Bible, and to Isaiah 40:3 ("preparing the way in the wilderness"), which he reads as John inaugurating the messianic last days.

### The "greatest born of women" saying as a later gloss
Tabor reads the Q saying "among those born of women there's none greater than John" as originally a plain statement that was softened by a later editor who did not want John to rank above Jesus. He points to the Hebrew Matthew (Even Bohan), published by George Howard, as preserving some original readings of Q, though he says he will not go into those details here.

### Chronology and geography of the joint baptizing campaign
Tabor takes John 3:22 as evidence that "Jesus and John the Baptizer teamed up." In his reconstruction, Jesus is baptized in the fall of 26 CE, John is arrested early in 27 CE, and John baptizes at Aenon near Salim in the north, where there was abundant spring water. Jesus baptizes in Judea. Tabor adds that some of Jesus's core disciples, later among the twelve, were probably former disciples of John. He uses Mark 1:9 and 1:14 to show how much time Mark's narrative skips between the baptism and Jesus's preaching in Galilee.

### The Suba cave as a ritual baptism site
Tabor describes the cave's stratigraphy. Early Roman material (from Herod the Great down to the Bar Kokhba revolt, which he calls "first and very early 2nd century") sits in a layer of roughly two meters. He identifies this as "the time of John the Baptist and Jesus." He points to:
- thousands of fragments of small juglets, which he argues were not used for carrying water;
- a footprint-shaped rock basin with an inclined channel, which he reads as an anointing installation for the right foot, possibly linked to a dedication ritual before immersion;
- deliberately broken vessels and steps leading down to the water;
- flotation samples and the absence of cooking or eating remains, so the site was not inhabited.

He notes that a spring and a shaft fed the pool with rainwater, which he says was considered clean for ritual purposes. He admits the function of the foot basin is uncertain ("we still don't understand exactly what it is").

### The John image, the medallions and the Proto-Evangelium of James
The wall carving of John (raised hand, staff, hair in a bun, which Tabor links to Nazirite practice) is Byzantine, not first century. Its face was damaged, possibly by later rival groups. Tabor links the cave to clay medallions showing Elizabeth fleeing with the infant John and a soldier grabbing her shawl. He connects these to the Proto-Evangelium of James, which he dates to the late second or early third century, where Elizabeth flees to the "mountain of God" and the mountain opens to receive her. Tabor presents this as later local tradition about the cave, not as historical evidence for the first century.

### Method: reading Mark with the narrative of John
Tabor argues it "makes good historical sense in terms of method" to read Mark as the earliest account while including the narrative portions of John, such as geography, travel and chronology, while setting aside the high-christological discourses. He says John's Passion account comes from a witness he calls "the disciple whom Jesus loved," whom he thinks is James the brother of Jesus, though he calls that "another topic." He suggests that the John material is not likely to have been invented.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **John is only a forerunner who fades once Jesus appears.** Tabor argues the sources give John his own role and that Q presents him as "more than a prophet."
- **The traditional Jordan baptism site is the only plausible one.** Tabor proposes a site in the hill country west of Jerusalem and argues the Gospels place baptizing in Judea and at Aenon.
- **The "none greater than John" saying is original.** Tabor argues it was softened by a later editor.
