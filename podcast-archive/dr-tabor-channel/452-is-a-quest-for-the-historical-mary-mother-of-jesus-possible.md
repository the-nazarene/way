## Summary

The host opens by asking whether a historical Mary can be recovered at all, and what the world she lived in was like. Tabor's answer, drawn from his new book *The Lost Mary*, is that a partial picture is possible if one assembles small "threads" from early and late sources and tests them against archaeology. ## Key Teachings

### 1. Earliest sources: Paul, Mark, and the silence of John

Tabor says the earliest text about Mary does not name her. Paul's letter to the Galatians describes Jesus as "born of a woman, born under the Torah," which for Tabor establishes Jesus as flesh and blood and not a phantom or pre-existent heavenly being. Paul also calls Jesus "of the seed of David according to the flesh" (Romans 1:3, which Tabor gives with some hesitation). Paul also mentions "James, the Lord's brother." Mark 6:3 is the first Gospel to name Mary and to list the brothers James, Joseph ("Jose"), Jude, and Simon. Tabor treats Mark as probably the earliest Gospel but insists that earliest does not mean most authentic, since each Gospel has its own theology. He also notes that John refers to "his mother" many times but never names her, a detail readers rarely notice because they supply the name from memory.

### 2. Herod's death, the Messianic context, and Daniel 9

Tabor places Jesus' birth around 5 BCE on his reckoning, with Herod the Great's death c. 4 BCE. He reads the period through the Roman installation of Herod in 40s BCE-era politics and the expectation of a Davidic Messiah. The key text is Daniel 9's 70 weeks, which he explains as 490 years arranged in jubilee cycles. He says the Dead Sea Scroll community reckoned timing from this same framework and that its predicted date passed without the expected event, after which its teacher died and a period of about 40 years was anticipated. Tabor sees a parallel in Mark 1:15 ("The time is fulfilled, the kingdom of God is at hand") and in Mark 13's prediction of Jerusalem's destruction roughly 40 years after Jesus' death. He also cites Habakkuk 2 ("If it tarries, wait for it") as a verse that such groups used to explain delay, and he notes that Paul quoted the same Habakkuk verse in a different sense.

### 3. The turbulent year and the Sepphoris revolt

Drawing on Josephus, Tabor describes three messianic claimants who rose up in the summer and fall after Herod's death: Judas son of Hezekiah in the north (who sacked the royal armory at Sepphoris), Simon of Perea, and a shepherd king in Judea. The Syrian legate Varus, Tabor says, brought three legions to suppress them. Tabor argues that Nazareth, about four miles from Sepphoris, would have had the burning city visible from it. He says he has excavated first-century levels at Sepphoris and found destruction layers "apparently from this period." He reads Josephus's report of about 2,000 crucifixions along the roads as the background for his chapter title. He imagines Mary, young and nursing, hiding while Messianic claimants were slaughtered, and he uses the Protoevangelium of James as a text he rejects for most purposes, while noting that some details in legendary texts may still contain usable material.

### 4. Luke 3 as Mary's genealogy

Tabor argues that Luke 3's genealogy is not a parallel to Matthew 1's line of Joseph but a separate Davidic lineage, which he suggests may be Mary's. He points out that it skips Solomon and runs through Nathan, a son of David by Bathsheba, and includes Levitical-sounding names such as Levi and Matthat, and a Janni that he connects to the Hasmonean king Alexander Jannaeus. He cites Richard Bauckham's view that the list is too unlike a harmonizing invention to be dismissed. He suggests the author of Luke-Acts may have drawn on family records kept by the Desposyni, the relatives of Jesus. He links the name "Eli" in the text to "Eliakim" (the name of a royal king), and proposes that "Eliakim" or "Joachim" may be Mary's father, with "as was supposed" marking Joseph as the legal but not biological father. Tabor notes that Matthew's genealogy names four women, each with an irregular sexual history, which he reads as a hint toward the story of Mary's conception.

### 5. The paternity question and "Yeshua ben Panthera"

Tabor lays out three possibilities for Jesus' biological father: Joseph (which he says most people assume, regardless of the infancy stories); an unnamed man from a family situation Tabor does not specify; or a Roman soldier, a possibility he says has been repeatedly attributed to him but which he says he never argued. He reports that the rabbinic Tosefta preserves sayings of Rabbi Eleazar, set in Sepphoris, that cite "Yeshua ben Panthera," and that Eleazar quotes this figure approvingly on the use of money from prostitution for building toilets. Tabor argues that the name's use in these earliest sources is not pejorative. He says the "panther" wordplay on *parthenos* (virgin) is a later explanation, that his graduate student traced its first proposal to a 19th-century German scholar, and that the church fathers themselves explained "Pantera" as a clan name. He points to the late second-century philosopher Celsus, reported through Origen's *Contra Celsum*, who describes a Jewish source telling a story about a Roman soldier named Pantera. Tabor references Chris Seeman's article on the Pantera tombstone of a Sidonian soldier on the Rhine and says Seeman showed the soldier's cohort was not in Sidon at the right time, though a young Jewish man might still have been conscripted into the army. Tabor says he corrected some mistakes in *The Jesus Dynasty* after Seeman's critique. He ends by saying that if he had to fill out a birth certificate he would write "Joseph?"

### 6. James as successor and the Jerusalem leadership

Tabor argues that Jesus' successor was James, not Peter. He cites Acts 1, where Mary and the brothers are present at the choosing of a replacement for Judas, and Acts 15, where James gives the decision on the Gentile question with "halakhic" authority. He cites the Gospel of Thomas, where disciples fear becoming orphans and are told to go to "James the Just, my brother," and Eusebius, who says Peter and John did not strive for the chief place and chose James. He recommends John Painter's *Just James*. He cites Hegesippus, a second-century Jewish convert from the Levant, who says James wore the priestly miter, and the Gospel of the Hebrews, where Jesus delivers "the garment of the priest" to James after the resurrection. Tabor says these details suggest a priestly element in James's identity and that Mary may have been connected to priestly and royal lines through Zechariah and Elizabeth's family and John the Baptist.

### 7. The unnamed beloved disciple as James

Tabor argues that the unnamed "beloved disciple" in John 18-20 is James, not John son of Zebedee. He says John son of Zebedee does not fit the "beloved" portrait. He cites Charlesworth's *The Beloved Disciple*, which argues for Thomas after about 500 pages, and Tabor says he does not find Thomas convincing either. He thinks the material in John 18-20 may draw on a Jamesian tradition distinct from the earlier "red-letter" sayings material, and that some details in it match Jerusalem archaeology. He links this to the Last Supper room and to the "upper room" tradition on Mount Zion, where he says the Church of Holy Mary of Mount Zion was built in the 4th century on a first-century house foundation that Shimon Gibson has been excavating for about ten years. He also mentions David Clausen's book *The Cenacle*.

### 8. Mary's later life and the question of her death

Tabor pictures Mary as a matriarch of the movement in Jerusalem, perhaps visiting figures such as Paul, while James led. He notes the New Testament's Gospels and Acts say little about her, and he reads this as the family fading from the narrative. He stresses that he does not know when she died, that people in the period could live into their 50s, 60s and 70s, and that large families of seven or eight children could arise over about 20 years of marriage because a child was born roughly every two years. He acknowledges that "there's a lot of could bes, maybes," and asks whether the picture is "concocted out of my own imagination." His answer is that it is built from combing through the available evidence.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That the nativity was peaceful, a "Silent Night" scene.** Tabor says the years around Jesus' birth were terrifying, with crucifixions, legion movements, and the burning of Sepphoris, and that the picture is "not Silent Night."
- **That "Pantera" is a pun on *parthenos* (virgin) from the beginning.** Tabor says the earliest rabbinic sources use "Yeshua ben Panthera" as a name in a non-pejorative way; the pun appears to have been proposed by a 19th-century German scholar, and the church fathers explained Pantera as a clan name.
- **That Jesus' father was a Roman soldier.** Tabor says he has never argued this, and that he considers Joseph most likely, with other possibilities bracketed.
- **That Luke 3 and Matthew 1 are parallel genealogies harmonized by fundamentalists.** Tabor says Luke 3 traces a different Davidic line, possibly Mary's, and that it should not be dismissed as invented names.
- **That people in antiquity were old at 40 and dead by 50.** Tabor says people could live into their 50s, 60s, and 70s, and that large families were possible because children were born every two years or so.
- **That Paul's "though he was rich" (2 Corinthians 8:9-type language) refers to a pre-existent divine Jesus.** Tabor argues Paul's Adam Christology in Philippians 2 describes humility and exaltation, not pre-existence, and that the "poor" language may point to giving up possessions for the movement.
- **That Joseph of Arimathea is a fictional invention (Ehrman's view).** Tabor disagrees and proposes that a family member of Jesus or a sympathetic figure facilitated a temporary burial; he presents this as a different reading of Mark and John.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught.** Tabor reads Paul's early letters as describing a flesh-and-blood Jesus and an Adam Christology (Philippians 2) that he does not think includes pre-existence, and he says later letters (Ephesians, Colossians) move toward a pre-existent divine Jesus. He sets Paul's "gospel" against an earlier movement of the family and followers, and the course he advertises is about that earlier movement.
- **James and the Jerusalem community.** This is the talk's most extended thread: James as successor (Acts 1, Acts 15, Gospel of Thomas, Eusebius), the priestly miter in Hegesippus, the Gospel of the Hebrews' garment, the beloved disciple as James, and the Mount Zion house church.
- **Nazarenes, Ebionites, Essenes.** Tabor cites "Ebionite materials" and the Gospel of the Hebrews, and uses the Dead Sea Scroll community's timetable as a comparison for apocalyptic expectation. He does not analyze the Nazarene or Ebionite identity in depth.
- **The Kingdom.** Tabor reads Mark 1:15 and the Daniel 9 timetable as expressing messianic and apocalyptic expectation; he describes the John-the-Baptist-era movement as egalitarian and communal, with Jesus calling followers to leave possessions (Mark 10).
- **Who Jesus thought he was.** Tabor's Davidic and Hasmonean-priestly framing, the Pantera and Eleazar discussion, and the messianic claimants of 4 BCE are all presented as part of how Jesus would have been understood by his contemporaries and family, though he does not claim Jesus' own self-understanding is recoverable.
- **How the Gospels and canon were formed.** Tabor discusses John's silence about Mary, Matthew's four women, the Protoevangelium's legend and its later influence, the Lukan genealogy as possibly drawn from family records, and the Mark-to-John trajectory.
