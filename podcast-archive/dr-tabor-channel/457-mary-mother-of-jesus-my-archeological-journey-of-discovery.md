## Summary

Tabor asks what can be known historically about Mary, whom he calls Miriam, once the Christianized image is set aside. He says the New Testament gives her only about ten scenes, mostly the birth and the cross, so he has to "fill in the blanks" using context: geography, archaeology, and the family references scattered through the Gospels and Acts. He states his aim as rediscovering "the Jewish woman she was in her own time," and says he sets theology aside.

His central claim is that Mary was from Sepphoris, the Galilean capital, not Nazareth, and that she grew up in an urban, possibly literate, household. He dates the Roman destruction of Sepphoris to about 4 BC, which he says is the date he uses in his book, and imagines Mary watching the city burn. He argues that Mark's references to Jesus's family, the Gospel of John's silence on her name, and Acts 15 point to a large Jewish family in which James took over leadership after Jesus. He supports the picture with his excavations at Sepphoris and at the Upper Room complex on Mount Zion, and with a critique of later art and doctrine (the Vatican statues, the perpetual virginity tradition). Tabor presents these as his reading and imaginative reconstruction. He concludes with an image of Mary at the center of a Pentecost scene and argues that she was the matriarch and inspiration of the movement, a view he develops in his book *Lost Mary*. ## Key Teachings

### Mary was from Sepphoris, not Nazareth
Tabor says Nazareth was a small village of perhaps a hundred people, while Sepphoris was the Galilee's urban capital and the largest city after Jerusalem. He cites Josephus describing Sepphoris as "the ornament or jewel of the Galilee," surrounded by 242 villages. He points to a later Byzantine tradition that Mary was born there to Joachim and Anna, and to Crusader-era and earlier pilgrim identification of a house of Mary in the city. He says a first-century domestic house was found beneath the Byzantine layers there. He also notes the Hebrew inscriptions he saw nearby and says the place is not easily excavated.

### An urban, possibly educated girl and the lost years
Tabor argues that growing up in a busy trade city, on roads leading to Egypt, Damascus, the coast and the Jordan, would have exposed Mary to commerce and foreign cultures. He asks whether she could have been educated, citing the image of Anna teaching a girl, though he admits the image is an artwork and not evidence. He reads Jesus's youth as an apprenticeship with Joseph as a *tekton*, a builder or stonemason, working in Sepphoris about 45 to 50 minutes' walk from Nazareth. He presents this as a replacement for speculative "lost years" in India or Egypt. He has an artist, Balage (name as transcribed), draw a reconstruction of Sepphoris from excavation reports.

### The destruction of Sepphoris and the Davidic-messiah context
Tabor dates the Roman destruction of Sepphoris, after Herod's death, to about 4 BC, and says Mary would have been nursing a child of about one year at the time. He links this to Judas son of Hezekiah's revolt, which he distinguishes from Judas the Galilean in 6 CE. He recounts an anecdote from an interview with John Dominic Crossan about the Sepphoris burning being a formative memory. He speculates that the trauma may have shaped Mary's sense of her sons, who are of Davidic descent, against armed revolt. He notes Josephus's list of about twelve messianic claimants, all killed, and says one of them urged not taking up the sword while still "inaugurating the kingdom of God." He frames this as his own imaginative suggestion about Mary's vision, not a documented fact.

### The family in Mark, and the rejection scene reread
Tabor cites Mark 6:3 as the Gospel's earliest reference to the family: the "tekton," son of Mary, brother of James, Joseph, Judas and Simon, with sisters present. He takes this as at least five sons and two or more daughters, and notes traditional names for three daughters, so seven or eight children. He reads Mark 3:31-35 (Jesus asking who his mother and brothers are) not as a rejection of family but as an inclusive statement in a crowded house, with the family left outside. He says this reading is not original to him.

### Matthew, Acts 15, and James's leadership
Tabor argues that John's Gospel never names Mary, so the "earthly family" dissolves in that Gospel. He reads Matthew 1:25 ("knew her not until she'd borne a son") as implying later children of Mary and Joseph, and treats the perpetual virginity tradition as a later development, citing the *Protoevangelium of James* (which he dates to the early second century) and Marcion's rejection of marriage. He says Jesus handed his mother's care to James, following the Jewish custom that the eldest surviving son bears this role. He argues that James, not John, should be called the beloved disciple, pointing to Irenaeus (around 180) as the earliest reference to John in that role. He notes that James presides over the Jerusalem council of Acts 15, dictates the letter to the congregations, and is named by Paul as one of the pillars (Peter, James, John), and that James's leadership is the more plausible reading.

### Archaeology at Capernaum and the Upper Room
At Capernaum, Tabor says the "house of Peter" more likely belonged to the Zebedee family, since Peter and Andrew were from Bethsaida. He describes a first-century house church beneath the later octagonal building and synagogue, and suggests Jesus and the family used it as a base of operations. On Mount Zion, he describes his work since 2008 with Shimon Gibson and Rafi Lewis, and a first-century building below the Crusader church of the Upper Room (Cenacle) and David's tomb. He reports Herodian stones reused in later construction and argues the house is the early headquarters of the movement, which Bargil Pixner had proposed in a 1990 *Bible Archaeology Review* article. He notes that the Byzantine church had a neighboring building preserved as "Mary's house," which he says is documented in the literature.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mary was only a birth-and-death figure with no life of her own.** Tabor argues that the context of her world (Sepphoris, trade, possible education) and the family references can reconstruct more of her life.
- **Mary was an illiterate peasant from Nazareth.** He argues she was from Sepphoris, an urban capital, and that she may have been educated or at least exposed to education.
- **Jesus's lost years were spent in India or Egypt.** Tabor argues for an apprenticeship as a *tekton* with Joseph near Sepphoris instead.
- **Mark 3:31-35 shows Jesus rejecting his family.** He reads it as an inclusive statement in a crowded house.
- **Mary was perpetually a virgin with no other children.** He reads Matthew 1:25 as implying other children and calls the perpetual virginity view a later development.
- **John, son of Zebedee, was the beloved disciple and the leader of the movement.** He argues for James as the beloved disciple and the leader of the Jerusalem community, citing Acts 15.
- **Art depicting Peter and Paul at the Vatican reflects the early movement's leadership.** Tabor notes James is absent from that art and argues he deserves a place next to Mary.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor's central argument is that James succeeded Jesus as leader, presiding over Acts 15 and receiving the care of Mary, and that the tradition has underplayed him.
