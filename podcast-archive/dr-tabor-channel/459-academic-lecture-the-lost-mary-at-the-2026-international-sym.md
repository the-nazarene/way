## Summary

Tabor opens with an autobiographical account of his interest in touching the past and then sets out his question: what can be recovered about Mary, the mother of Jesus, as a historical figure rather than a theological one? He presents this as the aim of his book *The Lost Mary*, which he says took about ten years to write. He admits that the gospel material on Mary is thin ("there are only 10 scenes"), and argues that the way to write a historical Mary is to supply context: the politics, geography and archaeology of Galilee and Jerusalem in her lifetime. He says he did not "make up things" but "tried to contextualize things so that we could say much more" (12:48).

His main lines of evidence are archaeological sites and Josephus. He discusses the Sepphoris region (a Byzantine church of St. Anne on Sepphoris's western slope, built over a first-century house, and literary references placing Mary's birth there, which he treats as uncertain). He recounts the revolt after Herod's death, the crucifixions Josephus reports (about 2,000 in the Galilee), and the danger of festival pilgrimages to Jerusalem at Pentecost. He reconstructs a large Jewish family of seven or more children, with Mary at the centre of household life. He proposes that the Capernaum house traditionally called Peter's was a Jesus "headquarters" and reads the crowded-house episode as a sign of family hospitality rather than family rejection, following John Painter. On the family's later history, he argues that Jesus handed his mother's care to his brother James at the cross, that James was the leading figure of the Jerusalem community (Acts 15), and that Mary's place in Acts 1 and the Upper Room tradition shows her in the Jerusalem group. He acknowledges that the John-as-caretaker tradition is common and that its earliest explicit reference he gives is Irenaeus around 180, so he presents his reading as a proposal with "variables."

The talk ends as a lecture closing rather than a formal conclusion. Tabor turns to Mary as a teacher of basic moral lessons (including "turn the other cheek"), urges the audience to tell their daughters how important women were, and closes with a Robert Fulghum reference. The recording ends with applause.

### Key Teachings

### Method: memory, context and imagination
Tabor's approach is to combine the sparse texts with what a Galilean Jewish family would have lived through. He compares the memory of events (his father and World War II, the Kennedy assassination) to the claim that the gospels were written decades later, and argues that people can remember a great deal across such spans. He is explicit that some of his reconstruction is imagined ("if I were writing a novel about Mary...") and that the book is designed to make readers feel present in the events. His own cue is "Where are we now?", a question he attributes to Shimon Gibson.

### Sepphoris, St. Anne and the question of Mary's birthplace
Tabor describes the Byzantine church of St. Anne on Sepphoris's western slope, built over a Roman-period domestic structure, which some early pilgrims identified as Mary's house. He notes that literary references say Mary was born in Sepphoris. He hedges repeatedly ("if Mary's from Sephus and maybe she isn't, we still know a lot about Sephris"), so the claim is a suggestion, not a settled finding. He also points out that Sepphoris was four miles from Nazareth and was the largest city in the Galilee, so the claim that Jesus's family would have known it is a matter of geography.

### The revolts of 4 BCE and Pentecost
He reconstructs the period after Herod's death from Josephus: the revolt in the north led by Judas son of Hezekiah, the burning of Sepphoris, and about 2,000 crucifixions. He imagines the family hiding in a cistern in Nazareth during these events. He also argues that the Pentecost festival after Herod's death would have brought Galilean families to Jerusalem at a dangerous moment, when Roman troops had to put down riots, and asks whether Mary and John the Baptist were present. He stresses that these are reasoned possibilities based on what Jewish families did, not reports from the sources.

### Capernaum and the "Jesus headquarters"
Tabor reads the crowded-house episode through John Painter's interpretation: Jesus's mother and brothers are at home, the house is full, and Jesus says the people around him are family. He reads this as a picture of Mary, not as a rejection. He describes the Capernaum house traditionally called Peter's, with first-century layers, and proposes it as a strategic base from which Jesus sent out groups, though he presents this as "I'm going to call this the Jesus headquarters," not as a claim from the archaeology.

### Mary's household and the family of Jesus
He assumes that Mary was the household's centre ("the hand that rocks the cradle runs the home") and that Jesus took charge of the family after Joseph disappears from the record, with the only Joseph-related story being the twelve-year-old episode in Luke. He lists the brothers (James, Joseph, Jude and Simon, plus sisters) and says Mary had a large family, with the "fading" of the family in later tradition, including the perpetual-virginity tradition.

### James as head of the family and the Jerusalem community
Tabor argues that Jesus handed the care of his mother to his brother James at the cross, not to the beloved disciple. He acknowledges that most readers identify the beloved disciple with John and that the earliest explicit reference he gives is Irenaeus around 180. He argues that Mary's presence in the Jerusalem community and Acts 15, where James gives the decision, fit a family in which James was the leading figure. He also cites Paul's report of an appearance to James and later Ebionite sources that place James at the Last Supper, with a candid note that the sources are in tension.

### Virginity and later doctrine
He contrasts Matthew 1:25 ("knew her not until she had borne a son") with the medieval and Lutheran view of Mary as perpetually virgin, and with the Protevangelium's account of Jesus' birth. He presents this as the point where, in his view, the discussion passes from history to theology.
