## Summary

Host Sam Tideman interviews James D. Tabor about his book *The Lost Mary: Rediscovering the Mother of Jesus*. Tabor's opening question is why a historian would focus on Mary, whom he calls "the best known, least known woman in human history." He argues that she is largely absent from the Gospels and that her role has been reduced over time, and he presents his book as a "quest for the historical Mary" parallel to the quest for the historical Jesus. His method is contextualization: placing Mary in her actual place and time, especially in Galilee, and reconstructing her life from Josephus, the Gospels, the genealogies, and archaeology at Sepphoris.

Tabor's main claims include: Mary was born in Sepphoris, not Nazareth; the family was Davidic and also priestly; Luke's genealogy in chapter 3 is Mary's and was drawn from family records; the Matthew and Luke virgin birth accounts are not simply reconcilable with each other; and James, the brother of Jesus, is the "beloved disciple" of John's Gospel and took Mary into his home. He attributes the fading of Mary to a later theology that devalued sexuality, marriage, and the earthly human side of Jesus. Tabor presents these as his own readings and says several are contested by most of his colleagues, and he is candid that some of his conclusions are speculative (for example, the wider family memory and the house arrangements).

The interview ends on a lighter note. Tideman and Tabor discuss how memories of historical events can persist across generations (Tabor compares the Irish rebellion of 1798 and the Kennedy assassination). Tabor says he would most like to visit the Nazareth household on a Sabbath to hear Mary, James, and Joseph. He also says Mary should be placed back at the center of the Pentecost scene and at the center of the early movement, and he calls her "the godmother of Christianity" in the discussion with Tideman.

## Key Teachings

### Method: contextualization and the "lost years"
Tabor says he treats Mary as a historical figure to be placed "in her place, in her time," asking where she was born, where she grew up, and what the best evidence is. He notes that Mary is the first character in the story of the movement, present at the birth of John the Baptist (Luke 1). He cites Luke's chronology: Mary, three months pregnant, stays with Elizabeth about three months, and he suggests she is present at John's birth. He describes filling the "missing years" between Mary's appearance in Luke and Jesus at about 30 by reconstructing a plausible household. He stresses that the New Testament has "a certain theological and doctrinal take" and is not doing what he is doing. He acknowledges he has about 10 to 12 Mary scenes to work from and that this is "very difficult" to write a book from.

### Sepphoris, not Nazareth
Tabor argues Mary was born in Sepphoris, the urban capital of Galilee, about four miles north of Nazareth, which he calls a suburb of Yafia, "the largest town." He says Josephus describes Sepphoris this way, and that the New Testament never mentions it. He ties the book to his own excavation work there in the 1990s and to an archaeology slideshow on his YouTube channel. The key event he uses is the destruction of Sepphoris after Herod the Great died in 4 BC, when revolts broke out. He describes Judas son of Hezekiah seizing the armory at Sepphoris and the Romans burning and destroying the city, then Herod Antipas rebuilding it during Jesus' childhood. He reports that the burn layers can still be found, and that a charred piece of wood can still smell after about 2,000 years. He also places Mary's parents, whom he names Joachim and Anna, in Sepphoris, where they owned land, and suggests Joseph was a *tekton* who was more likely a builder or stonemason than a carpenter. Tabor presents the family as living through this destruction, and he uses the imagined scenes of Mary hiding in a Nazareth cistern as Roman troops passed to make the period vivid. He labels these as imaginative reconstructions.

### Genealogies: Matthew vs. Luke, and Mary's line
Tabor argues the two genealogies cannot be harmonized. Matthew traces Joseph's line through Jacob and names four women (Tamar, Rahab, Bathsheba described as the wife of Uriah, and Ruth), whom he calls a possible "wink" at irregular sexual stories. He argues that Luke's genealogy (Luke 3) is Mary's, not Joseph's: the phrase "as was supposed of Joseph" and the name "Eli" suggest a different father for Joseph in the list, and Eli and Joachim can be the same name. He says this reading is supported by names that are priestly and Davidic. He identifies a son of David named Nathan (not the prophet) in a chart on page 103 of his book, linked to a third son of Bathsheba, and cites Zechariah's reference to the "family of David" and "the family of Nathan" as evidence that this lineage was known in the late Second Temple period. He credits Richard Bauckham (named in the transcript as "Richard Balcom") for the argument that families known for careful genealogies were Davidic. He describes Mary as "doubly royal," Davidic and priestly, and argues that women could transmit lineage, citing Hasmonean women such as Alexandra Salome. He mentions the Hebrew word *zera* (seed) and the Genesis "seed of the woman," and argues ancient Near Eastern and Hebrew thought recognized the woman's contribution. Tabor says that when colleagues call him a harmonizer, he answers that he is recognizing "irreconcilable" differences.

### The virgin birth accounts
Tabor distinguishes Matthew from Luke. In Matthew, Joseph is betrothed and has a dream that the child is "of the Holy Spirit," and Matthew 1:25 says he did not know her "until" she had borne a son, which Tabor reads as implying a later ordinary marriage. In Luke 1, Mary asks "How can this be?" and is told the Holy Spirit will come upon her. Tabor says he gives three options in his book's chapter "Mary's Secret": no human father (the usual evangelical reading), Joseph as the father (what he thinks most academic colleagues would say), or another man. He names a figure called Pantera who he presents as a relative of the family, not a Roman soldier. He compares Greco-Roman traditions of divinely begotten heroes and emperors and says he does not know Luke's worldview. He says the Ebionite and Nazarene traditions placed the divine sonship at the baptism rather than the birth, and that Paul's "born of a woman" (Galatians 4:4) and Jesus' "born of women" saying about John (Matthew 11:11) use an ordinary idiom, not a claim about a special birth.

### James as the central figure and "beloved disciple"
Tabor ranks James above Peter as a central leader of the Jerusalem movement, citing Acts 12 (James the fisherman is executed by Herod Agrippa, so the later James must be someone else), Acts 15 (the Jerusalem meeting, where a James speaks), and Acts 21 (James directs Paul to show he observes Torah). He says Paul calls James one of the "pillars" in Galatians, and that Paul is sarcastic about them. He notes that Luke's listing of Jesus' brothers is omitted even though Mark lists them. He then argues that James, not John the son of Zebedee, is the beloved disciple. He says the tradition that John is the beloved disciple goes back to Irenaeus around 180, and he argues that a firstborn son would be expected to hand his widowed mother's care to a brother. He cites the detailed high-priest material in John 18-20 as coming from a circle of tradition, which he links to James's priestly family ties. He identifies a house on Mount Zion, traditionally called "Mary's house" and "the house of John," which he proposes to call "the house of James." He also cites Hegesippus, an early second-century Jewish Christian, for James entering the sanctuary. Tabor's conclusion is that the "throne" James inherits in the church fathers reflects this succession, and that "brother" should be read literally, against later readings of cousins or stepbrothers.

### Why Mary was "lost": theology and celibacy
In his closing argument, Tabor argues that Mary faded in the tradition because of a theology hostile to sexuality, marriage, and ordinary human life, and an exaltation of celibacy. He calls this an "aversion to this world" that infiltrated the ancient world and even affected the Hebrew narratives in the New Testament. He says that Mary lost her religion (he says she was a Jew who would have gone to Mount Zion and the Temple area), her family, her womanhood, and her marriage, which he says was not consummated under the celibate view. He also says the Dead Sea Scrolls do not say the community was celibate, and that Jewish culture did not treat celibacy as a norm. He describes Mary as the one who taught Jesus, and says the phrase "the hand that rocks the cradle" applies.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mary is named and central in John's Gospel.** Tabor says the Gospel of John never names her and refers only to "his mother," which he calls her "fading away."
- **John the son of Zebedee is the beloved disciple and author of the Gospel.** Tabor argues that James the brother of Jesus is the beloved disciple, and that the John identification comes from Irenaeus around 180.
- **Matthew and Luke's genealogies both trace Joseph.** Tabor argues Luke's is Mary's line and that the two genealogies are irreconcilable.
- **Jesus' "brothers" must be cousins or stepbrothers.** Tabor reads "brother" literally, arguing the later Catholic and Eastern readings are driven by theology.
- **Mary was not Jewish or was only a vessel.** Tabor says she was a Jewish woman tied to Jerusalem and Mount Zion, with her own family, lineage, and role as teacher.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor quotes Paul's "pillars" remark in Galatians and his Galatians 4:4 "born of a woman" as evidence for an ordinary human birth, and contrasts Paul's stance with James in Acts 15 and 21.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Central to the talk. Tabor argues James is the leader after Jesus, the beloved disciple, and the heir to Mary's care, and he cites Acts 15 and 21, Hegesippus, and the house on Mount Zion.
- **The Nazarenes/Ebionites:** Tabor cites their Hebrew gospels as placing divine sonship at the baptism, not the birth, and as treating Joseph as father.
- **Gospels and canon:** Tabor's treatment of Mark as earliest, Luke's source access, John's sources, and the dating of the beloved disciple tradition to Irenaeus all bear on how the Gospels were formed.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor discusses "son of God" as a royal and Davidic title ("you are my son") and says the Ebionite and Nazarene traditions locate it at the baptism.
