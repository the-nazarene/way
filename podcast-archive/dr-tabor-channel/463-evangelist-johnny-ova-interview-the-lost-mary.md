## Summary

Host Johnny Ova interviews James Tabor about his book *The Lost Mary: Rediscovering the Mother of Jesus* (Knopf, 2025). The opening question is why Mary, whom Tabor calls "the best known least known woman in history," has been so thoroughly erased from the historical record. Tabor argues that the erasure has two sources: sparse information about her, and a theological tendency to make her a figure of the mother of God rather than a woman with a life. He says the family of Jesus fades from the New Testament, with John the Baptist exiting early and James, Jesus' brother, quietly taking over the Jerusalem movement after Jesus' death. He supports this with close readings of Acts 1 and Acts 15, Galatians 2, Mark 6:3 and Matthew 1:25, and with the claim that Paul, who "never met Jesus," represents a different relationship to Jesus than the family did.

Much of the interview moves to historical setting. Tabor argues that Jesus grew up near Sepphoris, a major urban center about four miles from Nazareth, and that the city's destruction by Roman troops in 4 BCE would have been witnessed by a nursing Mary. He describes Jesus' family as a normal Jewish household with several children, reads Isaiah 11 as the background to Mary's and Jesus' hopes, and argues for a form of messianic, non-violent subversion rather than armed revolt. He presents Mary as a formative teacher of her sons.

The interview ends with the chapter "Mary's Secret," which asks who Jesus' biological father was. Tabor says that most scholars would simply answer Joseph. He traces the name "Yeshua ben Pantera" through Jewish sources, Celsus, and the church fathers, and says the name may refer to a family clan rather than to a biological father. He presents this as a light thesis, not a settled conclusion, and the discussion closes there.

## Key Teachings

### Mary as an erased figure, and the family as a fading background
Tabor argues that the New Testament gives Mary only about a dozen mentions, that John never names her, and that she appears at the birth and at the cross but has no visible life in between. He contrasts this with the Apostles' Creed and Paul's "I have made a decision to know nothing among you but Jesus Christ and him crucified," arguing that the creed and Paul focus on Jesus' entrance and exit points and leave Mary's life unexamined. He says the family fades: John the Baptist is introduced and then imprisoned and beheaded, and James is hidden in Acts 1 under "his mother Mary and his brothers."

### James as leader of the Jerusalem movement
Tabor reads Acts 15 as showing James, not Peter, making the decisive ruling. He says Peter speaks, Paul speaks, and then James stands and says "My decision is," which Tabor reads as James deciding for the whole movement, including Paul's churches. He reads Galatians 2 as Paul's testy reference to "the pillars of the church" (James, Peter, and John), and says that in context Paul is asserting that his gospel stands on its own. Tabor says the Gentile ruling asked only that Gentiles turn from idolatry and keep sexual morality, not that they convert to Judaism. He mentions the Letter of James, noting that some scholars doubt James wrote it because of its polished Greek, and says Tabor thinks James's authorship is open, citing Josephus's practice of having Aramaic work translated into Greek. Tabor also notes Luther's remark that he doubted "any gospel" in the letter.

### Paul's distance from Jesus and "no longer know Christ after the flesh"
Tabor reads 2 Corinthians as Paul saying "We no longer know Christ after the flesh," and argues that Paul never met Jesus and was not part of the inner circle. He suggests that Paul, who was in Jerusalem during the Passover week and was "an enemy of this movement," had no access to Jesus' teaching in the way the family and apostles did. Tabor says Paul's appeal to a heavenly Christ, through visions and revelations, is different from the authority of those who knew Jesus in life.

### Naming the brothers and the beloved disciple
Tabor argues that Mark 6:3, naming "James, Joseph, Jude, and Simon" and sisters, is the earliest list, and that Matthew and Luke follow Mark. He argues that the beloved disciple at the cross, not named in John, is most plausibly James, since he is the eldest son and his mother is a widow. He says the tradition identifying the beloved disciple as John is late, citing Irenaeus around 180, and points out that Mary and the brothers are present at Pentecost (Acts 2).

### The family of Jesus as a normal Jewish household
Tabor reads Matthew 1:25 ("he knew her not until she had brought forth Jesus the firstborn") as implying that Joseph and Mary consummated the marriage afterward, and argues that "until" implies a later relationship. He rejects the perpetual virginity tradition he associates with the Eastern and Western churches and the view that the brothers were Joseph's children from a prior marriage or Clophas's children. He draws on his family history of children born about every two years to suggest that a large sibling group was normal. He reads Mark 3:31-35, where Jesus says "All of you are my mother and brothers," as positive rather than dismissive, and cites John Painter's *Just James* for a similar reading.

### Sepphoris, urban Galilee, and the Roman destruction of 4 BCE
Tabor argues that the usual image of Nazareth as a remote village is wrong. He says Sepphoris, the Galilee's largest urban center and capital, lay about four miles north, and that Jesus grew up in its orbit. He describes the revolt of Judas son of Hezekiah after Herod's death, the burning of Sepphoris by Roman legions around 4 BCE, and the likelihood that a nursing Mary saw the fires. He says the word *tekton* should be read as builder or stonemason rather than carpenter, and that Jesus' family may have worked in Sepphoris. He cites Josephus's counts of 250 Galilean villages and the description of Yafa as the largest town in the Galilee. He reports that he excavated at Sepphoris over three seasons.

### Isaiah 11, non-violent messianic subversion, and the Kingdom
Tabor argues that Mary and Jesus read Isaiah 11, with its "stump of Jesse" and "branch" (the sense of "Nazareth" as "branch town"), as the hope for a restored Davidic king. He reads its promise to regather the twelve tribes, to set rulers over them, and to include the Gentiles as political, not merely spiritual. He cites Luke 8 for Susanna, Joanna, and Mary Magdalene among supporting women, and Luke 10 for the seventy-two sent out in pairs. He presents the case for non-violent resistance as subversion rather than pacifism: Rome will not be overthrown by military force but by a social order where "male and female, bond and free, rich and poor are all together."

### Mary as teacher and matriarch
Tabor argues that Mary taught Jesus the basic ethics that children learn early, and that her influence is part of how the movement began. He frames her as the "matriarch of this whole movement" and as a teacher whose influence is reflected in James's letter. He presents this as one possibility among several readings, not as proven.

### Mary's secret: Joseph, another man, or no father
Tabor sets out three possibilities for Jesus' father: no father, a god, or a human father. He says he feels obligated to trace the human options: Joseph, or another man. He notes that many scholars assume Joseph and read the virgin birth as a way of honoring Mary. He traces "Yeshua ben Pantera" in Jewish sources from Sepphoris in the second century, in rabbinic stories about Rabbi Eliezer and a sage called Yeshua ben Pantera (where the stories are positive), and in Celsus around 180 to 190, who spun the Pantera name into a slur. He cites church fathers who explained the name as a Joseph family name, and notes John 8:41 ("We weren't born of fornication") as a possible hint of rumor. He says that "Pantera" may refer to a clan name and that his thesis is a "light" one.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mary is barely mentioned and has no life of her own.** Tabor argues she has a life and that breadcrumbs in the texts can be followed to build a fuller picture.
- **James was a minor figure.** Tabor argues that James took over leadership of the Jerusalem movement at Jesus' death and made the decisive ruling in Acts 15.
- **The beloved disciple was John.** Tabor argues it was James, since the John identification is a late tradition.
- **Jesus' brothers were stepchildren or cousins, and Mary was a perpetual virgin.** Tabor argues Matthew 1:25 and Mark 6:3 indicate Mary and Joseph had children together in a normal Jewish marriage.
- **Jesus was from a tiny village and was a carpenter.** Tabor argues that Nazareth was close to the urban center of Sepphoris and that *tekton* is better rendered builder or stonemason.
- **Romans were constantly brutalizing villagers in Galilee.** Tabor says Roman forces were mostly stationed at Damascus and worried about the Parthians, though they struck hard when trouble broke out.
- **"Yeshua ben Pantera" is a negative slur about Mary's rape by a Roman soldier.** Tabor argues the Jewish rabbinic sources treat the name positively, and that the slur story belongs to Celsus, around 180–190.
- **Jesus' family were jealous or opposed to him.** Tabor reads Mark 3 as showing Jesus welcoming his mother and brothers, not rejecting them.
