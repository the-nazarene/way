## Summary

Tabor asks what the early Jesus movement looked like if we take the Gospels seriously as descriptions of people around Jesus, rather than the familiar film image of Jesus and twelve men walking through landscapes. He argues that the movement was a mobile entourage of men, women, and children, and that women were central to it: they financed it, taught in it, and were its most reliable witnesses at the cross and tomb. He gives an estimate that women made up "25% female disciples and financial backers" and says that adding children would push the figure higher. He labels this a guess.

His evidence is mostly from the Gospels. He cites Mark's account of Jesus's mother and brothers, Mark's "hundredfold" saying, Luke 8 (women funding the movement, with Joanna, Susanna, and Mary Magdalene named), Luke 10 (the seventy sent out, and Mary sitting at Jesus's feet while Martha serves), and John 11 (Martha's confession). He ties these to Mary Magdalene, to Mary the mother of Jesus (the subject of his book *The Lost Mary*), and to James, whom he presents as the leader of the movement after Jesus's death. He also draws on Acts 15, the letter of James, Paul's list of pillars, and the Gospel of Thomas.

The talk ends with a call to like, subscribe, and read his book and blog posts. Tabor concludes that the Christian church later "repudiated" the band of sisters, and that only recently has this been "redressed." Throughout, he presents his reconstruction as an imaginative reading of the texts, and he says he is summarizing a case he develops fully elsewhere.

## Key Teachings

### The "lone twelve" picture is wrong, and the movement was an entourage
Tabor contrasts popular film images of Jesus walking with "just the 13 guys" with the Gospels' picture of a "roving campaign, a Jesus entourage, with men and women and children who've left everything." He pictures tents, pack animals, and nightly camps, and says the twelve were leaders within a larger body of "other students." He reads Mark 3 and Mark 10 as evidence that the family of faith expanded beyond blood ties.

### Jesus broke with Jewish purity and gender norms
Tabor describes first-century Jewish society as patriarchal, with arranged marriages, women confined to the domestic sphere, and women barred from forming a synagogue quorum (a point he says is later in time but "part of the first century tradition based on some of our earliest rabbinic texts"). He presents Jesus as a "Jesus Reformation" figure who relaxed Levitical purity rules (he points to Mark 7 and to Jesus touching lepers and the sick), and who let women travel with him and learn, which made women eligible to become disciples.

### Women financed the movement
Tabor reads Luke 8 as saying that the Galilean movement was sustained by women: "these women and many others, and the many others is feminine in the Greek." He names Joanna, wife of Herod Antipas's chief of staff (a detail he links to Luke's Joanna and to Herod's interest in Jesus), Susanna, and Mary Magdalene. He treats Mary and Martha's Bethany house as a female-led "safe harbor" and hub. He also notes Luke 10's seventy sent two by two as a team of about thirty-five, pointing out that the Greek allows couples.

### Jesus prioritized female education
Tabor's central cultural claim is that in Luke 10 Jesus accepted Mary sitting "at the rabbi's feet" rather than serving, which he calls "unheard of" and "a huge cultural shift." He says Jesus "pioneered" co-educational discipleship, and compares it to the later history of all-male colleges. He also says Martha confesses Jesus as Messiah in John 11, so women held a theological role.

### Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of Jesus, and the family
Tabor calls Mary Magdalene "right at the top" of the movement, with inner-circle status, financial support, and leadership. He argues that Jesus's mother Mary was "the matriarch of the whole movement" and treats "You are my mother and brothers too" as an open-house statement, not a rejection of family. He draws on the Gospel of Philip's reference to three Marys who followed the Lord (his mother, Mary Magdalene, and "his sister"), and suggests the family had at least two sisters. He notes that Mary, wife of Clopas, is at the cross.

### The women witnessed the cross and tomb, and James succeeded Jesus
Tabor argues that the women stayed when the men fled (he reads Mark's "they all forsook him and fled" as the climax), and that they were the witnesses to the crucifixion, burial, and empty tomb. He argues that Jesus gave his mother into the care of "the beloved disciple," whom he identifies as James the Less (pointing to a separate blog post), and that James then led the movement. He cites a sighting of Jesus to James, which he places in Galilee "according again to the Gospel of Mark," and says James took over leadership, "very few people know that." He presents Acts 15 as showing James announcing the decision to accept Gentiles, and the letter of James as non-Pauline and close to the Sermon on the Mount. He says Paul's list of pillars puts James first, then Peter, then John.

### Paul did not invent the mixed community
Tabor quotes Paul's claim that there is "no male, no female, no rich, no poor, no slave, no free" in the messianic group, and argues that this principle "has to come right from Jesus." He cites Acts for women leaders named Lydia, Phoebe, and Prisca.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Jesus movement was just Jesus and twelve men walking together.** Tabor argues the Gospels describe a large, mixed entourage of men, women, and children, with women financing and leading.
- **Jesus rejected his mother and brothers in Mark 3.** Tabor reads the saying as an invitation to the household, not a rejection of family.
- **Women could not be disciples in Jesus's time.** He argues Jesus broke with the culture by letting women travel and learn, and that he prioritized Mary's learning over Martha's service.
- **Peter took over the movement after Jesus died.** Tabor argues James was the leader, "admitted in all of our sources," and that Peter's leadership is a later assumption.
- **The women's testimony was unimportant.** Tabor argues the women's witness at the cross and tomb was crucial, and that the Gospels preserve an "unbroken historical chain" through them.
- **The mixed-gender community came from Paul.** Tabor argues Paul's "no male, no female" principle "has to come right from Jesus."
