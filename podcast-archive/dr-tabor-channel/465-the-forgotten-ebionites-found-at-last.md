## Summary

Derek Lambert opens by asking who the Ebionites were, since he had never heard of them. Tabor carries nearly the whole talk, which doubles as an overview of his 16-lesson "Christianity Before Paul" course. He presents the Ebionites as the late form of a "stream" running from the Dead Sea Scroll community, through John the Baptist, Jesus and James (the "MJJ" movement, with Mary and Mary Magdalene included), and on into later Jewish-Christian groups. He says the Ebionite label is hard to recover because nearly every source is hostile, and he uses a checklist of recurring traits (Torah-observance, rejection of Paul, anti-sacrifice, a Matthew-only gospel, a "poor ones" self-designation) to trace the group.

His main evidence includes Irenaeus (about 180 CE), who is the first to name the Ebionites and says they used only Matthew and rejected Paul; Epiphanius's description of the Nazarenes; Hegesippus as quoted by Eusebius, which Tabor uses for the succession of James and then Simon son of Clopas; and the Didache, which he treats as a "quasi-Ebionite" document. He also argues that the flight to Pella was real and that the group crossed the Jordan, using John 10:40, 1 Kings 17 and a map he calls his "Jesus hideout in Jordan." He follows the movement east and south, citing a 250 CE martyr named Conan, a 318 meeting with Pope Sylvester, the Odes of Solomon, the Pseudo-Clementines, and similarities to early Islam. Tabor offers these as a plausible reconstruction and frequently says he is "triangulating" from scattered "puzzle pieces," not reporting settled history.

The talk ends with Tabor summarizing the previous week's live session, which ran 2 hours and 45 minutes, and pointing viewers to the course.

## Key Teachings

### The "MJJ" movement and the Dead Sea Scroll stream
Tabor says the course is about a "Jesus movement" of Mary the mother, John the Baptist, Jesus, Mary Magdalene and James, and Paul, with the Ebionites as a later sect that picked it up. He argues that the Dead Sea Scroll community, which he says he would rather describe from the scrolls themselves than from Josephus, Philo or Pliny, shares traits with the Ebionites: apocalypticism, a prophet "like Moses," two Messiahs (priestly and Davidic), baptism, sacred meals, a council of twelve and three, a temple "not made with hands," spiritual sacrifices, a prohibition on divorce, and the self-title "the poorer ones." He says that no other Jewish group he knows of matches all of these except the John-Jesus-James movement.

### The Nazarenes in Epiphanius
Tabor draws on Epiphanius's chapter 18, which describes a pre-Jesus Jewish group he calls "Nazareans." He says they practiced daily purification, were Transjordanian, kept circumcision, Sabbath and festivals, and held that the Torah had been corrupted by priests who "sowed tares." They were vegetarian and rejected sacrifice, citing Jeremiah 7. He treats them as a distinct earlier group that is related to the Dead Sea movement and to the later Ebionites.

### Irenaeus and the earliest description of the Ebionites
Tabor calls Irenaeus (around 180 CE, in *Against Heresies*) the first to mention the Ebionites. Irenaeus says they used only the Gospel according to Matthew, repudiated Paul as an apostate from the law, read the prophets "in a somewhat singular manner" (his example is Isaiah 2 read literally), and adored Jerusalem. Tabor says Irenaeus does not distinguish Ebionites from Nazarenes, and that the later fourth- and fifth-century split into two groups is a development. He also says that some Ebionites denied the virgin birth, and that Origen and Jerome preserve material on them.

### Succession and the desposyni
Tabor argues that Jesus' brother James led the Jerusalem community. After James's death in 62 (killed, he says, through Annas son of Annas), Hegesippus, via Eusebius, names Simon son of Clopas, whom Tabor takes to be a cousin or brother of Jesus. He describes the Ebionites' self-designation as *desposyni* ("kinsmen of the Lord") and says they kept genealogies, which Herod the Great had partly destroyed at Sepphoris. He says Eusebius lists fifteen bishops of Jerusalem down to the Bar Kokhba revolt and suggests they may have been a council of twelve with three leaders, paralleling the Qumran structure. He notes that Vespasian hunted down the Davidides, that Domitian arrested grandsons of Jude, and that Trajan crucified Simon around 110.

### The flight to Pella and the move across the Jordan
Tabor accepts Eusebius's report that the community fled to Pella before 70, and he defends the tradition against scholars who see it as legend. He locates the "Brook Cherith" of 1 Kings 17 near Pella, ties it to John's baptizing at Aenon near Salim (John 3:22), and reads John 10:40 as Jesus withdrawing across the Jordan. He says some returned to Mount Zion after the war, where Bargil Pixner's "Church of the Apostles" lies over a first-century house, and that Mary died on Mount Zion rather than at Ephesus, which he calls a nineteenth-century idea. He also says the group later moved to Batanaea and to the Arava, and he reads "Arabia" in these texts as the Arava rift valley, not Saudi Arabia.

### Beliefs and later trajectory
Tabor describes the Ebionite Christology as a prophet-like Moses figure who received the Spirit at baptism and was not God, with "one God and his Messiah" and no confusion of the two. He reads the Didache 16 as apocalyptic and as containing a pre-Christian version of Revelation, and he links the group's end-time expectation to Daniel 9 and 11. He says the Didache's Eucharist uses bread and wine without any blood reference. He then traces the stream into the Elkesaites, the Mandaeans (whom he links to John the Baptist and to James McGrath's work), the Odes of Solomon (Ode 36), and the Pseudo-Clementines, which he says became more Gnostic. He ends with the claim that Jews or Judeo-Christians in Medina and groups described by Benjamin of Tudela share Ebionite traits, and that Muhammad's teaching on one God, Jesus as messiah but not God, dietary laws, and prayer toward Jerusalem parallels them.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That the Ebionites were named after a founder called Ebion.** Tabor says Epiphanius claims this, but the name more likely comes from the Hebrew word *Evyonim*, "the poor ones," as in Matthew 5:3.
- **That the Dead Sea Scroll community were Essenes, as Josephus, Philo and Pliny describe them.** Tabor says we should start from what the scrolls say about themselves.
- **That Mary went to Ephesus and died there.** He says there is no source for this before a nineteenth-century nun's vision, and that Mary died on Mount Zion.
- **That the Pella flight is a legend invented to get Christians out of Jerusalem in 70.** Tabor argues it is credible, citing Eusebius and the geography around Pella.
- **That "Arabia" in the Bible means Saudi Arabia.** He says the Hebrew/Greek Arabia refers to the Arava rift valley near the Dead Sea.
- **That Friday is the Sabbath in Islam.** He says it is not a Sabbath in Islam.
- **That the Eastern Orthodox view that Mary had no other children** is the earliest view. Tabor says this is a later tradition, and that the brothers were sometimes read as Joseph's brother Clopas's children.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor says the Ebionites rejected Paul as an apostate from the law and held that Paul was a thirteenth apostle who taught against the Torah. He also says Paul did not think highly of the original group. This is the talk's main contrast with Pauline Christianity.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** The talk's main thread is James's leadership in Jerusalem, his death in 62, Simon son of Clopas's succession, and the community's later movement across the Jordan.
- **The Nazarenes / Ebionites / Essenes:** Tabor treats the Dead Sea community as the start of the stream and the Nazarenes described by Epiphanius as a distinct pre-Jesus group that is related to it. He reads the later Ebionites as a continuation.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet and nonviolence:** Tabor reports that the groups held the Torah to be corrupted by priestly circles, rejected animal sacrifice, were vegetarian, and kept Torah for Jews while not requiring it of Gentiles who were "God-fearers."
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** He describes the Ebionite view that Jesus was a prophet like Moses who received the Spirit at baptism and was not God, with a clear distinction between God and the Messiah.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor says the Ebionites used only the Gospel of Matthew, lacked the first two chapters, and that the "Hebrew Matthew" survives in a form he cites from the fifteenth century.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** Not discussed in the transcript.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Tabor mentions apocalyptic expectation and the end near, linked to Daniel and the Didache, but does not develop the earthly Kingdom beyond that.
