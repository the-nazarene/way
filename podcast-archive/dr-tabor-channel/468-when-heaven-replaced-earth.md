## Summary

This is an interview-format video in which an unnamed interviewer asks James D. Tabor how his historical reconstruction of Mary, the mother of Jesus, as a Jewish woman of the first century bears on the devotional picture of her in Christianity. Tabor answers by setting Mary inside a wider shift he calls the "Great Reversal" in the Hellenistic world, in which the heavenly realm came to be treated as true home and the earth as a fallen, dark lower world. He argues that Mary is a prime example: the later tradition made her "queen of heaven," sinless and bodily-untouched by ordinary human needs, which is hard to square with an ordinary first-century Jewish woman.

Tabor's main evidence is partly philosophical and partly textual. He ties the shift to Neoplatonism and to Greek dualism, citing Plato's *Phaedo*-style picture of Socrates going to "the world of light," and a set of Greek gold-foil funerary texts he dates to the fourth or fifth century BC. He contrasts this with the Hebrew Bible's "locative" view of life on earth, which he attributes to his teacher Jonathan Z. Smith's vocabulary of "locative" versus "utopian" religion. He also points to Ecclesiastes and Job as biblical texts that express uncertainty about an afterlife, and to the Lord's Prayer and Jesus's Mary-and-Martha episode as evidence that the Kingdom is meant to come on earth.

The talk ends with Tabor's claim that Jesus's movement was socially egalitarian, with women and men, rich and poor, and slaves and free, meeting together, and that James the brother of Jesus carried a version of this concern for the poor into the synagogue. He suggests this social disruption may explain Jesus's execution. Tabor presents these points as his interpretation and, in several places, as things he cannot prove. The interviewer's questions about the Catholic Church and other mainstream Christian bodies go unanswered in any detail.

## Key Teachings

### The Great Reversal from earth to heaven
Tabor describes a Hellenistic-era change in which the heavenly world became the "higher, better" home and the earth was treated as the lower, fallen place. He calls it "the most dramatic shift in Western history" and says that every religion he knows of, including Judaism, apart from the Hebrew Bible itself, follows this reversal. He names the Neoplatonic "heavenliness" of the period as the intellectual background and credits later church fathers with pushing Mary toward a perfection that excluded ordinary bodily needs. He gives the example that the church fathers said Mary "never goes to the toilet," presented as a reductio of the trend.

### Locative versus utopian religion, and the Hebrew view of earth
Tabor uses Jonathan Z. Smith's terms "locative" (the sense that you are where you ought to be) and "utopian" (a home elsewhere) to frame the change. He says the Hebrew Bible is locative: humans are dust creatures who go back to dust, the earth is "a world of light and life," and "death is the dark phase." He draws on Robert Frost's poem "Birches" ("the earth's the right place to be") and on Genesis 1's repeated "very good" and the command to be fruitful and multiply. He offers the reversal of death as birth as the characteristic later move.

### Greek gold-foil texts and the Socratic farewell
As an example of the earlier Greek view, Tabor cites a body of about seventeen gold-foil texts he says are found in graves, rolled up and placed by the deceased's ear, and which he dates to "fourth, fifth century BC." He reads one line, "I'm a child of earth and starry heaven, but heaven alone is my home," as an instance of the heaven-as-home idea. He also recalls Socrates's words "Weep not for me. Weep for yourselves," as a sign of the Greek contempt for remaining in the fallen world. He presents these as the background against which Mary's heavenward image developed.

### Biblical uncertainty about the afterlife
Tabor argues that the Hebrew Bible handles the afterlife with restraint rather than assertion. He cites Ecclesiastes ("who knows whether the spirit of man goes up and the spirit of beasts go down") as a skeptical, quasi-scientific stance: things one does not know can be investigated, not affirmed. He reads Job's question "If a man dies, shall he live again" as longing or aspiration, not a stated belief. He suggests the Hebrew view would consider the afterlife either unknowable or "none of your business," since "the secret things belong to God."

### The Kingdom on earth: the Lord's Prayer and tikkun olam
Tabor argues that the Lord's Prayer ("Let your kingdom come... on earth as it is in heaven") places the Kingdom on earth, not in another realm. He ties this to the Jewish idea of *tikkun olam*, perfecting the world, and gives examples of the ideal: no killing, no war, no stealing, no lying, people loving their neighbors. He concedes that Jesus does speak of resurrection and the world to come, and says "we're not denying that," but he says the emphasis of Jesus's movement is building something here.

### Mary and Martha: women in the teaching circle
Tabor retells Luke's story of Mary and Martha as an example of Jesus teaching women and men together. He says Mary "sits with the men" and is "registered for the course," and that Jesus tells Martha that Mary "has chosen the best thing." He presents this as an egalitarian practice ("That's not heaven. That's now.") and says Paul picked up the theme but kept some social distinctions, while within gatherings "they should all be one."

### James the brother of Jesus and the poor
Tabor says he thinks James "learns this from his mother," though he says he cannot prove it. He describes James's warning about seating a poor man at the back of the synagogue (he notes James says "synagogue," not "church") and cites James's line that God has chosen "those rich in faith who are heirs of the kingdom." He says the social reversal for the poor is embedded in the Hebrew Bible but was being "spread in a egalitarian way," which he thinks is why Jesus was "finally" killed: "It's very disruptive to a culture to have this kind of social preaching."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mary as a perfectly sinless, bodily-untouched figure.** Tabor says the later church fathers made Mary so perfect that she "never goes to the toilet," and he argues that the idea of a heavenly Mary who has no ordinary earthly life is a product of the Greek reversal, not the Hebrew Bible.
- **Jews don't believe in an afterlife.** An interviewer says non-Jews often hold this view. Tabor replies that Judaism has an extensive tradition about the world to come and about heavenly events, but that its emphasis is on perfecting this world.
- **The religious life is about escaping the world.** Tabor's correction is that the biblical emphasis, reflected in the Lord's Prayer and *tikkun olam*, is that God's will should be done on earth, and that the Hebrew view values earthly life, marriage, and children.
