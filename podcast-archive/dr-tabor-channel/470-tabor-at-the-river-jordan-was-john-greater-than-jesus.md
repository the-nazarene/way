## Summary

Tabor asks whether John the Baptist was greater than Jesus, and argues that the gospel writers played down John's status, his kinship with Jesus, and his role in shaping Jesus's mission. He opens by claiming that Jesus and John were related, using the Greek word *syngenes* ("kin") in the Gospels as his basis. He says the early church saw a real blood family as a threat to Jesus's divine status and so pushed it aside. He then reads Luke 7 as Jesus calling John "more than a prophet" and saying none born of women is greater than John, and he argues that the clause making "the least in the kingdom" greater than John was a later addition by a "pious editor."

Tabor's main evidence is textual. He compares the baptism accounts in Mark, Matthew, Luke and John and says each successive Gospel makes John less prominent, ending with no baptism at all in John. He also points to Luke 11, where the disciples ask Jesus to teach them to pray "as John had done," and argues that the Lord's Prayer may originally have been John's prayer, which Jesus adopted. For physical evidence he cites Shimon Gibson's 1999 discovery of a cave near John's traditional home, which he says contains evidence of ritual baptism in the period of Jesus and John. Robert Beckford, in short interview clips, supports the view that the Gospel writers were focused on the Jesus story and used John only as a precursor.

The talk ends with Tabor's claim that the church's later emphasis on Jesus's divinity left John and Jesus's blood family as "the losers." ## Key Teachings

### 1. John and Jesus as kin
Tabor opens by stating that Jesus and John "are part of the same family," with Mary and Elizabeth as their mothers. He points to the Greek *syngenes*, which he glosses as "kin person" and says could mean cousin or aunt, "part of the same extended family." He later says he believes Jesus had four brothers, two sisters, and at least one cousin, and that the Gospel writers and early church fathers downplayed this family because it "challenged his divine status." The talk does not give a verse for the kinship claim beyond the general reference to the Gospels; the Luke 1 reference in the table is the chapter where Elizabeth and Mary appear.

### 2. Luke 7: "more than a prophet" and "none greater than John"
Tabor reads Jesus's words to the crowd as a direct statement of John's importance: "What then did you go out to see? A prophet? Yes, I tell you, and more than a prophet." He notes that in the Bible only Moses is called more than a prophet, so Jesus is placing John in very high company, identifying him as the promised messenger who prepares the way of the Lord. He then cites "among those born of women, none is greater than John."

Tabor argues the next clause, "Yet the one who is least in the kingdom of God is greater than he," is a later addition. He describes it as "a pious editor" gloss that "didn't exist in the earliest source of this saying," and says he "really doubt[s] if Jesus said that." He does not name the earliest source or the manuscript evidence for its absence in the talk.

### 3. Baptism accounts across the four Gospels
Tabor's central textual argument is a sequence. In Mark, which he calls "the earliest gospel," Jesus comes to the Jordan and is baptized by John, with only the bare fact recorded. In Matthew, John protests that he needs to be baptized by Jesus. In Luke, the baptism of Jesus by John is not explicitly mentioned; the heavens open "when all the people were baptized and when Jesus also had been baptized." In John, "no baptism [is] mentioned at all," and John the Baptist simply points to Jesus and steps aside.

From this progression Tabor concludes that the tradition moved toward minimizing John. He also reverses the standard reading of Jesus's baptism: "Jesus being baptized by John is actually putting himself in a kind of disciple relationship with John." He presents this as shocking to readers who assume the reverse.

### 4. The Lord's Prayer and John's teaching
Tabor observes that the Lord's Prayer ("Let your kingdom come. Let your will be done on Earth. Give us this day our daily bread") is the prayer Jesus taught his disciples. He then cites Luke's introduction, in which the disciples ask Jesus to teach them to pray "as John had done." He says the usual understanding is that John taught his disciples to pray, perhaps a different prayer. He argues that the Greek "can very well imply teach us that prayer that John taught his disciples," and that the prayer's elements show that "Jesus and John both share this prayer as kind of... the core of their message." His conclusion, stated as a possibility: "maybe John first came up with that prayer." He does not give the Greek word or a lexical source in the talk.

### 5. The cave near John's traditional home
Tabor says that a few miles from John's traditional home there is "new physical evidence" of John's role. He reports that in 1999, while conducting a routine archaeological survey, Dr. Shimon Gibson found the opening to an ancient cave. A speaker in the cave segment (the captions do not label the speaker) identifies it as "the cave of John the Baptist" and says it contains archaeological evidence that it was used for ritual baptism in the time of Jesus and John. The talk does not give publication details or dating evidence for the cave.

### 6. Divinity, humanity, and the losers
Tabor closes by linking the textual downgrading of John to later Christology. He says that "ever since Jesus was declared God, the church has struggled with the balance between his divine and human natures," and that "it is the divine that has won out." In his view, "the losers were John the Baptist and the rest of his blood family." Beckford, in an interview clip, adds that once Jesus and Mary are exalted "to such a heavenly position... there's then this tendency not only to lose sight of the others, but even deny their importance."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Popular belief:** John the Baptist was simply a forerunner who points to Jesus and steps aside. **Tabor's correction:** John was the prophet and the inaugurator of the movement Jesus joined; Jesus's own words in Luke 7 call him more than a prophet.
- **Popular belief:** Jesus baptized by John is the main character, with John in a subordinate role. **Tabor's correction:** Jesus's baptism by John places Jesus in a disciple relationship to John, and the scene is "flipped around" from the usual reading.
- **Popular belief:** The saying "none is greater than John" is followed by a limit that puts John below the least in the kingdom. **Tabor's correction:** The qualifying clause is a later editorial addition, and Jesus did not say it.
- **Popular belief:** Jesus taught the Lord's Prayer as his own, original prayer. **Tabor's correction:** Luke's introduction suggests John taught his disciples a prayer, and Tabor argues the prayer may originally have been John's.
- **Popular belief:** Jesus's blood family is absent from the Gospels by accident or because it was unimportant. **Tabor's correction:** The early church saw a real family as threatening to Jesus's divine status and removed it deliberately.
