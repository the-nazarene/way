## Summary

Host Megan Lewis opens with the basic question: what are the Dead Sea Scrolls? Tabor answers that they are manuscripts about 2,000 to 2,200 years old, found in caves near Qumran between 1947 and 1950, and that most scholars take them to be the library of a Jewish sect that called itself the people of the new covenant. Tabor describes three kinds of content: copies of the Hebrew Bible (he contrasts them with the Leningrad Codex, which he dates to about 1000 CE), other books such as 1 Enoch, and the sect's own writings, including the Thanksgiving Hymns. He argues that the sect's apocalyptic, messianic outlook, its teacher figure, and its "two messiahs" resemble the early Jesus movement more closely than anything he can attribute to the Pharisees or Sadducees. He also reads several scroll variants of the Bible (Malachi 3, Deuteronomy 32) as evidence that the texts were revised over time.

Lewis then asks how the sect compared with other Jews of its time. Tabor says we cannot know the wider population's views, and that this group was separatist, with a long initiation and a sharp line between members and "sons of darkness." He sees the Jesus movement as similarly separatist and notes shared features such as common property. He is careful to say the scrolls were not copied by Jesus' followers, but came from a shared milieu, and he says he will cover the differences in his course.

The interview closes with Tabor describing his ten-lecture online course, "Jesus and the Sea Scrolls," including the roughly 40 percent of scroll material that was not publicly available until 1991, which he says he helped publish. He ends with thanks and a note on his students' engagement. The talk is short and mostly the guest speaking.

## Key Teachings

### The sect, its scrolls, and its teacher
Tabor identifies the community as a sect that called itself the people of the new covenant, and says some people use the word Essene. He describes the Thanksgiving Hymns from cave one as "poetic" and "very personal," and thinks one may have been written by their teacher. Tabor says the sect's leader was called the "right teacher," usually rendered "teacher of righteousness," and that they believed he was a prophet like Moses and "the one to whom God has revealed all the mysteries of his prophets." Tabor says that some scrolls look forward to a coming Messiah and others speak of the teacher after his arrival.

### Apocalyptic expectation and chronology
Tabor argues that the group believed it was living at the end of the age. He says the scrolls contain statements such as "we're the last generation," and that they calculated from Daniel's 490-year period. He links this to Paul's "the time is grown very short" (1 Corinthians 7:29) as a parallel expectation of an imminent end. He says a lecture in his course covers the group's chronology.

### Two messiahs and the Malachi 3 variant
Tabor's example is Malachi 3. In the Dead Sea Scroll version, he says, "Another figure will come," so the text has two figures rather than one, and he says the sect believed in "a priestly Messiah and a Davidic Messiah." He contrasts this with the New Testament's habit of making Jesus "the prophet," "the priest," and "the king" together, and with Mark's use of the Malachi passage for John the Baptist.

### Deuteronomy 32 and the "sons of God"
Tabor says the scroll text of the passage about the Most High dividing the nations reads "sons of God" where the Masoretic text reads "children of Israel." He connects the scroll reading to the Septuagint's Greek and to the divine council in Psalm 82, arguing that the Masoretic version reflects a later effort to make the passage more nationalistic.

### Separatism and shared property
Tabor says the sect's initiation lasted three years, that members were "dipped in water," and that outsiders were "sons of darkness." He compares this to John the Baptist's "generation of vipers" and to Jesus' statement about "the mystery of the kingdom" that is given to his followers while "those outside" do not understand. He argues that both groups were separatist, that both pooled resources, and that both had texts instructing followers to sell their possessions.

### Copying versus shared milieu
Tabor says the first question scholars asked was whether the Jesus people copied the scroll people. His answer is no: "I don't think it's a matter of copying, but coming out of the same kind of milieu and the same sort of culture." He says the course lays out the differences as well as the parallels.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The scrolls are just older copies of the same Bible.** Tabor says the scroll copies contain thousands of variants, and he gives Malachi 3 and Deuteronomy 32 as examples where the readings differ in meaning.
- **The Dead Sea Scroll sect was simply "the Essenes."** Tabor says some people use that name, but he prefers "the people of the new covenant," the name the group used for itself.
- **Jesus was more accommodating than the sect.** Tabor says the Gospels show Jesus speaking in separatist terms ("to you is given the mystery of the kingdom"), and he argues the two movements shared that separatism.
- **The Jesus movement copied the scroll people.** Tabor says the answer is no: both came from the same milieu.
