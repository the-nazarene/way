## Summary

Tabor asks what the names "Ebionites," "Nazarenes," and "Essenes" actually refer to, and whether anyone who uses them can say where the label comes from. He argues the names are "not as helpful as getting the views together." He begins with the Dead Sea Scrolls, questioning the common assumption that they were written by the Essenes, and traces that label back to Josephus, whose Greek-style description of the Essenes he treats with suspicion. He then turns to the Ebionites, relying mainly on Epiphanius, the heresy-hunter whose catalogue of sects is the main ancient source, and on the Pseudo-Clementines, the Gospel of Thomas, Hegesippus (via Eusebius), and Paul's own letters. From these he reconstructs a set of traits: loyalty to James the Just, a view of Jesus as an ordinary human born of Joseph and Mary who was the "true prophet like Moses," rejection of Paul as a false apostle, avoidance of meat and wine, and rejection of the sacrificial laws as forgeries. Tabor also presents Epiphanius's section on a pre-Christian Jewish group, the "Nassareans," as a separate sect whose name is spelled the same way in Greek.

The second half of the talk widens into terminology more generally. Tabor compares the "Messianist," "Branch Davidian," "Messianic Jew," and "Jehovah's Witness" labels and argues that such names often began as descriptions and later hardened into membership tags. He suggests that the better approach is to describe a movement's beliefs and practices in its own texts, and he proposes the "followers of James" and "the Way" as more useful frames. Near the end he recommends a 10th-century Arabic source edited by Pines and an article he plans to share. The talk closes with Berman's question-and-response framing and no firm conclusion; the reasoning is presented as Tabor's working view, and he notes that the name Ebionite was probably not used by the group itself.

## Key Teachings

### Names are labels that must be traced to sources
Tabor's method is to ask, of any group name, "Where did you get your idea?" He argues that the Essene label for the Dead Sea Scrolls rests on Josephus, whose description of the Pharisees and Sadducees sounds like Greek philosophical schools, so Josephus should not be trusted uncritically on the Essenes either. He offers a possible Hebrew etymology for "Essene" (from *oseh*, "doers," linked to Matthew/Luke's "be doers of the Torah" and Romans 2) but says he does not know whether it is correct. He notes that the Dead Sea group never called itself "Essene," and that the Ebionite name probably comes from the Hebrew for "the poor ones," not from a leader named Ebion as Epiphanius claimed.

### Ebionite traits drawn from Epiphanius and the Pseudo-Clementines
Tabor reads Epiphanius's long chapters (heresies 29 and 30) as the main description and summarizes them. He lists these features:
- Central authority of James the Just, supported by the Pseudo-Clementines and the Gospel of Thomas (saying 12, "Go to James the Just").
- Jesus as a natural human, born of Joseph and Mary, not born of a virgin.
- Jesus as "the true prophet," tied to Deuteronomy 18 and to the closing verses of Deuteronomy, which say no prophet like Moses has arisen since.
- Christology that Tabor says would fail the "was there a time when he was not?" test: the spirit rests upon Jesus, and the baptism texts are read as "this day have I begotten you" in the Gospel of the Hebrews, the Gospel of the Nazarenes, and the Gospel of the Ebionites. He links this to Arius, and says the Ebionites were "worse" in the same direction.

### Rejection of Paul and of later revelation
Tabor argues the Ebionites rejected Paul as a false apostle and a "13th apostle" who claimed revelation directly from Jesus. He develops the argument from Galatians 1, where Paul says his gospel did not come from man, and from 1 Corinthians 15's list of appearances, which he reads as categories of leadership with James at the head. He suggests the Ebionite reply would have been, "How do you know it was Jesus?", while James would have pointed to having lived with Jesus as his brother. He also claims Paul's "weak brother" language in Romans 14 targets James, who (in this reading) avoided meat and wine.

### Diet, sacrifice and the Torah
Tabor reports from Hegesippus (via Eusebius) that James never touched wine or meat, and says the Ebionites read John the Baptist's "locusts and wild honey" as a plant food. He cites the Slavonic Josephus on a figure who ate no prepared food. He argues that the Ebionites appealed to Jeremiah 7 ("I did not command you about burn offerings") against the sacrificial legislation of Exodus 25 through Numbers 10, and that this anticipates the source-critical "P" material. He suggests they wanted to return to a pre-flood, Isaiah 11-style peaceable kingdom, and that they "might" have been nonviolent, though he presents this only as a possibility.

### The Nazarenes and the Nassareans
Tabor distinguishes Epiphanius's section 18 (against the "Nassareans," a Jewish group the church father lists before Christianity appears in his catalogue) from the later Christian "Nazarenes." He quotes Epiphanius on the pre-Christian group as Jews who keep circumcision, Sabbath and festivals, practice daily baptism (*hemerobaptists*), reject astrology, and refuse sacrifice and meat. He suggests the Christian "Nazarene" label may derive from the Hebrew *netzer* ("branch") in Isaiah 11, rather than from Nazareth, and argues the label was probably applied to the Jesus movement from outside.

### Terminology and the "Way"
Tabor argues that early followers would have described themselves by practice, such as "followers of the Torah," "doers," "the new covenant," or "the Way," not by a single name. He compares this to "Messianic Jew," "Jew for Jesus," "Branch Davidian," and "Jehovah's Witness," labels that began as descriptions and became tags. He proposes that the "Way" (Acts, Dead Sea Scrolls, the Didache's "two ways") is the most plausible self-description, while the names Ebionite and Nazarene are later external categories.

### Sources and reading strategy
Tabor recommends Pines's edition of an Arabic text (10th century, by a Muslim writer debating Christians) as a source for Jewish-Christian characteristics, and notes a parallel with George Howard's Hebrew Matthew, the Even Bohan. He says he has been reading Epiphanius and that Epiphanius is "prejudiced" but may preserve real details about Paul. He encourages listeners to read the sources directly.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Essenes wrote the Dead Sea Scrolls.** Tabor says this is the most common assumption, but argues it rests on Josephus, whose description of the Essenes he questions, and that the scrolls' authors never called themselves Essenes.
- **The Ebionites were named after a leader called Ebion.** Tabor says this is Epiphanius's suggestion and that the name more likely comes from the Hebrew for "the poor ones."
- **The Ebionites got their name from holding poor views of Christ.** Tabor dismisses this as "worthless historically," calling it a folk etymology that makes no sense as a self-name.
- **The Nazarenes were simply early Christians.** Tabor argues that Epiphanius's "Nassareans" are a separate, pre-Christian Jewish group, and that the "Nazarene" label may come from *netzer* in Isaiah 11.
- **The "Apostolic Fathers" were an ancient collection.** Tabor says this is a modern grouping assembled by scholars in the 4th or 5th century, not a category the ancients used.
- **Ancient groups carried membership cards.** Tabor says names like Essene, Ebionite, and Nazarene were more like descriptive tags than formal membership, and that groups defined themselves by practice and belief.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor argues that the Ebionites rejected Paul's claim to direct revelation from Jesus and that they followed James's leadership. He reads Paul's Romans 14 and Galatians 1 as responses to this disagreement.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Tabor centers the talk on James the Just as the Ebionite authority, supported by the Pseudo-Clementines, Gospel of Thomas, and Hegesippus (via Eusebius). He suggests the movement's successors (the *Desposyni*) were the family of Jesus.
- **The Nazarenes/Ebionites/Essenes:** This is the talk's main subject. Tabor argues for tracing each name to its sources, and reads Epiphanius's sections 18, 29, and 30 as the key evidence.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet and nonviolence:** Tabor reports that the Ebionites refused sacrifice (appealing to Jeremiah 7), avoided meat and wine (citing Hegesippus), and may have been nonviolent (presented as a possibility).
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Tabor mentions the Isaiah 11 peaceable kingdom and the wheat-and-tares parable, read as a future judgment and restoration.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor presents the Ebionite view that Jesus was an ordinary human and the "true prophet like Moses," and he contrasts this with later Trinitarian and Arian debates.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Tabor notes that the Gospel of the Hebrews, Gospel of the Nazarenes, and Gospel of the Ebionites circulated under different names, and that the Didache was known only from a late 19th-century copy.
