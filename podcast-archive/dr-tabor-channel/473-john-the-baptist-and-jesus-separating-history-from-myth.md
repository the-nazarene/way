## Summary

Tabor's Zoom session for his Patreon "Tabor Research Community" discusses James McGrath's book *John the Baptist and Jesus: Separating History from Myth*, which Tabor introduces as one half of a two-book project. The other half, *Christ Maker*, is a more technical volume. The question throughout is what can be said historically about John the Baptist, and how John's movement connects with Jesus, Paul, the Gospels, Josephus and the Mandaeans. Tabor takes questions from about 35 viewers and puts most of them to McGrath.

McGrath's core position, stated near the end, is that John is the key to explaining much of the early material that historians have underrated. He says John was a figure in his own right who baptized for the forgiveness of sins, that Jesus was originally his follower and regrouped the movement after John's imprisonment, and that the movement later splintered. He reasons from convergence: where several independent sources imply the same thing without emphasizing it, he treats that as more secure. His main evidence includes the Josephus passage on John, which he finds plausibly authentic because it lacks Christian features and connects John's execution to Aretas's war with Antipas. He also cites Mandaean texts, which he says preserve some independent material on John, while noting they are late and need caution. He admits he cannot say which of his claims are most secure, and names only his core conviction about John's centrality as the one he holds most firmly.

The session ends with a list of reading suggestions for Mandaean material and with McGrath saying that the question of how far Jesus's and John's teachings differ is open. Tabor and McGrath agree on most points, and Tabor's comments often add further evidence rather than challenge McGrath.

## Key Teachings

### Convergence as a test for confidence
McGrath says his confidence depends on how well a theme is attested across the tradition, following Dale Allison's emphasis on the gist of material rather than atomized sayings. The strongest case, in his view, is where several sources imply a conclusion that none of them states outright. He gives the image of Jesus overshadowed by John as an example, since sources either acknowledge it or deny it. He says the Nazirite requirement and the priestly background are one area where the material seems to point in a direction without any source saying so directly.

### The criterion of embarrassment
McGrath treats the criterion as useful but limited. It shows that an author who records something that cuts against his own portrait probably did not invent it. It does not prove historicity. He points out that Mark is not embarrassed by John's baptism of Jesus, while Matthew and John show discomfort. He argues the historicity of Jesus's baptism rests less on the criterion than on the fact that Jesus seems dependent on John, so that it would be surprising if Jesus never underwent baptism. Tabor adds that the Gospels show a progression from Mark to Matthew to Luke to John, with the baptism gradually being played down.

### Josephus on John
McGrath argues that the Josephus passage on John (executed at Machaerus, after a defeat by Aretas, with the Herodias marriage in the background) is plausibly authentic. It does not mention Jesus, it contains content that Christians would not likely have written, and it is written in Josephus's own style of stepping away from the political narrative. He cites the Arabic paraphrase of the Testimonium Flavianum (Smyth) as showing how a Christian paraphrase drops the most Christian material. Tabor adds that nothing in Josephus would explain why John's execution is linked to Aretas's war against Antipas, whereas the Gospel tradition's account of the Herodias marriage explains the link. McGrath describes this as two independent sources that each make the other more sensible.

### Baptism, its repetition and its ritual setting
McGrath argues that John's baptism was probably repeatable, or at least more plausibly so than a one-time act, especially if it was comparable to sacrifice. He points to the Mandaean practice of repeated baptism and to the Christian problem of post-baptismal sin, which he thinks suggests Christians turned a repeatable rite into a once-for-all one. He also argues that the ritual setting probably included more than dipping in water, possibly including exorcism and other accoutrements, and that the Didache's Eucharist and Mandaean practice could help triangulate back to John's baptism. He lists this as a topic that deserves a book of its own.

### Purity, sin and Mark 7
McGrath separates ritual purity from sin. Conception and childbirth, he notes, involve impurity, yet they are not sinful within marriage. He argues that the Christian tradition largely dropped Jewish purity concerns, and that Mandaeism keeps a minor purity immersion. Tabor cites the archaeologist Shimon Gibson's work on stone vessels, arguing that possessing such objects did not show strict observance and that Mark 7's depiction of the Pharisees may describe a sectarian practice rather than the general population. Tabor also cites Jonathan Adler's work on how Torah observance becomes visible in the archaeological record around the Hasmonean period.

### John and Jesus: continuity and difference
Discussing the fasting–feasting contrast from Matthew and Luke, McGrath argues that the Gospel tradition does not depict John's and Jesus's habits as indicating divergence. Jesus's point is that the audience rejected both, so the fault lies with the audience. He suggests that John taught not displaying one's righteous works (a teaching he links to the Ginza Rba's almsgiving saying), and that Jesus applied this to fasting in a way John had not. He calls the overall pattern continuity, with Jesus building on John's foundation. Tabor adds that Jesus's apocalyptic expectation (Mark 9:1) was still strong.

### Messianic self-understanding of John
McGrath considers three models for how John might have seen himself: a priestly Messiah, an Elijah figure (with Elisha as the stronger successor), and a Samuel figure who anoints the king. He says that all three may have been present, and that the developing tradition later combined priestly and royal roles, citing the Protoevangelium of James and Zechariah 6. He is less clear that John himself made that combination. Tabor adds Qumran's Messianic banquet text and the Hasmonean precedent of priest-kings.

### The wider influence of John's movement
McGrath argues that John's movement was a catalyst for later figures such as the "sign prophets" Josephus describes, who styled themselves on Elijah, and that Jesus's followers may have carried on the idea of a transmitted spiritual portion, as in the Elijah–Elisha double-portion story. He describes John as a gathering figure whose movement splintered after his death, with some followers seeing Jesus as its continuation and others looking for another. He says this core conviction is the one he is most confident about.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The criterion of embarrassment proves historicity.** McGrath says it only makes it more probable that an author did not invent an embarrassing detail, and that it cannot settle the historicity of the baptism, since Mark is not embarrassed.
- **John's baptism was a one-time rite.** McGrath says nothing in Josephus or the Gospels supports this and argues that John's baptism was probably repeatable, as Mandaean practice suggests.
- **Jesus and John were sharply divided.** McGrath argues the Gospels' fasting contrast shows the audience's rejection of both, not a rupture between them, and that Jesus was building on John.
- **Jewish purity practice was universal.** McGrath and Tabor cite stone vessel evidence and the absence of mikvaot in early synagogues to argue that not everyone practiced purity strictly, so Mark 7 may describe a sectarian practice.
- **Paul's silence about John is evidence that John did not matter.** McGrath says Paul writes to people who already know the story, so his silence reflects the shift in focus onto Jesus rather than John's irrelevance.
