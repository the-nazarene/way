## Summary

Tabor asks whether the historical Jesus taught the traditional Christian doctrine of eternal hellfire, in which the wicked are cast into everlasting fire with Satan and his angels. He frames the question around the words usually translated "hell" (Gehenna, Sheol, Hades) and around Edward Fudge's 1982 book *The Fire That Consumes*, which argues that the New Testament pictures a fire that destroys rather than torments. Tabor also notes that the King James "hell" is a mistranslation read into Hebrew and Greek.

Ehrman carries most of the substantive argument. He says Gehenna, a valley outside Jerusalem associated with child sacrifice, became a byword for a God-forsaken place, and that the feared outcome there was being denied a decent burial and having one's remains defiled, not eternal torment. On Sheol, he argues from Hebrew poetic parallelism that the word means the place where the dead are laid (grave, tomb, pit), not a place of punishment. He also doubts that the Lazarus parable in Luke 16 goes back to Jesus, because its ending presupposes Jesus' resurrection. In his telling, the soul-to-heaven-or-hell view appears gradually: he sees hints in Paul's later letters (Philippians), in Luke, and a full-fledged version by the early second century, when most Christians had given up a future bodily resurrection. Ehrman says his course will argue that Jesus' own views were traditional Jewish apocalyptic expectations of judgment and new creation, which were later transformed.

The talk ends as a promotional conversation. Tabor and Ehrman describe the free two-lecture course "What the Hell?" ## Key Teachings

### Gehenna as a burial problem, not eternal torment
Ehrman says Gehenna, the valley south of Jerusalem, is "probably most infamous in the Hebrew Bible because uh there are indications that child sacrifice was practiced there," which the biblical text condemns. The valley became "the most kind of God-forsaken place on the planet." In his reading, the threat of being cast there was not eternal torment: "the problem with being cast there isn't that you'll be tormented forever is that you won't be given a decent burial." He connects this to the ancient concern for proper burial and the fear of a defiled corpse. Tabor, for his part, raises the idea that Gehenna was a refuse site, and Ehrman accepts that trash burning in the valley is a relevant background, though he treats the child-sacrifice associations as the main point.

### Sheol and the translation problem
Ehrman argues that "hell" is a poor rendering of both Gehenna and Sheol. He says the English tradition, going back to Tyndale and then the King James Version, rendered both as hell. He notes that Sheol appears "maybe 40 times or something like that," mostly in poetry, and that in Hebrew poetry the parallel line is typically a word such as grave, tomb, or pit: "the synonym used in the next line is always something like grave uh, or tomb, or pit, or something like that." From this he concludes that going to Sheol "just means dying and being buried." He explains the popular "hell" reading partly by association with the Greek Hades, where everyone goes at death regardless of character. Tabor cites Psalm 139 ("if I make my bed in Sheol") as an example of how the King James reads it as "hell."

### The Lazarus parable (Luke 16) is probably not from Jesus
Tabor asks whether Luke's rich man and Lazarus shows that Jewish thinking was already dividing the dead into compartments of good and bad. Ehrman answers that these passages probably do not represent what Jesus said. His main reason is that "the whole story ends on a on the concept that is predicated on Jesus having been raised from the dead." He also observes that the text does not condemn the rich man for wickedness or Lazarus for righteousness; it only says one is rich and the other poor. He adds that the ending, in which the brothers "won't repent" even if someone rises from the dead, points to the early church's setting.

### From Jewish apocalyptic expectation to the soul's afterlife
Ehrman's thesis for the course is that Jesus' own views "are more kind of traditional Jewish apocalyptic views of the time," and that these were later "transformed into what now people think about the afterlife." He places the change in stages: the idea of the soul going to be with Christ "starts creep[ing]" into Paul's later writings, especially Philippians; Luke shows "hints" of it; and in the early second century "the majority of Christians no longer believe in a future resurrection of the body." Tabor mentions Robert Grant's idea that more Hellenizing, Gnostic ways of thinking were partly fueled by the failure of apocalyptic expectations, and Ehrman agrees that delayed expectation made such alternatives comforting.

### Apocalyptic judgment as an answer to the problem of justice
Ehrman explains apocalyptic thinking as a response to the problem of suffering among the righteous: "if Jews are living righteously and doing the following the Torah and they're living horrible, miserable, painful lives, where's the justice if they die and that's it?" On his account, the answer was that "this is not the end of the story," since God would judge and bring in a new creation. Later Christian and Gnostic ideas, he says, explained the same concern in a different way, through a vertical ascent rather than a future resurrection.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Gehenna means eternal fire where the wicked are tormented forever.** Ehrman says the valley's associations are with child sacrifice and with the denial of proper burial, and the danger was defilement of the body.
- **Sheol is hell.** Ehrman argues that Sheol, in Hebrew poetry, is the place of the dead, parallel to grave, tomb, or pit, and that the "hell" translation came from later readers.
- **The King James "hell" reflects the original text.** Tabor says the word is a mistranslation read into Hebrew and Greek, and Ehrman says Gehenna and Sheol were rendered as "hell" by English tradition.
- **The rich man and Lazarus is a teaching of Jesus about the afterlife.** Ehrman doubts it goes back to Jesus, since the ending assumes his resurrection, and he says the story does not explicitly condemn the rich man's morals.
- **Jesus taught that the soul goes to heaven or hell at death.** Ehrman argues Jesus' views were Jewish apocalyptic expectations of judgment and new creation, and that the soul-based view developed later.
