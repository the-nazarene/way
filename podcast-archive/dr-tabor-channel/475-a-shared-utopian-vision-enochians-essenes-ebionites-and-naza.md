## Summary

Host Jacob Berman asks who the Ebionites were and whether they were heretics or the first Christians. James Tabor answers that they were "Christian" only in a broad sense, as a messianic group, and that the better question is whether a continuing "thread" runs from the Hebrew Bible through pre-Christian and early Christian groups. ## Key Teachings

### 1. A "blue thread" of peace and no slaughter in the Hebrew Bible
Tabor says Genesis 1 gives a vegetarian diet to humans and animals alike, that Eden is a vegetarian region, and that violence fills the earth only after the flood. He calls the recurring ideal of peace, a "peaceable kingdom," and a world without slaughter the "blue thread." He ties it to Isaiah 11 (the Davidic messianic chapter ending with no hurting or slaughter on God's holy mountain) and to Isaiah 66, where the one who kills an ox or a lamb is compared to one who kills a human being. He argues that the Hebrew word *shakat* ("slaughter") in Isaiah 11 is often mistranslated as "destroy" or "corrupt," which Waters says reshaped his own reading.

### 2. Restorationist and realized apocalypticism
Tabor's central thesis is that a restorationist movement arose in late Second Temple Judaism, "maybe a hundred years or more" before Jesus. It wanted to return to the ideal of Genesis and to bring the messianic age into the present. He calls the idea "realized eschatology" or a "futuristic" theology lived now. He reads apocalypticism not as doom but as the halting of the present world of violence and injustice in order to begin a new one. He cites Daniel's "ticking clock" and Paul's "appointed time... grown very near" as evidence that these groups were calculating an imminent turn.

### 3. Jeremiah 7, Psalm 51, and the critique of sacrifice
Tabor argues that Jeremiah 7 is a sarcastic attack on the temple sacrifice system and that the Greek verb translated "rip apart" describes brutal slaughter. He quotes Jeremiah's claim that God did not command burnt offerings when Israel left Egypt, and Jeremiah 8's charge that the "false pen of the scribes" made the Torah a lie. He contends that Psalm 51 rejects sacrifice in its body while its final verses look like a later scribal addition that restores temple sacrifice. He says Mark 12's widow and the "love God and neighbor" teaching extend the same critique, and that the Priestly source's silence about sacrifice before the tabernacle reflects a priestly interest in centralizing it.

### 4. Divorce, Sabbath and "from the beginning": Jesus as restorationist
Tabor reads Jesus's divorce answer in Mark 10 ("from the beginning it has not been so") and his Sabbath saying in Mark 2 ("the Sabbath was made for man") as the same restorationist move, appealing to Genesis as the ideal. He adds that Paul's teaching on marriage, as in Luke 20's exchange on the resurrection, reflects the same reference point, and he reads Paul's flexibility on food in Romans 14 as accommodation to a majority rather than endorsement of the "weak" view.

### 5. Epiphanius, the Nazarenes, the Ebionites, and the Hebrew Matthew
Tabor relies on Epiphanius (described as a fourth-century writer) to describe a pre-Christian group of Nazarenes in Trans-Jordan. He says they kept the Torah, admitted Gentiles under the laws of Noah, rejected sacrifice and flesh-eating, and held that the Torah had been corrupted by priests. He says they did not accept Jesus as Messiah. He then describes the later Ebionites as "the poor ones" who shared these traits. Tabor and Waters both stress that the early and later groups share attributes, and Tabor argues that the "trajectory" matters more than the names. Tabor and Waters both use the Hebrew Matthew preserved by medieval rabbis (named in the captions as Even Bohan, tied to Shem-Tov ibn Shaprut) to argue for a text that exalts John the Baptist and differs from the Greek Matthew, including the reading "wild honey and honey cake" in place of "locusts and wild honey."

### 6. Enoch, the Nephilim, and the consumption of animals
Waters argues that the Ethiopic and Aramaic versions of 1 Enoch 7 say the Nephilim sinned against the beasts, birds, and fish by consuming their flesh, while the Greek version seems to describe a different sin. He connects this to Pseudo-Clementine Homilies (book 7, in his account), where Peter says the Nephilim taught humanity animal-eating and that cannibalism followed. Tabor agrees the Enoch material fits the "something went wrong from Genesis 1-3 to Genesis 9" scheme. He also notes the Genesis Apocryphon's version of the post-flood grant, where the grant is to "the green things and the grass of the earth" but not blood, which he says reads as a restriction on flesh.

### 7. Diet, the Nazirite question, and the Dead Sea Scrolls
Tabor says that James the Just, according to Hegesippus, abstained from flesh and wine from birth, and that this is not the Nazirite vow of Numbers. He argues that the King James "meat" often means "bread" or sustenance, and that Josephus's account of John the Baptist (roots and plants that grow of themselves) and of a desert ascetic reflects the same ideal. He cites Luke 21:34 (the heart not made heavy by "drunkenness and dissipation") and the Curetonian and Sinaitic Syriac readings, which Waters says add "eating of flesh," and which he argues preserve an early Greek or Syriac reading that the canonical Greek later softened. Tabor presents the Dead Sea Scrolls (Damascus Document on David's wives; Community Rule's "temple of humans") as evidence of the same movement.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Ebionites were just fourth- and fifth-century heretics condemned for rejecting Paul.** Tabor says the label obscures a longer stream from Genesis and the Dead Sea Scrolls, and he proposes speaking of a tendency or movement.
- **Vegetarianism in these groups is hippie-style or animal-rights ethics.** Tabor says it is an ideal of a peaceable kingdom and of restoring Eden, not a modern animal-rights position.
- **James's abstinence from flesh and wine was a Nazirite vow.** Tabor says the Nazirite rules in Numbers are temporary and purpose-driven, and do not explain a lifelong ascetic practice.
- **"Meat" in the King James Gospels refers to meat.** Tabor says the Greek refers to bread, and that "meat" historically meant sustenance.
- **The Nephilim sinned by cannibalism.** Waters says the Ethiopic and Aramaic versions of 1 Enoch say they sinned against the animals.
- **Apocalyptic means only doom and destruction.** Tabor says it denotes the ending of the present violent order in order to begin a new one.
- **John the Baptist ate locusts.** Tabor says the Hebrew Matthew reading of "honey cake" changes the picture, and that "locust" may be a misreading of carob (locust bean).
- **Remis in Genesis 9 means "everything that moves" as animals.** Tabor says the Hebrew word means creeping things, and is a separate category.
