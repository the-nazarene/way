## Summary

Tabor gave this lecture to the Sisters of Maryknoll Institute as part of his book *The Lost Mary*. His question is what can be known historically about Mary, the mother of Jesus, as distinct from the theological Mary of later doctrine and devotion. He opens with an anecdote about a 1962 visit to Jerusalem and his guide's remark about how deep the first-century surface lay, and presents his project as an attempt to answer "What is this place? Where are we now?" He observes that the New Testament gives Mary only about ten scenes, and that the Apostles' Creed moves from her virginal conception straight to the crucifixion, so she is "best known least known woman in history."

His method is to reconstruct her world from archaeology, geography and historical context rather than from her few scenes alone. He argues that Mary's home town of Nazareth sat about 3 to 4 miles from Sepphoris, the Galilean capital that Herod Antipas rebuilt, and that Jesus and his father were probably skilled builders working on that city rather than village woodworkers. He presents Mary as a widowed head of a household of extended family, and he places her last years in Jerusalem, at a house on Mount Zion that he links to the early Jerusalem community and to James, the brother of Jesus. He ends by arguing that the core of Jesus' teaching came from Mary, and that James may be the "beloved disciple" of John. Tabor presents these as his own readings of the texts and archaeology. The lecture closes with a note that he expects Q&A, and the transcript ends there.

## Key Teachings

### The historical Mary versus the theological Mary
Tabor says Mary is less studied historically than Jesus, and he attributes this partly to devotional needs that place her "out of the human realm." He argues that a historian should reconstruct her Jewish village life, family and social world, and that devotional treatments do not answer those questions. He notes that the Apostles' Creed passes from "born of a virgin" to "crucified, died, and buried," leaving her life mostly unexamined. He also says the three main views on Mary's other children (her biological children; children of Joseph by an earlier marriage; children of Joseph's brother Clopas) are addressed in the book, but he does not settle the question in the lecture.

### Sepphoris, Nazareth and the 4 BC revolt
Tabor describes Sepphoris as the largest urban centre of the Galilee, about 3 to 4 miles from Nazareth, and presents Nazareth as a small village in its shadow. He cites Josephus calling Sepphoris "the jewel of all the Galilee," and notes that Herod Antipas later made it his capital. He describes a Byzantine church dedicated to Saint Anne on the western slope of Sepphoris, built over an earlier structure that pilgrims identified as the house of Mary's parents, and he argues that the identification is old and reflects local memory. He then describes the revolts after Herod the Great's death, including the rising in Sepphoris led by Judas son of Hezekiah and the Roman response under Varus, which he links to about 2,000 crucifixions in 4 BC. He suggests Mary may have been an infant's mother living through this period, though the Luke 1 and 2 timing he mentions is his own inference.

### Jesus as a builder, not a village carpenter
Tabor argues that the Greek word *tekton* is better rendered as "builder" than "carpenter," and points to Jesus' stone and foundation imagery. He cites Mark 6:3 and the Matthew phrase "son of a tekton," and argues that a son of a tekton would have been a skilled apprentice in a business. He concludes: "I'm going to say Jesus is not a woodworker. He is building Sepphoris." He also rejects the popular stories of Jesus studying in Egypt or India, and he mentions a lead-isotope study of some bones from tombs possibly linked to Jesus' family, which he says points to a Galilean (Sepphoris-area) water supply rather than Jerusalem.

### Mary as household manager and the family's world
Tabor describes the family as a blended household with four named brothers (James, Joseph, Judas and Simon) and sisters whose names he does not give, and he notes the names of the brothers but not of the sisters. He calls Mary a widowed mother who ran the household's finances, food and budget, and he draws on Proverbs 31's "virtuous woman" as a model. He also argues that rabbinic opinion favoured teaching girls to read and study Scripture, but he admits he cannot prove that Mary's daughters or Mary herself were taught. He reads Luke 8:1-3 as showing that the Jesus movement included women, among them Mary Magdalene, Joanna, wife of Herod's steward Chuza, and Susanna, and that these women supported the movement from their means.

### Mary's life in Jerusalem and the Mount Zion house
Tabor follows Bargil Pixner's argument that the Cenacle or Upper Room on Mount Zion sits over the foundations of a first-century house church, and says he has worked at the site with archaeologist Shimon Gibson for many years. He notes that a Byzantine church (Hagia Zion) was built beside the house and helped preserve the stones, and he cites the Madaba map and the Bordeaux Pilgrim and Epiphanius on the survival of the house. He says of Mary's last years: "Every indication is that she did." He identifies the Dormition Abbey site, which tradition associates with Mary's death, as within about 20 yards of the house, and he cites Stephen Shoemaker as the leading expert on the Dormition traditions.

### Mary, James and the Jerusalem community
Tabor argues that James, the brother of Jesus, took over leadership of the church, and that Mary lived with him in Jerusalem. He says "I think he's the beloved disciple," and argues that the fisherman John, who is said to have fled, would not have been the one entrusted with Mary's care, while the brother who lived with her in Jerusalem would have been. He cites Acts 1 ("Mary and his brothers were there") and treats this as evidence for her presence in the early community.

### Mary as teacher of Jesus
In the closing section Tabor argues that Mary's role in the Lukan birth narratives implies she was chosen as a fully capable spiritual and intellectual parent, not a surrogate, and that she therefore shaped Jesus' teaching. He says the "essential core gospel message" came from her, and he presents a set of "five things Jesus taught" that he attributes to her influence, though the lecture does not list all five. He also notes that some non-Catholic listeners resist this, citing the family's initial disbelief and Mark's "who are my mother and brothers" saying, and he offers his reading of that saying as affirming family rather than rejecting it.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mary is barely present in the Bible, so little can be known of her.** Tabor argues that reconstructing her world from geography, archaeology and context yields a fuller picture than her few New Testament scenes alone.
- **Nazareth was an isolated backwater.** He argues that Sepphoris was a major city 3 to 4 miles away, and that the family would have lived in its economic orbit.
- **Jesus was a village carpenter.** He argues that *tekton* means builder, and that Jesus and his father worked as skilled builders in Sepphoris.
- **Jesus spent his childhood abroad (Egypt or India).** He rejects these popular theories and proposes that Jesus stayed in Nazareth working in his father's business.
- **Mary went to Ephesus late in life.** He says this is a late 19th-century story and that, from what he can tell, she did not go to Ephesus.
- **Jesus rejected his family in Mark's "who are my mother and brothers" passage.** He reads it as affirming his family and as an invitation for them to stay.
- **John, the fisherman, took Mary into his home.** He argues that James, the brother who lived with Mary in Jerusalem, is the more likely figure.
