## Summary

Tabor asks what he calls "the greatest shift" in Western thought from antiquity onward, and whether that shift was a gain. He labels it "the great escape." He places the pivot around 300 BC, in the Hellenistic period, when a worldview that located humans on a good earth gave way to a dualist one that treats the material world as a prison from which the soul must escape. He says his framework comes from his teacher Jonathan Z. Smith, whose work on "the dynamics of religious persistence and change" he follows, and he contrasts an older "lockative" worldview with a "utopian" or escape-oriented one.

His evidence runs in three layers. First, he reads the Hebrew Bible's view of death (Genesis 2:7, Psalm 115:17, a passage from Job) as dust, silence and Sheol, with no immortal soul. Second, he sets this against Mesopotamian and Greek material: the Gilgamesh epic, Achilles in Odyssey book 11, Socrates's death, Empedocles, Plato's cave allegory, and Greek gold tomb tablets (lamellae) that give the dead soul instructions for the underworld. Third, he traces the change into Jewish apocalyptic writing (1 Enoch 39) and Roman texts (Cicero's account of Scipio's dream), arguing that this dualism then "saturated" Christianity and Islam. He presents the Hebrew and New Testament hope as a restored earth, with resurrection understood as transformation and the "age to come" replacing the heaven-escape model. He says the view "wasn't the view of Jesus," but says "we'll get to that" and only partly does so in this talk.

The talk ends with a short personal account of evangelical students asking him whether he is sure of going to heaven, a note on his own formation, and an invitation to his member Zoom meetings in October and to his books. The talk is almost entirely Tabor speaking; there is no guest. Several of his questions, including whether the Dead Sea Scrolls community held the dualist view, are raised but not answered within the hour.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Two worldviews: the lockative cosmos and the utopian escape
Tabor sets an older "lockative" view against a later "utopian" one. In the older view, religion is about etiquette and conformity, the gods are in heaven, humans belong on the earth, and the goal is to fulfill one's appointed role in a bounded cosmos, marked by festivals and harvests. In the later view, the material world is "an evil imprisoned realm," the soul is alien to it, and salvation means "rescue from mortality, death and the despotism of earthly rulers." He attributes the shift to a "cosmic crisis" when the cosmos was expanded into seven planetary spheres, so that the earth became "the pit at the bottom" of a system of gates and rulers. He describes this as a "bad idea" that became contagious. He frames the whole argument as a question about what religion is for: "any religion... ask three questions" about what one is saved from, how, and for what.

### 2. The Hebrew Bible's original view of death
Tabor argues that Genesis and Job present no immortal soul. He translates *nephesh* as "living breather" and notes that the dead are called "dead nephesh." He reads Genesis 2:7 as the breath of life shared by all breathing animals, and Psalm 115:17 as the dead being cut off from praise. He describes the dead as "silence," "dark, silent, asleep," in *Sheol*, which he calls "not a location" but a state of being dead. On Genesis 2:17 and Adam's lost tree of life, he says the point is "not so much a loss of immortality... it's that humans never received it." He reads the Job passage ("as waters fail from a lake") as a statement that the normal fate is not to rise again. Tabor acknowledges that resurrection appears later, but he treats the earlier view as the baseline.

### 3. Comparative material: Gilgamesh, Achilles, Socrates
Tabor uses the Gilgamesh epic to show that the Mesopotamian view of death was similar: Siduri's advice to "let your belly be full" and accept the good earth, and the rejection of the quest for eternal life. He cites Achilles in Odyssey book 11 saying he would rather serve as a hired hand on earth than rule among the dead. He gives Socrates's "weep not for me" and Empedocles's "I was once a fish, a bird, and now a man" as the Greek turn toward soul-escape and transmigration. He also presents Socrates's refusal to take his own life as a sign of the idea that one was "sent here for a purpose."

### 4. The gold tablets and Plato's cave
Tabor presents the gold lamellae placed by the ear of the dead as evidence of the new soul-geography. He reads one short inscription, in which the soul is thirsty and is told to drink from the "everflowing spring," and a longer one that describes the underworld and how to get past the "lake of forgetfulness" by knowing who one is. He glosses this as "gnosticism," saying "knowledge" comes from the Greek *gnō-*, and reads the soul's self-description as "a child of earth and starry heaven, but my race... is heaven alone." He reads Plato's cave allegory as the same movement: the real world is above, and the earthly world is shadows. He notes this is a metaphor, not a literal cosmology.

### 5. Heavenly ascent and the Roman spread
Tabor argues that heavenly ascent "shattered the Hebrew dome." He points to 1 Enoch 39, where Enoch is taken up and sees the resting places of the righteous, and where the patriarchs are "no longer sleeping in the dust." He cites Cicero's account of Scipio the Younger's dream, where the spirit is "the true self" and the earth looks tiny from above. He says this spread through the Roman world and then affected Jews and Christians. He poses but does not settle the question of whether the early Jesus movement and the Dead Sea Scroll community held this view.

### 6. The Hebrew and New Testament hope: a restored earth
Tabor's counter-argument is that the Hebrew and New Testament hope is for the earth itself. He lists Psalm 139 (God present even in *Sheol*), Isaiah 25-26 (swallowing up death, "dwellers in the dust, awake"), Daniel 12 ("many of those who sleep in the dust shall awake"), and Zechariah 14. He calls this a "linear" history with the dead "dropping off" and resting until the end, when "everything that people think of as heaven comes down to the restored earth." He describes the Jewish goal as *tikkun olam*, fixing the world. On Jesus, he says the teaching is about "this age and the age to come" (Luke 20:34), so resurrection is "transformation not resuscitation." On Paul, he cites Romans 8 for creation's liberation from decay and for a "spiritual body" that is still a body. He also says there is no clear "evacuation plan."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Popular belief:** the Bible teaches that the soul escapes the earth to go to heaven. **Tabor's correction:** the Hebrew Bible has no immortal soul; the dead rest in *Sheol* and "silence," and the hope is for the restoration of the earth.
- **Popular belief:** "the world is not my home; I'm just passing through" (a hymn Tabor sang as a conservative evangelical). **Tabor's correction:** this is Hellenistic dualism, not the Hebrew or early Christian view; the earth is the place for love and the place God's kingdom comes to.
- **Popular belief:** resurrection means the dead body being revived. **Tabor's correction:** resurrection is transformation into a perfected, glorified body; he cites Michael Jackson's "Thriller" video as the sort of image he rejects (a passing comparison, not an argument).
- **Popular belief:** the Christian question "if you died tonight, would you go to heaven?" is what Jesus taught. **Tabor's correction:** Jesus, as Tabor reads the Gospels, answered "What can I do to inherit eternal life?" with "keep the commandments," and his teaching is about "this age and the age to come."
