## Summary

Tabor asks what the earliest followers of Jesus actually meant by "resurrection," and whether their belief in it could coexist with knowing that Jesus' body lay in a tomb. He argues that resurrection of the dead in the Jewish sense means a bodily return to life at the end of days, not the survival of an immortal soul. He sets this against the Platonic idea of the soul escaping the body, which he says has shaped Western readings of the Bible. He grounds the Jewish background in Ezekiel 37, Daniel 12, and the Sadducee debate in Luke 20, and treats the raisings of Elijah, Elisha, and Jesus as revivals of corpses rather than resurrection in the full sense.

His central evidence is 1 Corinthians 15. Tabor calls Paul's list of appearances (Cephas, the Twelve, the 500, James, and himself) the only first-person eyewitness testimony to the risen Jesus that survives. He argues that Paul received this tradition in Jerusalem from Peter and James (Galatians 1:18-19), and that Paul describes the risen Jesus as a "life-giving spirit" with a transformed body, not a revived corpse. By contrast, he holds that the appearances with flesh, wounds, and food in Luke 24 and John 20-21 are later and apologetic, and that Matthew 28:11-15 shows Jews in the tradition knew the body had been moved. He dates Mark through John to roughly 70-100 CE and treats the gospels as pseudonymous. He says Part II, which he has announced but which is not part of this talk, will examine the empty tomb narratives and argue that the first burial was temporary and that Joseph of Arimathea later moved the body to a family tomb.

The talk ends with a note that the late evangelical scholar Michael Heiser reviewed Tabor's essay and urged readers not to dismiss it, and that the two exchanged comments. Tabor presents this as an open exchange rather than a settled result.

## Key Teachings

### Resurrection is bodily return to this world, not the immortal soul
Tabor contrasts two ideas: the survival of an inner self after death (Socrates, Cicero, and later Clement, Origen, and Augustine via Plato), and resurrection, which he defines as the return of the whole person to embodied life. He draws on Ezekiel 37's dry bones as the core image, and on Daniel 12:1-4 ("multitudes of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake") as the first clear statement of resurrection at the end of days. He says the Hebrew idiom and the Greek word *anastasis* ("to stand up") both point toward bodily return.

### Corpse revival is not resurrection
He lists the Hebrew and Gospel raisings (1 Kings 17; 2 Kings 4; Mark 5:41-43; Luke 7:11-17; John 11:43-44) and argues that these people died again, so they are revivals, not resurrection in the sense Jews and Jesus' followers expected. He cites Jesus' answer in Luke 20:34-40 to the Sadducees as evidence that the risen dead would be in a transformed, angel-like body, with no marriage.

### Paul's "spiritual body" and the first witnesses
Tabor reads 1 Corinthians 15:35-50 as Paul's insistence that the risen body is *pneumatikos*, a spiritual body, and that "flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God" (15:50). He argues that Paul's list of appearances (15:3-8) was received in Jerusalem from Peter and James, so the earliest tradition, before Luke and John, denied a reanimated body. He presents Paul's own claim to have "seen the Lord" as a first-person witness in the same category.

### The gospels' appearances are later and apologetic
He argues that Mark, which has no appearances in its earliest form, is the earliest gospel, and that Matthew's appearances are theological (the Great Commission at 28:18-19 is "the world of theology," not history). He says Luke 24:39 and John 20:24-29 and 21:9-14, with flesh, bones, and fish, were written against pagan critics such as Celsus, who charged that the appearances were hysteria. He calls these accounts "secondary and legendary" for the reasons above.

### The body was moved, and Matthew admits as much
Tabor reads Matthew 28:11-15 ("the disciples came and stole him away") as evidence that a Jewish-circulated story already acknowledged the body was gone, and says the "part was true" element is that the body was taken at night and buried by Joseph of Arimathea, with Pilate's permission. He argues that people in Jerusalem knew where the body was, and that followers would have honored the tomb in the way Jews honor the tombs of the tzadikim. This is the bridge to Part II.

### Dating, pseudonymity, and distance from the first community
He dates Paul to the 50s CE and the gospels to 70-100 CE or later. He says the gospel names are traditional titles added later, so "we don't know their real authors." He says the Q material and the letter of James focus on apocalyptic ethics and the imminent kingdom, with no incarnational theology, and that James mentions "his brother Jesus" only twice. He concludes that the Jerusalem community's faith is best reconstructed from Paul, James, and Q.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Resurrection means the immortal soul surviving death.** Tabor says this confuses Greek (Platonic) immortality with Jewish bodily resurrection.
- **Resurrection means a corpse being revived.** He says raisings like Lazarus's were revivals, and that resurrection in the Jewish sense is a transformed body at the end of days.
- **The earliest followers believed in a risen body with flesh and wounds.** He argues that this comes from Luke and John, which were written later and against pagan critics.
- **An empty tomb would be impossible to reconcile with resurrection faith.** He argues that followers could honor a known tomb as Jews honor the tombs of the righteous, and says this is a question for Part II.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor reads Paul's 1 Corinthians 15 as an early, first-hand account that already differs from the later gospels' physical resurrection, and says Paul's theology of atonement, baptism, and the Eucharist moves away from Jesus.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** He relies on Galatians 1:18-19 and the letter of James to argue that Jerusalem's faith was focused on apocalyptic ethics and the kingdom, not incarnational theology.
- **The kingdom on earth:** He argues from Isaiah 65:17 and Revelation 21 that the biblical hope is God's renewed earth, not escape to heaven.
- **Resurrection, afterlife, and hell:** The talk is mainly a contrast between Platonic immortality and Jewish bodily resurrection at the end of days.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** He dates the gospels to 70-100 CE, calls them pseudonymous, and says Mark (with no appearances) is the earliest, while Matthew, Luke, and John add later apologetic material.
