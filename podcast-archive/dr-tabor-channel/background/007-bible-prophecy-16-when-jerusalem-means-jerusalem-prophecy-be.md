## Summary

Tabor opens by asking how Christian readers of prophecy have related to Jerusalem as a real, physical place, as opposed to a heavenly or spiritual one. He counts the references: "Jerusalem" appears 764 times in the RSV, and he separates geographical mentions from prophetic ones that "project a vision of jerusalem in the future." He says he is most interested in the prophetic passages that point to an "on the ground" Jerusalem, one people actually go to, as opposed to waiting for Jesus to take believers "either over here or to heaven." His main text is Isaiah 2:2-4, which he calls the "banner verse" for the groups he studies and notes is inscribed on a wall at the United Nations. He traces a shift from the mainstream church's "supersessionist" reading, which spiritualizes Israel, Jerusalem and Torah, to the 19th-century dispensationalism of John Nelson Darby. Tabor argues that Darby's system restored a literal future for the Jewish people in the land and encouraged Christian Zionism, which he says now dominates American evangelical fundamentalism.

To test this, Tabor walks through four case studies: Charles Taze Russell's Bible Students and their 1910 call for Jews to return to Palestine; Horatio and Anna Spafford's American Colony in Jerusalem, founded after the 1871 Chicago fire and the drowning of their four daughters; Andrew Dugger's 1933 move of Church of God (Seventh Day) followers to Jerusalem, with Herbert Armstrong as a former student; and David Koresh, who came to Jerusalem in 1985 and, in Tabor's account, read Revelation 14 literally and later died at Waco in 1993. Tabor describes his paper as having two strands: a messianic consciousness in which the believer acts as the figure of the end-time (Koresh), and a more popular strand in which Christians urge Jews to rebuild the land so that Jesus can come, which he calls "sinister" for Jewish readers.

The talk ends with a question from the audience about the anti-Semitism of Darby and of some Christian Zionists. Tabor says Darby was "very anti-semitic," and he notes that the support for Israel rests on a prophetic timetable that requires Jews to gather in the land before Christ comes. He calls Christian Zionism's stance "a love that's parenthesized by that caveat," and he says it can turn political. He does not resolve these tensions; he presents them as the subject of his paper, which he says is unpublished.

## Key Teachings

### Literal versus spiritual reading of Jerusalem
Tabor separates "geographical references" to the city from "prophetic references" that imagine a future Jerusalem, often "an ideal or even perfected jerusalem." He says he is interested in the prophetic passages read "on the ground," where people physically travel to the city, rather than in a heavenly transposition. He compares this with other visions of a relocated "new Zion," such as Mormons in Utah and Missouri, and the Hussite Taborites, who named a town Tabor near Prague.

### Isaiah 2 as the central proof text
He reads Isaiah 2:2-4 at length, emphasizing "in that day" and "at that time" phrasing, and says the nations "will say come let us go up to the mountain of the lord." He points out that Isaiah 2 "is not a messianic passage" because it names no messiah and attributes the event directly to God. He adds that the Soviet Union donated a statue of a man beating a sword into a plowshare, quoting Isaiah, to the UN in 1956.

### Supersessionism and the spiritualization of the Hebrew Bible
Tabor describes the mainstream church's position as "supersessionism": Israel becomes "spiritual Israel," Jerusalem becomes "heavenly Jerusalem," and the Torah becomes "written in the heart." He traces this to Paul's allegory in which "jerusalem above" is the "mother of us all" and earthly Jerusalem corresponds to the slave woman Hagar. He presents this as the view that the church fathers laid down, while noting that Paul's Jewish opponents would not have welcomed it.

### Darby, dispensationalism and Christian Zionism
Tabor credits John Nelson Darby, a former Anglican clergyman in the 1830s, with reversing the spiritualizing trend through dispensationalism, which spread through the Schofield Bible. In his account, Darby's view is that Christians share in the "spiritual blessings" while "the physical ones still belong to the jews," so Jews remain "the physical heirs of the promised land" and should gather and prepare it for the messiah. Tabor says this view now dominates American evangelical fundamentalism "by far," which he estimates at "90" percent (the transcript cuts off the figure's unit).

### Herzl, Russell and the early Christian Zionist network
Tabor says Theodor Herzl was connected to Christian Zionists, including a figure he names "Heckler" (Hechler), who introduced him to Kaiser Wilhelm II. He quotes a childhood dream Herzl recorded in his diary, in which the Messiah sends him to tell the Jews to return. Tabor states that David Lloyd George and the Balfour Declaration were shaped by Darby-influenced Christian Zionism, and that the Balfour declaration's language "is a statement of faith based upon interpretation of the prophets." He says Russell, who "would hate" being called the founder of Jehovah's Witnesses, founded the Bible Students and in 1910 urged Jews to return to Palestine at a New York Hippodrome meeting, speculating that the Jews would be back in the land before 1914.

### The Spaffords and the American Colony
Tabor tells the story of Horatio and Anna Spafford, who lived in Chicago, were devoted to helping fire victims after 1871, and lost their four daughters in a shipwreck on a trip to Europe. He reads the story of Anna's telegram and Horatio's hymn "It Is Well with My Soul." He argues that Anna took the loss as a call to go to Jerusalem to prepare for Jesus' return in 1881, and that the group lived as a communal, partly celibate community that later became the American Colony Hotel. He reports a rumor from the 1890s about the group's sexual practices, which he says was "probably perfectly innocent," and he uses the story to illustrate the difference between groups that move to the land and groups that say "we can be anywhere."

### Dugger, Armstrong and the Mount Zion excavation
Tabor describes Andrew Dugger, who in 1933 convinced Church of God (Seventh Day) followers to move to Jerusalem and choose twelve apostles, seventy elders and a sanhedrin-like council by casting lots. He says Herbert Armstrong was one of the seventy and later broke with Dugger. He recounts that after the 1967 war, Armstrong's Ambassador College sponsored an excavation south of the Temple Mount led by Benjamin Mazar, with funds of $5 million and 50 students in 1968. Armstrong's shift, in Tabor's account, was from a heavenly Jerusalem to a concern about "clearing away" the rubble so that Christ could sit on a literal throne.

### David Koresh and the 144,000 on Mount Zion
Tabor says Koresh came to Jerusalem in 1985 and went first to the Christian Mount Zion, believing it was the site described in Revelation 14, where the Lamb stands with 144,000 followers. He reports that Koresh concluded the empty plot near the Dormition Abbey was big enough for 144,000 people and that he expected to fight with Israel in the last battle. Tabor connects the Waco tragedy to a date-based apocalyptic timetable from Daniel (the 70 weeks, 1335, 1290 and 1260 days) that Koresh expected to be fulfilled in 1995, and he argues the government "delivered david an apocalypse two years too early in 1993."
