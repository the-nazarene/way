## Summary

Tabor revisits a 1989 article he wrote for a volume edited by Morton Smith, titled "What the Bible Really Says About..." His assigned topic was "death, afterlife, and the future," which he narrows here to death and afterlife. The question he sets is what the Hebrew Bible and related Old and New Testament and Apocrypha texts actually say about death, rather than what readers assume they say. He calls his view a "triple deck" cosmos, in which the dead go down to Sheol and do not ascend. He argues that the Hebrew Bible's default view is that death ends active life and that the dead do not come back. His evidence includes Genesis 3 ("dust you are"), Psalm 80, the Saul and Samuel episode, Job, Ecclesiastes, and parallels from Mesopotamian and Homeric texts. He traces a later development through Isaiah 26, Isaiah 24-27, Daniel 12, Wisdom of Solomon 2, and 2 Maccabees 7, moving from nothingness to life in Sheol, then to immortality of the soul, then to bodily resurrection. He presents Paul's "spiritual body" as the most developed form of this idea.

The talk ends on a moral and theological note. Tabor argues that people who say they would not be good without an afterlife have a problem, citing Plato. He concludes that the older Hebrew view has its own wisdom, since it places the choice of life or death in the present and leaves God's ultimate judgment unknown. ## Key Teachings

### Dust, breath, and Sheol
Tabor reads Genesis 3 ("dust you are... to dust you shall return") as the key text for death. He translates nephesh as "living breather," a term that covers animals as well as humans. Living breathers are mortal, and "their thoughts perish" (Psalm 146 is not cited here; the point rests on the Genesis language). The dead go to Sheol, which he describes as a place of unknown, shadowy existence. He links Sheol to the Greek Hades, meaning "unseen," and says the Hebrew bible gives little information about the place. He says that in the Hebrew Bible nobody returns from Sheol except Samuel, who appears to Saul through a medium. Tabor says Sheol is not torment at this stage; it is more like being "taken out of the game."

### The cosmos and the ordering of Genesis
Tabor describes the ancient Near Eastern cosmos as a three-tiered world: heaven under a firmament, earth, and chaotic waters below and on either side. In his reading, Genesis 1 is not creation from nothing ("that's a Greek idea") but an ordering of heavens and earth out of chaos. Psalm 115 is quoted: "the heavens are the Lord's, but the earth he has given to the sons of men... the dead do not praise the Lord." He presents Deuteronomy's statement that the Torah is "not in heaven" and not across the sea but "right here in your hand and in your heart." He mentions Enoch, Gilgamesh's Noah figure, and the Isles of the Blessed as exceptions or legends, not the normal destination.

### Job and Ecclesiastes as lament
Tabor reads Job as a lament that reflects the standard view: "man dies and is laid low... as waters fail from a lake." He argues that Job's wish that his words be engraved in rock with an iron pen is a request for a legal record. The "redeemer" in Job 19 is a legal advocate who will read the case before God. In his reading, this is not a resurrection or messianic promise. He notes that the King James rendering invites that reading, and he says most scholars agree the translation "handles messiah away" in a way the text does not support. Ecclesiastes is presented as a despairing view that the fate of humans and beasts is the same, and that death brings no return. Tabor compares this to Homer, where Achilles' ghost says he would rather be a servant in the world of light than a king among the dead.

### Late development: Isaiah, Daniel, Wisdom, and Maccabees
Tabor argues that belief in resurrection appears late and in stages:
- **Isaiah 26** says "your dead will live" in a passage he calls part of the "Isaiah apocalypse" (Isaiah 24-27), which most scholars date to late, likely exilic or post-exilic times. He notes the wording is ambiguous ("your body" in the singular, possibly addressed to God).
- **Daniel 12** is "the clear one," but resurrection is not universal: "many of those who sleep in the dust" awake, some to everlasting life and some to shame. Tabor notes Daniel sits among the Writings, at the end of the Hebrew Bible.
- **Wisdom of Solomon 2** presents the wicked view of death ("our life is short and sorrowful... no one has been known to return from Hades") and then the righteous view that the souls of the righteous are in God's hand.
- **2 Maccabees 7** tells of seven brothers and their mother who are tortured. He reads the fourth brother's declaration that it is good to be put to death, to hope in God for raising, and the tyrant's line that there will be no resurrection for the tyrant.

He lays out a sequence: nothing, then life in Sheol, then immortality of the soul, then bodily resurrection. He places the Sadducee and Pharisee debate in the Maccabean period.

### Paul and the resurrection body
Tabor calls Paul "the most developed view" of resurrection. He cites 1 Corinthians 15 on a "physical body and a spiritual body," and says the resurrected will bear "the image of the heavenly." He draws on 1 Enoch's image of heavenly clothing waiting in heaven, arguing that the believer puts off the earthly body, is naked in Sheol for a time, then receives a new body. He says Paul's view explains how resurrected people can move through walls and not gain weight, and he adds that Paul told people not to ask exactly how it will look. He cites Romans 8 for the new "family" of God's sons, with Jesus as the firstborn, who will judge angels. He also reads the Sadducee question in the Gospels as a joke about marriage in the resurrection, with Jesus answering that people will be "like angels."

### Martyrdom and the question of motive
Tabor takes up the claim that the afterlife is necessary for morality, and that the idea of afterlife grew from martyrdom. He cites Plato's view that a person who is good only for reward is not good. He concludes that the Hebrew view has merit because it places the choice of "life or death" before the person now, and leaves God's ways "none of your business." He says he would not argue against the Hebrew view theologically, and that he wants the other views to be placed alongside it.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Genesis is creation out of nothing.** Tabor says Genesis 1 is an ordering of heavens and earth out of chaos, and that creation from nothing is a Greek idea.
- **Job 19 proclaims a living redeemer who will raise him from the dead.** Tabor says the "redeemer" is a legal advocate reading Job's case, and that the King James reading "handles messiah away."
- **Sheol is hell.** Tabor says Sheol is a shadowy place of forgetfulness and oblivion, and that torment, if it comes, comes much later.
- **The Hebrew Bible teaches heaven or immortality for the righteous.** Tabor argues these ideas appear late, in texts such as Wisdom of Solomon and Daniel, and that the older view is that the dead do not return.
- **Faith is only worthwhile if there is an afterlife reward.** Tabor argues the Hebrew view is valuable because it places the choice of life and death in the present, and does not rely on reward.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Jesus vs. Paul on the resurrection:** Tabor reads Jesus' answer to the Sadducees (people "like angels") as close to Paul's view of the transformed body, and presents Paul as the most developed form of the idea, though he does not contrast Jesus and Paul on this point directly.
- **Kingdom on earth:** Tabor reads Isaiah 11's peaceable kingdom as a literal, earthly hope and notes that the dead are not explicitly included in it. The Gospels' picture of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob at table in the kingdom is presented as a later development.
- **Resurrection, afterlife, and hell:** The talk is almost entirely about this. Tabor traces a development from Sheol to immortality to bodily resurrection, and treats Sheol as neither heaven nor hell.
- **Pharisees vs. Sadducees:** He places the debate in the Maccabean period and reads Wisdom of Solomon as taking the Pharisaic side against a Sadducean denial of afterlife, though he is not using these sources to describe the Essenes or the Nazarenes or Ebionites.
- **Torah:** Tabor cites Deuteronomy's claim that the Torah is "in your hand and in your heart," and argues that the Torah does not depend on heaven or an afterlife.
