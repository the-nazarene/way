## Summary

The talk is an extended interview in which James Tabor presents his new English translation of Genesis, which he calls a "transparent" translation. The question is how a translation can let readers see the Hebrew behind the English and avoid the theological layers that Tabor says later English versions have added. Tabor is the main speaker throughout, and LeBlanc mostly asks questions and comments. The transcript has no speaker labels, so attributions here follow the flow of the conversation.

Tabor's method, as he describes it, is to translate the Leningrad Codex word by word, render a repeated Hebrew term with the same English word each time, keep Hebrew idioms and repetitions, and place Dead Sea Scrolls and Septuagint variants in footnotes rather than in the text. He says he worked from the Hebrew alone to avoid influence from Alter, Fox, Friedman, or older English versions, and that he consulted the Chicago Hebrew Bible scholar he names (spelled "Robert Hawk" in the captions) during the work. He presents the method as the product of about fifteen years of studying every occurrence of key terms.

The main interpretive claims concern Genesis 1 to 3. Tabor argues that Genesis 1:1 with its "at the first of Elohim creating" construction describes how this planet was ordered from a chaotic, desolate state, not the creation of the universe from nothing. He reads *Elohim* as "the force of all forces," rejects the reading of *nephesh* as an immortal soul (calling it "living breather"), argues that the world is good rather than a prison, and reads the eating of the tree of knowledge as a coming-of-age event rather than a fall. He supports these readings mainly with comparisons across the Hebrew Bible (Jeremiah 26:1, Psalm 82, Exodus 6:3, Deuteronomy 1:39) and with word-play in Hebrew, such as *nakash* ("serpent") and *arum* ("shrewd") in Genesis 2 to 3. The talk ends with Tabor's wish to extend the method to the Torah and the wider Tanakh, and with LeBlanc's remark that the book's footnotes and white-space section breaks are worth reading.

## Key Teachings

### A "transparent" translation method
Tabor says the aim is for readers to "hear see and feel the original text without knowing Hebrew." He translates the Leningrad Codex, which he calls "our oldest complete Hebrew manuscript," and he says he does not judge the text. Where the Dead Sea Scrolls or the Greek Septuagint differ, the differences go into footnotes. He keeps one English term for one Hebrew term where possible. His example is *nephesh chayah*, which he renders "living creature" throughout rather than varying it. He also keeps Hebrew repetition for emphasis, such as "eating you will eat," and reads "Isaac loves his food" in Genesis 27 as three separate statements of love. He describes his governing principle as translating "on the basis of the words and in connection with the words," and he cites an old Jewish adage: "one who translates a verse literally is misrepresenting the text but one who adds anything of his own is a blasphemer." He says he stays close to the literal but avoids what he judges to be nonsense.

### "At the first of Elohim creating": ordering rather than creation
This is the centerpiece. Tabor argues that Genesis 1:1 should not read "In the beginning God created the heavens and the Earth." He says the Hebrew is a temporal construct (*bereshit*), and he compares it with Jeremiah 26:1, "in the beginning of the reign of Zedekiah," to argue that it introduces a starting condition rather than a single act. In his rendering, verses 1 and 2 describe what the earth was like when God began: "desolate and empty," with darkness on the deep and a wind or spirit "fluttering" over the waters. He compares this to pictures of the Moon or Mars. He concludes that the text is "not telling you How the Universe began it's telling you how this particular planet" was ordered in seven stages. He also says the text has no *ex nihilo* idea, which he calls "an idea that's so famous in Latin" but "not the idea here." He rejects the idea that the chapter describes DNA-like coding, and he does not settle the evolution question, saying only that it should be discussed in this context.

### *Elohim* as "the force of all forces"
Tabor explains that *Elohim* is plural with a singular verb, and he reads the plural as compounding meaning, which he renders "the force of all forces." He cites Psalm 82 (which he calls "82") and the use of *elohim* for judges to show that the word can mean other powers. He treats *El Shaddai* as a further epithet. For the four-letter name (YHWH), he cites Exodus 6:3 and the phrase "I will be what I will be" (*ehyeh asher ehyeh*). He argues that the name is not a limiting label and that this distinguishes the God of Genesis from deities with a beginning. He compares the usage to Rabbi Jonathan Sacks's preference for "I shall be what I shall be." He does not decide how the four letters should be pronounced and says that is a separate topic. In the translation he writes the four letters as "yhvh" so that readers can see where the name is.

### The goodness of the material world
Tabor argues that the repeated word *tov* ("good") in Genesis 1, and the summary that all God made was "very good," rejects the dualism that he says runs through most religions. He calls the Hebrew Bible "monistic" and rejects what he calls "Gnostic Hellenistic views." He says the world is "not a prison" and is "to be celebrated." He backs this with a physical point about the elements that make up the body and with references to physicists, Ken Wilber's work, and Roger Penrose (named in the captions as a physicist and philosopher). These comparisons are asides, and Tabor says he does not want to spend long on them.

### *Nephesh*: living breather, not immortal soul
Tabor translates the man in Genesis 2 as becoming a "living breather" rather than "a living soul." He says the standard rendering comes from "Greek Hellenistic dualism" and that *nephesh* actually means the throat, or the breathing creature. He notes that fish also have the "breath of life," that a *nephesh* can be dead, and that "soul" in older English simply meant "individual." He connects this to the tree of life in Genesis 3, saying that eternal life is not inherent in the human. He adds that other passages speak of the spirit returning to God, but he says whether that is a mystical idea "would have to be discussed." The human is "soil" (*adam* from *adamah*, rendered "soil man") and returns to dust.

### The knowledge of good and bad: coming of age, not fall
Tabor argues that "knowing good and bad" (he avoids "evil" as theologically loaded) means experiencing it, in the way "Adam knows his wife." He cites Deuteronomy 1:39, where children "do not yet have the knowledge of Good and Evil," to argue that eating the fruit is a maturation step. He says the human was created to become a god-like being who makes choices, and that leaving Eden is "a necessary consequence of growing up" rather than a punishment. He contrasts this with the Christian concept of original sin, which he says he struggled with personally. He compares it to the Jewish *bar mitzvah* and "age of accountability" in Christianity, and he says "we are little Gods." He also says that humans can do anything, good or bad, which he sees as frightening.

### Serpent, nude, shrewd: a Hebrew word-play
Tabor says Genesis 2:25 uses *arum* ("nude" or "naked"), and Genesis 3:1 uses *arum* for the serpent, which he renders "shrewd." He notes that the serpent's name, *nachash*, is in his reading "the shining one" and cites its link with bronze or brass ("the brass serpent," which he says Hezekiah destroyed). He describes the passage as symbolic and "mythological," and says it addresses the human encounter with knowledge. He sees the naked pair as "innocent" and "like children" before they gain that knowledge.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Genesis 1:1 describes creation of the universe from nothing.** Tabor says the Hebrew is a temporal construct describing how this planet was ordered from a prior state, and that *ex nihilo* is "an idea that's so famous in Latin" but not in the Hebrew.
- **The first verse is a single completed act ("In the beginning God created...").** Tabor argues the construct introduces a process, and he compares it to "at the first of the reign of Zedekiah" in Jeremiah 26:1.
- **The human has an immortal soul (*nephesh*).** Tabor says *nephesh* is the breathing creature or throat, and that animals and fish share the same term.
- **The Eden story is a fall into sin and punishment.** Tabor reads it as a coming-of-age event, and he rejects the Christian concept of original sin as foreign to Jewish thought.
- **The material world is a dark, fallen prison.** Tabor argues the Hebrew Bible calls creation "very good" and rejects Gnostic and Hellenistic dualism.
- **"Lord" in English versions is the name of God.** Tabor says the capitals follow the Jewish practice of not pronouncing the name, and that the name is actually present in the text as the four letters.
- **"Help meet" and "wife" are fixed meanings.** Tabor says *ezer kenegdo* means "a partner, a helper that will correspond to him," and that the Hebrew word for "wife" is the same as "woman."
- **"Gentile" is the meaning of *goy* in Genesis 12.** Tabor says it means "nation."
- **The Hebrew *nachash* is simply "snake."** Tabor says it is a word-play with *arum* and means "shining" or "shrewd."
