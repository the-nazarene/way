## Summary

Tabor opens Part 1 of a two-part series on why interpreters have repeatedly failed when predicting the "End of the Age." Part 1 is preliminary: it defines the terms ("End of the Age," "latter days," "time of the end," "age to come") and surveys the Hebrew Bible and early Jewish texts for what these phrases mean. Part 2, he says, will deal with the timing of the apocalypse, how and why prophecies fail, and the standard reactions to failure.

Tabor argues that the phrases describe a period or event rather than a calendar date. He says the King James "end of the world" wording in Matthew 24 misleads, and that the Hebrew Bible has little about the end of the world as later Christians picture it. He walks through Daniel 12, Isaiah 2 and Micah 4, Jeremiah, Ezekiel 38, Isaiah 24-27, Ezekiel 37-40 and Zechariah 14. He observes that these passages describe restoration, peace, regathering and resurrection, but that none of them gives a timetable. He then turns to Hosea 6:1-3 and the Second Temple habit of reading "after two days... on the third day" as a countdown, and to Daniel 9's 490 years. He cites Paul, 1 Peter, James, Revelation and the Dead Sea Scrolls as examples of expectations that the end was imminent.

The talk ends with the Habakkuk commentary (pesher) from Qumran. Tabor reads its response to delayed prophecy: the final age "shall be prolonged and shall exceed all that the prophets have said." He leaves the question of how to handle failed prophecy open for Part 2. The transcript ends with a music cue, not a conclusion.

## Key Teachings

### "End of the Age" is a period, not "end of the world"

Tabor argues that the King James rendering of Matthew 24 ("end of the world") is "a really bad translation" and "very misleading"; he says it should read "End of the Age." He states that "technically speaking it's always in all the texts we're going to cover the End of the Age meaning a period of time." He notes that Isaiah's "new heavens and a new earth" is about a renewed earth more than an ending, and that the Olam Haba/"age to come" idea in later Judaism carries the same confusion.

### The phrase "time of the end" and its Hebrew terms

Tabor works through Daniel's uses. In Daniel 12 the phrase is literally "time of the end" (*kets*, from the verb for cutting off). In Daniel 8:19 the word is *moed*, an "appointment," so "history is going to meet at a certain point." Daniel 11:40 says "at the time of the end the King of the South will attack the King of the North." He cites Mark 10:30 to show that "eternal life" was already linked to the "time of the end" or "coming age" in the Second Temple period.

### "Latter days" has no built-in clock

Tabor surveys about a dozen uses of "latter days" or "last days" in the Hebrew Bible. Genesis 49:1 may refer to the days to come for the tribes; Numbers 24:14 may concern Moab; Deuteronomy 31:29 may describe the exile. He states that "the latter days it doesn't have a clock running on it so to speak." Context decides the meaning. Isaiah 2 and Micah 4 (the nations streaming to Zion to learn Torah, and swords beaten into plowshares) and Jeremiah 23 and 30-31 (with the New Covenant and restoration of the tribes) go beyond ordinary history, and he reads them as the Messianic or apocalyptic sense. He mentions the UN wall inscription quoting Isaiah 2.

### The Davidic figure and the Messianic passages

Tabor says there are "only 10 passages" in the Hebrew Bible about a Davidic figure, and none calls him "Messiah," though priests and kings were anointed. Isaiah 11 (a *netzer*, "branch," from the stump of Jesse), Jeremiah 23:5, Micah 5:1 ("until the time when this ruler appears") and others give no timing. He says the figure regathers the ten tribes, brings peace among the nations and fits Isaiah 2's "last days" scenario. He notes that Christians read the figure as Jesus, while Jews who do not accept Jesus still expect a Messiah.

### Apocalyptic texts: Isaiah 24-27, Ezekiel 37-40, Zechariah 14

Tabor calls Isaiah 24-27 the "Isaiah apocalypse," a label he attributes to scholars. He reads it as containing the swallowing up of death, the resurrection of the dead ("thy dead shall live," Isaiah 26:19), the punishment of Leviathan and the dragon, and a reign of the Lord on Mount Zion. He says the sense of the passage "doesn't seem to fit" the Assyrian period. Ezekiel 37-40 (the two "sticks" joined, then Gog and Magog, then the rebuilt Temple) and Zechariah 14 (the battle over Jerusalem, the Mount of Olives splitting, the Lord king over all the earth) are also treated as apocalyptic. Tabor says Zechariah 14 was written around 520 BCE and that its battles did not match the Assyrian, Babylonian or Roman sieges.

### Hosea 6 and the "two days, third day" countdown

Tabor reads Hosea 6:1-3 ("after two days he will revive us and on the third day he will raise us up") as a source of Second Temple chronology. He notes that in the ancient world the third day was when a person was truly dead, and explains the "Saved by the Bell" custom as coming from coffin bells, not from boxing. He points to John's Lazarus story, where the wait until the third day shows that Lazarus was really dead. He notes that Psalm 90:4 ("a thousand years... as yesterday") was taken literally in the Second Temple period, so that "two days" became two thousand years and the Messiah was expected on "the third day." He calls the Second Temple period "a Heyday of apocalyptic expectation."

### Daniel 9 and the Second Temple countdown

Tabor says Daniel 9's 490 years (the "70 weeks") was the calculation used in this period, and that Second Peter appears to revise Daniel's interpretation. He defers detailed treatment of Daniel to Part 2.

### Expectations of imminence: Paul, the Gospels, James, Revelation, Qumran

Tabor cites Paul's letters: "the appointed time of the end," "the time has grown very short," and advice not to marry or start businesses because "the form of this world is already passing away." He says Paul expected to live to see the coming of Jesus. He lists Matthew, Mark and Luke's "this generation will not pass" sayings, Luke as "maybe the latest of those three," the Johannine Gospel as not dealing with it much, 1 Peter ("the end of all things is at hand"), James ("be patient... the Lord's coming is near") and Revelation ("I'm coming soon"). He says the Dead Sea Scrolls community, from "around 100 BC BCE" through the first century, believed it was in the end times, and that Qumran texts refer to "the time of the end" some twenty or thirty times in his notes.

### The Habakkuk commentary and the prolonged age

Tabor's closing example is the Habakkuk commentary (pesher) from Qumran, columns six and seven. The commentary says God told Habakkuk to write what would happen to the final generation but did not make known when the end would come. "Read speedily" refers to the Teacher of Righteousness, to whom God revealed the mysteries of the prophets. The commentary then says there is "yet another Vision concerning the appointed time," and that "the final age shall be prolonged and shall exceed all that the prophets have said." Tabor reads this as the community's response to delay. Habakkuk 2:4 ("the righteous will live by faith") is, in his reading, the faith to wait longer. He contrasts Paul's use of "by faith" with the Qumran sense. He sets this aside for Part 2.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"End of the world" is the biblical term.** Tabor says the KJV's "end of the world" in Matthew 24 misleads; the Hebrew and Greek concern an "age" or period, and the Hebrew Bible does not really speak of the end of the world as later Christians picture it.
- **"Saved by the Bell" comes from boxing.** Tabor says "we think the origin actually has to do with" coffin bells used in 17th and 18th century European and American burials, for people who might be in a coma.
- **Prophecy has clear timing in the Hebrew Bible.** Tabor says the latter-days and Messianic passages carry no timetable and that the time-clock developed in the Second Temple period.
- **Prophecy can be proven false.** Tabor says prophecy "fails" only in the sense of interpretations failing, since one can always say the event is still coming unless the earth is gone.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor cites Paul's "time has grown very short" and his advice to forgo marriage and business, presenting Paul as an example of the imminence expectation shared by early followers of Jesus. He states that the early followers were "Jewish followers of Jesus" and "weren't really Christian at that point."
- **Essenes/Dead Sea Scrolls:** The Habakkuk commentary is Tabor's main case for how a community responded when prophecy was prolonged. He notes a community that continued to calculate and then wrote that "the final age shall be prolonged."
- **Kingdom on earth:** The Messianic age he describes includes regathering the tribes, peace among nations, Torah going out to the nations, and "the Lord will become king over all the Earth" (Zechariah 14). He treats these as this-age events, not as the end of the world.
- **Resurrection, afterlife:** Isaiah 26:19 ("thy dead shall live") and Hosea 6's third-day revival are the resurrection texts he discusses. He ties Mark 10:30's "eternal life" to the "coming age" as a Second Temple development.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor says the Davidic Messianic figure is never called "Messiah" in the Hebrew Bible, and that Christians identify him with Jesus while Jews who do not accept Jesus still await one. He does not state his own view of Jesus in this part.
- **Gospels and canon:** Tabor dates Matthew 24 to after 70 CE, and says Luke is "maybe the latest" of the Synoptics and John "doesn't even deal with it much," tying the gospels' treatment of imminence to their date.
