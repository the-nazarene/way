## Summary

Tabor, a historian of ancient religions, presents a class on five "great words" (Hebrew *davar*, "matters" or "concepts") from the opening chapters of Genesis, using his own translation, the Transparent English Bible. He says these five concepts were largely dropped as Judaism, Christianity and Islam absorbed a dualistic, Hellenistic view of the cosmos, and he argues they are the basis for understanding how resurrection of the dead developed, as distinct from the immortality of the soul.

Tabor's five concepts are: *nephesh* (a living "life-breather," applied to humans, land animals, birds and fish); *nishmat chayyim* (the "breath of life," which he describes as a process rather than an entity); *Elohim* and related divine titles (El Elyon, El Shaddai), which he reads as "the force of all forces"; the self-designation of YHWH as "I will be what I will be," which he treats as a description rather than a magical name; and *tzedakah* and *mishpat* (righteousness and judgment), the "way" of YHWH that Abraham is charged to keep. He supports the afterlife half of this with Ecclesiastes 3:19, Daniel 12, Ezekiel 37, Isaiah 26:19 and 2 Corinthians 5, and he presents the Greek immortal soul as the contrasting view.

The talk ends with his critique of dualism, which he says devalues the body, sexuality and the world and turns life into a pilgrimage away from earth. The captions cut off mid-sentence in that closing passage ("but why is it a horrible place"), so the talk's last point is not fully captured.

## Key Teachings

### Nephesh: the living life-breather
Tabor's first word is *nephesh*, which he translates "living life-breather" and ties to the act of breathing. He cites Genesis 1:20 (swarms of living creatures in the waters), Genesis 1:24 (living creatures from the land) and Genesis 2:7 (the man becomes a *nephesh*). His point is that humans, land animals and fish share one term. He says the King James "living soul" in Genesis 2:7 is the source of later confusion about an immortal soul, since "soul" appears only once in that chapter. A dead body, he notes, can still be a "dead *nephesh*."

### Nishmat chayyim: the breath of life as process
Second is the "breath of life" (*nishmat chayyim*) breathed into Adam's nostrils in Genesis 2:7. Tabor treats it as the breath that makes a *nephesh* alive, not as a separate immortal entity. He compares Job 27:3 ("as long as my breath is in me and the spirit of God is in my nostrils") and the flood account (Genesis 7), where all creatures in whose nostrils was "the breath of a spirit of life" die. He reads Genesis 3:19 ("for dust you are and toward dust you will return") as the reverse of 2:7, and Ecclesiastes 3:19 as stating that humans and beasts share one fate. He says the Hebrew *ruach* ("wind" or "spirit") in Job is literal breath, not the Holy Spirit. He also says the idea that the human spirit "goes upward" while the beast's goes "downward" (Ecclesiastes) is a very late view affected by Hellenistic thought.

### Elohim, El Elyon and El Shaddai: the "force of all forces"
Tabor argues that *Elohim* is a plural form with a singular verb in Genesis 1:1, which he reads as a composite "force of all forces." He says Genesis 1:1 concerns the creation of the land and skies of this planet, not the universe. He links El Elyon ("the most high God") to Melchizedek's blessing in Genesis 14, and El Shaddai, which he connects to *shad* (breast, nurture) and to destruction or protection, to the self-introduction in Genesis 17:1. He notes that the Psalms 42–83 (the "Elohim Psalms") generally use *Elohim* where other psalms use the YHWH name, and cites Psalm 51.

### YHWH: "I will be what I will be"
Tabor's account of the divine name (Exodus 3) is that the Hebrew is an imperfect verb of "to be," so the name means something like "he will be" or "he is," with the King James rendering "I am." He says the point is the concept of "pure being" or the "ground of all being" (he cites Paul Tillich), not a magic pronunciation. He argues that the Hebrew Bible refuses to personify God as the god of a specific object or place, and that names designate objects, while the force of all forces "can't be named" and therefore can't be limited. He also says the sound "Yehovah" is secondary to the meaning.

### Tzedakah and mishpat: the way of YHWH
Abraham is said in Genesis 18 to be known so that he will charge his household "to guard the way of YHWH" by doing *tzedakah* (righteousness, which he associates with behavior) and *mishpat* (judgment, which he associates with judges' decisions). He presents this pair as the purpose of Abraham's blessing to "all the nations," and contrasts it with the "Ten Words." He cites a saying attributed to Jesus of Nazareth about judging others, which he takes as an expression of *mishpat*. He also links this to the Hebrew idea of repairing the world (*tikkun olam*) and to Deuteronomy 29:29 on what is "revealed" to humans.

### Resurrection, not the immortal soul
This is the central contrast. Tabor says that at death the breath departs, the body returns to dust, and one's "plans" or "doings" perish, and he cites Ecclesiastes 3 and the Dead as "those who sleep in the dust" (Daniel 12). He reads Ezekiel 37 (the dry bones) as a recreation of Genesis 2:7, and Isaiah 26:19 ("thy dead shall live... awake and sing for joy") as the biblical hope. He describes resurrection in Daniel as "awakening," which he says later became "standing up," and he says the Sadducees' question to Jesus in the Gospels reflects the same view, though he gives no verse. He says 2 Corinthians 5 preserves an older Hebrew idea that the dead are without bodies and await re-clothing, and he presents the Greek immortal soul, which he says is "imprisoned" in the body, as the "bad idea that's taken over the world," also in Hinduism and Buddhism. He says original Buddhism lacked it.

### Critique of dualism and the body
In closing, Tabor argues that a dualistic view separates people from the earth, the body and sexuality, and that it turns celibacy and asceticism into virtues, treating the world as a dark place to pass through. He presents the Hebrew emphasis as "get it done": bring justice and light to the world in this life. The talk breaks off during this argument.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The human has an immortal soul, and animals do not.** Tabor says Genesis 2:7 uses the same term *nephesh* for humans and animals, and that the KJV "living soul" misled readers.
- **The "breath of life" is a separate entity inside a person.** He calls it a process: breathing is life, and when breath departs, the person is dead.
- **Genesis 1:1 describes the creation of the universe.** He says it concerns the land and skies of this planet, with the focus on Earth.
- **YHWH is a magic name that must be pronounced correctly.** He says the name conveys a concept ("I will be what I will be") and that its pronunciation is secondary.
- **Elohim is just a generic word for God.** He reads it as a plural "force of all forces" and as a designation, not a generic noun.
- **Resurrection means reanimating a corpse, like a zombie.** He says the biblical idea is a recreation of the person, with the imagery of awakening from sleep in the dust.
- **Ancient Judaism taught the immortal soul.** He says the Hebrew Bible's view is resurrection, and that the immortal soul came from Hellenistic influence.
- **Shiol is a place where the soul goes.** He describes it as a state more than a place.

- **Jesus vs. Paul:** Tabor cites Jesus' saying on judging and his response to the Sadducees, and Paul's 2 Corinthians 5, as evidence that the older Hebrew view persisted in the New Testament. He presents Paul as one source of the older view, not as opposed to it.
