## Summary

Tabor's Part 2 asks "when": whether the prophetic texts of the Hebrew Bible, Daniel above all, give a timetable for the end of the age, and what happened to the people who tried to calculate it. He argues that most of the Hebrew prophetic material describing the end (nations gathering against Jerusalem, two Messiahs, world peace) has no clock attached. Daniel 9's "seventy weeks," read as 490 years, is the exception, and it made the late Second Temple period a time of heightened expectation. He traces how the starting point of that period was shifted forward (from Cyrus to Ezra and Nehemiah) and how Daniel's beasts and kingdoms were reapplied from Antiochus IV to Rome. He reads Josephus (*Jewish War* 6.310-313) as evidence that the Roman-era Jewish revolt was driven by a misread prophecy, and cites the Dead Sea Scrolls' Habakkuk commentary, which he dates to roughly the first century BCE, as a community that also calculated and then "prolonged" the end.

Tabor then surveys five strategies people have used when prophecy fails: spiritualizing the fulfillment (the church as the kingdom), inserting an undetermined gap in the prophetic clock (Darby, dispensationalism, Lindsey, Scofield), recalculating with jubilee years or other units, a replay at the end of six millennia, and giving up on calculation altogether. He leans toward the last, supported by 2 Peter 3, a rabbinic saying in Sanhedrin 97b-98a, and H.H. Rowley's 1942 warning. He also argues that the 19th- and 20th-century literalist turn, driven by Zionism and the 1967 war, reset the apocalyptic clock for modern readers of Mark 13 and Luke 21.

The talk ends as a lecture rather than a debate. Tabor reads from an unpublished article from his Patreon series, offers a cautionary note about World War II, closes with Rowley's quotation, and points listeners to his notes, which include an appendix of about 30 New Testament passages about the nearness of the end. The central claim, which Tabor states as his own view rather than as settled fact, is that apocalyptic schemes have had a "100 failure rate so far."

## Key Teachings

### The "when" question and the 490-year clock

Tabor separates the generic prophetic language of the Hebrew Bible ("the latter days," "the time of the end") from texts that give a measurable interval. He says the generic passages have "no clock running," and that the "when" only became urgent for groups reading Daniel. Daniel 9's seventy weeks, which the talk reads as 490 years, is the one passage he calls "much more specific." Interpreters in the late Second Temple period, he says, were "absolutely convinced" of this final period. The start date was the decree to restore Jerusalem, which Tabor places at Cyrus (539 BCE, citing Isaiah 45 and the last chapter of 2 Chronicles). Because the Romans occupied the land instead of a Redemption following, he says, interpreters moved the start forward to Ezra and Nehemiah (around 450 BCE), "a sliding scale," so the period reached into the first century CE. He reads Mark 1 ("the time is fulfilled") as a reference to this calculation.

### Daniel's kingdoms and the reapplication from Antiochus to Rome

Tabor argues that Daniel was reread as the sequence of world kingdoms had to be updated. Daniel 2 (the statue, and a kingdom that "will never be destroyed" in Daniel 2:44) and Daniel 7 (four beasts) were initially tied to Antiochus IV Epiphanes, who desecrated the temple in 167 BCE. After Rome took Jerusalem in 63 BCE, Rome became the "fourth kingdom," and interpreters "began to count the heads and the horns." He cites 2 Esdras 12:10-12 as a text whose author revises Daniel 7 for the Roman period. He also notes that Daniel 11 begins with a historical description of Antiochus and then shifts to "at the time of the end the King of the North will come," an opening he says allowed later readers to project a further final ruler.

### Josephus and the "ambiguous Oracle"

Tabor's central historical evidence is Josephus, *Jewish War* 6.310-313, which he reads as Josephus writing after 70 CE. Josephus says the revolt was incited by "an ambiguous Oracle" found in the Jewish scriptures, that one from their country would rule the world. Tabor says he is "absolutely convinced" the Oracle is the seventy-weeks prophecy of Daniel. Josephus's point, as Tabor reads him, is that the oracle actually referred to the Roman general Vespasian and his son Titus, who took Jerusalem in 70. Tabor credits Robert Eisenman with drawing attention to the passage and notes that Tacitus and Suetonius repeat the tradition. He says the footnote in a standard translation admits scholars disagree about the referent.

### The Dead Sea Scrolls and the prolonged end

Tabor says the Dead Sea community also calculated. He cites a later text describing "the final ten jubilees" (490 years) and mentions "the 9th and 10th jubilees," which he thinks the community placed itself in. He dates their teacher of righteousness to roughly the first third of the first century BCE. The Habakkuk commentary (pesher), which he says is dated by carbon dating, comments on "How long, O Lord" and says God did not make known to Habakkuk when the end would come. The commentary then says "the final age shall be prolonged and shall exceed all that the prophets have said." Tabor reads this as the first "throw up your hands" move: the clock is delayed, not broken. He also connects the rabbis' and early Christians' later uses of the same Habakkuk verse.

### Five strategies when prophecy fails

Tabor lists five responses, borrowing the sociological phrase about cognitive dissonance, which he calls "cognitive dissidents" in the transcript:
1. **Spiritualize the fulfillment.** He says the church fathers argued the kingdom did come, through the church, and that the Jews were cast out.
2. **An undetermined gap.** Darby, dispensationalism, Hal Lindsey and the Scofield Bible place a pause in Daniel's clock with a final seven years still to come.
3. **Recalculate the units.** If the seventy weeks are jubilee years (49 years each), the span is 3,430 years, which Tabor says some interpreters have run forward to 2023. He notes Newton's and Miller's (1843-44) recalculations.
4. **A replay at the end of six millennia**, based on the six days of creation and the Masoretic chronology, followed by a millennium.
5. **No more calculations.** Tabor's "favorite," which he links to 2 Peter 3:3-8 and to the rabbinic and Billy Graham "rule."

### Rabbinic caution, 2 Peter and Paul's urgency

Tabor reads 2 Peter 3:3-8 as a response to scoffers who ask where the promised coming is, and claims the author replied that "a day with the Lord is a thousand years." He notes this is "probably" written by a school of Peter, not Peter himself. In the Babylonian Talmud (Sanhedrin 97b-98a), he cites Rabbi Yonatan's curse on those who calculate the end and his view that Isaiah 60:22 ("in its time I will hasten it") means the Messiah's timing depends on merit. He says Paul, by contrast, believed the end would come in his lifetime, citing "the appointed time has grown very short" and the passing of "the form of this world." Tabor notes that the later letters attributed to Paul (2 Timothy 4:6-7) show a settling realization that the end had not come.

### The literalist turn after 1917 and 1967

In the last section Tabor argues that the medieval and early modern Christian tradition moved apocalyptic material from earth to heaven and allegorized it (Augustine, Luther calling the Pope the Beast). He says this changed after the Balfour Declaration (1917) and the rise of Zionism; drawing on Ari Morgenstern's *Hastening Redemption*, he argues Zionism itself was partly inspired by Messianic expectation. He says the 1967 Six-Day War "resets the apocalyptic clock," so that Mark 13 and Luke 21 ("this generation") are read as referring to the present. He cites Lindsey's *The Late Great Planet Earth* and Tim LaHaye's *Left Behind* series, and names TV preachers who still teach this scenario.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"The end of the world" is a biblical term.** Tabor says the phrase is not in the Hebrew Bible, which speaks of "the time of the end," "the last days," and "the age to come."
- **Daniel's prophecies predict a specific date that can be calculated.** Tabor argues that calculation rests on choosing a start date and unit, and that the results have all failed so far.
- **Prophecy failed only for popular movements.** He says the Dead Sea community and the Jesus movement both reckoned the time, and that Josephus shows the failure shaped the revolt.
- **The Millerites and modern dispensationalists are the main examples of failure.** Tabor says the 70 CE destruction was the "disappointment of all disappointments."
