## Summary

Tabor asks what the Hebrew prophets were for, if not for predicting dates of the end. He sets aside the "when prophecy fails" approach to Bible prophecy and proposes that the prophets (mainly Isaiah, Jeremiah, Hosea, Amos and Micah) were "speaking for God" and laying out a set of core concerns. He presents five "great themes": knowing God through a self-described character formula; the "Abrahamic plan," in which Abraham's household keeps "the way of Jehovah" by doing righteousness and justice; a "servant Nation" meant to model that way to the world; the nations being drawn to that model, with Isaiah 2 and Micah 4 as his main pictures; and a final new creation hinted at in Isaiah 65-66 and Isaiah 25-26. His evidence is mostly the Hebrew text itself, read aloud in English (the RSV, with his own renderings of key words such as *hafats* and *yada*), plus cross-references across Torah, Prophets and Writings.

He argues that the prophets' message is conditional ("if you return to me, I will return to you") and ends with Malachi's warning of a curse on the earth if hearts do not turn. He reads this as a possibility humans could bring about, not a fixed prediction. He closes by urging listeners to take up the "politics of God" as an ideal rather than join a religion, and by framing the talk as a Sabbath reflection. The talk does not discuss Jesus, Paul or the early Jerusalem community. Tabor's claims are his own interpretations, and the talk is informal and read partly from notes.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Knowing God by the self-description formula
Tabor starts with Jeremiah 9:23-24, where God says the one who boasts should boast in understanding and knowing him, "doing steadfast love, justice and righteousness in the earth," in which God "delights." He links this to Exodus 34, where God reveals his name, and to a five-part formula (merciful, gracious, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love and truth) that he says occurs seven times across Torah, Prophets and Writings. He lists the Hebrew terms behind the five values as *hesed* (loving kindness), *mishpat* (judgment/justice), *tsedaqah* (righteousness), *emet* (truth) and *emunah* (faithfulness, trust). He presents this as the basis of the prophetic message.

### 2. The Abrahamic plan
Tabor reads Jeremiah 4:1-2 ("Nations shall bless themselves in him") alongside Genesis 12:2-3, which he splits into seven elements. He explains the cohortative ("let me make you a great nation") and argues that the verb usually rendered "curse" (*qalal*) means to "treat lightly," so that those who regard Abraham's mission as unimportant are set aside. He cites Genesis 18 as a passage where God says he has "known" Abraham (*yada*), not "chosen" him, and notes that Abraham's household includes people who join it, not only biological descendants. The household is to "keep the way of Jehovah" by teaching righteousness and justice. Tabor treats the Ten Words (the Ten Commandments) as the concrete breakdown of that way, quoting Rabbi Hertz on the Decalogue as the "Fountainhead" of Jewish teaching. He ties this to Jeremiah 7 and Hosea 4:1-2, which list murder, theft, adultery and false swearing as the failures of the land.

### 3. The servant Nation
Tabor reads Isaiah 41:8-9 and Isaiah 42:1, where the servant is chosen "for" a mission, and treats the roughly twenty servant references in Isaiah 40-53 as pointing to a people who represent God's ways to the world. He draws on Isaiah 42 ("in whom my soul delights") and notes the emotional language of *hafats*. He contrasts this with the King James rendering "peculiar people" in Exodus 19 and argues the right sense is a "treasure" (*segullah*), a people whose conduct draws others. He links this to Jeremiah 30-31, which he reads as a "dream" of a renewed covenant, and to Isaiah 51:2. He presents Hugh Schonfield's *The Politics of God* as making the same point: the model is an example, not a dominating nation.

### 4. The nations come up to learn
Tabor says the nations will be drawn to the mountain of the Lord (Isaiah 2 and Micah 4): "that he may teach us his ways and we may walk in his paths." He reads Isaiah 56 as showing foreigners and eunuchs joining Jehovah's covenant, and Isaiah 11 as showing the wolf and the lamb, no violence "on all my Holy Mountain," and the earth "full of the knowledge of the Lord." He calls this the Universal principle: the prophets' vision is for the whole world, not one ethnic group.

### 5. A new creation, glimpsed through a veil
Tabor says the fifth theme is the least clear. He reads Isaiah 65-66 ("Heaven is my Throne... the Earth is my footstool"; "a new heavens and a new Earth") and Isaiah 25-26 (death "swallowed up," a feast for all peoples). He argues that the new creation seems to grow out of the old creation, as the first covenant develops into the new, and he explicitly contrasts this with a later "apocalypticism" in which God suddenly intervenes from the skies. He notes that the prophets' texts are often conditional, and he closes with Malachi's warning of a curse of "utter destruction" if hearts do not turn, which he suggests could be brought on by human conduct.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Prophecy is mainly about predicting the end times or calculating dates.** Tabor says the prophets were "speaking for God," and that the "five great themes" concern God's character, covenant and mission.
- **Daniel and Revelation are dangerous, so avoid them.** Tabor says he is not taking that approach today and treats Daniel's placement in the Writings as a rabbinic question.
- **"Peculiar people" in Exodus 19 means a badge of honor for being odd.** Tabor argues the sense is a treasured people whose justice draws the nations, not a group that sets itself apart.
- **The Ten Commandments are simple childlike platitudes.** Tabor argues they are comprehensive principles with geopolitical implications, and says they are the basis of the Abrahamic plan.
- **Apocalyptic rescue is the prophets' hope.** Tabor says the prophets' new creation grows out of the old creation, and that the sudden heavenly rescue belongs to a later apocalyptic view.
- **"Chosen" means privileged.** Tabor argues "chosen" (from *yada*) refers to a mission of representing God's ways to the world, and that membership in Abraham's household is open to others.
