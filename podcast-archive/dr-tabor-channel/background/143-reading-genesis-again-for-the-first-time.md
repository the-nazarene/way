## Summary

This is an extended interview in which James Tabor, a historian of early Christianity, discusses his new "transparent" English translation of Genesis with host Jacob Berman. Tabor carries most of the talk. The stated question is how a reader can see and feel the Hebrew behind an English text, and how a fresh translation changes assumptions people bring to Genesis. Tabor says his method is to translate the Leningrad Codex, the oldest complete Hebrew Bible manuscript, as a fixed control text. He keeps Hebrew word repetition, rhythm, and recurring terms, and he footnotes variants from the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Septuagint, and the Samaritan Pentateuch rather than building an eclectic text.

Tabor's main examples are Genesis 1:1, which he reads as describing the "ordering" of the land, skies, and water rather than the creation of the universe; the Eden story, where he reads the "knowledge of good and bad" as growing up into moral agency rather than a fall; and the Hebrew nephesh, which he reads as "living breather" rather than an immortal soul. He also accepts the basic documentary-source picture of Genesis but says he is translating it as a unified literary work. He presents his readings as arguments from the Hebrew, not as settled results, and he repeatedly says Genesis is outside his specialty of early Christianity.

The talk ends with a Q&A on Dead Sea Scroll variants, Isaiah 53, a reported Syrian or Armenian manuscript of sayings of Jesus that Tabor says he has not verified, and a personal anecdote about a planned trip to Baghdad. Tabor closes by promoting the translation's reduced sale price.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Transparency as a translation method
Tabor says he prefers "transparent" to "literal," so that the reader can "peer through" the English to the Hebrew and "feel" it. He keeps repeated Hebrew words and idioms rather than smoothing them out, so that the rhythm of the original shows. He gives an example: Jacob "lifted his feet" rather than "went to Beersheba." He also says he marks every masculine and feminine, flags words added in English, and represents Hebrew "white spaces" (natural pauses in the manuscript) that other translations drop. He says he works from the Leningrad Codex and places variants in more than a thousand notes, and he contrasts this with modern translations that build an eclectic text from what scholars judge most authentic. He names Everett Fox, Robert Alter, and Richard Elliott Friedman as predecessors, and says he deliberately did not consult Fox or Alter while working, to avoid being influenced by them.

### 2. Genesis 1:1 as "ordering" rather than creation
Tabor argues that the usual rendering "In the beginning God created the heavens and the Earth" is "not only wrong" but misleading. He proposes that the verse begins a temporal clause ("at the first of Elohim creating the skies and the land"), and that the chapter describes a desolate, chaotic earth (tohu va-bohu) being put into order. He says this also reframes the chapter: it concerns the ordering of land, sea, sky and life, not the origin of the universe. He acknowledges that some translations keep the traditional wording so that buyers will not be put off, and he says the phrase "the first of" is the reason he thinks the traditional sentence is wrong.

### 3. The Eden story and "knowledge of good and bad"
Tabor says the phrase usually rendered "knowledge of good and evil" should be "good and bad," and that it is used elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible for children who "do not yet have" that knowledge (citing Deuteronomy 1:39 from memory). He reads the Eden story as a story of growing up into moral agency, comparing it to the Gilgamesh plant story, and he says the humans are "not falling" but "growing up." He ties this to Paul's Philippians 2, arguing that the passage contrasts Jesus, who "emptied himself," with the first Adam who grasped for eternal life. He says he does not think Paul held that Jesus had divinity in heaven before his incarnation.

### 4. Death, "soul," and the "living breather"
Tabor argues that the phrase translated "living soul" in Genesis should be "living breather," meaning any creature that takes in air. He says this undercuts the common body/soul anthropology drawn from Genesis. He says the Hebrew sheol is the pit or grave, not a place where an immortal soul lives, unlike Greek Hades. He says the Hebrew Bible contains a development of ideas about death and resurrection, and he points to an article on his website for detail. He also makes a point about Isaiah 53: the Dead Sea Scroll reading of "from the travail of his soul he will see light" seems to presuppose a return from the grave, which he says shows how early readers interpreted the text.

### 5. Sources and the redactor
Tabor says he accepts the documentary picture of Genesis (J, E, P, D) as broadly correct. He gives examples such as the two creation accounts and the double flood account, and he cites Friedman's book *The Bible with Sources Revealed* as a color-coded presentation. But he says that for this translation he treats Genesis as "a unified literary work," and he describes the final editor as "much more creative" than a mere compiler. He compares this with the Gospels: Matthew and Luke rewrote Mark, and harmonizing them "destroys" them. He says he prefers Mark as earliest but says this is a separate debate with Derek Lambert, which he has discussed on another program.

### 6. Dead Sea Scroll and other variants
In the Q&A, Tabor reads several variants from a Dead Sea Scrolls edition of the Bible. Genesis 1:5 has "daytime" in the Dead Sea Scroll copy (4Q Genesis), where the Masoretic Text has "day." In Genesis 1:14, a Dead Sea Scroll copy has "they were" where the Masoretic Text reads "let them be," which he says reflects a different narrative stance. He says that where variants like these exist, the translator has to decide whether to build a new text or keep a control text, and he has chosen the control text.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Genesis 1:1 describes the creation of the universe.** Tabor argues it is a temporal clause describing the ordering of land, sea and sky on a desolate earth.
- **Eden is a "fall" of humanity into sin.** Tabor says the knowledge of "good and bad" is a stage of maturing into moral agency, and that the humans are "growing up," not falling.
- **The "living soul" of Genesis is an immortal soul distinct from the body.** Tabor says the phrase means a "living breather," any creature that takes in air.
- **Sheol is like Greek Hades, a place where the soul lives on.** Tabor says it is the pit or grave.
- **The nakash of Genesis 3 is a serpent, and so the story is about Satan.** Tabor says the word means a shining or shrewd creature, and that the Satan figure belongs to Job.
- **Rachel is beautiful and Leah plain.** Tabor says the Hebrew describes Leah's eyes as "tender" or "soft," not plain.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor reads Philippians 2 as contrasting Jesus with the first Adam, and says he does not think Paul held that Jesus had divinity in heaven before his coming. He adds that this reading depends on a correct reading of Eden.

- **Torah and the sources:** Tabor accepts the documentary picture but reads Genesis as a unified literary work, and he notes that Genesis is "clearly the most influential single text" in his view.
