## Summary

Tabor asks what Isaiah 40-66 (which he calls "Isaiah 40 through 66," noting that scholars often call it "second Isaiah") is actually saying, since the section is systematic but hard to follow on a first reading. His answer is that the section repeats five themes, each returning four to six times across widely separated passages. He identifies these as the preparing of the way, the coming of God, the proclamation of good news, the servant, and the new heavens and new earth. His main evidence is the textual structure: he says that in the Hebrew text, closed and open spaces (which he reads as the letters samekh and pe) mark off units, and that he has categorized these units by hand. He also argues that the Dead Sea community, John the Baptist, and Jesus read these themes as a program for a prepared people to inaugurate God's kingdom on earth.

The lecture moves through the five themes using Isaiah 40:3, 58, 57:14, 62:10, 40:9, 59:16-21, 63:1-6, 41:8, 42:1-6, 43:10, 42:19, 57:15, 66:1-5, and 65-66. Tabor argues that the "servant" is a collective Israel and, within it, a faithful remnant. He reads the "suffering servant" material as describing that remnant, which is despised and rejected, and he treats the "Son of Man" of Daniel 7:14 as a group. He contrasts this with the Christian reading of Isaiah 53 as a single individual. He ends by reading the new heavens and new earth as an earthly, historical age in continuity with Genesis 1, rather than the transcendent transformation he finds in Revelation. He closes by summarizing the five themes so the audience can trace them on their own.

The talk is entirely Tabor's own presentation. Tabor frames his positions as readings of the text and as "historical" claims about Jesus, and he does not cite external scholarship in detail. ## Key Teachings

### 1. Five themes marked by "white spaces" in the Hebrew text
Tabor says Isaiah 40-66 is organized by closed and open spaces in the Hebrew text, which he reads as the letters samekh and pe, and which he calls units of thought. He says he went through the section by hand, marking these units in an English Bible and sorting them into five recurring themes. Each theme comes back four to six times, often many chapters apart, which he says is why the section feels unstructured to readers.

### 2. Preparing the way (Isaiah 40:3; 57:14; 58; 62:10)
Tabor starts with Isaiah 40:3, "prepare the way in the wilderness," which he says the Dead Sea community quoted at the start of its Charter (his term for its founding document). He notes that the Hebrew word translated "desert" is *Arava*, a specific location in the Rift Valley near the Dead Sea, and that the Dead Sea group took it as a directive to go and live there. He reads John the Baptist's quotation of the verse as a call from the wilderness to prepare the way, though he says John was not living there. He treats the highway imagery as metaphor, with a literal side he says becomes clear later.

Tabor then turns to Isaiah 58, arguing that preparing the way means preparing a people through moral action. He reads the "fast" of Isaiah 58 as a call to loose the bonds of wickedness, feed the hungry, shelter the homeless, and clothe the naked, and says fasting is not only abstaining from food. He links the verse "they shall ... raise up the foundations of many generations" (58:12) to the plural "paths to dwell in," which he takes as a call to restore more than one road. He also reads 57:14 ("clear the way") and 62:10 ("cast up the highway") as the same theme, with 62:10 raising a sign over the peoples.

### 3. The coming of God and the proclamation of good news (Isaiah 40:9; 59:16-21; 63:1-6)
Tabor says the way is prepared so that God, "the Lord God," can come "as a mighty one" (40:9). He reads the same verse's image of the shepherd who gathers the lambs and carries them in his bosom as the tender side of this coming. He ties the proclamation to a messenger on a high mountain and to the feminine "Zion" as the one who announces good news, linking it to Miriam and the women with their tambourines after the Exodus.

For 59:16-21 he highlights the statement that God "saw that there was no man" and "no intercessor," and argues that the passage has no messiah in it. He says the *goel* ("redeemer") is God himself, not an anointed human. For 63:1-6 he reads the figure coming from Edom, with garments from Bozrah, as a vision of God marching north toward Jerusalem alone. He says the geography (Edom south of the Dead Sea, Bozrah across from Tamar) is essential to understanding the passage. He concludes that the Christian term *Marana tha* refers to God himself, *Adonai* and Jehovah, rather than to Jesus.

### 4. The servant, the light to the nations, and the remnant (Isaiah 41:8; 42:1-6; 43:10; 42:19; 53)
Tabor notes that "servant" appears in these chapters about 16 times and is used in a new prophetic way: it is applied to Israel, "the seed of Abraham my friend" (41:8), where elsewhere it is used of people such as Moses. He reads 42:1-6 as the servant who will bring justice to the nations and be "a light to the nations," and 43:10 as the servant as witness to the one God. He notes the apparent problem of 42:19, where the servant is "blind" and "deaf," which he takes as the condition of Israel in exile.

He then argues that the servant material narrows to a faithful remnant within Israel, a small group who "get rid of their blindness and deafness" and begin to proclaim, and who are often despised by the majority. He cites 57:15 and 66:1-2 for a group of "poor and contrite" people in whom God dwells, rather than in a temple. He reads 66:5 ("your brethren hate you and cast you out for my name's sake") as the description of this remnant. He says the "suffering servant" hymns (42, 49, 50, 52-53) are commonly read as a single individual, but that he thinks they describe this group.

### 5. Jesus, John, and the Son of Man as a collective (Daniel 7:14; the Talmud)
Tabor argues that the historical Jesus understood himself, with his cousin John, as inaugurating this prepared people. He says Jesus taught themes from Isaiah such as "blessed are the poor" and "blessed are the meek," and that when asked about suffering, Jesus said "yes, that's for us." He reads "Son of Man" as the collective of Daniel 7:14, not a single person. He ties the opposition the group faced to the corrupt priesthood in Jerusalem, citing the Talmud's curse on the priestly families who robbed the people and beat them, and naming the house of Anus and Caiaphas. He reads "take up a cross" as a call to face execution, noting that crucifixion was a Roman punishment reserved for non-citizens and slaves. He adds that the Gospels were written after Paul's influence was strong, and that this shows especially in Mark.

### 6. The new heavens and new earth (Isaiah 65-66; Genesis 1; Jeremiah 31:31)
Tabor argues that the new heavens and new earth of Isaiah 65:17 and 66:22 are an earthly age, "a new genesis," using the same Hebrew verb *bara* as Genesis 1. He describes its features as people living out a hundred-year lifespan, planting vineyards and building houses, the wolf and lamb dwelling together (also in Isaiah 11, which he calls the messianic chapter), and the earth "full of the knowledge of the Lord." He contrasts this with Revelation, where he says the first heaven and earth pass away and a heavenly Jerusalem descends, which he reads as a more spiritual vision. He notes intimacy in the phrase "before they call I will answer," and ties it to Jeremiah 31:31 and the Torah written in the heart. He closes by saying that the book ends with all flesh coming to worship "from one new moon to another and one Sabbath to another," and that the Valley of Gehenna is a garbage dump, not a place of torment.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Fasting is only abstaining from food.** Tabor argues that the fast of Isaiah 58 means loosing the bonds of wickedness, feeding the hungry, and sheltering the poor, not simply going without food.
- **"Take up your cross" is a vague metaphor for hardship.** Tabor says it means facing execution, since crucifixion was a Roman punishment for non-citizens and slaves.
- **The "suffering servant" is a single individual (often read as Jesus).** Tabor argues that the servant passages describe a faithful remnant of Israel, and that the Son of Man is a collective.
- **Marana tha refers to Jesus.** Tabor says that in the Hebrew Bible it is Adonai and Jehovah who come.
- **The new heavens and new earth are a wholly transcendent, heavenly transformation (as in Revelation).** Tabor reads Isaiah's version as an earthly, historical age with people living out their lifespans.
- **The goel of Isaiah 59 is a messiah.** Tabor says the goel is God himself and that there is no messiah in this material.
- **Gehenna is hell as a place of torture.** Tabor says the Valley of Gehenna was a garbage dump south of Jerusalem.
