## Key Teachings

### The source and scope of the series
Tabor says the series title comes from the 1989 volume and his chapter in it. He notes that the volume covers many topics, and he says the Bible "speaks in a single voice" is an assumption that different parts of the Bible might challenge. He says that his chapter covers the Hebrew Bible and the New Testament together, and that the series will also cover the ancient Near East, Egypt, and Greece, since he regards these as background. He says he will follow the article "primarily" but will also go outside it.

### "Is there something or nothing?"
Tabor frames the basic question as a yes or no about what happens when a person dies. He gives the skeptical Greek tombstone view ("I was not, I am not, I don't care"), which he says has versions in Latin, and describes the reasoning of someone who does not believe in a pre-existence and therefore sees no reason for an afterlife. He says he is not addressing people's personal beliefs, but the history of those ideas.

### Religion as a system of salvation
Tabor argues that the central question in most ancient systems, including the Greek, Egyptian, ancient Near Eastern, and Hebrew Bible traditions, is what happens to a person after death. He says that religions "are religions of getting eternal life" and "religions of Salvation," and that gods helping people in this world is a secondary concern. He lists the candidate models he will examine: reincarnation, the immortal soul, resurrection of the dead, and resurrection of the body.

### The modern secular alternative and the question of justice
Tabor uses Freud, whom he calls an atheist, as a representative of the view that nothing lies beyond death and that the universe is cold and mechanical. He draws on the Freud–Jung correspondence, which he owns. He argues that the human hope for justice is what pushes people toward belief in an afterlife, since "if death is the end" there is no ultimate justice for the suffering and wrongs in human history. He references what he calls "The Good the Bad and the Ugly" of human history.

### Future and apocalyptic endings
Tabor touches on the future in terms of ends, both cosmic and historical. He lists possibilities such as humanity destroying itself, pollution, an asteroid strike, or the sun becoming a giant star. He sets these against the believing view that some form of consciousness or soul survives the death of the body, and says most religions add a final judgment and a determined fate beyond this world. He says apocalyptic ideas are a theme he discusses often, and he refers to "individual apocalypses" as the end of each person's life.

### The planned archaeology and episode format
Tabor says he has slides, and he mentions archaeology of tombs, particularly from the Roman and Herodian periods and from ancient Near Eastern contexts. He says the episodes will be released every three or four days so viewers have time to absorb each one, and that the series is "not a formal course."
