## Summary

Tabor opens a new YouTube series on death and the afterlife in the ancient Western world with a first session on the ancient Hebrew view. He says the series will later cover Babylonian, Greek, and Egyptian ideas, the emergence of resurrection in Jewish groups, and then Jesus and Paul. His question for this talk is what the Hebrew Bible itself says about death, before later doctrines of an immortal soul or resurrection were added.

Tabor's answer is that the Hebrew Bible presents a sober, non-dualistic view. He argues that in Genesis 2:7 a human becomes a *nephesh*, a "living breather," not an immortal soul, and that the "breath of life" is simply what keeps a living creature breathing. He reads the Eden story (Genesis 2-3) as a story about the tree of life and the loss of access to it, not about a fall into sin or a doctrine of original sin. His main textual evidence for the state of the dead is a set of passages (Psalm 88, Job 3, Job 14, Ecclesiastes 3) describing *Sheol* as a silent, dark pit where rich and poor, great and small, slave and master lie together. He also cites Psalm 115:17 for the division between the heavens (God's), the earth (humanity's), and the realm of death. He argues that the Hebrew view is "depressing" but "realistic," and that the first hints of a hope beyond death appear late, in Isaiah 24-26 and Daniel.

The talk ends with Tabor pointing listeners to his article "What the Bible Really Says About the Future" and promising more installments. He closes without fully taking up resurrection, which he says he will trace later.

## Key Teachings

### The ancient cosmos and the place of the dead
Tabor describes the Hebrew picture of the world as a flat disc (the earth) with waters around its edges, beneath a dome or firmament with waters above it and gates that can open for floods and rain (he ties this to Genesis 6). The sun, moon, and stars sit under the dome. Below the earth is *Sheol*, which Tabor calls "the world of the Dead." He says this structure is shared across the ancient Near East and into early Greek thought, with differing creation stories. He stresses that heaven is the dwelling of God and the angels, and that the earth is the human realm. Psalm 115:17 is his main proof text for this three-part division: heavens belong to Yahweh, the earth is given to humankind, and "the dead do not praise Yahweh nor do any that go down into silence."

### Humans as *nephesh*, not immortal souls
Tabor's central lexical argument is about Genesis 1:24 and 2:7. He notes that the word *nephesh* means "living breather" and that it is used of animals as well as humans, so a human is "a living creature," not a being with a separate soul. He says the phrase "breath of life" (*nishmat*) is also used of animals and does not mean an immortal soul. He argues that the King James rendering "Man became a living Soul" misleads readers into thinking of an immortal spirit. In his account, when a human dies, the breath stops and the body returns to dust (Genesis 3:19 and Ecclesiastes 3:19-20 are the text he quotes for this, with Ecclesiastes 3:19 cited directly).

### Eden, the tree of life, and death as lost access
Tabor reads Genesis 2:9 and 2:17 as follows. The tree of life confers eternal life as a gift, and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil (better rendered "good and bad," *tov* and *ra*) signals moral accountability, which he links to coming of age. He argues the warning "in the day that you eat it you will die" does not mean immediate death, since the couple does not drop dead, and that the death in view is the loss of access to the tree of life. He points to Deuteronomy 1:39, where "little children" lack knowledge of good and evil, as the only other use of the phrase, and suggests that this is the right context. He explicitly says the Genesis text contains no "fall" doctrine and no original sin, which he says come from later Christian theology (he mentions the catechism and Paul). He notes that the serpent (*nachash*, which he glosses as "shining one") is not identified as Satan in the text. He says Genesis 3:22-24 presents expulsion from Eden to keep humans from the tree of life. Tabor notes that Revelation's final chapters restore access to the tree of life, but says nothing in the Hebrew Bible restores Eden.

### *Sheol* as the common fate of all the dead
This is the core of Tabor's argument. He cites Psalm 88 (a dying man "reckoned among those who go down to the pit") to show that *Sheol* is a pit, grave, or depths, dark, silent, and forsaken. He then turns to Job 3, where Job wishes he had never been born and says that in death "the wicked cease from troubling," "the weary are at rest," "the small and the great are there and the slave is free from his master." He argues that Job's picture shows no reward or punishment, only rest and levelling. He cites Job 14 for the image of a man who "lies down and rises not again" until the heavens are no more. He ends the section by noting that Moses, Abraham, and the great figures of the Hebrew Bible also died and were buried. He also says the King James translators' "hell" for *Sheol* is a "great mistranslation," and that "hell" in the English sense of torment is not the Hebrew idea.

### The hope of restoration comes late
Tabor says the Hebrew Bible's negative view of death has a response, but it comes late and is not in Genesis or the Torah. He names Isaiah 24, 25, and 26 (which he calls "the Isaiah apocalypse," and which may be later than Isaiah's own time) as the earliest hint, and Daniel as one of the latest books. He also notes that the new heavens and new earth in Isaiah 65-66 still include people who live to a hundred and then die. He says the ideas of eternal life, immortalization, and resurrection come later and that he will trace how and whence they arise. Near the end he sums up the Hebrew view as an experience of decay that cannot be reversed: "once the cells begin to deteriorate it's gone" (he is describing the view, not endorsing a scientific claim).

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Genesis 1:1 means creation from nothing.** Tabor says the verse is a temporal clause about when God began to order a chaotic earth and sky, and that "creation from nothing" is a later philosophical idea.
- **The "breath of life" gives humans an immortal soul.** He argues *nishmat* is shared with animals, and that the human becomes a living breather, not an immortal spirit.
- **Adam and Eve dropped dead the day they ate the fruit, or the story describes a "fall" into sin.** He says they did not die that day, the death in view is loss of access to the tree of life, and there is no original sin in the text.
- **The serpent is Satan.** He says the Hebrew word *nachash* describes a creature, and the text does not identify it as Satan.
- **"Hell" is the Hebrew afterlife place of punishment.** He says *Sheol* is a pit or grave where all the dead rest together, and the King James "hell" is a mistranslation.
- **The Hebrew Bible teaches eternal life or resurrection from the start.** He says these ideas appear late (Isaiah 24-26, Daniel) and are not in Genesis or the Torah.
- **The new creation restores Eden.** He says Isaiah 65-66 still has people who die, so no Eden restoration appears in that passage.
