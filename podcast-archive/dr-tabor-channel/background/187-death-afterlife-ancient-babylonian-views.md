## Summary

Tabor asks what the ancient Mesopotamian (Sumerian and Akkadian) traditions said about death and the afterlife, and how those views compare with the Hebrew Bible material covered earlier in the series. He opens with a recorded Sumerian musical performance, then places the "old Babylon" of Abraham's background against the later Babylon that destroyed Jerusalem in 586 BCE. His central text is the Gilgamesh Epic, which he dates loosely to roughly 3000 to 2500 BCE for the historical king and to about 1200 to 1000 BCE for the surviving clay tablets, with his main focus on tablet 11, where Gilgamesh seeks eternal life.

Tabor argues that the Babylonian and Hebrew worlds share a three-tiered cosmos (heaven, a flat earth over water, and an underworld), a view of order in which humans are meant to stay in their assigned place, and a death that is irreversible and gloomy, a shadowy existence in the "land of no return." He supports this with passages from the Enuma Elish, the Gilgamesh Epic (Enkidu's dream of the House of the Dead, Siduri's advice to Gilgamesh), Genesis 1-3, and Job 3, and with a paraphrase of the historian of religion Jonathan Z. Smith. He also draws parallels between the Gilgamesh plant episode and the Eden story, while noting that the two are not the same story.

The talk ends without a firm conclusion. Tabor calls the parallels "amazing" but says the shared motifs reflect a widespread ancient Near Eastern culture. He closes by promising a next session on Homer and the ancient Greeks, which he says share the same tripartite cosmos.

## Key Teachings

### The three-story cosmos and cosmic order
Tabor says the archaic cosmology reflected in Homer, the Hebrew Bible and ancient Near Eastern texts was "a three-story universe": a vault of heaven above, the earth as a flat disc surrounded by water, and an underworld below, which he calls Hades, Sheol, or the land of no return. He reads the opening of the Enuma Elish ("when on high the heavens had not been named") alongside Genesis 1 and says both describe chaos being ordered by naming and separating. He notes that in Genesis God says "let there be light" and names things, and that in the Enuma Elish the firm ground "had not been called by name." Tabor adds that Hebrew tradition also has Elohim chopping up the sea monster Rahab (or Leviathan) to calm the waters and let the land emerge.

### Religion as "etiquette" and the human place
Citing Jonathan Z. Smith (whom Tabor studied with at the University of Chicago in the 1970s), Tabor describes these religions as "religions of etiquette," in which the cosmos is a network of relationships and each god or human must "know its place and fulfill its appointed role." Humans were meant to be at home on the ordered earth, to serve the gods, keep the decreed social order, and offer prayer and sacrifice. In this framework, death is not non-existence. The dead persist as a shadow in the underworld, cut off from the light of life.

### The land of no return: Enkidu's dream and the House of the Dead
Tabor quotes the old translation (which he attributes to Pritchard's anthology of ancient Near Eastern texts) of Enkidu's dream from tablet 7. The House of the Dead is a place of total darkness where the dead "drink dirt and eat Stone" and "wear feathers like birds," and where the crowns of kings are heaped together and the former kings now serve food to the gods. He notes that the Babylonian underworld is more populated than the Hebrew Sheol. He compares this with Job 3, where Job wishes he had died at birth, and with the picture there of the wicked ceasing from trouble and the slave being free from his master. Tabor also argues that the English word "hell" originally meant only "a hole," so the Hebrew-equivalent image is a pit, not a place of fire.

### Siduri's advice: enjoy life, because immortality is not available
On the way to Utnapishtim, Gilgamesh meets the tavern-keeper Siduri, who tells him that when the gods created humankind they "set aside life in their own hand." Her advice is to let the belly be full, make each day a feast, and dance and play. Tabor treats this as an invitation to live well in the present, not as a call to dissipation or as simple Epicurean hedonism. He links it to a line he attributes to Paul, "let us eat drink and be merry for tomorrow we die," which he reads as the same logic of accepting mortality.

### The plant and the snake, and the Eden parallel
Utnapishtim sets Gilgamesh a test of staying awake seven days, which he fails. He then tells him of a plant at the bottom of the sea that gives eternal life. Gilgamesh retrieves it, but while he sleeps a serpent eats it. Tabor says the story is not the same as Genesis 2-3, but the motifs are similar: a lost chance at eternal life, a human who cannot share the gods' immortality, and a serpent. He also contrasts the Eden expulsion (where humans gain knowledge of good and evil and enter human choice and violence, as Cain kills Abel) with the Gilgamesh ending, in which humans simply accept their limits.

### Archaeology and the 19th-century reading of the Bible
Tabor describes Austin Henry Layard's excavation at Nineveh in 1849, which uncovered an Assyrian library with copies of Akkadian texts, including the Enuma Elish and the Gilgamesh Epic. He says these discoveries seemed to parallel Genesis, provoking both claims that the Bible copied Sumerian tales and efforts by defenders of biblical inspiration. He describes Layard as a "hunter of treasures" who was, in part, trying to prove the Bible, and says many 19th-century explorers carried a Bible in one hand and a spade in the other.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- The Siduri advice to "make each day a feast" is often read as Epicurean or dissolute hedonism; Tabor argues it is about enjoying the life one has and playing one's role.
- "Hell" in English translations suggests a fiery place of punishment; Tabor says the word originally meant "a hole," matching the Hebrew pit or Sheol.
- The parallels between Gilgamesh and Genesis (the flood, the serpent and the plant, the Eden motifs) prove that the Bible copied Sumerian tales; Tabor says the motifs are shared across a widespread ancient Near Eastern culture, while the stories are not the same.
- Some 19th-century readers assumed Genesis was simply copying Gilgamesh, and some defenders of inspiration treated the discoveries as proof of the Bible; Tabor is critical of both approaches.
