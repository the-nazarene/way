## Summary

Tabor asks how Greek ideas about death and the afterlife changed between Homer (roughly the 8th century BCE) and Plato (the 4th century BCE), and he calls the change a "great reversal" in which death becomes life and life becomes a kind of death. He presents this as a description of the shift, with the causes to be taken up later. He sets it against a shared baseline from the ancient Near East and the Hebrew Bible: a three-tiered cosmos, with the dead in a dark, silent underworld (Hebrew *sheol*, Babylonian *kur*/"land of no return," Greek *Hades*) and no real return from it.

His evidence is textual and archaeological. From Homer he uses Odyssey 11, where the shade of Achilles would rather be a poor laborer on earth than rule over the dead. From the later period he uses the gold *lamellae* (funerary gold foil inscriptions, which he calls "cue cards for the dead"), the cave allegory in Plato's *Republic* Book 7, the fragment of Empedocles on past lives, and the death of Socrates in 399 BCE as reported in Plato's *Phaedo*. Tabor argues that by Plato's time the soul is treated as an immortal, heavenly self trapped in a body, and that this idea "takes over the world." He ties it to the foundations of Judaism, Christianity and Islam, and to modern notions of karma and reincarnation.

The talk ends with a promise of a later segment on what he calls the "Hellenistic reversal," where humans who belong on earth come to believe they belong in heaven. This is the third segment in his series. The talk does not discuss Jesus, Paul, James or the Jerusalem community directly.

### Key Teachings

### 1. The shared Near Eastern and Hebrew baseline
Tabor begins with a three-level cosmos: heaven above, the flat earth where the living "breathe," and an underworld of silence. He reads Psalm 115, noting that "the heavens are the Lord's" but the earth is given to humans, and that "the dead do not praise" the Lord. He also cites the *Gilgamesh* epic, where the tavern-keeper Siduri tells Gilgamesh that the gods "allotted to him death but life they retained," and advises him to enjoy food, family and music. Tabor stresses that the dead still exist in these texts, so the view is not annihilation, but it is "not life."

### 2. Homer: the "breathless dead"
In Odyssey Book 11, Odysseus visits the underworld and speaks with Achilles, who is honored among the dead. Achilles rejects Odysseus's consolation: "I'd rather slave on Earth for another man... than rule down here over all the breathless dead." Tabor reads this as the Homeric baseline. The dead are honored only in memory, and life above is still preferred. He also notes that the shade needed blood to speak, and that blood is life in the Hebrew view too.

### 3. The gold lamellae as "cue cards for the dead"
Tabor describes gold foil inscriptions found in tombs, rolled and placed near the ear of the deceased. He says 17 are known, mostly dated from the 4th to 5th century BCE, with one from the early Roman period, and that one is displayed at the Getty Museum in California. He notes that some scholars link them to the Orphic religion, but he finds their content generic. The longer version describes a spring of memory (which he reads as Mnemosyne) to drink from, and a warning to avoid the spring of forgetfulness (Lethe). The soul answers the guardians: "I am a child of Earth and Starry heaven but my race is of Heaven alone." Tabor reads this as a confession that secures passage to heaven, and compares it to later Christian confession of faith. He is careful to say the reading is his own interpretation.

### 4. Plato: the cave, the immortal soul and the body as prison
Tabor reads the allegory of the cave from *Republic* Book 7 in some detail. Prisoners see only shadows on a wall and take them for reality. The one who escapes sees the true world, and the "chair" they see is only a shadow of the ultimate Form of chair, which exists in the heavenly realm. Tabor reads the golden plate's "heaven alone is my home" together with this allegory. He summarizes Plato's position as the soul being immortal and heavenly, the body being disposable, and death being a release. He also cites the *Phaedo* argument that the soul, which moves the body, is superior to it and so cannot be the body. He calls this "Hellenistic dualism."

### 5. Empedocles and the cycle of rebirth
Tabor quotes the fifth-century philosopher Empedocles: "I was once a fish, a bird and now a man, I wept I wept when I saw this Dreadful place." He takes this as evidence of transmigration and as a sign that even an advanced life does not feel like home. He contrasts this with Genesis 1, where creation is pronounced good.

### 6. The death of Socrates and "death is the healer"
Tabor reviews Socrates' trial (399 BCE, at about age 70, for "corrupting the youth" and impiety) and his refusal to flee into exile. He presents the *Phaedo* scene in which Socrates drinks the hemlock, tells his disciples to "take care of yourselves," and asks them to pay a rooster to Asclepius, the god of healing. Tabor's preferred reading is that death is the healing: Socrates is leaving the cave of the physical world. He says Socrates treats death as a post or duty to be completed, and rejects suicide. Tabor also notes that Plato wrote after Socrates' death, so the reported dialogues are Plato's presentation.

### Misconceptions Addressed

- **The dead in the older tradition are simply annihilated.** Tabor says the Hebrew, Gilgamesh and Homeric dead still exist in a shadowy underworld. The problem is that it is "not life," not that it is nothing.
- **Plato's cave describes the underworld.** Tabor says it is "not Hades" but an allegory of the soul's relation to reality.
- **Socrates and Plato are the same voice.** Tabor notes that Plato wrote after Socrates' death, so Socrates' ideas come to us through Plato's writing.
- **Death is a tragic loss.** Tabor reports that Socrates tells his disciples that death is not a loss, and he compares this with Christian funerals where the preacher calls death a happy day.
