## Summary

Tabor asks what the Greek gold tomb inscriptions, usually called the Orphic "Golden Plates" or lamellae, tell us about the soul's destiny after death. He calls them "cue cards for the Dead," thin gold sheets rolled up and placed by the ear of the deceased, to guide the soul through the underworld. He notes that only 17 have been found, that they tend to date from the fourth and fifth centuries BCE (with one possibly from the early Roman period), and that he saw one on display at the Getty Museum. He also says the "Orphic" label may be an association and that the texts are fairly generic.

His answer rests on the text he reads aloud, which he presents as a fuller version of the same message. The soul is parched and asks for water from the "lake of memory" rather than the "lake of forgetfulness." When the Guardians question it, it confesses that it is "a child of Earth and Starry heaven" but that its race is "of Heaven alone." Tabor reads this as the earliest Western evidence that humans have a heavenly origin and destiny, and he describes it as a "first hint" that the soul goes to Hades but does not belong there. He compares the confession to later Christian creedal statements, though he does not develop that comparison in detail.

The talk ends with his emphasis on the line "heaven alone is my home," and his claim that the inscriptions say the soul is not at home in Hades. ## Key Teachings

### The lamellae as "cue cards" for the soul
Tabor describes the gold leaves as practical guides for the dead, placed beside the ear so the soul would know what to say and do in the underworld. He stresses that they are found in tombs, that they are rolled, and that only 17 are known from a much larger original number he suspects. He presents them as the best evidence for a specific belief about the soul.

### The dual origin: earth and "star-filled Heaven"
The key line, which he reads as the inscription's central claim, is "I am the son of Earth and star-filled Heaven, but from Heaven alone is my house." Tabor takes this as the first literary-archaeological evidence of what he calls "Hellenistic dualism": the soul is partly earthly and partly heavenly, and its true home is heaven. He draws attention to the phrase "Heaven alone," which he treats as the decisive wording.

### Knowledge of identity as the key to passing the Guardians
Tabor reads the Guardians' questions ("who are you, where are you") as a test of whether the soul knows the "Mysteries" and the cosmos. He says the soul's bold answer shows that it knows its origin, and he connects this to the word "Gnostic" as a term for knowing who you are. He links this to the idea of a "confession of Faith" that secures the soul's passage upward.

### The forgetting and remembering waters
In the fuller version, the soul is warned not to drink from the lake of forgetfulness (Lethe), which leads to forgetting and to an endless cycle of rebirth. Instead it should seek the cold water from the lake of memory. Tabor reads this as a rejection of reincarnation: the soul that remembers its heavenly origin can escape the cycle.

### Heavenly destiny and "lordship" among heroes
Tabor reads the promise of "lordship among the other Heroes" as referring to lordship in heaven, not a better place in Hades. He notes that later Greek accounts divide Hades into compartments, but he argues that this inscription places the soul's real destiny above.
