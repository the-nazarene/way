## Summary

Tabor asks how ancient Egyptian views of death and the afterlife worked, and how they relate to later Western ideas of final judgment. This is the fourth talk in his series on death and afterlife in the ancient Western world, following the Hebrew Bible (Sheol), Babylonian texts (Gilgamesh), and Homer. He presents the material as a "very brief overview" and says he will assemble the fuller story later.

His account rests on three bodies of Egyptian texts: the Old Kingdom Pyramid Texts, which he says were written for pharaohs and their associates on pyramid walls; the Middle Kingdom Coffin Texts, tied to the coffin itself; and the later "Books of the Dead," a name he attributes to the German scholar Lepsius. His central example is the Papyrus of Ani, which he dates to about 1550 BCE and which he says was personalized for an elite individual. He describes the judgment scene: the heart is weighed against a feather, Anubis leads the proceedings, Thoth records, and Ammit, the crocodile figure, devours those who fail. He reads from the 42-item negative confession and argues that Egyptian "magic" should be understood as religion, requiring devotion and a pure heart.

He ends by comparing this judgment scene with Revelation 20:11-15, where the dead are judged "by what was written in the books" and death and Hades are thrown into the lake of fire. He says the motifs overlap strikingly, though he does not claim direct borrowing and says the two are "not the same." He closes by arguing that Egypt helped establish the idea that the next world may matter more than this one, a theme he connects to Plato, and says the next talk will cover Hellenistic views.

## Key Teachings

### Three kinds of Egyptian afterlife texts
Tabor separates the material by period and genre. The Old Kingdom Pyramid Texts were, in his description, mainly for pharaohs and "associates of the pharaoh," inscribed on pyramid panels and walls, and were "more of a corporate or a national thing" than a personal handbook. The Middle Kingdom Coffin Texts were prayers and spells associated with the coffin or mummy. From the late Middle through New Kingdom came the Books of the Dead, which he says were named by Lepsius, whose name the captions render as "Lepsius." He describes the goal in all three as moving the soul through the underworld toward a "happy afterlife."

### The judgment and its stakes
Tabor summarizes three things the deceased faces in the underworld: judgment, rebirth into paradise with eternal life if judgment is passed, and annihilation if it is failed ("You don't exist anymore"). He describes the dangers along the way as animals, monsters and obstacles, which he says is why the Book of the Dead is so long. He calls the stakes "high stakes."

### The weighing of the heart
This is his most detailed scene. The heart, removed from the body and kept in a jar, is placed on a scale against a feather. Tabor uses the image of a blind figure of Justice from the Supreme Court to illustrate the ancient idea of weighing right and wrong. The question, in his words, is "how many sins and failures and mistakes are you going to have in your life?" He notes that the heart should not outweigh the feather. Anubis, with a jackal head, does the judging; Thoth, the scribe, keeps a list of 42 questions; and Ammit, a crocodile figure, devours the failed. He also describes scarab amulets inscribed with a request to "guard my heart," which he suggests functioned like a lie-detector test, and says that in his reading the aim is a pure heart rather than sinless perfection.

### The negative confession and the Papyrus of Ani
Tabor reads several items from the 42 negative statements: "I've not told lies," "I've not stolen," "I've not cursed a God," "I've not been violent," "I've not told or discussed secrets." He treats the list as a window into Egyptian social expectations. He says the Papyrus of Ani (c. 1550 BCE, per his dating) was written for a named individual of the elite, who "would have paid a significant sum," and is "tailored to your death and afterlife." He credits Wallace Budge, who in 1888 obtained the papyrus from an antiquities dealer and gave it to the British Museum.

### Magic as religion
Tabor says that calling these texts "magical" is "an accommodation to a certain western way of misunderstanding magic." In his view, magic in this context means religion: devotion and a prepared, pure heart, not a trick that works by the right formula. He adds that some Egyptians "maybe... took it quite literally," since they removed the heart and placed it in a jar with the dead.

### Comparison with Revelation 20
Tabor reads Revelation 20:11 onward, pointing to the throne of judgment, the books opened, the book of life, the dead judged "by what was written in the books," and the lake of fire. He says the motifs are "very interesting," though "not the same," and that the New Testament view is distinct from the ancient Hebrew view in its final removal of the present world. He compares the "books" to the Egyptian record-keeping and the "book of life" to the Anubis symbol. He also cites a saying he attributes to Jesus of Nazareth about giving account for every idle word and for the "thoughts and intents of the heart," which he presents as a parallel to the heart-weighing. He says that how such motifs passed into New Testament texts is a question he will return to, and that Hellenistic and Roman-period syntheses are where the combinations appear.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That the final judgment with books and a heart-based accounting is purely a Christian idea.** Tabor says Revelation's judgment scene, though "not the same," overlaps in motifs with the Egyptian judgment (books, weighing, the lake of fire), so it has a broader background than is usually assumed.
- **That Egyptian magic is a set of tricks that work by reciting the right formula.** Tabor argues that "magic really means religion," requiring devotion and a pure heart, and says the Western reading of the word misunderstands it.
- **That the Egyptian judgment is about being perfect.** He says the 42 statements are not a claim of flawlessness but of a heart that is pure.
