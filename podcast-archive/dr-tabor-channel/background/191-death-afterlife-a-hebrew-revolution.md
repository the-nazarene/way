## Summary

This is the fifth video in Tabor's "Death and Afterlife in the Ancient World" series. It asks how the Hebrew view of death moved from the early picture of all the dead going to Sheol (the transcript's "shio") toward the idea of resurrection of the dead during the early Roman period. Tabor, speaking alone, presents the question as one of "eschatology," which he defines as "the idea of the last things," and says it grew from a demand for justice, or theodicy: if God is just and the world is good, the dead who missed the coming age need some kind of vindication.

Tabor's evidence is a sequence of Hebrew Bible passages read in roughly chronological order. He begins with Ecclesiastes' "a time to be born and a time to die," then moves through Isaiah 2 (the mountain of the Lord and "swords into plowshares"), Isaiah 11 and Jeremiah 23 (the ideal Davidic ruler), Isaiah 65 and 25 (a new creation and the swallowing up of death), Job (the "my Redeemer lives" passage), and finally Daniel 12:1-3, which he calls "I think only absolutely clear idea of Resurrection the dead in the Hebrew Bible." He also draws on Plato, the Egyptian Book of the Dead, a Quaker painting by Edward Hicks, and the Isaiah wall at the United Nations as illustrations of how these visions have spread.

The talk ends with a preview of later installments. Tabor says he will go on to Hellenistic dualism and how resurrection "really develops," describing a late Second Temple debate among Pharisees, Essenes (possibly the Dead Sea Scrolls community), and Sadducees, and he argues that the Platonic view eventually "completely takes over the world." He closes by saying modern talk of an afterlife often ignores these Hebrew and early Jewish-Christian alternatives.

## Key Teachings

### Eschatology grows out of a cycle that feels unsatisfying
Tabor contrasts an early Hebrew cosmology, in which "the Earth is the place to be" and humans follow "a one-way trip from birth to death," with a later linear view of history. He uses the image of filming a lake for a hundred years: without a story moving toward an end, the repeated seasons are not satisfying. The "last things" he lists are judgment, resurrection of the dead, a new age, the kingdom of God, and perhaps a new creation.

### Isaiah's vision: universal Torah, peace, and Jerusalem at the center
Tabor reads Isaiah 2 as an early eschatological vision in which "all the nations shall flow into" the mountain of the Lord, the Torah goes forth "from Jerusalem," and swords become plowshares. He dates Isaiah to the eighth century BCE and stresses that the Torah in this vision is universal, not specifically Jewish, pointing to the Ten Commandments and the Sabbath in Genesis 2. He notes that this passage mentions no dead and raises the problem he calls theodicy: the millions who died earlier seem to "miss out."

### The ideal ruler: a Davidic figure who is not yet called "Messiah"
Tabor argues that Jeremiah 23 and Isaiah 11 introduce a righteous Davidic ruler. He says that across roughly twelve passages this figure is never called "Messiah," although the word means "anointed," as priests and kings were anointed. He adds that "Christ" is a Greek rendering of the same description, not a personal name. He reads Isaiah 11:2-5 (the Spirit of the Lord, judging "with righteousness," the "meek of the Earth") as a background for the Jesus traditions of "blessed are the poor" and "blessed are the meek," though he gives no verse for those sayings.

### Job's longing for a record and a vindicator
Tabor reads Job's statement "oh that my words were now written" as a demand for justice: if a life record existed, a later reader could vindicate the unjustly treated person. He describes the Hebrew word usually rendered "Redeemer" (go'el) as a legal advocate, not a messianic figure, and he says some readers have wrongly Christianized the passage. He reads Job as a "hint" at vindication but says it may or may not refer to seeing God.

### Isaiah 25 and 65: death swallowed up and a new creation
Tabor reads Isaiah 25:6-8 ("he will swallow up death forever") and Isaiah 65:17 ("the former things shall not be remembered") as hints that death will pass away and that a restored, Edenic state may include the dead. He notes this is "not exactly resurrection," and says the Apostle Paul later quotes the death-swallowing idea in 1 Corinthians 15 and 1 Thessalonians 4.

### Daniel 12: the clearest resurrection text in the Hebrew Bible
Tabor calls Daniel 12:1-3 "the I think only absolutely clear idea of Resurrection the dead in the Hebrew Bible." He notes Daniel's late place in the Hebrew canon (in the Writings, not the Prophets) and that its setting is the Babylonian period. He reads "many of those who sleep in the dust of the Earth shall awake" as a return to this world in a transformed state, and he contrasts it with Job's "we will not awake from our sleep." He distinguishes this from the Greek "immortal soul," which he says people often confuse with the Hebrew view.

### Three distinct views of the afterlife
Tabor closes by urging listeners to separate three ideas: the ancient Egyptian view, Hellenistic (Platonic) dualism of an immortal soul, and the early Jewish and Christian view of bodily resurrection developing in the late Second Temple period. He says that in the first century the Pharisees and Essenes affirmed resurrection, while the Sadducees, "like job's friends," denied it, and that Platonic thought later "completely takes over."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Job's "Redeemer" as a Christian prediction:** Tabor says some readers have "mistranslated and misread and christianized" the passage, and that "redeemer" (go'el) here means a legal advocate, not a savior figure.
- **"Christ" as a personal name:** Tabor says "Christ" means "the anointed one," a description of role like priest or king, not a name.
- **Immortal soul and resurrection are the same idea:** Tabor argues people "mix up" the Greek immortal soul with the Hebrew and early Jewish view that the dead will be raised, and that these are "very distinct ideas."
- **The UN's Isaiah wall expresses Isaiah's own vision:** Tabor says it is "not really Isaiah's Vision," since Isaiah centers on Jerusalem and the Torah, not New York.
- **Modern afterlife talk reflects the ancient Jewish view:** Tabor says modern speech about going "to a home beyond heaven" is neither the Hebrew nor the early Jewish or Christian view.
