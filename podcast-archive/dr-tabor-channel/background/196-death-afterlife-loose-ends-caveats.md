## Summary

Tabor opens this segment as a loose-ends session for his tenth video in a series on death and the afterlife in the ancient Western world. He answers questions from viewers and from his college course, and he says at the outset that he is not addressing his personal beliefs. He presents himself as a historian of ancient religions, interested in texts, languages, and context. The central question is whether the Hebrew Bible and the New Testament affirm that humans survive death through an immortal soul or some other afterlife, or whether they expect the dead to be raised at the end of the age.

Tabor argues that the Hebrew Bible does not affirm afterlife. He reads Job 19:23-27 as a wish to be vindicated in memory and to see God, not a belief in life after death. He treats Isaiah 26 and Daniel 12 as the only real hints of resurrection, and he reads the Psalms and Jonah as being about God's presence and rescue. He discusses Enoch, Elijah, and Samuel at Endor as apparent exceptions that he says do not hold up. In the New Testament he reads Luke 16 and Luke 23:43 as statements about Hades rather than heaven. He reads Paul's texts (2 Corinthians 5, Philippians 1, 1 Thessalonians 4, 1 Corinthians 15) as consistent with a future bodily resurrection, with the dead "asleep" until Christ returns.

The talk ends with caveats. Tabor argues that the "material versus spiritual" dichotomy is misleading for Hellenistic thought, and that a linear, end-of-the-age view of the resurrection is less dualistic than it first looks. He says the question is partly about "state of being" rather than place. He closes by saying the next lecture will cover how early Christian writers "Hellenized" the message, citing Oscar Cullmann (transcribed as "Coleman") on the resurrection being largely lost.

## Key Teachings

### Job 19 and Job 14: longing, not afterlife
Tabor follows the JPS rendering of Job 19:23-27 and argues that Job wants his words written down so that someone may later vindicate him. He says the phrase "I would behold God while still in the flesh" expresses a wish to meet God in this life. He calls the familiar reading of "my redeemer lives" as an affirmation of afterlife a misreading, noting that the book gives no final answer to Job's suffering. In Job 14:7-14 he reads the tree-that-sprouts image as a contrast with humans, who "lie down and rise not again." He says that in this passage Job "longs for Resurrection" but "never affirms it."

### Isaiah 26 and Daniel 12: the only real hints
Tabor dates the Isaiah apocalypse (chapters 24-28) to an uncertain setting, possibly the Exile, and notes that its style differs from the rest of Isaiah. He reads verses 13-14 as saying the enemies who ruled Israel are "Shades" (refaim) who "will not live." He argues that verse 19 is ambiguous: the Hebrew "thy dead" (plural) against "my body" (singular) may reflect textual corruption, and the literal reading may be "my dead carcass." He describes the Daniel 12:1-3 passage as the clearest statement of resurrection in the Hebrew Bible, noting that it speaks of "some to everlasting life and some to shame," while the word "many" leaves open whether all are raised.

### Psalms and Jonah: presence of God, not life after death
Tabor reads Psalm 139 (the "whither shall I flee from your presence" passage) as about God's omnipresence. He argues Psalm 16, which Acts 2 quotes as a prophecy of Jesus, is about God preserving the psalmist from death, and that Peter misapplies it. He treats Psalm 23 and Jonah 2 the same way: the "belly of the fish" is a near-death experience rescued by God, not an afterlife text. He says these passages "christianize" or are taken out of context.

### Enoch, Elijah, Saul and Samuel: exceptions that don't hold
On Genesis 5:24 Tabor says "God took him" does not say where Enoch went. He links the later belief that Enoch went to heaven to Hebrews 11:5 and to the growing Enoch literature. On Elijah he notes that 2 Chronicles 21:12 treats Elijah as still around after his ascent and does not describe him as in heaven. On Saul and the medium at Endor (1 Samuel 28) he argues that Samuel is in Sheol, that the medium's vision is ambiguous, and that the dead in the Hebrew Bible have little power. He says Isaiah mocks the dead as chirping like birds.

### Luke 16 and Luke 23: Hades, not heaven
Tabor reads the Lazarus and rich man story (Luke 16:19-31) as a description of two states in Hades, with Abraham's bosom and torment in the same realm, and not a picture of heaven. He notes that the rich man's request to send Lazarus back to warn his brothers, and Abraham's reply that even someone rising from the dead would not persuade them, makes the point that Moses and the prophets are sufficient. On Luke 23:43 ("today you'll be with me in Paradise") he says that "Paradise" is probably a blessed compartment of Hades, and notes that the punctuation could be different. He says the sentence could also refer to the future resurrection.

### Paul: resurrection at the end, consistently
Tabor argues that Paul is consistent. In 2 Corinthians 5:1-8 he says the "at home with the Lord" language is about being in God's keeping between death and resurrection, and that the Judgment seat and the final body come later. In Philippians 1:21-24 he reads Paul's "depart and be with Christ" as a wish to rest until the end, not an immediate transition to heaven. In Philippians 3:8-11 and 3:20-21 he reads resurrection as an end-of-the-age event tied to the parousia. In 1 Thessalonians 4:13-17 he says "asleep" means dead Christians who have not yet been raised, and that "God will bring with him" refers to God raising them. He concludes that "death is death it is not life," and that in early Jewish and Christian thought life comes through being raised with a new, glorified body.

### Caveats: dualism, linear time, and "material" versus "spiritual"
Tabor argues that a linear view of history, with a new creation at the end, is not necessarily less dualistic than a vertical view, and that Paul's "Jerusalem above" (Galatians 4:26) fits either reading. He says that in the Hellenistic cosmology the lower world is "thick and heavy" and the higher world "light and more Airy," so the material/spiritual split is not the modern one. He suggests the real difference may be "state of being" rather than time or place, and he raises the question of whether Paul's "spiritual body" differs from a Socratic immortal soul. He acknowledges that Romans 7 also contains Platonic-sounding language about the inner self and the flesh.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Job 19 affirms life after death.** Tabor says Job's words express a wish for later vindication and for seeing God in this life, not a belief in afterlife.
- **Enoch and Elijah went straight to heaven.** He says Genesis 5:24 does not say where Enoch went, and the chronicler treats Elijah as still on earth; the heavenly reading grew later.
- **Samuel's appearance at Endor shows the dead are alive and active.** He reads the dead in Sheol as powerless shades.
- **Psalms 16, 23, 139 and Jonah 2 are about afterlife.** He says they are about God's presence and rescue, and that Acts 2 misapplies Psalm 16 to Jesus.
- **Hades is divided into hell and heaven.** He reads Luke 16 as a picture of two states within Hades.
- **"Depart and be with Christ" means going to heaven at death.** He reads Philippians 1:21-24 as resting in Christ until the resurrection.
- **"Absent from the body" means present with the Lord in heaven.** He reads 2 Corinthians 5 as the interim before the final body.
- **Material and spiritual are opposites in the ancient world.** He argues that Hellenistic cosmology saw one reality with graduations, not a modern physics-style split.
