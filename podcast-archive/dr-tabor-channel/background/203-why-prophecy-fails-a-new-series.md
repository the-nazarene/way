## Summary

Tabor opens a new series, "WHY Prophecy Fails," on biblical prophecy. He distinguishes his approach from the popular "what and when" material on YouTube and from the academic literature on failed expectations. The academic question is how a group copes when its prophecies about the end do not come true, which he links to Leon Festinger, Henry Riecken and Stanley Schachter's *When Prophecy Fails* (1956). His own focus is the "why": what happens between readers and these texts, which he describes as a category mistake. He says the series will begin with the Dead Sea Scrolls and cover Daniel, Mark 13, Paul, Revelation, and later movements such as William Miller, Charles Taze Russell, Herbert W. Armstrong and Hal Lindsey.

Much of the talk is a catalogue of Tabor's books, free PDFs, courses, a conference, and a planned tour of Israel, so the substantive argument is concentrated in a few passages. The central claim is at [20:32] and [22:11]: the Hebrew Bible contains only ten basic texts about a future ideal Davidic king, and neither Jesus nor any other claimant in history has fulfilled any of them. Tabor says the popular claim of "over 150" fulfilled prophecies therefore rests on a different category of text.

To show that prophetic meaning can be revised, Tabor cites 2 Esdras 12:10, which gives an interpretation of Daniel 7's beasts that differs from the angel's explanation in Daniel. He also cites a Habakkuk commentary from the Dead Sea Scrolls and 2 Peter on delay, and calls the strategy of stretching prophetic timelines "the elasticity of Prophecy" or "moving the goal post." He ends by previewing the series, announcing a Dead Sea Scrolls course, and inviting viewers to subscribe and sign up for his newsletter.

## Key Teachings

### The "why" as a distinct question
Tabor separates the "why prophecy fails" question from the descriptive "what and when" approach common on YouTube. He describes academic work on failed expectations (the social and psychological study of groups whose predictions did not come true) and says he has explored that angle in other books, videos and articles. This series, by contrast, aims to examine what happens between readers and ancient texts that leads them to treat prophecy as a predictive timetable. He presents the phrase "category mistake" from the video description, and the talk's opening frames the series around that idea.

### Ten Davidic texts, and none fulfilled
At [20:32] Tabor says the Hebrew Bible has "only 10 basic texts" about the coming of a future ideal Davidic king, and that he will go through all ten. At [22:11] he states that neither Jesus nor any other claimant has fulfilled any of them. He names other claimants: Bar Kokhba, the "dozen Messiahs" Josephus mentions from the late Second Temple period, the Teacher of Righteousness and others in the Dead Sea Scrolls, and anyone in history who has claimed to be the Davidic figure. He says he expects this claim may "be seen as over dramatic," and separates it from the question of whether Jesus fulfilled anything else. The ten texts are not itemized in this talk; he defers them to the series.

### "Messiah" is not the term these texts use
Also at [22:11], Tabor argues that the ten texts never call the Davidic figure "the Messiah." He suggests that people who ask "Dr Tabor, do you think Jesus is the messiah?" are using a term these ten texts do not contain. This is part of his argument that the conventional framing of prophetic fulfillment starts from a later vocabulary.

### 2 Esdras 12:10: God's interpretation revised
Tabor presents 2 Esdras (in the Apocrypha) as a redo of Daniel, and he reads 2 Esdras 12:10 in which a seer claiming to be Ezra receives an interpretation of the vision in which the eagle-like figure is the fourth kingdom. The text states that this interpretation "was not explained to him as I now explain it to you." Tabor notes that Daniel 7 has an angel reveal the vision's meaning, so 2 Esdras appears to give a different explanation of the same vision. He asks whether God revises his interpretations of prophecy and says the question is "really interesting." He also says the book of Revelation is a redo of this material, though he treats that connection as something to be developed later in the series.

### Habakkuk pesher and 2 Peter: delay and stretching
Tabor says the Dead Sea Scrolls' prophetic commentary on Habakkuk states that God changes prophecy and fulfillment and that certain things expected to come do not come, but "to him it's not really delay." He links this to 2 Peter, which he says responds to failed expectations of the Lord's return. He quotes 2 Peter's line that "with the Lord a thousand years is a day" and presents it as an example of stretching the timeline. He calls this the "elasticity of Prophecy" and "moving the goal post," and says the rubber band eventually snaps when pulled too far. He also notes that Paul expected to be alive at the Lord's coming, while Paul probably died as a martyr in Rome (Tabor's own assertion, which he does not source in the talk).

### Historical cases of failed prediction
Tabor sketches a series of failed predictions: the Millennium around 1000–1200 CE; the Augustinian reading in which the church is the kingdom, which for some would have put the end near the year 1030; William Miller's 1843–44 date and its link to the Seventh-day Adventists; Charles Taze Russell's predictions, including 1914 and the later reinterpretation as an invisible coming, which he links to the Jehovah's Witnesses; Herbert W. Armstrong's *The World Tomorrow*; and Hal Lindsey's *The Late Great Planet Earth* and the secret Rapture. He says Adolf Hitler was cast as an Antichrist in the 1940s. He summarizes the failure rate of end-time predictions as "100" percent, presenting this as realism rather than cynicism.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus fulfilled over 150 Hebrew Bible prophecies.** Tabor says he does not accept this and that the Hebrew Bible contains only ten basic texts about a future Davidic king, none of which Jesus or any other claimant has fulfilled.
- **Jesus is "the Messiah" as a term used in the prophecies.** Tabor argues the ten texts never use the word "Messiah" for the Davidic figure, so the term does not come from those texts.
- **God's prophetic word is fixed and cannot be revised.** Tabor cites 2 Esdras 12:10 and the Habakkuk commentary to argue that ancient Jewish texts themselves allow for revised interpretation and delayed fulfillment.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor describes Paul as an apocalyptic prophet and interpreter of Bible prophecy whose expectations failed, and cites Paul's expectation of being alive at the Lord's coming, though he gives no reference for that quote.
