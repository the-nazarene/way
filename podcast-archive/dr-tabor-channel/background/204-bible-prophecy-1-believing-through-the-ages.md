## Summary

Tabor opens a new series on Bible prophecy by asking what happens "when prophecy fails." He wants to know how interpreters respond to failed predictions (reinterpreting, making excuses, claiming fulfillment, or giving up) and why prophecy fails, by examining the texts that people use and their original historical contexts. He says that, across roughly 2,500 years, the enterprise of predicting the end has a "100% failure rate," while acknowledging the common reply that the prophecy itself never fails and only the interpreters do. He frames the series as descriptive and historical rather than cynical.

The talk is a roadmap rather than an argument, so Tabor's evidence here is mostly a list of case studies. He plans to cover the Dead Sea Scrolls and the "Teacher of Righteousness" (which Michael Wise calls the first documented messiah), Josephus's accounts of eleven other messianic figures, Mark 13, Paul, Revelation and Daniel, the Bar Kokhba letters, the Apostolic Fathers and the Didache, Montanism, Church fathers who calculated chronologies, amillennialism and Augustine, the Millerites, Ellen G. White, Charles Taze Russell, Herbert W. Armstrong, and Hal Lindsey's *The Late Great Planet Earth*. He also plans to examine the ten basic Hebrew Bible "messianic" texts and ask whether Jesus or anyone else has fulfilled them.

The talk ends with a teaser. Tabor says he will begin next week with the Dead Sea Scrolls and the "first Messiah," and he names the full-preterist view (that Jesus has fulfilled every Hebrew Bible prophecy) as a possible closing topic. The presenter speaks throughout as a monologue; there is no guest.

### Key Teachings

### Prophecy failure as the organizing question
Tabor's central framing is the "when prophecy fails" phenomenon, a term he attributes to scholars. He wants to track how interpreters respond to disappointment: whether they reinterpret, make excuses, claim fulfillment, or abandon their views. He says that "why prophecy fails is just as important" as the texts themselves. He describes the aim as examining "all the major texts that people use" and their nature, and asking whether a historical reading "in their own time and in their own context" sheds light on the enterprise.

### The "100% failure rate" claim and the reply he expects
Tabor says he tells his students on the first day, "not cynically or sarcastically," that they are studying an enterprise that "so far has a 100% failure rate." He anticipates the response that Bible prophecy never fails and that only its interpreters have been wrong. He treats this as "a fair point certainly should be discussed," and notes that current online interpreters often say the earlier ones were wrong "but now I've got the truth."

### Messianic claimants of the late Second Temple period
Tabor plans to use the Dead Sea Scrolls to examine the Teacher of Righteousness, whom Michael Wise calls "our first documented messiah in history." He then turns to Josephus, who records eleven messianic figures besides Jesus, including Judas the Galilean and a figure captioned as "Thus" (likely Theudas, whom Tabor says is mentioned in the New Testament). Tabor's summary of their fate is blunt: "they were all killed some beheaded some crucified," and "put out of the way" by Jewish and Roman authorities. He uses this to frame the "incredible wave of Messianic expectation" and disappointment of that period.

### Early Christianity as an apocalyptic movement
He proposes examining earliest Christianity as an apocalyptic movement, starting with the "Jesus apocalypse" in Mark 13, which he calls "the original" of three parallel versions. He also plans to look at Paul, "our earliest witness to the Jesus movement," and at Revelation as a Christian reinterpretation of Daniel. He sets these alongside the Bar Kokhba revolt, citing letters said to be written by Bar Kokhba himself, and the Apostolic Fathers, asking whether "the Hope [is] beginning to fade."

### Chronology-making and amillennialism
Tabor notes that some Church fathers tried to calculate chronology for the end, working through the 1260, 1290 and 1335 days and the seventy weeks of Daniel 9. He describes amillennialism as the view that "the church is the kingdom" and that the prophecies have largely been fulfilled, with the final second coming and resurrection still held out. He says this view "definitely goes back to Antiquity," with Augustine and others, and links it to modern "preterist" variants.

### Modern date-setting movements
Tabor surveys American movements, beginning with the Millerites of the 1840s, whose numbers he calls "pretty impressive." He notes that Ellen G. White, credited as founder of the Seventh-day Adventist Church, said Miller "was right but he was wrong about what he expected but his numbers were right." He then covers Charles Taze Russell and his 1914 prediction, Herbert W. Armstrong and his 1975 booklet *In Prophecy*, and Hal Lindsey's dispensationalism. He says Lindsey popularized a view that traces back to John Darby and the Scofield Bible, centered on Israel's 1948 founding and the Six Day War.

### The Hebrew Bible's messianic texts and the full-preterist claim
Tabor plans to examine "10 basic texts" that Christians use as messianic prophecies and to ask whether Jesus or anyone else has fulfilled them. He notes that Christians usually say the unfulfilled parts await a future fulfillment by Jesus. He plans to close by discussing full preterists, who say Jesus has fulfilled "every single Prophecy of the Hebrew Bible."
