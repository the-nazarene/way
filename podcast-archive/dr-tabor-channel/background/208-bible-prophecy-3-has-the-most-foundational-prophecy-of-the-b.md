## Summary

This is the third video in Tabor's series on the interpretation of biblical prophecy. It asks whether the most foundational prophecy in the Bible, the visions of Daniel, has failed. Tabor argues that Daniel matters more than Revelation, which he calls "highly derivative" of Daniel (around 01:33). He first explains Daniel's place in the Christian canon (among the Major Prophets) versus the Hebrew Bible (among the Writings), and says the book was contested in the formation of both canons (00:00 to 04:42).

He then works through Daniel 2, the statue of gold, silver, bronze, iron and clay, and its four successive kingdoms. The standard historical reading he gives identifies them as Babylon, Media-Persia, Greece (Alexander and his successors) and the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes, whom he dates to the second century BCE (about 17:56 to 21:05). Daniel 7 repeats the scheme with beasts, a little horn and a "son of man" coming on the clouds. Tabor reads the persecution by the little horn (forbidding circumcision, Torah and festivals, and the three-and-a-half-year period) as matching Antiochus and the Maccabean revolt (about 39:56 to 44:35).

His main evidence is the mismatch between the text's expectations and what followed. The Maccabean state was small and lasted about a century, and Rome took over Judea in 63 BCE, so the stone did not fill the earth and no everlasting kingdom followed. He says most scholars therefore place the book in the Maccabean period. Interpreters who want the prophecy to hold must either stretch the fourth kingdom's legs and feet through Rome into modern times, or treat it as fulfilled in an earlier era, which he says leaves the later expectation unfulfilled (46:11 to 49:18). Tabor ends by saying he will take up Daniel 8, 9, and 11-12 in later videos, and he closes with a plea to study history before plotting the future (50:54).

## Key Teachings

### Daniel's canonical placement and the problem of which books "count"
Tabor notes that Daniel sits among the Major Prophets in Christian Bibles but in the Writings (Ketuvim) of the Hebrew Bible. He says this reflects the book's disputed status: it "wasn't accepted by all quarters of the Jewish worldwide Community," and the Hebrew Bible was formulated late, before and into the time of Jesus. He draws a parallel to Revelation's uncertain place in the early Christian canon, and to the Epistle of Barnabas. He also explains the codex as a practical response to the difficulty of handling many scrolls at once.

### The four kingdoms of Daniel 2 and the standard historical reading
Tabor summarizes the dream of Nebuchadnezzar: a statue with gold head, silver chest, bronze belly and thighs, iron legs, and feet of iron and clay, struck by a stone "cut out by no human hand." He presents the usual scholarly sequence as Babylon, Persia (Cyrus), Alexander the Great, and Antiochus IV in the Seleucid kingdom, which operated in the Holy Land. He stresses that the sequence is ordered and cannot be rearranged to fit later empires. He describes the fourth kingdom as "partly strong and partly brittle," and notes that the Seleucid timeline is close to the period of the Dead Sea Scrolls and Jesus.

### Antiochus, the Maccabees, and the failure of the "stone" to fill the earth
Tabor identifies Antiochus IV (also called Antiochus Epiphanes) as the persecutor who tried to stamp out Judaism, with the Maccabees resisting and overthrowing him. He argues that the Maccabean state, which "only lasted about 100 years," cannot plausibly be the stone that broke the kingdoms and "filled the Earth." He says that under a straightforward reading "you'd have to say Daniel is a fail Prophet," but he adds that for many interpreters the prophecy "never failed." He presents this as a question of interpretation.

### Daniel 7: the beasts, the little horn, and the "son of man"
He reads the four beasts (lion with eagle's wings, bear, winged four-headed leopard, and a terrible beast with ten horns) as parallel to the statue's kingdoms. The little horn with "eyes of a man" and "a mouth ... speaking great things" is Antiochus, who "made war with the Saints." Tabor says the Saints are the faithful who suffer martyrdom, which he calls a first in Western history. He notes that the "Son of man" is Aramaic bar enosh, an idiom for a human being, and he explicitly warns against jumping to Jesus: "don't jump to the New Testament here don't jump to Jesus let's hold it we're looking at the vision." He observes that the chapter's own interpretation identifies the son of man with "the Saints of the most high."

### Attempts to stretch the prophecy and the preterist option
Tabor surveys how interpreters have extended the fourth kingdom to Rome, and then to later powers. He shows a chart running from 30 BCE to the present, and another that identifies the iron-and-clay feet with the United Nations and the United States. He says these readings require stretching "those legs out some way," and he compares the attempt to the Tower of Babel. He describes the "so-called preterist view," in which the church and the Roman period fulfill the prophecy, and says it is hard to square with a Roman world that has not been the kingdom of God.

### Second Esdras (4 Ezra) as an early updating of Daniel
Tabor cites 2 Esdras 12:10 (which he calls "second Estrus" or "fourth Ezra," in the Apocrypha) as an example of a text that re-reads Daniel's fourth kingdom as Rome. He presents this as a repeated pattern: "every interpreter wants it to be later and later and later so that it'll fit his or her own time." He ties this to the Dead Sea Scroll community's expectation that the kittim (Romans) would be utterly defeated, which he says did not happen, and refers listeners to his previous video on that group.

### Methodological dilemma and the stated thesis
Tabor frames the problem as a choice: "you throw it out, you historicize it and say it did happen but then it failed in terms of what happened after Antiochus, or you try to stretch it out." He says most scholars take the first route. He notes that the modern interpreter faces "1500 to 2000 years" of stretching if the prophecy is to remain valid. He does not argue for a particular final answer in this video; he defers it to the next episodes.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Revelation is the primary prophetic text of the Bible.** Tabor says most people, especially Christians, assume this. He argues Daniel is more fundamental and that Revelation derives from it.
- **Daniel's visions are a straightforward future timeline.** Tabor argues that the standard historical reading places the fourth kingdom in the Seleucid period (Antiochus IV), not in the modern era, and that a plain reading fails.
- **Daniel's "son of man" is Jesus.** He warns against jumping to the New Testament and says the chapter itself interprets the figure as "the Saints of the most high."
- **The Maccabean victory fulfilled the stone prophecy.** He says the small Maccabean state did not fill the earth or set up a kingdom that lasted forever, so this reading does not work.
- **A prophecy that appears to fail must be stretched into modern times or else thrown out.** Tabor presents this as the dilemma most people face, not as a resolution; he defers his own answer.
