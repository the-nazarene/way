## Summary

Tabor asks why the "time of the end" in Daniel 8 has not arrived, given that the chapter's own angelic interpretation seems to tie the vision to the events of the 160s BCE. He argues that a straightforward historical reading identifies the little horn with Antiochus IV Epiphanes, whose persecution (c. 168 to 164 BCE) desecrated the Jerusalem temple, and that the vision was fulfilled when the Maccabees rededicated the sanctuary. He grounds this in the text of Daniel 8, in the consensus of critical historians (whom he says "universally agree" on the identification), and in his reading of 1 Maccabees. He then traces how later interpreters pushed the prophecy forward: the stretching of the four kingdoms to Rome and beyond, the "day for a year" calculations of William Miller (1843/1844) and Adam Clark (1966/1967), and the Seventh-day Adventist and Hal Lindsey developments. He reports that his own father wrote in the margin of Clark's commentary that the 1966 date would not be fulfilled, and that the Six Day War of June 1967 then brought Jerusalem under Jewish control.

The talk ends by saying that Daniel 8 seems to have been fulfilled in 164 BCE, but that the promises of an everlasting kingdom and the coming of the Messiah were not, which Tabor says sets up Daniel 9 and the 490-year period as the next episode. Tabor carries the entire talk as a monologue; there is no guest or host.

## Key Teachings

### The interpretation is given in the text itself
Tabor's starting point is that Daniel 8 is unusual because its meaning is "transparent" and "given within the prophecy itself." He reads the ram as the Persian kings (Cyrus and successors), the he-goat as Alexander the Great, the broken great horn as Alexander's death, and the four horns as the four divisions of his kingdom. The little horn that arises from one of them is, for him, Antiochus IV, a figure "historians universally agree" on. He notes that the little horn is not named in the vision itself.

### Antiochus's persecution as the fulfillment
Tabor reads the "continual burnt offering," the "transgression that makes desolate," and the "2300 evenings and mornings" (Daniel 8:14) as corresponding to Antiochus's desecration of the Jerusalem temple. He reads 1 Maccabees 1:20 onward: Antiochus enters the sanctuary, removes its furnishings, later sets up a "desolating sacrilege" on the altar, burns the books of the Torah, and forbids circumcision. He ties the 2300 "evenings and mornings" to roughly 1150 days, about three and a half years, from 168 to 164 BCE. He then reads 1 Maccabees chapter 4 as the rededication of the sanctuary "exactly what the prophecy says," with the Hanukkah legend of the oil lasting eight days.

### Why the "end" did not come
Tabor argues that Daniel 8:17 and 8:19 ("the time of the end," "the latter end of the indignation") and the wider Daniel 2 and 7 framework (an everlasting kingdom that breaks all earthly kingdoms) were not fulfilled in 164 BCE. The Maccabean period of Jewish independence lasted about a century before Rome took control in 63 BCE. He lays out the dilemma: either the prophecy failed and was a speculation of persecuted people, or it must be replayed in the future, which he says leads to a future temple, sacrifices, an outside ruler from the west and north, and the figure Christians often call the Antichrist.

### Stretching the kingdoms forward
Tabor describes a long tradition of stretching the four kingdoms of Daniel 2 and 7 so that the fourth reaches Rome and then the ten toes become later unions (he mentions the United Nations, the United States, and the European Union as candidate proposals). In this reading Antiochus becomes a "type" of a final evil ruler. He says Paul also appears to read Daniel 8 as pointing to his own time, though he gives no verse reference for that quotation.

### Date calculations: Miller, Adventists, and Adam Clark
Tabor explains William Miller's method: he dated the 2300 "days" as years, starting from 457 BCE (the decree of Artaxerxes in Ezra 7, which Miller linked to the decree in Daniel 9), and arrived at 1843, adjusted to 1844 after the "no year zero" correction. When Christ did not return, the Seventh-day Adventists, and Ellen G. White's visions, reinterpreted 1844 as Christ's entry into the heavenly sanctuary. Tabor says he covers this in his book "Why Waco" (on the Branch Davidians and David Koresh).

### Adam Clark's 1966/1967 date and the 1967 war
Tabor describes Adam Clark's five-volume commentary (1825), which dates the 2300 years from Alexander's invasion of Asia in 334 BCE, giving 1966 and then, with the zero-year correction, 1967. He notes that June 1967 (the Six Day War) gave Jews sovereignty over Jerusalem for the first time since 70 CE. He mentions that Hal Lindsey's "The Late Great Planet Earth" followed, and that Christian Zionist support for rebuilding the temple followed too. He uses his father's handwritten marginal note in the Clark volume ("no way... is this going to get fulfilled in 1966") as a personal illustration.

- **The "Antichrist" is simply someone against Christ.** Tabor says the term in his account refers to the figure who would stand instead of the Messiah, "the flip side of the Messiah," and that Jews and Christians have read it differently.
