## Summary

Tabor asks whether the longest continuous prophecy in the Hebrew Bible, Daniel 11 and the start of Daniel 12, was written after the events it describes and presented as foretelling them. He frames it as the last of three chapters he has been working through in his Bible prophecy series. Daniel 10 sets the scene on the Tigris, where an angelic figure reveals "the book of truth" and what will befall Daniel's people "in the latter days."

His answer rests on how closely Daniel 11 tracks known history. He reads the "king of the north" and "king of the south" as dynastic titles for the Seleucids and Ptolemies, and he walks through the wars between them in verses 5 to 20. Verses 21 to 35 he identifies as Antiochus Epiphanes: the flattery of those who abandon the covenant, the stopping of the daily offering, and the "abomination that makes desolate," which he ties to the events recounted in 1 Maccabees. He also points out that the prediction does not follow Antiochus's end. The Maccabees' victory is not narrated, and the predictions in verses 40 to 45, in which Antiochus conquers Egypt and dies between the seas and the holy mountain, were never fulfilled. Tabor concludes that the material from verse 36 onward may be a different, Antiochus-like figure, or a later writer's expectation. He also mentions that the second-century BCE events are "accurate" in detail. The talk ends without a final verdict on authorship. Tabor says he will address the dating, authorship, and the reapplication of these texts in a later installment, which will cover Rome, later Christian readings, and modern expectations. ## Key Teachings

### The king of the north and king of the south as dynastic titles
Tabor argues that the two "tracks" of Daniel 11 are "king of the north" (Seleucids, Syria) and "king of the south" (Ptolemies, Egypt). The phrase names whoever holds the dynasty, not one individual; the north-south alliance and marriage in verses 6 to 7 (the "daughter of the king of the south" who comes to the north) serve as his example. He says the wars between the two houses are complex, with five back-and-forth conflicts. He notes that this detail is "very accurate" and that "all the interpreters agree" on the identifications, including the Oxford annotated Bible's note tying verses 9 to 10 to Seleucus II's 242 BCE campaign against Egypt.

### Verses 21 to 35: Antiochus Epiphanes
Tabor reads verse 21 ("a contemptible person to whom royal majesty has not been given") as Antiochus Epiphanes. He cites the features he sees: he gains the kingdom by flattery, acts deceitfully, and "does what neither his fathers nor his father's fathers have done." He describes the Hellenistic demand that Jews abandon their covenant, the profaning of the temple, the halting of the "continual burnt offering," and the setting up of the "abomination that makes desolate." He says these events, around 167 to 164 BCE, are recounted in 1 Maccabees, and advises readers to read its first four or five chapters. He suggests the motive for the attack on Judea was money, since the temple held offerings from Jews worldwide.

### The break at verse 36 and the unfulfilled predictions
Tabor's main argument for a later or different layer is the break at verse 36. He points out that "the king shall do according to his will" would naturally refer to the king of the north, yet the description (exalting himself above every god, indifference to "the gods of his fathers," honoring "the one beloved by women") does not fit Antiochus, who was loyal to Zeus. Verses 40 to 45 predict a war with Egypt, Libya and Ethiopia, and an end between the sea and the holy mountain. He states: "None of these predictions was fulfilled." He says the "book of truth" framing and the phrase "until the time of the end" mark this section as the unfulfilled expectation, and he raises the possibility that its writer was someone other than the author of the earlier material.

### Michael, resurrection, and the end (Daniel 12)
Tabor summarizes the close of Daniel 11 and Daniel 12: Michael "the great prince," a "time of trouble such as never has been," deliverance for those "written in the book," and resurrection of those "who sleep in the dust of the earth," some to everlasting life and some to shame. He describes this as a Jewish view of angels in charge of nations in this period. He notes that Daniel is told to "go your way" and rest until the end, and that he "will stand in your allotted place at the end of days." He says he will return to the final ending in a later installment.

### Parallels across Daniel: 2, 7, 8, 9
Tabor links Daniel 11 to earlier chapters: the four kingdoms and the stone of chapter 2, the "little horn" of chapter 7 that "fits Antiochus pretty well," the 1,150 days and "2300 mornings and evenings" of the daily sacrifice in chapter 8, and the prince and desolating sacrilege of chapter 9. He argues these repetitions point to a single focus on Antiochus, with the Maccabean victory left out of the account.

### Reapplication to Rome and later readings
Tabor says the prophecy could be reapplied after its original setting. The fourth kingdom, he notes, came to be read as Rome, which entered the land in 63 BCE, a century after the Maccabean state's independence. He says that over later centuries the text has been applied to new rulers, including by people now expecting "this king of the north" to come again. He notes that the word "antichrist" is not used in the text, and that "a final evil ruler that ushers in the end of the age" is a later idea he traces from here. He flags this as the subject of the next installment's overview of how critical historians read these texts.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Daniel 11 is a genuine forecast of the future.** Tabor argues that its detail matches events up to Antiochus Epiphanes, and that the unfulfilled predictions in verses 40 to 45 are the point where a forecast would have failed, so the text's claim to foresight is doubtful.
- **"King of the north" always means one specific ruler.** He says the phrase refers to whoever holds the northern (Seleucid) throne, which is why the two tracks run through many kings.
- **The figure in verse 36 onward must be Antiochus.** He says the description, especially the disregard for "the gods of his fathers" and the honoring of "the one beloved by women," does not fit Antiochus, who was loyal to Zeus.
- **The text itself uses "antichrist."** He notes that the word is not used; the "final evil ruler" idea is a later development he traces from the text.
