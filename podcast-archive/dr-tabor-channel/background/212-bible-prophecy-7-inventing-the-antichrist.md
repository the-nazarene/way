## Summary

Tabor asks where the idea of a final evil ruler, the "Antichrist" in his scare-quoted usage, came from, and why it became so central to Bible prophecy. He traces it to the visions of Daniel 7, 8, 9, and 11, which he says all describe Antiochus IV Epiphanes, the Seleucid king who desecrated the Jerusalem temple in the 160s BCE. The Maccabean victory did not bring the end the texts seemed to expect. Tabor argues that later readers kept the prophecy alive by "moving" the fourth kingdom and its final ruler forward in time. He gives Dead Sea Scrolls material as the earliest evidence: a fragment he calls the "Son of God text" (4Q246), and a War scroll from Cave 1 that identifies the Kittim with Rome. He also describes how the same method was used in the modern period, citing David Koresh's reading of the 1991 Gulf War, which he says Koresh tied to Daniel 11.

Tabor's position is that Daniel 11 is the crucial text. He reads verses 21 to 35 as clearly describing Antiochus, but he finds that verses 36 to 45 do not fit him well, and he suggests the writer was already reaching beyond him. He acknowledges that most critical scholars would read the whole chapter as Antiochus, and he presents his own reading as one he is "not convinced" by, not as settled. The episode ends with Tabor saying he will return to Daniel repeatedly as he surveys later interpretations. He previews the next episode, on the Davidic Messiah, and asks whether any claimant has fulfilled those prophecies.

## Key Teachings

### Daniel's "final evil ruler" is first Antiochus IV
Tabor says that Daniel 7 (the little horn), 8, 9, and 11 all "trace down to this single figure," Antiochus Epiphanes. He lists the parallels: the profanation of the temple, the removal of the continual burnt offering, the "abomination that makes desolate," and the persecution of the righteous. He points readers to First Maccabees 1 to 4 (in the Apocrypha), which he says describes these events and was written shortly after the Maccabean victory. He also notes that the Maccabees established an independent Jewish state and rededicated the temple, which is still commemorated at Hanukkah, and he links this to John 10.

### Daniel 11 contains a "break" that opens the prophecy to later application
Tabor marks Daniel 11:35 ("until the time of the end") as a kind of pause. He reads verses 21 to 35 as Antiochus. In verses 36 to 45, he says, the language about a king who "shall do according to his will" and magnifies himself "above every God" fits Antiochus poorly, and at verse 40 the king of the north and king of the south change identity. He argues that this openness is why later interpreters could keep applying the text. He also says the "indignation" of verse 36 is spoken of as the filling up of Gentile sins, and that God did not intervene to set up the kingdom after Antiochus.

### The Antichrist as a "successor" figure, and the Dead Sea Scrolls
Tabor says that "inventing the Antichrist" means creating successors to Antiochus when the end failed to arrive. He calls the later figure an "anti-type" of Antiochus. The earliest example he gives is 4Q246 (which he calls the "Son of God text," cataloged in the transcript as "K4 qumran 246"), which he dates to the end of the first century BCE. He reads it as describing a coming evil ruler who will "be called the son of the most high God" and who will make people serve him and "trample" provinces until the people of God take over. He acknowledges the text is fragmentary and disputed, and that some scholars, including David Flusser, have argued for a good messianic reading. Tabor rejects that reading, though he notes that Christians were excited by the phrase because Luke 1 uses "son of the most high" of Jesus.

### The Kittim as Rome and the moving of the fourth kingdom
Tabor says a War scroll from Cave 1 at Qumran reads Daniel 11 as the king of the Kittim invading Egypt and cutting off the "Horn of Israel." He notes that scholars once read "Kittim" as the Maccabees but now take it to mean Rome. He argues that this shift lets the fourth kingdom become Rome, so the final ruler comes from Rome and the expected end slides into the first century CE. He describes how Pompey's siege of Jerusalem, the conflict between Hyrcanus and Aristobulus, and Caligula's attempt to place a statue in the temple all fed the expectation. None of these events brought the kingdom of God, and Tabor says the Romans held the Holy Land for centuries afterward.

### The 490-year clock and resetting the starting point
Tabor says Daniel 9's 490-year period gave readers a timetable. Because the starting date could be moved (from Nebuchadnezzar's time, to Cyrus's decree in 539 BCE, and onward), interpreters could place the final ruler in any era. He says Jewish communities of the late first century BCE and early first century CE thought they were close to the end because of this clock. He also says Paul's reference in 2 Thessalonians to the "man of sin" in the temple probably reflects this same expectation after Caligula's attempt.

### Modern application: David Koresh and the Gulf War
Tabor recounts his involvement with the Waco siege and says Koresh read Daniel 11:20-45 as describing the first Gulf War and Saddam Hussein. Tabor says Koresh understood the "king of the South" as the United States and its allies, and expected the end around 1995. Tabor uses this to show how readers across history have found their own rulers in these chapters. He also argues that the prophecy did not fit Hitler either, since the Allies triumphed after World War II, and that the end did not come.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Antichrist is a single, future figure predicted in Daniel and Revelation.** Tabor argues the "Antichrist" is a successive, shifting figure, created when earlier identifications failed, and that Daniel's visions originally describe Antiochus IV.
- **Daniel's prophecies predict an end-time scenario only.** Tabor says the chapters were read as describing the end, but that they were originally written about Antiochus and were later reapplied to Pompey, Caligula, Titus, and modern rulers.
- **The Qumran "Son of God" text proves a Christian-style messianic prophecy.** Tabor argues that 4Q246 describes an evil ruler, not a good Messiah, although he acknowledges that Christians were excited by its wording.
- **Spiritual or symbolic readings preserve the prophecy's hope.** Tabor says the text does not read this way and that such interpretations leave the promised victory unfulfilled.
