## Summary

Tabor asks whether the first five books of the Hebrew Bible (the Torah or Pentateuch) contain messianic apocalyptic material and an eschatology, meaning an expectation about the end of the age. He says the answer is "yes and no depending on your point of view." He presents three passages that are often cited as predicting a coming figure: Genesis 49:10 (Shiloh), Numbers 24:15-17 (the star and scepter), and Deuteronomy 18:15-18 (the prophet like Moses). He takes them one at a time, weighing translation options, a gematria calculation, the later history of messianic claims, and the Christian interpretation that merges the three roles into one figure.

Tabor repeatedly frames the material as a historical and analytical survey of interpretations, as he would teach it in a university classroom, and says none of it is his personal view of Daniel or Revelation. He treats the Shiloh and prophet-like-Moses readings as live possibilities and notes that the star-and-scepter text does not name the Davidic line, so he did not count it among his ten Davidic prophecies. He does not reach a firm conclusion on whether the Torah predicts the Messiah. ## Key Teachings

### Genesis 49:10: "until Shiloh comes"

Tabor introduces Genesis 49 as a poetic chapter, possibly older than the final form of Genesis, in which the dying patriarch Jacob blesses his sons "in days to come." Judah receives a lion image and a ruler's scepter that "shall not depart from Judah." Tabor reads this as applying to Davidic kings from Judah down to Zedekiah in the sixth century BCE. The harder clause is "until he comes to whom it belongs and to him shall be the obedience of the peoples." Tabor says the three Hebrew words usually rendered "until Shiloh comes" are a translation problem. He notes a town of Shiloh north of Jerusalem and a possible association of Shiloh with peace. He also gives a variant reading, "literally until what is his is brought to him," which he prefers to the King James-style rendering, and he flags the "obedience of the peoples" as a phrase that seems to look toward the Gentile world. He says he does not think Genesis 49:10 is quoted or alluded to in the New Testament, though he would check the Dead Sea Scrolls.

### Gematria: Shiloh and Moses

Tabor gives a mystical reading he presents as an interpretive option. In gematria, Hebrew letters carry numerical values. He says the letters of Shiloh total 345, the same as the word Moses, so some mystical interpreters read Shiloh as "the new Moses." He ties this to the Dead Sea Scrolls, where he says the community believed its Prophet or teacher was the prophet like Moses, a point he says he covered in his episode on the first Messiah.

### Numbers 24:15-17: the star and scepter

Balaam is a non-Israelite prophet hired by the king of Moab to curse Israel. Tabor stresses that Balaam claims to receive messages from God and that his oracles turn into blessings. In the oracle, Balaam sees a figure "but not now" and "not near," so the prophecy points to the future. The text says "a star shall come forth out of Jacob and a scepter shall rise out of Israel" and that it "shall crush the forehead of Moab." Tabor notes that the text does not mention Judah or David, which is why he did not count it among his ten Davidic prophecies. He then traces its later use to the second Jewish revolt of about 132 CE, when Bar Kokhba (called "son of the star" in the Messianic reading) was proclaimed a Messiah, with Rabbi Akiva acknowledging him. Tabor adds that Josephus reports a dozen or more first-century Messianic figures who were defeated by Rome, and that the star-and-scepter prophecy remained a live reference point through those movements.

### Deuteronomy 18:15-18: a prophet like Moses

Tabor reads Deuteronomy 18:15-18 as Moses' final address, in which the Lord promises "a prophet like me from among you from your brethren." He emphasizes the qualification "like me," and he links it to the closing note of Deuteronomy 34:10 that no prophet has arisen in Israel like Moses, "whom the Lord knew face to face." He describes Moses' unusual intimacy with God: seeing the "timuna" or form of God, speaking "mouth to mouth," going into the tent of meeting, and seeing the back of God's glory in Exodus. He argues that this intimacy is what makes the figure distinctive. He rejects the rabbinic suggestion that the prophet is Joshua, arguing from the book of Joshua that Joshua consulted the priest through the Urim and Thummim and was never described in these extravagant terms. Tabor also notes that the Torah passage states that the prophet will be given God's words and will speak "all that I command him."

### Christian synthesis: king, prophet and priest

Tabor explains that Christian interpretation combines the three figures into one. Jesus is the Davidic king, the prophet like Moses, and a priest "after the order of Melchizedek," a reading of Psalm 110 that Tabor says he thinks is mistranslated. He describes the Antichrist as the "flip side" of the Christ figure in Christian interpretation, which he compares to the evil figure in Daniel. He says that some texts he will cover have multiple figures, such as a prophet, a priest and a king in the middle of the scheme.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That the Torah plainly predicts a Davidic Messiah in every passage.** Tabor says Genesis 49:10 is unclear about its referent, and the star-and-scepter text does not mention Judah or David, so he did not count it among his ten Davidic prophecies.
- **That the prophet like Moses must be Joshua (the rabbinic view some hold).** Tabor argues that Joshua consulted the priest by lot and was never described with Moses' intimacy with God.
- **That the second Jewish revolt was a clean Roman victory.** Tabor says it was "almost a stalemate" and that the Romans needed Spanish auxiliary forces to put it down.
- **That the Messianic claims of the period were unique to Jesus.** Tabor says Josephus reports a dozen or more first-century Messianic figures, and that Jesus was one of them.

- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor notes that Christian readings place Jesus as the Davidic king and the prophet like Moses at once, but he treats this as a later synthesis of separate texts rather than something the Torah states directly.
