## Key Teachings

### 1. What a "floating prophecy" is
Tabor defines floating prophecies as texts without fixed chronology, whose dates "can float down through history" and get reassigned. He says the reason such a text keeps being applied to new crises is that it is generic: "each time there's a crisis with the Jews or in our own day for the last 75 years or so there's been a state of Israel that you could begin reading this and applying it to." He also says such readings require the prophecy to describe "the worst time ever," which makes it always seem to be the future.

### 2. Isaiah 2:1-4 and the "latter days"
Tabor notes that Isaiah 2 is dated only by the superscription in Isaiah 1:1 (kings Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah). The vision in 2:1-4 begins with "the latter days," which he argues means something like "afterward" rather than a specific end of history. He reads the mountain of the Lord's house as Jerusalem exalted over the nations, with the nations streaming up to receive Torah. He points to the Isaiah wall opposite the United Nations, and the Soviet-era statue of a sword beaten into a plowshare, as examples of how the passage is used as a "dream" of peace.

### 3. Joel as the "floating" prophet
Tabor says Joel 1:1 gives only "the word of the Lord came to Joel the son of Pethuel," with no king or period. He reads Joel 1-2 as a locust plague that becomes an invading army, "a nation has come against my land," and then the "day of the Lord" (described as a day of darkness and gloom). He notes that Joel's language, "a final kind of battle," is what leads readers to treat the text as end-times prophecy. He compares Joel's account of the land as "the Garden of Eden" before the army and "desolate Wilderness" after it with the Ukraine war, which he cites as an example of how readers match ancient imagery to modern events.

### 4. Joel 2:18-32 and the "after" passages
In his reading, God becomes "jealous for his land" and restores it (he cites the passage as "21:18"; the verse is Joel 2:18-19), and the restoration includes rain and an outpouring of the spirit (Joel 2:28-32). Tabor says Joel 2:28 uses "afterward," which he treats as the same vague "latter days" frame. He argues that the people who call themselves preterists are confident the passage was fulfilled in 70 CE, but that Joel 3 does not describe the Christian church and can only be applied to it by "heavy heavy allegorization."

### 5. Joel 3: judgment of the nations and the valley of Jehoshaphat
Tabor reads Joel 3 as God gathering the nations to "the valley of Jehoshaphat," which he says "many think" is the Kidron Valley but which he thinks is more symbolic. He notes that Judah and Jerusalem's exile is the setting, and he lists the conquerors who could fit (Babylonians, Persians, Greeks, Romans, Byzantines, Muslims, the Ottomans, the British). He says that "most Scholars would put this probably as Babylon." He says Joel 3:9-10 reverses Isaiah 2:4 ("beat your plowshares into swords"), and he treats the "harvest" and "valley of decision" imagery as the basis for the Revelation passages he mentions. He reads the promises in 3:17-18 (Jerusalem holy, "strangers shall never pass through it again," mountains dripping with wine, Judah inhabited forever) as unfulfilled in any literal sense, which is why readers must either claim they happened or move them into the future.

### 6. The three ways to read these texts (partly unfinished)
Tabor says there are "three ways to go." The first is that everything happened around the time of Jesus and was fulfilled allegorically, which he says the New Testament seems to do by applying these verses to "this generation." The second is the dispensationalist or futurist view that it will all happen again. He argues that neither fits well, and states the dilemma as: "either the Bible's wrong or Jesus is wrong in Mark 13... or Joel is wrong." The transcript ends before he names the third option. He does say that he believes the early Jesus movement, including the authors of Mark 13 and Revelation, "did think it was going to happen in their own day."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That Joel 1-3 describes a single final invasion of Israel:** Tabor says the text's "final kind of battle" reading is not supported by its generic language, and that the same passages have been applied to Babylon, Rome and later powers.
- **That Joel 3 describes the Christian church:** He says applying it to the church requires "heavy heavy allegorization," and that "when you get down to the details it doesn't apply at all."
- **That the Hebrew phrase "latter days" points to a fixed end of the age:** He argues the phrase often means simply "afterward," so the text has "no chronology."
- **That these prophecies are being fulfilled in today's news:** He warns that online readers are matching Joel and Isaiah to the 2023 war, and urges caution.
