## Summary

Tabor asks whether Zechariah 9-14 can be read as a prophecy about the present Hamas-Israel war, and whether a final siege of Jerusalem is being predicted. He opens by noting that many online commentators were mapping Bible prophecy onto the war that began on October 7, and he presents this talk as a verse-by-verse counterweight. His central claim is that these chapters are a "floating" prophecy: they cannot be confidently dated or assigned to an author. He contrasts them with Zechariah 1-8, which he says can be dated to about 520-515 BCE, and notes that chapters 9 onward drop the formula "the word of the Lord came to Zachariah."

Tabor argues that the text is organized as two "oracles" (9-11 and 12-14), with chapter 14 loosely attached. He walks through place names (Damascus, Gaza, Ashkelon, Ekron, Ephraim, Assyria, Egypt) and shows how each could be matched to modern events, then explains why such matches do not fit well. He points to the shepherd material in chapters 11 and 13, which he connects to New Testament citations, and to a parallel he draws with the Dead Sea Scrolls' Damascus Document. He also compares the situation to Isaiah, whose book he says is commonly divided into several authorship blocks.

The talk ends with a pastoral appeal rather than a conclusion about the future. Tabor urges listeners to read the passages together, in historical context, and to be wary of preachers who use prophecy to stir up expectations. He says he will take up Isaiah in the next video.

## Key Teachings

### Floating prophecy and the dating problem

Tabor frames Zechariah 9-14 as a "floating" text in the sense he introduced in video 13 on Joel: prophecies that are hard to date and attach to a period, and sometimes to an author, even when they appear under a known name. He says the first eight chapters of Zechariah are in "very secure territory" for dating, clustering around 520-515 BCE. From chapter 9 the framing changes: there is no dated setting, and the chapters begin with "the word of the Lord" without the usual introduction. He describes chapters 9-11 as "Oracle one" and 12-13 as "Oracle two," with chapter 14 beginning in a manner that resembles the second oracle but is not connected to it. He also cites the book of Isaiah, which he says most scholars divide into sections (1-39, 40-55, 56-66) written at different times, as a precedent for thinking of one book's name covering several layers.

### Modern readings and the place-name problem

Tabor demonstrates how the opening of chapter 9 can be read as describing the present conflict. Hadrach and Damascus, Tyre and Sidon (which he ties to Lebanon and Hezbollah), Ashkelon, Gaza and Ekron are all names he says people are matching to current events. He notes that Ashkelon is a Jewish city where he has friends, and that the Iron Dome system is stopping rockets there. He observes that "Palestine" derives from Philistia, the name he says the Romans began to use, and that Zechariah 9:6 mentions Philistia. His point is that the text's details do not line up cleanly with any scenario he has seen, from Zionism's beginnings to the present. He also questions the assumption that Assyria means modern Iraq or Egypt means a present-day place, noting that "most interpreters who are applying it to this time would say Egypt is a symbol of captivity."

### The shepherd, the thirty pieces of silver and Zechariah 11

Zechariah 11 presents the prophet as a shepherd of a flock "doomed to slaughter," who takes two staffs (Grace and Union), removes three shepherds in one month, and is paid thirty pieces of silver that are then thrown into the treasury. Tabor says this is hard to fit to the last week of Jesus' life or to Judas Iscariot. He argues that the gospel writers, Matthew especially, are finding phrases and applying them "in a kind of a this is like that fashion but not literally," in the manner he attributes to the rabbis. He also reads the worthless shepherd passage at the end of chapter 11 as a warning rather than good news.

### Zechariah 12-13: the pierced one, the struck shepherd and the Dead Sea parallel

In chapter 12 Tabor reads Jerusalem as "a heavy stone" and a "cup of reeling" that will be the object of a siege. He notes that Zechariah 12:10 ("they will look on him whom they pierce") is quoted in Revelation, and that Christians read it as the second coming of Jesus while Jews may read it differently. He suggests some have proposed Josiah as the pierced figure but says "we really don't know how to fit it in." Chapter 13:7 ("strike the Shepherd that the Sheep may be scattered") is the passage Tabor identifies as the one Jesus quotes on the night of his betrayal, on the way to Gethsemane. He says that the Dead Sea Scrolls community also read this material, with the Teacher of Righteousness as a possible referent, and that the Damascus Document (copy B) quotes it, possibly with the shepherd understood as a wicked high priest or as their slain teacher. Tabor says he is teaching a course on the Dead Sea Scrolls and used this as an example.

### Chapter 14: the siege, the Mount of Olives and the Feast of Tabernacles

Chapter 14 opens with a day of the Lord in which Jerusalem is besieged, its spoil divided, and half its population exiled, before the Lord fights against the nations. Tabor describes the Mount of Olives splitting, living waters flowing from Jerusalem, a plague on the nations' people and animals, and the survivors going up each year to keep the Feast of Tabernacles. He says Christians read the feet on the Mount of Olives as Jesus, and notes that the living-waters passage is used in Jewish liturgy. He also points to the final verses, where the bells of the horses and the pots in Jerusalem are "holy to the Lord," which he reads as a picture of universal holiness and perhaps of a rebuilt temple with sacrifices.

### A call for caution and historical reading

Tabor compares these prophecies to Daniel 9 and 11, which speak of another destruction of Jerusalem and a time of trouble "worse ever," and says he is not sure people want that scenario. He concludes that the historical background of these texts is uncertain, that scholars cannot pin down when interpreters began applying systems of end-times reading to them, and that the Bible should not be treated as a "roulette wheel." He recommends the HarperCollins Study Bible, produced by the Society of Biblical Literature, as a resource that takes a historical view.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Zechariah 9-14 is a current-events roadmap.** Tabor argues that the place names and images can be matched to the 2023 war only by selecting pieces, and that the passage does not fit any scenario cleanly.
- **Zechariah 11's thirty pieces of silver and shepherd describe Judas and Jesus' last week.** Tabor says the passage does not literally fit the betrayal, and that the gospel writers apply phrases from prophecy in a non-literal way.
- **Zechariah 12:10 refers to the second coming of Jesus.** Tabor notes that Christians read it this way, but says the identity of the pierced figure is unknown and that Jewish readings differ.
- **Prophecy is a roulette wheel to be opened at random.** Tabor says the Bible's prophetic texts have historical contexts and layered authorship that should be read verse by verse.
