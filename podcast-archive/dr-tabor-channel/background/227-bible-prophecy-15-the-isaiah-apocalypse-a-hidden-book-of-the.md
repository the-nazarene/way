## Summary

Tabor asks what Isaiah 24-27, which scholars call the "Isaiah apocalypse," says and why it has been so influential, even though it is rarely read on its own. He opens with a search for "Israel war Hamas Bible prophecy" and an article in the Christian Post, which reports Benjamin Netanyahu saying "we shall realize the prophecy of Isaiah" and quotes Isaiah 60:18. Tabor uses this to contrast thematic quotation of Isaiah with claims that a passage predicts a specific event. He then criticizes the article's reading of Isaiah 60:16 as a reference to Jesus, arguing that the "Lord" and "Redeemer" there are Yahweh.

The main part of the talk is a reading of Isaiah 24-27, which Tabor argues stands out from the surrounding chapters because it is cosmic rather than tied to a particular king or invasion. He reads its warnings of a laid-waste earth, a broken everlasting covenant (which he tentatively links to the Noahic covenant of Genesis 9), a feast for all peoples, the swallowing up of death, and the bodily resurrection of "your dead" in Isaiah 26:19. He cites the Harper Collins Study Bible notes as agreeing that the chapters are "floating," that Isaiah probably did not write them, and that they were later embedded in the book. He also suggests that Paul in 1 Corinthians 7 and Jesus in Mark 13 draw on this material.

The talk closes with a summary of why these chapters have been reapplied across history: their universal, generic language makes them available to later apocalyptic writers. Tabor ends by urging viewers to read Isaiah 24-27 directly and to use a good study Bible. The talk is almost entirely Tabor speaking; the only other voice is the quoted Netanyahu speech and the Christian Post article as he reports them.

## Key Teachings

### The "floating" prophecy and the Isaiah apocalypse as a unit
Tabor introduces this talk as part of a series on "floating prophecies," passages embedded in the prophets that are hard to date and do not clearly point to specific historical events. He identifies Isaiah 24-27 as a set piece of four chapters that scholars call the Isaiah apocalypse, and says it differs in style from Isaiah 1-23 and 28-39. He presents it as the "floating prophecy par excellence." He has already covered Joel 3 and Zechariah 9-14 in earlier episodes (13 and 14).

### Universal scope rather than Jerusalem alone
Tabor argues that the chapters speak of the whole earth. He reads the opening, where "the Lord will lay waste the Earth," and says that although the Hebrew word can be rendered "land" and confined to the Holy Land, the reading becomes more cosmic as the text goes on. He points to the judgment on "the host of Heaven in heaven and the kings of the Earth" and the "cities of the Gentiles" as evidence that the scope is wider than ancient Israel. He notes Jerusalem and Mount Zion are lifted up within this universal frame.

### Authorship and date
Tabor says that scholars (he cites the Harper Collins Study Bible notes) do not think Isaiah wrote these chapters. He gives the reasons as style, vocabulary, and the lack of any claim to be by Isaiah. He places them within the larger scholarly division of Isaiah into First, Second (chapters 40-55), and Third (chapters 56-66) Isaiah, and says these four chapters are embedded inside First Isaiah. He does not offer a specific date for them.

### Resurrection of the righteous dead (Isaiah 26:19)
Tabor describes the Hebrew view of death in Isaiah 26:13-14: the other lords "are dead they will not live," and the dead become "Shades" in Sheol. He argues that the passage affirms that wicked nations will not rise. Only those who struggled and "brought forth nothing" are raised, in Isaiah 26:19. He calls this, together with Daniel 12, "the only reference we have in the Hebrew Bible" to the resurrection of the dead. He adds that the Hebrew reads more literally as "my dead body shall rise," and suggests a translator may have softened that. He is explicit that the resurrection is limited to the righteous.

### The feast, the veil, and the swallowing of death
Tabor reads Isaiah 25:6-8 as a banquet of "wine on the leaves" for all peoples, the destruction of the veil over the nations, the swallowing up of death, and the wiping away of tears. He notes that the Dead Sea Scrolls and the New Testament later develop this as a "Messianic banquet." He presents the feast as a universal reward for the righteous poor and needy, not a triumph for Israel alone.

### Leviathan, exile, and regathering
Tabor links Isaiah 27:1, where the Lord punishes Leviathan the fleeing serpent, to an emerging apocalyptic idea of a dragon or chaos power that God subdues. He reads Isaiah 27:12-13 as the one clearly geographic element in the chapters: from the river Euphrates to the brook of Egypt, Israel is gathered, including the lost northern exiles from Assyria and those driven to Egypt, who will worship at Jerusalem. He contrasts this with Joel 3 and Zechariah 14, which he says are more narrowly about a siege of Jerusalem.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Netanyahu's "prophecy of Isaiah" as a prediction of the current war:** Tabor says the quote from Isaiah 60:18 is thematic, the kind of line people take from the Psalms or prophets to express hope, not a claim that every line predicts the war.
- **Isaiah 60:16 as a reference to Jesus:** Tabor calls this reading "utterly ridiculous" and says the Lord and the Redeemer are Yahweh, with the Messiah a distinct figure.
- **Isaiah 24-27 as Isaiah's own prophecy about a specific historical event:** Tabor says scholars do not attribute it to Isaiah and it does not claim to be his.
- **Isaiah 26:19 as a general resurrection of all the dead:** Tabor argues the text affirms resurrection of the righteous only, while the wicked are left in the grave.
