## Key Teachings

### The shared commitment to literal historical reading
Tabor's central claim is that the conservative literalist and the historical critic both read prophetic texts as referring to the conditions of the authors' own world. The scholar asks what the authors intended and the dispensationalist asks what the text predicts, but both take "Jerusalem" to mean Jerusalem. He puts the scholar's question this way: "a scholar would be looking at the past and trying to understand what the authors had in mind." [07:54] He says the critical reader would have to admit that the authors expected literal events before their eyes, though they did not happen, and that the "strange bedfellows" label fits because the literalist and the critic agree on that much. [23:38]

### Jerusalem, Israel and the modern political turn
Tabor traces how the reading of "Israel" and "Jerusalem" as the Jewish people and the literal city became common in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, after John Darby's teaching. He cites events he treats as turning points: the British replacing the Ottoman Turks, the 1947-48 partition and independence, the 1967 capture of the Old City, and the 1948 war that expelled Jews from the Old City. He notes that the Ottoman census of 1900 (which he says appears on Wikipedia) counted more Jews than Muslims or Christians in the Old City, and that Jerusalem was a Muslim city in terms of control, not population. [11:05] [12:40] He gives Zechariah 14 (the nations gathered against Jerusalem) as an example a literalist would apply to the present. [06:22]

### Allegorical and typological reading: Paul and Augustine
Tabor presents the allegorical tradition as a long-running alternative. He says Augustine in the City of God made the Temple and Antichrist into generic spiritual categories and that the root goes back to Paul, who spoke of an earthly and a heavenly Jerusalem and called the Galatians passage an allegory. He says that for most of church history few readers took prophecy as geopolitical events at the end of the age, with apocalyptic flare-ups as exceptions. [15:50] [17:25] He notes that the idea of a Jerusalem above was also present at Qumran and in other Jewish groups that imagined a heavenly temple. [22:07]

### Preterism: Schweitzer's view and Tabor's position
Tabor says he takes a preterist reading of Revelation, placing it in the time of Nero and Domitian, and that he follows Albert Schweitzer in holding that the early followers, Jesus included, expected the end of the age incorrectly. He says the predictions "fail to come about for me that's fairly bottom line." [17:25] He ties this to his apocalypticism course, saying his students will sometimes object that they are "full preterists" who think all the prophecies happened. [18:58]

### Full preterism as apologetics
Tabor argues that full preterism (the view that all prophecy, including 1 Thessalonians 4 and 1 Corinthians 15 on resurrection, has already been fulfilled) is "really a child of apologetics." He describes its method as ticking off Mark 13, Matthew 24 and Luke 21 as fulfilled, with the church as the kingdom and the church as the new Israel, and says this is an attempt to "save the day for prophecies that seem to have never happened." [20:33] He objects that the Messiah has not done what Daniel 2 and Daniel 7 describe and that the world still appears to be "a world of sin and death." [22:07]

### Paul's expectation of literal events
Tabor reads 2 Thessalonians (which he says many scholars do not regard as authentically Pauline, though it reflects the Pauline school) as expecting a literal "man of sin" to sit in the Temple of God, a scenario he links to Caligula's 41 CE order to place his statue in the Jerusalem Temple. He argues that Paul's Jerusalem-above imagery does not mean Paul expected only a spiritual outcome, since Paul "is reading Daniel like all the other interpreters of the first century and he's expecting very literal things to happen." [22:07]

### The Daniel 9 chronology and self-fulfilment
Tabor says the 490 years of Daniel 9 are hard to stretch, and reports that a contemporary literalist (John Haggee, in a video Tabor cites) argues the prophetic clock stopped in 70 CE and restarted in 1917. [25:14] He warns that some believers "decide to take it into their own hands," citing Dennis Rohan's 1960s attempt to help prophecy along by destroying a Jerusalem mosque, and says Muslims use this example to claim Christian forces want the mosque torn down. [26:47]

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Literal prophetic reading belongs only to fundamentalists.** Tabor argues that critical historians also read prophecy literally in the sense of taking the authors' words and historical setting seriously; they differ only on fulfilment.
- **Jerusalem and Israel in prophecy always mean the church or spiritual Israel.** Tabor says the allegorical reading is a later development, traced to Paul and Augustine, and that the authors of the prophetic texts meant the literal city and people.
- **Jews were absent from Jerusalem until recent times.** Tabor says Jews were always in the Holy Land, and that in the 1900 Ottoman census they outnumbered Muslims and Christians in the Old City, though Muslims held political control.
- **Full preterism is a sound way to read prophecy.** Tabor says it is an apologetic attempt to claim that all prophecies were fulfilled, and that it does not match the Messiah's unfinished work or the continuing "world of sin and death."
