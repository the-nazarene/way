## Summary

Tabor asks "When, Where, What?" about the war of Gog and Magog, which many readers treat as the final battle before the end of the age. He calls Ezekiel 38-39 a "floating prophecy": it is introduced as happening in "the latter years" or "the last days," yet he argues it does not clearly fit any historical moment. He starts from the end of the story in Revelation 20, where a thousand-year period is followed by an attack from Gog and Magog on "the camp of the Saints" and then a judgment at the Great White Throne. He then moves back to Ezekiel 37, where the valley of dry bones is interpreted as the regathering of Israel under a Davidic king, and reads Ezekiel 38-39 as the invasion that follows that restoration.

Tabor surveys the main interpretive camps. He describes the dispensationalist reading in the Scofield Bible, whose footnote identifies the northern power as Russia. He describes the more recent reading that identifies the enemy as Islamic fundamentalism, which he says has gained traction since 9/11. He also describes the allegorical reading, in which the passage symbolizes the struggle of good and evil. To test these, he uses the table of nations in Genesis 10 and a map from the Jewish Study Bible, and he notes that Gomer, Meshech, and Tubal are placed in roughly modern Turkey. He draws on the approach of academic study Bibles such as the HarperCollins Study Bible, which read the texts in their original settings and distinguish layers of composition.

The talk ends without a firm verdict. Tabor concludes that "the streams of interpretation" diverge, that a historian's approach is to place the texts in their own time, and that a future video will cover Islamic eschatology and the early church fathers. He also gives a brief, unsourced account of Nazi plans in North Africa and Palestine during World War II as one example of how each generation has tried to fit the prophecy to its own crises.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Revelation 20 as a sequence of events
Tabor reads Revelation 20 "as a sequential set of events": Christ's visible return (chapter 19), the binding of Satan for a thousand years, a "first resurrection" in which the souls of the beheaded "come to life and reign with Christ," then the release of Satan and the assembly of "Gog and Magog" against "the camp of the Saints and the Beloved city." Fire comes down from heaven, the devil is thrown into the lake of fire, and the dead are judged "by what was written in the books." He takes the "Book of Life" to be a record of each person's deeds, which he calls a works judgment, and he says the passage shows that the resurrection described is not "resuscitating corpses." He explicitly sets aside the debate over the thousand years for another video.

### 2. Ezekiel 37 and the regathering of Israel
Tabor argues that Ezekiel 37 must be read through its own interpretation, not as a general resurrection. The bones are "the whole house of Israel," meaning a scattered people in exile. God promises to "open your Graves" and "bring you home," to unite Judah and the northern tribes "into one nation," and to set "my servant David" as king over them. Tabor says the regathering of Israel is "the major theme of the Hebrew prophets," and that Jesus apparently expected it, citing the sayings Matthew and Luke share, in which the Twelve will sit on twelve thrones over the twelve tribes.

### 3. Ezekiel 38-39 and the invasion
Tabor reads the figure of Gog as a person, "the chief Prince of Meshech and Tubal," and Magog as his land. The coalition includes Persia, Kush, and Put, with Gomer from "the uttermost parts of the north." The invaders strike "the quiet people who dwell securely" in "unwalled villages," and they are destroyed by fire, hail, and earthquake. Burial takes seven months, and birds and beasts consume the dead. Tabor stresses the passage's stated purpose: God's glory will be shown "among the Nations," and Israel will know that its earlier exile came from its own iniquity. He notes that the passage does not match Revelation 20 closely, since Ezekiel speaks of the whole of Israel's return rather than a partial resurrection.

### 4. Three approaches to the text
- **Dispensationalist (futurist):** Tabor uses the Scofield Bible (a 1945 printing) to show the footnote that identifies the northern power with Russia and ties the passage to Zechariah 12 and 14, Revelation 14, and Revelation 19. He also notes that Scofield reads two invasions, one in Revelation 16 with an army of "200 million" gathered at Armageddon.
- **Modern political application:** Tabor says that since 9/11, and especially with Isis, al-Qaeda, and Hamas, many interpreters have shifted the enemy from a European or Nazi-like power to Islamic fundamentalism. He calls this reading likely to "get a lot of traction."
- **Allegorical or symbolic:** Tabor says many Christians and other believers take the passage as a symbol of "the forces of Good and Evil struggling through history," though he doubts this fits the text's concrete details, such as seven months of burial.
- **Historical-critical approach:** Tabor describes how academic study Bibles treat these chapters: they place the texts in their original contexts, distinguish layers of authorship, and note that Ezekiel's chapters 40-48 "becomes very very different" from the earlier material. He concludes that the options are the "feudal hopes and dreams" of ancient writers, allegory, or futurism.

### 5. Genesis 10 and the geography of the names
Using a map in the Jewish Study Bible, Tabor follows the table of nations: the sons of Shem, Ham, and Japheth. He places Gomer, Meshech, and Tubal in Asia Minor, south of the Black Sea, and Put and Kush in North Africa. He identifies Javan with Greece and Cyprus. He says that "Kittim" in the Dead Sea Scrolls was later applied to the Romans in Jesus' time. He treats these identifications as "our best guess," and he cautions that anyone who applies them today should recognize that the geography is contested.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That Gog and Magog is a battle that is about to happen in our time.** Tabor argues the passage is a "floating prophecy" that has been reapplied to each era (Russia, Nazi Germany, and Islamic fundamentalism), and none of the applications has yet been shown to fit the text.
- **That Revelation 20's resurrection means corpses are revived.** Tabor says the "Sea gave up the dead" detail and the judgment by written records show a different picture, which he links to his series on ideas of the afterlife.
- **That Ezekiel 37 is a general resurrection of the dead at the end of the age.** Tabor argues the immediate interpretation given in the text is the regathering of the exiled tribes of Israel.
- **That Ezekiel 38 identifies Russia.** Tabor presents the Russia identification as the Scofield footnote's reading, and he treats the name-identifications from Genesis 10 as a "best guess" that depends on contested geography.
