## Summary

Tabor asks whether the 2023 war in Israel (his description calls it the war "to destroy Hamas and other militant Muslim groups") was predicted in biblical prophecy. He works through what he calls "floating prophecies" of the Hebrew Bible: passages about the last days that he says are hard to date or attach to a specific author, such as Isaiah 2, Joel 3, Isaiah 24-27, Zechariah 9-14, and Ezekiel 38-39. He presents this episode as the seventh text in that group, with Psalm 83 as the focus.

Tabor's main argument is that Psalm 83, attributed to Asaf, is a psalm of a nation surrounded by hostile neighbors and that its enemy list can be mapped onto modern Arab and Muslim groups and states. He ties the psalm's wording ("Come, let us wipe them out as a nation") to the Hamas charter. He also says the psalm's ending, in which the enemies turn to the Lord, links it to Isaiah 2 and Zechariah 14. He is careful to say the mapping is loose: he notes that the psalm seems to imply divine action rather than Israeli military action, and that the enemies' ancient gods differ from Allah. He stresses that the outcome described in these texts "has not come about," and that the details are "very very difficult to merge all together."

The talk ends as a preview. Tabor says the next episodes will survey current interpretations, including the older Lindsey view of a revived Roman Empire and newer views that point to a Muslim caliphate. He also introduces the preterist position, which places fulfillment in the first century and cites Mark 13 ("this generation"), and says he will examine it later. He closes by urging viewers to read the texts critically.

## Key Teachings

### Floating prophecies as a category
Tabor defines these passages as set pieces that "seem to be kind of floating through time." They concern "the end of the age" but cannot always be dated or tied to their traditional author. His example is Isaiah 24-27, which he says "seems to be inserted" and whose style and vocabulary differ from the material around it. He says Joel is an entire book that fits this pattern. The category is what unites the episodes, and he treats Psalm 83 as an addition to that list rather than a new kind of text.

### Psalm 83 as a victory psalm of Asaf
Tabor reads Psalm 83 from the Revised Standard Version. He calls it "a song of celebration" and "actually a victory psalm," attributed to Asaf, whom he identifies as a singer or poet in David's time. He notes that the psalm uses the divine name Elohim rather than Yahweh in its opening, and says the psalm "is one of those floating prophecies." He also says, "it might have applied in the time of David I certainly think it did," so he regards the psalm as having an original historical setting before any modern application.

### The enemy coalition mapped onto modern groups
Tabor reads the psalm's enemies as a conspiracy: "they conspire with one accord" and "make a covenant." He takes the peoples listed (the tents of Edom, the Ishmaelites, Moab, the Hagrites, Gebal, Ammon, Amalek, Philistia with Tyre, Assyria, and the children of Lot) as a regional coalition. He matches them loosely to places on a map: Edom to Jordan, Ishmaelites possibly to Saudi Arabia, and Assyria to Iraq, with Iran possibly included. On the other side, he lists Hamas, Islamic Jihad, ISIS, and the Islamic Brotherhood as "the roots of Hamas." He also mentions that the Palestinians have drawn their name from Philistia. He presents these as the kind of reading "people would read this today," not as settled identifications.

### Where the mapping strains
Tabor is explicit that the fit is imperfect. The psalm asks that the enemies be pursued "with your tempest" and that they be made to "seek your name, O Lord," which he says "seems to imply that they will be defeated and then turn to the one true God." He observes that "it doesn't really fit exactly today with Israel's war against Gaza," since the psalm seems to rely on God acting. He also points out that the ancient gods of Moab, Ammon, and Philistia were "completely different than the one God," and that it "would be really hard to compare that to the Muslim faith in Allah."

### Connections to Isaiah 2 and Zechariah 14
Tabor argues that the psalm's conclusion, in which the enemies come to know that the Lord alone is "the most high over all the earth," can be read alongside Isaiah 2 ("in the last days, the nations will come up and worship") and Zechariah 14 ("the Lord Yahweh or Jehovah will be king over all the earth"). He notes that Zechariah 14 includes punishment for nations that will not come up to Jerusalem. He uses these links to show how a reader might bring the psalm into an end-times frame.

### Interpretive caution and method
Tabor's stated method is to "read the text" and "get a good translation," ideally with a critical edition. He recommends the Harper Study Bible for its historical-critical notes and the Oxford Bible maps. He warns that "interpreter beware" and says that the specificity of details is what drives modern prophecy belief: the reader who takes Jerusalem literally "charts this prophetic path" and expects events to occur. He contrasts this with a historical-critical reading that "would put it back in the time that it was written."

### Modern interpretations and the preterist view
Tabor previews the rest of the series. He says newer interpreters have moved away from the older Lindsey view of a revived Roman Empire allied with Arab nations, and now argue that Europe will be an ally of Israel while the threat is "a Muslim caliphate." He says he will look at the preterist view, which holds that "none of this has any remote application to anything in the future" and that the prophecies were fulfilled in the first century. He says most preterists link this to Mark 13 and the phrase "this generation will not pass away till all these things are fulfilled," and says he will examine that claim later.
