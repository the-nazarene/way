## Summary

Tabor asks whether the New Testament Book of Revelation contains an older, pre-Christian Jewish apocalypse that has been reworked by Christian editors, and whether that earlier text can be recovered. His premise is that Revelation is built on Daniel and that the explicit references to Jesus can be separated from the rest. He argues that the Jesus references "hang Loosely on the text" and cluster in chapters 1 and 22 and the letters of chapters 2-3. He also notes a few mentions in the middle chapters, such as "the testimony of Jesus" and "come Lord Jesus." He says Josephine Massyngberde Ford first suggested to him that a pre-Christian version might trace back to John the Baptist's circles, and he presents his own 2017 blog post and a free PDF reconstruction. He leans on Zechariah 14 and the Isaiah 6 "holy, holy, holy" passage to argue that many titles given to "the Lord" in Revelation are about the God of Israel, not Jesus. Tabor is explicit that he cannot prove the earlier text ever existed. He also frames the work as a corrective to current prophecy interpreters who read Revelation as a map of the present war.

The talk ends with homework. Tabor asks viewers to read the PDF and the blog post before his next video, where he plans to work through the reconstruction. He suggests the earlier text may have been known to Paul or to the Essene-related community at Qumran before 70 CE. In the closing Q&A, an unnamed interviewer asks him to explain the idea, and Tabor describes Ford's suggestion, his own extension of it, and his view of ancient interpolators.

## Key Teachings

### Revelation is built on Daniel and layered over time
Tabor says Revelation "is built on the Book of Daniel particularly Daniel 7" and the surrounding chapters, and that other images come from elsewhere. He compares it to Second Ezra (Fourth Ezra in some Bibles), which he dates to "probably ... the first century BCE," and which is also an interpretation of Daniel. His model is a series of interpretations, with the Greek Christian Revelation as "the latest." He says all New Testament books depend on earlier texts and traditions, so Revelation should not be exempt.

### The Jesus references are concentrated at the edges
Tabor says he noticed that "the explicit references to either quote Jesus or Christ or Jesus Christ outside these seven letters to the churches in Chapter 2 and three are mostly clustered in one and 22 with a few in the middle chapters." He bolded every mention of Jesus in his screen example and argues that most can be removed "without detracting from the thought or even the flow of the sentences." His example is Revelation 1:1, which he reads with the Jesus phrases removed. He points to 1:4-5 ("from the one who will be") and 1:9 ("your brother who share with you in Jesus") as passages where the text reads smoothly without the name. In his reading, the "testimony of Jesus" in 1:2 and the call to keep God's commandments and hold to "the faith of Jesus" in chapter 12 can also stand as generic Jewish language.

### "The Lord God" in Revelation is God, not Jesus
Tabor argues that the compound divine name in Revelation 1:4 ("who is and who was and who is to come") refers to YHWH. He says the second-coming theme in Zechariah 14 is about the Lord God of Israel, not a Messiah: "if you read the context it's the Lord God who comes." He criticizes Evangelical readers who identify the coming judge with Jesus. For Revelation 1:8 and the "holy, holy, holy" acclamation, he says "that's not Jesus folks that's a quote from the book of Isaiah chapter 6," which he attributes to the God of Israel. He describes Christian deification of Jesus as "a kind of a form of trinitarianism."

### Where the earlier text may fit historically
Tabor suggests that if the pre-Christian version was written in Nero's time, "it would be before 70 AD or CE when the Romans destroyed Jerusalem." He says it could have been known to Paul "long before the Book of Revelation was written," or to the Essene-related community associated with Qumran. He notes that the Dead Sea Scrolls' datable figures "pretty well stop historically speaking in the first century BCE," and he says the community "lasted on up into the first century." He says he is "absolutely convinced" that the "great City" in Revelation 11:8 is originally Rome or Babylon and was made into Jerusalem by Christian editing. The "Nero" framing comes from the description and the talk's introduction; Tabor himself says the Nero-era setting "was particularly this version of it."

### The method and its limits
Tabor describes an academic course at the University of North Carolina at Charlotte called "embedded texts," which looked for older texts inside the New Testament, as scholars have proposed for Q. He calls his reconstruction a method of reading "less interpolated" text, not a proof. He says "I can't prove that it existed," but he thinks readers "are going to see something pretty impressive" in the PDF. He also says the approach shows how much more complex the texts are than prophecy interpreters usually admit.

### Interpolation and the Q&A account
In the closing Q&A, Tabor says Ford's commentary was disliked, but he loved it and took "the seed of the idea" from her: that "John the Baptist maybe and his followers had come up with an apocalypse" with "a thin veneer of Christianity kind of layered over it." He compares Revelation to Job, which has a core with an introduction and conclusion added. He says "ancient interpolators are really dumb," and that interpolated texts "always go too far," using the Josephus Testimonium as an example. He notes that Ford's idea is not his own and that he extended it.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Revelation is essentially about Jesus.** Tabor says the Jesus references are concentrated in the frame material and can be removed from the rest of the book without breaking its flow, so the core may be a non-Christian Jewish apocalypse.
- **"The Lord God" in Revelation is Jesus.** He argues that Christians read Jesus into passages about the God of Israel, citing Revelation 1:4 and 1:8, Zechariah 14, and Isaiah 6, and calls this a kind of trinitarian deification.
- **The scenario is mapped out and the end is imminent.** He says that when you read the text itself, it is "much more complex than you're usually being told by prophecy interpreters" who claim the Armageddon battle is already mapped out.
