## Key Teachings

### Revelation draws on an earlier Jewish prophetic base
Tabor argues that the Book of Revelation, especially in its original form, rests on a Jewish foundation that "has nothing to do with the Jesus movement," though he says it is "every bit as biblical as any other apocalypse." He lists the sources he believes it builds on: Isaiah 24-27 (the "Isaiah apocalypse," with the Leviathan, the sealed people and the marriage banquet), Daniel's four kingdoms, Ezekiel 37 (the twelve tribes) and 38 (Gog in the land of Magog), Zechariah 14 or Joel 3 for the final battle over Jerusalem, and the synoptic apocalypse in Mark 13. He presents this as a "base text" that later Christian readings overwrite.

### The futurist scenario and its chronology
Tabor describes the scenario most evangelicals now apply: the seals (wars, earthquakes, famines, pestilence), an abomination in the Temple, a final evil ruler who demands worship in Jerusalem, a Great Tribulation, the return of the Son of Man, and a final battle. He says Mark 13 presents the "sign spoken of by the Prophet Daniel" and that 2 Thessalonians, which he attributes to Paul's followers, expects a "man of sin" to sit in the temple of God. He notes that this did not happen in the first century, and that people nonetheless expected it.

### Stretching the prophetic clock: 6,000 years and 70 jubilees
He explains a 6,000-year pattern that counts from Adam and Eve, and says the millennial reign is expected to follow. He then describes the 70 weeks of Daniel 9 being recalculated as 70 jubilees (490 times 49 years, read as 70 jubilees of 49 years), counted from Israel's entry into the land. He reports that some people see this period ending in 2023, just before the October 7 attack, and says the jubilee timing was linked to Yom Kippur and Sukkot. He presents these calculations as widely circulating online without endorsing them.

### Dispensationalism stops the clock
Tabor argues that Darby's dispensationalism, from the 1830s, let interpreters "stop the prophetic clock seven years before the end," the last week of Daniel's 70 weeks. Once the clock is stopped, and Jews have returned to the land, prophecy can be "read literally again." He ties this to William Miller and Charles Russell, both of whom he says preached that the time was at hand, and to the 1917 second edition of the Scofield Bible, published after General Allenby drove the Ottoman Turks from Jerusalem. He says the Scofield Bible made dispensational literalism widely accessible.

### Jerusalem, Zionism and the historical record
Tabor argues that Jerusalem in these prophecies means the actual city, and that many Jews have lived there continuously. He cites the Ottoman census of 1900, which he says showed a Jewish majority in the Old City's four quarters, and notes that Palestine was a name given by Hadrian in 135 CE. He says the 1948 establishment of Israel and the 1967 restoration of Jewish control over Jerusalem excited dispensationalists, and he describes Christian Zionism as a movement that supports Jewish return while expecting Jews to accept Christ later.

### The rapture as a later doctrine
Tabor says the rapture, the idea that the church will be taken to heaven before the final seven years, is "something new," popularized by Darby and his followers, and that "no one in the early church" held it. He presents this as the point where the dispensational scenario separates the church from the tribulation. He says he will examine this point in the Hagee video he plans next.

### Paul's different view of Israel (previewed)
Tabor briefly previews a later episode. He says he thinks Paul "stands aside from this prophetic scenario," is "more of a replacement guy," and does not hold the dispensationalist idea, even though Paul is credited with the rapture concept. He says he will test this view in a later episode.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Prophecy as a purely future, literal scenario.** Tabor argues that the texts have a historical first referent (the Dead Sea Scrolls community and the Jesus movement both read their own times in them), and that later literal readings are a separate step.
- **The rapture is ancient.** He says no one in the early church held the view, and that Darby and his followers popularized it.
- **Jews were absent from Jerusalem before 1948.** He cites the 1900 Ottoman census and claims the Jewish population was a majority in the Old City until the 1940s, when Jordan expelled Jews in 1948.
- **The 70 weeks of Daniel are 490 years and end in the first century.** He notes that some interpreters have recalculated them as 70 jubilees, extending the period into the present, and that dispensationalists stopped the clock before the final week.
- **Scofield's chronology is accurate.** He says few people today believe creation was in 404 BC, which he presents as the date in the Scofield margin.
