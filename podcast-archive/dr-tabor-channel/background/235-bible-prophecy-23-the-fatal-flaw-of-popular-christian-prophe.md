## Summary

Tabor sets out to test the popular dispensationalist scenario of the end times, using a segment of a TBN interview in which Pastor John Hagee presents his book on Bible prophecy. The question is whether the sequence Hagee lays out (a secret Rapture of the church, a seven-year Tribulation under an Antichrist, a 75-day gap, then a millennial kingdom) is what the texts actually say. Tabor argues it is not. He reads 1 Thessalonians 4, 2 Thessalonians 2, Mark 13, Daniel 9, 11 and 12, and Isaiah 65 in sequence and claims they describe one continuous event (the *parousia*, the judgment, the resurrection, and the Tribulation) with no split into multiple comings. He also argues that Hagee's "prophetic clock" depends on a gap in Daniel's seventy-weeks prophecy that the text does not support, and that Hagee's new heaven and new earth is not what Isaiah describes.

Tabor's main evidence is textual sequence plus historical dating: Paul's letters are early (he dates 1 Thessalonians to about 50 or 51 CE), the word "rapture" comes from the Latin Vulgate's *rapio* and not from the Greek, and the idea of a separate pre-Tribulation rapture appears no earlier than the nineteenth century with John Darby. He also shows that the same Daniel numbers can be used to produce other dated sequences, including one from a Jewish teacher he mentions, which he presents as evidence that the method is too flexible to be reliable.

The talk ends with Tabor saying he is not offering a rival chronology. He notes that preterist readings (that the prophecies were fulfilled in 70 CE) and spiritualizing readings also exist. He concludes that the dispensationalist scheme "does allow prophecy not to fail," but that the four texts he names (Mark 13, Daniel 12, Isaiah 24-27, and related passages) all describe God's people being tested in the Tribulation, not removed from it.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The Rapture and the second coming are one event in Paul
Tabor goes through 1 Thessalonians 4 (the dead in Christ rising first, the living "caught up together with them in the clouds") and reads it as one sequence: the Lord descends, the dead rise, the living follow, and all meet the Lord in the air. He says the Greek verb is *harpazo*-type language ("to be taken up") and that the Latin *rapio* is the source of the English "Rapture." He adds that the word is not in the New Testament and that the doctrine of a separate Rapture is no older than John Darby in the 19th century, which gives "three comings" instead of two. Tabor says Hagee reads the Greek word for "bring" (*ago*) to mean the dead are in heaven and will be brought back, which he thinks is a mistake, since the text has them rising to meet the Lord in the air.

### 2. 2 Thessalonians 2 rules out a pre-Tribulation "secret" coming
Tabor says that many critical scholars think 2 Thessalonians was edited, but he treats its eschatology as Pauline. He reads 2 Thessalonians 2:1-3 as saying that the "assembling to meet him" cannot happen until a rebellion comes first and the "man of lawlessness" is revealed. On this reading, the gathering is not an anytime event that precedes the Antichrist. He ties this to Billy Graham's older teaching that "Christ could come tonight," which he says the sequence in Paul does not allow.

### 3. Mark 13, Daniel, and Paul share one sequence
Tabor lays out the order in Mark 13 as: desolating sacrilege (the "final evil ruler"), Great Tribulation, the Son of Man coming in the clouds with the angels gathering the elect, then the fig tree. He says Daniel 11 and 12 follow the same order: the king of the north, a time of trouble worse than any before, resurrection of the dead (those asleep in the dust awaking, shining like stars), and the sealing of the book. He ties this to Paul's resurrection language in 1 Corinthians 15 (a spiritual body). He argues the resurrection comes after the Tribulation, not before it, as Hagee has it. He also mentions that Caligula's attempt to set up his statue in the Jerusalem Temple (in the 40s) shows that Paul and his readers were already expecting a desecration followed by a final event within their own lifetimes.

### 4. The seventy weeks of Daniel 9 cannot be split
Tabor says the early Jesus movement and the Dead Sea Scrolls-era writers read the seventy weeks as one unbroken period of 490 years. He breaks the text into 49 years, 62 weeks (483 years), and one final week. He says that Hagee and other dispensationalists must place a gap between the "anointed one cut off" and the last seven years, and he argues there is nothing in the text that stops the clock. He calls this the "Fatal flaw of dispensationalist theology." He also notes that the explanation of the 75-day gap and the 1290 and 1335 days in Daniel 12 has to be reconstructed mathematically, and that this is not what the text plainly says.

### 5. Hagee's "prophetic clock" and the 1948/1967 signs do not hold
Tabor quotes Hagee's claim that the clock "stops ticking" when Israel is out of the land and starts when Jews return, and he cites Judges as the basis for a fifty-year pattern. He points out an inconsistency: Hagee had the clock stop at the death of Jesus, but here it stops at 70 CE. He notes that Jews remained in the land after 70 and that the major expulsion came under Hadrian after the Second Jewish Revolt (132-135 CE). He reviews the Balfour Declaration (1917), the 1922 League of Nations mandate, and 1967, and then shows that a Jewish teacher he consulted produced a different chronology from the same Daniel numbers (beginning with Muhammad's death in 632, through 1892, to 1967). His point is that the method can produce any date one wants.

### 6. The new heaven and new earth in Isaiah 65 is earthly, not heavenly
Tabor argues that Isaiah 65:17-25 describes a renewed earth with Jerusalem, long lifespans (a child who dies at a hundred years is still a "curse" for the sinner), people building houses, planting vineyards, and a peace in which the wolf and lamb feed together. He connects this to Isaiah 2 and Isaiah 11 and says these texts "can't really be christianized." He contrasts this with Hagee's New Jerusalem, which he says is a heavenly, cube-shaped city over an unchanged earth, and with 2 Peter 3, which he reads as the first creation being dissolved. He says the Hebrew Bible does not share this view.

### 7. The four Tribulation texts put God's people in the suffering
Tabor closes by saying Mark 13, Daniel 12, and Isaiah 24-27 all describe the people of God being tried, tested, and suffering through the tribulation. He says this makes the scheme in which the church is spared the Tribulation hard to fit into the texts, however appealing it may be.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Rapture and the second coming are two separate events.** Tabor says the Rapture as a separate, pre-Tribulation event is not in the New Testament. The word comes from the Latin Vulgate, and the doctrine dates from John Darby in the 19th century. In Paul, the gathering, judgment and resurrection are one event.
- **Christ could come at any moment, secretly.** Tabor argues that 2 Thessalonians 2 says the gathering cannot happen until the rebellion and the man of lawlessness come first.
- **The church is taken to heaven and spared the Tribulation.** Tabor argues that Mark 13, Daniel 12, and Isaiah 24-27 all describe God's people suffering through the Tribulation, not being removed from it.
- **Daniel's seventy weeks have a gap of nearly two thousand years.** Tabor says the text gives one unbroken 490-year sequence and that nothing in it stops the clock.
- **The prophetic clock stops and restarts with the Jews' presence in the land.** Tabor shows Hagee's clock stops at different points (the death of Jesus, then 70 CE) and that the Daniel numbers can produce many different dates.
- **The new heaven and new earth is a heavenly, spiritual world.** Tabor reads Isaiah 65 as a renewed earth where life, farming and peace continue, and reads 2 Peter 3 and Revelation as not describing a lasting earth.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor treats Paul's 1 Thessalonians and 2 Thessalonians as the earliest New Testament statements on the end and reads the Gospel of Mark 13 as consistent with them. He does not compare Jesus' teaching with Paul's on this point in the talk.
