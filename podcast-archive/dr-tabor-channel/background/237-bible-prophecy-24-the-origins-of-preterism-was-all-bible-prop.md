## Summary

Part 24 of Tabor's Bible Prophecy series, on the origins of preterism (the view that prophecy was fulfilled in the past, in the first century). Tabor's question is what apocalyptic groups do when a predicted end does not arrive. He lists four responses that he has seen: reinterpret the failure as success, redate the prediction, recalculate and then stop calculating, or add an open-ended "gap" that makes the prophecy impossible to falsify. He then separates partial preterism (the kingdom was set up at Pentecost and Christ now rules, the view he grew up with in the Churches of Christ) from full preterism (everything, including the second coming, was fulfilled in 70 CE). He argues that Luke was written after 70 CE, because its account of Jerusalem's siege matches Josephus, and that the church moved the expected kingdom into heaven over the following centuries. His own stated reading is historical: the expectation of an imminent kingdom did not come about, and the church adjusted. He says he is suspicious of apologetic attempts to harmonize all of prophecy and that the next episode will weigh the strengths and weaknesses of each view.

## Key Teachings

### Four ways groups cope with a failed date
Tabor describes what apocalyptic groups do when a prediction fails: they reinterpret it (it did happen, just not in the way predicted), redate it (with new calculations from Daniel), recalculate and then stop ("it's up to God"), or add an indeterminate gap (2 Peter 3). He says the gap strategy makes prophecy unfalsifiable, and he notes that the view that it already happened and the view that it is still coming both answer the failure question the same way.

### Partial and full preterism
Tabor describes partial preterism as holding that the kingdom was established at Pentecost and that Christ now rules from heaven, with a future second coming. Full preterism holds that everything in prophecy, including the second coming and the resurrection, was fulfilled in 70 CE. He says full preterism is "out on YouTube so commonly today," and that the church as a whole never adopted it.

### Luke 21 as a post-70 text
Tabor argues that Luke 21 describes Jerusalem's destruction with details that match Josephus, so Luke was written after 70 CE. He says Luke reinterprets Mark 13's expectation of the end as already past, and he treats the difference as a sign that the preterist reading was already developing in the gospels.

### The expectation moved to heaven
Tabor reads the history this way: Mark expected a literal destruction of the temple and a literal return; Paul expected the dead to rise and the living to be "caught up" at the return; later texts put believers in heavenly places; and third- to fifth-century writers, including Augustine, moved the kingdom to heaven. He cites Papias as an early figure who expected a physical kingdom and whom Eusebius later criticized for that. He says the church never went full preterist, because it kept a final judgment and a resurrection of the dead.

### The apologetic warning
Tabor says he is suspicious of attempts to harmonize all prophecy. Both camps need to read part of the Bible symbolically to keep it true (preterists for the events already past, futurists for the church age), and he asks his audience to consider what would follow if the authors did not mean to postpone the end, and did not mean to speak symbolically.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Misconception:** When a predicted date passes, the group drops its belief. **Tabor's correction:** Groups more often reinterpret, redate, recalculate, or add a gap, and the "it already happened" answer is a common one.
- **Misconception (implied by full preterism):** All prophecy in Scripture was fulfilled in 70 CE. **Tabor's correction:** He calls full preterism one position among several, notes the church never adopted it, and says the expected kingdom did not come about.
- **Misconception (implied by partial preterism):** The kingdom is already here and Christ rules now. **Tabor's correction:** He argues that Isaiah 2's peace has not arrived and that the church's history of violence does not fit a kingdom already established.
- **Misconception (implied by the gap view):** A gap in the chronology keeps the prophecy accurate. **Tabor's correction:** He says an indeterminate gap makes the prophecy unfalsifiable, and he is wary of it.
