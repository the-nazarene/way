## Summary

Tabor presents this as the finale of his 25-part series on Bible prophecy and asks why prophetic texts are read so differently today. He sets out three contemporary approaches: the preterist view (prophecies already fulfilled, mainly in the first century), the continuous historical view (prophecies unfolding in successive stages through history), and the futurist view (prophecies still awaiting fulfillment). He argues that all three are "driven by apologetic motivations," meaning they work to keep the prophecies from being shown to have failed. To sort the disputes, he proposes five terms (literal, symbolic, poetic, spiritual, actual) and a distinction between predictive prophecy and contingent, ideal, or utopian visions. He also recommends a "critical historical approach" that reads each text in its own time and place.

His main evidence comes from Isaiah 2 and Revelation 19-21, which he uses to show how the five terms overlap and get contested. He then works through Mark 13 and its rewritings in Luke 17 and Luke 21. In his reading, Mark places the "desolating sacrilege," the great tribulation, and the coming of the Son of Man in one tight sequence drawn from Daniel. Luke, by contrast, separates the generation of Jesus from the final coming and reads the destruction of Jerusalem as a limited judgment. Tabor takes this as evidence that the gospel writers reinterpreted Daniel, and that the "until" in Luke 21 leaves an open gap that later readers have stretched.

The talk ends with practical advice: read the Hebrew Bible and New Testament closely in their historical settings, use the study Bibles he recommends, and download the PDFs he linked. He also shares a personal note about Albert Schweitzer as a model for his historical approach.

## Key Teachings

### Daniel as the engine of later prophecy
Tabor argues that Daniel became the most influential prophetic book because it offers a "chronology of the end times," a sequence of four world kingdoms followed by God's kingdom. He says Daniel's motif of a final evil ruler who defiles the Temple, followed quickly by the kingdom of God, sets a "clock ticking" that Mark, Paul, and Revelation all pick up. The historical referent is Antiochus IV (dated by Tabor to 167), but since the end did not come in that period, Daniel's final chapters (ending with a tribulation and resurrection) were left open to later reinterpretation.

### Floating prophecies and the problem of "until"
He lists Hebrew Bible texts he calls "floating" because they are not tied to any specific event: Isaiah 2 ("in the last days"), Joel 2-3, Isaiah 24-27, Isaiah 65, Zechariah 9-14, and Ezekiel 37-39 (the War of Gog). Because these texts lack a clear historical setting, he says they remain available for any later figure or battle to be matched to them. He contrasts this with Luke's "until" in Luke 21, which he says creates an undetermined gap in the timeline.

### The five terms: literal, symbolic, poetic, spiritual, actual
Using Isaiah 2 as the example, Tabor argues that "I take the Bible literally" breaks down, since literal swords would hardly apply to modern warfare. He says the real question is the idea behind the image: the nations turning to the one God and finding peace. The "spiritual" reading (already fulfilled in the church or in Christ's heavenly reign) is one he heard growing up, and he questions whether it works, using the image of writing "fulfilled" across the UN wall. The "actual" test asks whether war, death, and tears have really ended.

### Three interpretive camps and their apologetic motives
- **Preterist** (which he calls "full preterist" for the view that all has already happened): the Son of Man's coming was fulfilled in 70 CE, and Christians now reign with Christ. He notes Ephesians as a text some scholars think came from later followers of Paul, and says Paul's early letters have a more apocalyptic outlook.
- **Continuous historical**: prophecies (for example the beast's heads and horns in Daniel and Revelation) unfold across history, with figures such as the Black Plague or Charlemagne counted as stages.
- **Futurist**: prophecies have not yet been fulfilled in any actual sense, and will be fulfilled at the end.
Tabor's critique is that all three try to save the prophecies from failure, and he is "suspicious" of the assumption that scripture must be true, which drives them.

### Mark 13, Luke 17 and Luke 21: how the gospels reinterpret Daniel
He reads Mark 13:14 as a direct borrowing from Daniel's "desolating sacrilege," with the tribulation and the Son of Man's coming tightly sequenced ("in those days"), so that Mark leaves no gap. He reads Luke 17 (drawing on material shared by Matthew and Luke but not in Mark) as separating Jesus's generation from a later, literal and flood-like coming of the Son of Man. In Luke 21 he sees a rewriting in which the desolation of Jerusalem is the 70 CE event, the Jewish people suffer a limited wrath, and "until" opens an unknown interval before the Son of Man appears.

### Contingent, ideal, and utopian versus predictive
Tabor distinguishes predictive prophecy ("in the last days") from texts that are contingent ("this might happen if something else happens") or ideal and utopian. He uses Isaiah 2 and Isaiah 11 (the wolf and lamb) as beautiful ideals rather than predictions, citing the Soviet Union's statue of a sword-into-plowshare figure as an example of an ideal taken up by people who did not see it as fulfilled prophecy. He argues that treating Zechariah 14 as a future prediction would mean wishing for another great tribulation, which he finds unacceptable.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"I take the Bible literally, so it means what it says."** Tabor says this is a simplistic idea. Taking Isaiah 2's swords literally would make little sense for modern warfare, so the real question is what the image means.
- **"Prophecy was fulfilled spiritually in the church or in Christ's heavenly reign."** He says this is a view he heard as a child, but that the "actual" test (whether war, death, and tears have ended) shows it is not fulfilled in any real sense.
- **"Mark 13 and its 'this generation' refer only to the events of 70 CE, with the coming of the Son of Man already past."** Tabor argues that preterists stretch the text by treating the Son of Man's coming as already fulfilled while human history goes on, and that Daniel 7's kingdom has not visibly arrived.
- **"Every biblical prophecy is a future prediction waiting to happen."** He argues that many prophetic texts are contingent or ideal visions (like the wolf and lamb of Isaiah 11) and should be read in their historical setting rather than as predictions.

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Tabor says Paul's early letters express an urgent, apocalyptic expectation (not marrying, not changing lifestyle because the end is near), and that Ephesians, which speaks of believers already reigning with Christ, is probably a later text.
