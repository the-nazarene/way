## Summary

Tabor asks whether the end is really near, and frames his answer around the track record of apocalyptic predictions. He says apocalyptic expectation has a "100% failure rate" across history, and he claims to be able to list "conservatively 50 movements from the time of Jesus up into the 1900s." He argues that failed predictions produce one of two responses: either the calculators are told to "quit calculating," or they are told they "made a mistake with your calculations" and should redo the math. His own experience supports the pattern. He describes his involvement in the 1993 Waco, Texas standoff with the Branch Davidians, whose leader David Koresh had reportedly set the date 1995, and he cites the 1970s expectation of 1975 in Herbert Armstrong's group and among the Jehovah's Witnesses.

Tabor's second main point is that the biblical text stays the same while its contexts change. He notes that "apocalypse" means "to pull back the curtain or to reveal," and that the book of Revelation was written about 2,000 years ago by people who thought its events were near. He says that over the last 2,500 years "the text has stayed the same but you got all these contexts and the contexts are fluid and everchanging." He also argues that believers often protect prediction from failure by claiming "prophecy can't fail because God inspired it," so that "what is actually failing is our interpretation of it." He connects this to current anxiety, citing Pew polling in which many Americans say they think "we might be living in the end times," and to disasters and dates such as 2030 that keep the expectation alive.

The talk ends on a note of contemporary observation rather than a formal conclusion. Tabor says that talk of the world's end was once dismissed as "doomsday crackpot" material, but that apocalyptic framing now has broad currency. The six-minute excerpt does not reach a final thesis. It serves as an overview, and Tabor points viewers to his longer 25-part series on Bible prophecy for the full academic and historical analysis.

## Key Teachings

### The track record of failed predictions
Tabor's central claim is that apocalyptic expectation has never once been correct. He says "every single attempt by a group, an individual, any trend or movement in world history has ended up not in fact being the end," and he puts the count at roughly fifty movements from the time of Jesus into the 1900s. He presents this as a pattern that the believer has to explain, and he identifies two typical responses: "Quit calculating. All bets are off," or a recalculation with the claim that the earlier math was wrong. He does not name specific movements from the first fifty, so the count rests on his own assertion in this talk.

### Modern cases: Waco, Armstrong and the Jehovah's Witnesses
Tabor offers recent examples. He says he was "involved in 1993 the Waco Texas tragedy" with the Branch Davidians, an apocalyptic, biblically prophetic group that he says came out of Seventh-day Adventism. He says he worked with the FBI to try to persuade David Koresh to surrender peacefully. He reports that Koresh "had come up with the date of 1995," so the group was not expecting an attack in 1993. He describes the 1970s expectation around 1975 in Herbert Armstrong's Worldwide Church of God, whose book *1975 in Prophecy* he calls "the most gloom and doom thing you've ever seen," and he says the Jehovah's Witnesses also had a 1975 expectation. He argues that something changed in the United States in the 1900s, though the talk does not spell out the full reasoning.

### The text is fixed, the context is not
Tabor explains the word "apocalyptic" from the Greek for "to pull back the curtain or to reveal," and links it to the Apocalypse of John, the book of Revelation. He stresses that the book was written about 2,000 years ago by readers who "thought it was near." He argues that a text can remain the same for two and a half millennia while its interpretive contexts keep shifting, and that this is why the predictions keep changing dates. He also says the term can be used generically, but among Jews, Christians and, to some degree, Muslims (because of apocalyptic material in the Quran), it is usually tied to the expectation that the end "could be near."

### Protecting prophecy from failure
Tabor identifies a recurring move among believers: "prophecy can't fail because God inspired it," so the problem is always "our interpretation of it." He describes this as a kind of back-burner belief that is "a very powerful force in the United States," and he suggests that whether the end comes in "next year" or "10,000 years" may not matter to the believer, which keeps the expectation insulated from disconfirmation. He is less exact about who holds this view and does not cite a specific source for it.

### Current signs and the temptation to date-set
Tabor points to present-day attention to events such as asteroids, the "roaring of the waves of the sea," the dying of whole species, and what he calls "a lot of" such events. He notes that when there is "widespread kind of disaster that involves segments of the population, apocalypticism just rises up again," and that people "start their calculations." He mentions the 2,000-year mark since Christ's death and the speculation that 2030 might be the end, and he predicts more such speculation "in the next few years." He treats these as a sign of how readily expectation returns, and he also jokingly mentions believers saying the Antichrist "needs to get here right now so he can do his final seven years."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Belief that prophecy has failed only because people misread it, so the text itself is reliable.** Tabor argues this is the protective move: "what is actually failing is our interpretation of it" is how believers keep the prophecy intact after dates pass.
- **Belief that apocalyptic talk is fringe.** He says that talk of the world's end "used to be" dismissed as "doomsday crackpot" material, but he argues that such belief is now widespread, as shown by polling.
- **Belief that a particular date is especially likely to be the end.** He mocks the 2030 speculation and the 2,000-years-since-Christ framing, pointing to the pattern of failed dates as the reason for skepticism.
