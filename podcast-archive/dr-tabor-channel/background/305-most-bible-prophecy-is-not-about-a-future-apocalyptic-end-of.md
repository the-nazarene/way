## Summary

Tabor asks why most of the Hebrew Bible is not about the future and why modern readers, especially Evangelical readers discussing the Rapture and the Antichrist, treat "prophecy" as end-times prediction. He starts with the Jewish arrangement of the Bible into Torah, Prophets (Neviim) and Writings, noting that the first prophet in that list is Joshua, not Isaiah, and that Daniel is not among the Prophets at all. He says Daniel was placed in the Writings "because of its dating," and that the compilers "weren't sure what to do with it."

His central claim is that in the biblical world a prophet is "somebody that speaks for God," so prophecy means divine speech delivered through a human, not foretelling. He points to Moses, whom the Torah calls the greatest prophet, and to the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20; Deuteronomy 5) as examples of prophetic material that is not about the end of the age. He also notes that the New Testament's formula "this is what was spoken by" the prophet can introduce a text that is not about the Messiah, such as Matthew's use of Hosea. He proposes "communication" as a better word than "prophecy," and ends by saying that when one reads the material, "most of it is not about the future it's about the present of that person." The talk is brief (about 3.5 minutes) and gives only a few examples, so its evidence is mostly the structure of the Hebrew Bible and a handful of texts.

## Key Teachings

### The Hebrew Bible's three-part order reflects a different concept of prophecy
Tabor says that the Jewish arrangement (he mentions the JPS translation) is "a very uh important conceptual difference," not merely a change in book order. The collection runs Torah, then Prophets, then Writings. In his telling, the Prophets section begins with Joshua, and Daniel sits in the Writings, not the Prophets. He presents this as evidence that the category "prophet" was never primarily about the end times.

### Moses is the greatest prophet, and his prophetic material is mostly not eschatological
Tabor notes that Deuteronomy records Moses speaking about "what will befall Israel in the last days," but he describes that content as "basically blessings and curses and then different things about the tribes." He contrasts this with popular choices for "greatest prophet" (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Zechariah), arguing that the Torah's own choice points to a concept of prophecy tied to its historical setting.

### Prophecy is divine speech, and God speaks on many subjects
Tabor argues that because "a prophet is somebody that speaks for God," any content God delivers through a human counts as prophetic. He cites the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20; Deuteronomy 5) to show that legal and ethical material can be prophetic without being about "the End of the Age." He says modern readers who equate prophecy with eschatology are confusing two things, and he says this is "so important" for understanding the culture.

### New Testament usage follows the same broad sense
Tabor observes that New Testament writers introduce quotations with "this is that which was spoken by" a prophet. He gives Matthew's application of the Hosea line "out of Egypt I've called my son" as an example, and says this quotation "isn't even about the Messiah," though the analogy works in terms of the language. The point is that the formula marks a text as God's speech, whatever its subject.

### "Communication" is a better term than "prophecy"
Tabor proposes that "communication" would be "a better term" than "prophecy," since the Bible's prophetic books, read carefully, record God speaking "thus says the Lord" about the immediate situation. He says that once one starts reading, "most of it is not about the future it's about the present of that person."

- **Isaiah or Jeremiah is the first prophet.** In the Jewish arrangement the first prophet is Joshua.
- **Moses's references to "the last days" are mainly about the future.** Tabor says that Deuteronomy's content is mostly blessings, curses and remarks about the tribes.
