## Summary

Tabor asks how a prophecy belief survives repeated failure. He argues that the date-setting prophecies of William Miller, Ellen G. White, and Charles Taze Russell did not end their movements, because each group reinterpreted the failed event or moved its fulfillment to a new date. He then argues that the "double coming" scheme associated with John Nelson Darby and the Plymouth Brethren, popularized by Hal Lindsey and in his account by John Hagee, goes further: it places an invisible first coming before the visible signs, so no sign can ever count as a refutation.

His evidence is mostly historical narrative. He describes Miller's Bible-by-concordance method and his calculations from Daniel and Revelation, including a 2300-day calculation he says was read as pointing to June 1967. He traces White's reinterpretation of the 1844 date as Jesus leaving the heavenly sanctuary, and Russell's 1914 "invisible presence." He also contrasts the Dead Sea Scrolls' Teacher of Righteousness, whose predicted final battle did not occur on schedule, with the rabbinic response in Sanhedrin 98 and the reading of Habakkuk 2 as a call to wait in faith.

The talk ends with Tabor's summary that the dispensationalist scheme, by expecting Christ "at any time," lets its adherents avoid falsification: "you can't falsify it." Tabor presents this as his own analysis of the pattern; the talk contains no audience or guest voices.

## Key Teachings

### Calculation and the authority of the text
Tabor argues that the persuasive power of these movements came from apparently rigorous arithmetic on biblical numbers, not only from charismatic leaders. He says Miller worked through a Cruden concordance, reading every verse on major words, and derived 1843 and then 1844 from Daniel and Revelation. Tabor distinguishes this from cults built around a person: a Millerite's faith rested on "I did the numbers" and could survive the leader's fall.

### Redefining the event: White, Miller, and the 1844 "sanctuary" move
After the 1844 disappointment, Tabor says White took the position that the date had been right and the event misunderstood. In his account, Jesus had left the heavenly holy of holies, where he had been atoning for sin for about 1800 years, rather than returning visibly. Tabor notes this allowed the prophecy to stay "true" while the expected event was deferred indefinitely. He suggests the Adventist movement has grown because of this method.

### Russell and the invisible presence
Tabor cites Russell's book *Millions Now Living Will Never Die* and his later claim that the presence had come invisibly, in place of a visible return. Tabor says this kind of redefinition causes no visible cognitive dissonance for many followers. He ties Russell's move to the same pattern: the date is kept, the event is reinterpreted.

### Darby's double coming and the unfalsifiable scheme
This is the central claim. Tabor credits Darby and the Plymouth Brethren with a "double coming": Christ first comes invisibly for the saints (the Rapture), and later comes visibly with them. He says this makes the prophecy "foolproof": Christ could come at any moment, so no signs need be watched and no date can fail. Tabor notes that signs such as the mark of the beast are placed after the first coming, so their absence does not count against the system. He says Hagee did not originate the idea but is its most vocal advocate, and he describes Lindsey's system as dispensationalist, making it hard to refute because the Rapture has not yet occurred.

### The Dead Sea Scrolls: a failed Teacher-of-Righteousness prophecy
Tabor uses the Qumran material to show that this problem predates modern movements. He says the Teacher of Righteousness predicted that within forty years the men of Darkness would be cut off and the final battle with the Kittim (which he identifies with the Romans) would take place. He points out that the Romans came and were not defeated, and that Pompey in 63 BCE and later Augustus's appointment of Herod the Great show nothing of the expected kind happened. He says the group would have faced cognitive dissonance, though he does not describe the community's actual response in this talk.

### Habakkuk 2 and the rabbinic response
Tabor argues that the original meaning of Habakkuk 2 was to keep faith when the vision "tarries," not, as Paul later used the verse, to affirm justification by faith in the sense of forgiveness of sins. He says Sanhedrin 98 records the rabbis deciding that no more predictions of the Messiah's coming would be made, and that God has prolonged the time and it will come whenever he decides. Tabor presents this as the wiser alternative to date-setting, and contrasts it with dispensationalism's continued expectation without any falsifiable commitments.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That the Millerites' failure always ended the movement.** Tabor says it did not: Adventists and Jehovah's Witnesses grew because later leaders redefined the expected event and kept the date.
- **That the Millerites were all waiting on mountaintops in ascension robes.** Tabor says he checked this and it is "actually true," so he does not challenge the popular story here. He adds that the calculations, not the spectacle, convinced most people.
- **That Paul's use of Habakkuk 2 reflects the verse's original sense.** Tabor argues the original meaning concerns keeping faith when a vision delays, not forgiveness of sins.
