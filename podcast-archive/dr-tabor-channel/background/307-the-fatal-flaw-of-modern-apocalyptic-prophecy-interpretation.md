## Summary

Tabor asks why modern "Bible prophecy" charts, which plot a countdown to the end of the world, keep failing. His answer is that these readings misunderstand what the texts were doing in their own time. He calls the problem a misreading of the genre, and he sorts prophecies into two kinds. The first are "floating" prophecies, such as the Isaiah 2 and Isaiah 11 visions (inscribed on the wall in front of the United Nations), which express hopes without any date attached and can be applied to any future moment. The second are "specific" prophecies, which interpreters tie to dates, chronologies and named powers. Tabor argues that the specific ones, especially Daniel 11 and Revelation, are best explained historically. In his reading, Daniel 11 concerns the Maccabean period, with verse 40 onward describing events that "never happened" in the way later interpreters expect. He argues that Revelation, in its lament over Rome (Revelation 18), was written in the light of the destruction of Pompeii, and he presents these texts as expressions of first-century expectations that the author believed were imminent.

Tabor supports his case with a recollection of his Chicago teacher, who said that what people "most expected did not happen and what they least expected came about," and with a quotation from H. H. He also gives the modern example of David Koresh, who (per the captions) applied Old Testament texts to himself as a messianic figure. ## Key Teachings

### 1. "Floating" versus "tied" prophecy
Tabor's first rubric is what he says he "made up": a floating prophecy is one that expresses an ideal with no anchor in time. He uses Isaiah 2 (nations beat swords into plowshares, and "the Torah" goes forth from Jerusalem) and Isaiah 11 (the wolf dwelling with the lamb, the stump of Jesse) as examples. Such texts "could happen a hundred years from now a thousand," and he says they "express beautiful Concepts that many of us would want to affirm." What turns a floating prophecy into a live calculation is a claimant who says it is happening now. Tabor's example is David Koresh, whom the captions render as "David Kish" and "Vernon H," who identified himself with many such texts, including the "two Messiahs" idea. Tabor's point is that the prophecy itself gives no timetable; the interpreter supplies one.

### 2. Calculation from specifics: Daniel 2, Daniel 8, Luke 21
The second rubric concerns prophecies that supply numbers and sequences. Tabor says Daniel 2's four kingdoms "we can count," and that interpreters then add days and chronologies to create precise dates. He notes that New Testament-era readers likely identified the fourth kingdom with Rome, though he adds that earlier readers took it as Alexander's empire. He then points to Daniel 8 (the sanctuary defiled by Gentiles and later vindicated) and to the "times of the Gentiles" in Luke 21, which he says were read as implying a future date when Jerusalem would no longer be trampled. He ties this to the 1967 situation, saying that for most of the relevant history Jerusalem was under the Ottoman Empire and was not Jewish, so "Nations certainly aren't going to go up" there in the way the calculations imagined. He credits Hal Lindsey with building on these ideas after the mid-20th century, when people could "go on the ground now."

### 3. Daniel 11 as a Maccabean text
Tabor argues that Daniel 11 has the strongest scholarly foundation of the book. He says the material from verse 40 onward ("at the time of the end") follows Antiochus Epiphanes, and that the Dead Sea Scroll community read Daniel 11 in the post-Maccabean period. Interpreters who project the passage forward, identifying a final evil ruler, a "man of sin" (from 2 Thessalonians) sitting in a temple, and the "King of the North" and "King of the South" entering the "glorious land," are, in Tabor's view, not reading the historical context. He claims that "none of it happened verse 40 on" in the sense the projecting interpreters expect, and that the troop movement predictions have repeatedly failed.

### 4. Revelation 17-18 and the lament over Rome
Tabor says that he has argued that Revelation 18, the lament over Rome, was written in the light of the destruction of Pompeii. He cites the imagery of ships at sea weeping over a city that "in one hour has been engulfed in flames." He adds that people in Rome could have seen the eruption from a distance, and he notes that Pliny the Elder was on the shore. He argues that the author would have known that Rome itself was not destroyed, so the text is best read as a warning that the author expected to be a precursor to a greater catastrophe. Tabor presents this as his own argument, not a settled consensus.

### 5. Rowley's warning and the cycle of failed expectations
Tabor reads from H. H. Rowley's *The Relevance of Apocalyptic* (1944). He quotes Rowley's point that "for more than 2,000 years a Hope Has proved illusory" and that interpreters who think the consummation is imminent "prove mistaken." Tabor draws the parallel to modern readers who map Daniel 11 or Revelation 17 onto contemporary powers, and says it is a pattern that should give pause. He mentions that Rowley wrote a year before Hitler died, and that the pattern of "final evil rulers" recurs across eras.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That Isaiah 2 and Isaiah 11 give a timetable.** Tabor says these are floating prophecies: they express hopes with no date, and they become calculations only when a claimant says they are being fulfilled now.
- **That Daniel's four kingdoms and chronologies yield a future date.** Tabor says that counting, with added days and chronology, is what generates the specific dates interpreters propose.
- **That Daniel 11:40 onward and Revelation describe events still to come, which will match modern powers.** Tabor argues that these texts are best read in the Maccabean and first-century contexts and that their projected fulfillments have repeatedly failed.
- **That Revelation's lament over Rome predicts a future destruction.** Tabor argues it was written in the light of the Pompeii eruption, which the author expected to be a precursor.
- **That the 1967 control of Jerusalem and the rebuilding of a temple are necessary steps in prophecy.** Tabor argues that the calculations behind these expectations rest on misreadings, and that the modern calls to "help the Fulfillment" are dangerous.
