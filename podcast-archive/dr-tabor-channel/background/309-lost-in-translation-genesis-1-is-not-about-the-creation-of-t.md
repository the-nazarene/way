## Summary

Tabor asks whether the first verse of the Bible, usually rendered "In the beginning God created the heavens and the Earth," is a mistranslation that has shaped how readers understand Genesis and even the Bible as a whole. He argues that Genesis 1 is not a statement about creating the universe out of nothing. In his reading, it describes God beginning to order and fashion this particular planet, which is already unformed, dark, and water-covered. His main evidence is grammatical and comparative. He points to the Hebrew *bereshit* as a construct phrase that works as a temporal clause, "when God began," with verses 1-3 forming one sentence. He notes that Rashi (whom Tabor dates to the 11th century CE) proposed this reading, that the 1980s Jewish Publication Society *Tanakh* adopted a temporal rendering, and that Jeremiah 26:1 uses the same construction. ### Key Teachings

### 1. The traditional rendering is a long-standing translation choice, and it persists for commercial reasons
Tabor says the wording of Genesis 1:1 is nearly identical across the King James Version, the American Standard Version (1901), the RSV (which he dates to "the 1950s"), the ESV, and the NIV. He suggests that this uniformity reflects habit and marketing more than linguistic necessity. He argues that a translation that departs from a verse "that people practically know by heart" would have difficulty finding buyers.

### 2. *Bereshit* as a temporal phrase, and verses 1-3 as one sentence
This is the core argument. Tabor says *bereshit* is in the construct case, from *rosh* ("head" or "first"), so the phrase means "at the beginning of" or "when God began." On this reading, verses 1-3 form a single sentence describing the state of affairs when God began to create or fashion the planet: an unformed, empty, dark, water-covered earth with a wind sweeping over the waters. The focus is on the start of the ordering process, "not the beginning of the planet" and "not the rest of the universe," in his words.

### 3. A precedent in Rashi and the Jewish Publication Society
Tabor presents the temporal reading as an existing interpretive option in Jewish tradition. He says Rashi suggested it in the 11th century CE, and that the *Tanakh* published by the Jewish Publication Society in the 1980s "had the courage" to render it that way. He uses this to argue the reading is not his invention.

### 4. The Earth in view, and the ordering of a chaotic planet
Tabor describes the passage as "geocentric and anthropomorphic," focused on Earth, and compares the initial state to the Moon and planets as seen through telescopes. He reads the sequence that follows (separation of waters, emergence of dry land, sky, life in the sea, air, and land, then humans) as the "creation hymn" of Genesis 1:1-2:3. The passage concerns the shaping of an existing condition, not the origin of matter.

### 5. Parallel grammar in Jeremiah 26:1, and *ruach* as wind
Tabor says Jeremiah 26:1 contains "exactly the same phrase in Hebrew" ("at the beginning of the reign of") and that this shows the construction marks the start of an unfolding event. He also points to his footnotes, which give *ruach* as "wind" and note plural forms, and he argues that "the spirit of God" in verse 2 should be read as wind over the waters. ## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Genesis 1:1 means God created the universe from nothing.** Tabor says this is a philosophical or scientific reading that the Hebrew does not support. He argues the verse describes God ordering an already existing, unformed planet.
- **The traditional English wording is what the Hebrew says.** Tabor says the consistent English rendering reflects tradition and marketing, and that Hebrew grammar, the construct form of *bereshit*, supports a temporal reading.
- **The temporal reading is a novel modern idea.** Tabor says it goes back to Rashi and was adopted by the Jewish Publication Society in the 1980s.
