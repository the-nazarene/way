## Summary

Tabor asks what Jesus meant when, according to Mark 11:17, he quoted Jeremiah and called the Jerusalem Temple a "den of robbers." He argues that the English "den of thieves" mistranslates both Jesus's Greek (*spelaion lestōn*) and Jeremiah's Hebrew. In his reading, the Greek *lestēs* and the Hebrew verb *parat* describe violent, ripping predators, a "lair of ravenous beasts," not petty thieves. He takes this as evidence that Jesus opposed the sacrificial system and did not think God had ever commanded it.

His main evidence is textual. He compares Mark 14:48 and Mark 15:7 to show that *lestēs* means armed insurrectionists, and he links Mark 15:27 (the two men crucified with Jesus) to the same meaning, since Rome crucified rebels rather than thieves. He then reads Jeremiah 7:21-23 as saying God did not command burnt offerings and sacrifices when Israel came out of Egypt. He presents this as a challenge to the Pentateuch, which he says contains sacrificial material added by "Priestly writers." Isaiah 35:9, Isaiah 11 and Isaiah 56 are used to sketch an ideal where no animals are slaughtered and the Temple is "a house of prayer for all nations." He adds that Isaiah 56 shows a scribal insertion, since the sacrifice language is absent from the surrounding context.

The talk ends with Tabor's invitation to a follow-up series on "obvious interpolations" in the biblical text. He presents the whole argument as his own reading ("I believe," "I'm convinced"), and he flags several points, such as the reconstruction of Jeremiah's Hebrew and the interpolation claims, as matters for readers to check in the texts he supplies.

## Key Teachings

### 1. "Den of robbers" is a mistranslation; the words mean violent predators
Tabor says the Greek *spelaion lestōn* means "a cave of violent ones," with *spelaion* meaning cave, den, or lair. He argues that *lestēs* is "really badly translated as a robber," since English readers picture thieves dividing loot in a cave. In his view the word means an armed brigand or dagger-man, and he cites Josephus's use of the term for the Sicarii. He cites Mark 14:48 (Jesus asks whether he has come out "against a lestēs") and Mark 15:7 (Barabbas among rebels who had committed murder) as uses of the same word.

### 2. Jeremiah's Hebrew describes ravenous beasts, not thieves
For the Hebrew behind Jeremiah 7:11, Tabor says the first word for cave is "very simple" and the second is *parit*, from *paratz*, "to break apart, to shatter, to shred." He proposes rendering the line as "a lair of ravenous beasts." He supports this with Isaiah 35:9, where "no lion will be there nor any ravenous beast" uses the same root. The Temple is thus compared to a predators' den in which priests tear apart animals.

### 3. Jesus opposed the sacrificial system
Tabor argues that Jesus's reference to the Temple as a lair of beasts is a critique of the slaughter of animals in the Temple, which he likens to a modern slaughterhouse. He reads Luke 18 (the parable of the two men at prayer, where the one who repented offered no sacrifice) as evidence that Jesus did not regard sacrifice as necessary. He says this will "shock people," and he presents it as his conclusion about Jesus's intent.

### 4. God did not command sacrifice at the Exodus; Priestly writers added it
Relying on Jeremiah 7:21-23, Tabor reads God's words as saying that when he brought Israel out of Egypt he "did not speak to your fathers or command them concerning burnt offerings and sacrifices," and that the command was instead to obey his voice. He concedes that Exodus contains many sacrificial laws, and he explains them as additions by "Priestly writers that are pro-sacrifice" who "have added these accounts to the Torah." He also cites Amos (justice rolling down "like waters") and says Jeremiah 7 says the "false pen of the scribes" has made the Torah a lie. He presents this as a textual claim he is "convinced" of, and he invites readers to read the chapters in context.

### 5. Isaiah 11 and Isaiah 56 describe a sacrifice-free ideal, with a possible scribal insertion
Isaiah 11 ends with the wolf, lamb, leopard and lion, and the "holy mountain" where they "will not hurt or destroy" (Tabor glosses "destroy" as *shet*, "slaughter"). He reads this as the messianic ideal, which he links to Jesus as the Messiah in the eyes of his followers. In Isaiah 56 he points to the phrase "my house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples" and marks some words as crossed out in his slide. He argues that a "Priestly scribal interpolator" added the language about sacrifice because worry about the Temple economy was growing. He notes that the rest of the chapter contains no sacrifices.

### 6. Solomon's dedication prayer mentions prayer and foreigners, not sacrifice
Tabor directs viewers to the dedication of Solomon's Temple, which he locates at "First Kings chapter 6." He says the prayer itself "never even mentions sacrifice" and speaks of prayer, drawing near to God, and foreigners praying toward the Temple. He says the surrounding narrative of thousands of offerings is the work of the priestly writers. (The chapter reference is as the speaker gives it; verify it before citing.)

### 7. Crucifixion as a penalty for insurrection, not theft
Tabor argues that the "two thieves" of Mark 15:27 were not common thieves: "the Romans don't crucify people for stealing." He says crucifixion was reserved for brigands, rebels and insurrectionists, and that Jesus was crucified as an insurrectionist, even though the witnesses against him were false and claimed he would destroy the Temple. He ties this to the Temple confrontation as a continuation of Jeremiah's prophetic action.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Den of thieves" means a den of robbers.** Tabor argues the Greek *spelaion lestōn* and Hebrew *parit* describe violent predators and armed insurrectionists, not petty thieves.
- **The two men crucified with Jesus were thieves.** Tabor says Rome crucified rebels and insurrectionists, and that *lestai* in Mark 15:27 means violent men, not thieves.
- **God commanded sacrifice from the Exodus onward.** Tabor reads Jeremiah 7:21-23 as saying God did not command burnt offerings at the Exodus, and attributes the sacrificial laws in the Torah to later Priestly writers.
- **Jesus endorsed the Temple sacrificial system.** Tabor argues Jesus opposed the slaughter of animals there and wanted the Temple to be "a house of prayer for all nations."

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Torah:** Tabor argues that sacrificial material in the Pentateuch (e.g., Exodus) was added by Priestly writers, and that Jeremiah 7 calls such scribal work a "lie." This is the talk's main claim about how the Torah was composed.
- **Sacrifice:** The talk's central question is whether Jesus and the prophets opposed or accepted animal sacrifice, with Tabor answering that the original ideal excluded it.
- **Jesus's teaching:** Tabor reads the Temple action in Mark 11 as a prophetic critique in Jeremiah's tradition, and reads Luke 18 as consistent with it.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** Isaiah 11 and Isaiah 35 are read as the ideal future with no predatory violence and a Temple of prayer for all nations.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Tabor notes that Jesus's followers believed he was the Messiah, and reads Isaiah 11 in that light, though he does not develop the point.
- **Gospels and the trial:** The crucifixion section links the Markan account to the charge of insurrection, and Tabor says the witnesses against Jesus were false.
