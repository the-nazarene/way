## Summary

Tabor asks whether Genesis 2:7 gives Adam an immortal soul that sets humans apart from animals. The King James Version says God "breathed into his nostrils the breath of life" and "man became a living soul," and Tabor argues that this wording, carried into English translations, has misled readers for centuries. He reads the key Hebrew terms as *nishmat chayim* (breath of life) and *nephesh chayah* (living creature), and says *nephesh* is etymologically "a breathing living breather," essentially "a creature."

His main evidence is internal to Genesis. He notes that the same phrases describe animals: Genesis 1:20 and 1:24 call sea creatures and land creatures "living creatures," and Genesis 1:30 and Genesis 7 apply "breath of life" to beasts and birds. He concludes that the difference between a living body and a dead one is breathing, not a separate ontological "soul." He then cites Ecclesiastes 3:19-21, which he says echoes Genesis 2:7 and treats humans and beasts as sharing one fate. He reads the verse about the spirit of man rising as a probable later interpolation, possibly reflecting Hellenistic influence. He also reads Paul's quotation of Genesis 2:7 in 1 Corinthians 15 as the source of the "immortal soul" idea.

The talk ends as a promotion for Tabor's own translation of Genesis, which he says includes about a thousand notes in the back. He closes with his method ("from the words on the basis of the words and in connection to the words"), saying he will continue the series with more examples.

## Key Teachings

### 1. "Breath of life" and "living soul" are not a special human endowment
Tabor argues that the phrases in Genesis 2:7 describe ordinary animal life. He glosses *nishmat chayim* as the "breath of life" and *nephesh* as a creature that breathes. Because the same language describes animals, he says the phrase marks creaturely life, not an extra immortal element in humans.

### 2. The same vocabulary is used for animals in Genesis 1 and 7
He points to Genesis 1:20 and 1:24, where the Hebrew word translated "living soul" or "living creature" (*nephesh chayah*) applies to fish, birds and land animals. Genesis 1:30 applies "breath of life" to every beast and bird. In Genesis 7 the animals entering the ark are "all flesh," and in verse 22 "everything in whose nostrils was the breath of life died." He notes that Hebrew also has a term for a "dead breather" (a corpse), so the contrast is between breathing and not breathing, not between body and soul.

### 3. Translation choices create the "soul vs. body" split
Tabor says translators have rendered *nephesh* as "soul" in a way that sets it apart from the body, which he calls a misleading translation. He says that when the King James and American Standard renderings call animals "beasts" but call Adam a "living soul," the English makes a difference the Hebrew does not. He suggests that "living being" would also be misleading unless it were applied consistently to fish and other creatures.

### 4. Ecclesiastes 3:19-21 treats humans and beasts alike, and verse 21 is probably later
He reads Ecclesiastes 3:19 as stating that humans and beasts share one fate: "as one dies so dies the other... man has no advantage over the beasts." He says this echoes Genesis 2:7. He then treats verse 21, which asks "who knows whether the spirit of man goes upward and the spirit of the Beast goes down," as a likely interpolation, and links the developing idea of a distinct human soul to the Hellenistic period.

### 5. Genesis 3:19 and Paul's use of Genesis 2:7
Tabor cites Genesis 3:19 ("you are dust and to dust you shall return") as the companion text. He says Paul quotes Genesis 2:7 in 1 Corinthians 15 ("the first man Adam became a living breather but the last Adam is a lifegiving spirit"), and he says that the reading Paul gives this text is "what people call today an immortal Soul." The point is that the immortal-soul reading comes from later interpretation, which he says Paul shares, and not from the Hebrew wording in Genesis itself.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Belief:** Genesis 2:7 says God gave Adam an immortal soul, something special that animals lack. **Tabor's correction:** The phrases "breath of life" and "living soul" describe the same creaturely life shared with animals, so the verse does not establish a distinct immortal soul.
- **Belief:** The King James wording ("living soul") is the accurate rendering and should be kept. **Tabor's correction:** The KJV and ASV translation choices create a body/soul split that the Hebrew does not contain; the English ("beasts" vs. "living soul") introduces the distinction.
- **Belief:** Body and soul are two different kinds of being. **Tabor's correction:** The difference between a living and a dead creature is breathing; he says there is "no difference in terms of the body or even of the being."
