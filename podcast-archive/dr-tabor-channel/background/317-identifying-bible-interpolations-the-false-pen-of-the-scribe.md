## Summary

Tabor opens a new video series on identifying interpolations, meaning passages or phrases that later editors may have added to a base text to counter or supplement it, in both the Hebrew Bible and the New Testament. His stated question is how a reader can tell which words are original and which were inserted, and he proposes asking what interest a later scribe would have had in altering the passage. He calls the episode "The False Pen of the Scribes" after Jeremiah 8:8, which he translates as "the lying pen of the scribes has made the Torah into a lie." He says the series runs alongside his "Lost in Translation" series, since both aim at a more original text.

His method is demonstrated first on the Testimonium Flavianum in Josephus's *Antiquities of the Jews*, which Tabor dates "we think" to about 90 CE. He reads the passage with the phrases he considers Christian additions marked: "if it be lawful to call him a man," "he was the Messiah he was the Christ," "he appeared to them alive again the Third Day," and others. He says that with these removed, a core text remains that could plausibly be Josephus's, and he cites John P. Meier (the captions render the name "John Meyer") on this debate. He then applies the same logic to sacrifice texts, arguing that passages expressing rejection of sacrifice, such as Psalm 51, Isaiah 56, Isaiah 66, and Hosea 6:6, were overwritten with pro-sacrifice clauses that would have been objected to in later periods. He closes by saying the series will continue with more passages.

The talk is almost entirely Tabor speaking on screen with notes, with no guest. The method is presented as heuristic: Tabor says his "buzzer" test and the question of who would object to a passage are hunches to test, not proof, and his interpolation proposals are his own judgments.

## Key Teachings

### 1. A method for spotting interpolations
Tabor describes a working method. First, identify phrases that a given author would not plausibly have written, especially where a passage is "disjunctive" and interrupts the flow. Second, ask what the uninterpolated text was meant to do and who would have objected to it. He says that a stripped-down version of the text often reads more smoothly. He describes teaching this in his early Christianity classes, where students struck out what they thought was non-original, and says the goal is to develop a critical sensibility. He notes that the work is not always able to identify who did the interpolating.

### 2. The Josephus Testimonium
Tabor reads Josephus's passage with uppercase-marked interpolations. He treats "if it be lawful to call him a man," the phrase about teaching those who "received the truth with pleasure," "he was the Messiah he was the Christ," and the resurrection-on-the-third-day clause as likely Christian additions. He says the phrase "the tribe of Christians so named from him are not extinct" makes sense without the additions. He says he does not think Josephus was a Christian. He mentions the view that the passage is wholly forged, which he rejects, and says the reconstructed version is "very possibly from Josephus." He points to Meier, *A Marginal Jew*, volume one, pages 57 to 88, for the scholarly debate.

### 3. Jeremiah 7 and 8 and the "lying pen of the scribes"
Tabor connects the title phrase to Jeremiah's temple sermon in chapter 7, where God says in the first person that on the day he led Israel out of Egypt he gave no command about burnt offerings and sacrifices. He reads Jeremiah 8:8 as a denunciation of scribes who had turned the Torah into a lie. He says this is the context for his claim that Priestly and sacrificial material may have been added to texts that originally rejected sacrifice. He flags that he has treated Jeremiah 7 in the other series and will return to the Priestly sacrifice question in more detail later.

### 4. Psalm 51: the psalm of repentance without sacrifice
Tabor argues that Psalm 51, David's repentance after the Bathsheba affair, originally offered forgiveness through a broken and contrite heart, not through sacrifice. He quotes verses on washing, cleansing, truth in the inward being, and "you have no Delight in sacrifice." He treats the final lines as an addition: verse 18's "do good to Zion... rebuild the walls of Jerusalem," which he says presupposes post-destruction and post-exile conditions, and verse 19's "then you will Delight in right sacrifices and burn offerings," which he calls "heavy-handed" and "tacked on." He ties this to Luke 18:9-14, where Jesus, as Tabor reads him, commends the penitent man praying at the temple without sacrifice.

### 5. Isaiah 56 and Isaiah 66: the house of prayer for all peoples
Tabor argues that Isaiah 56:6-7 originally described the temple as a "house of prayer for all peoples," with foreigners who keep the Sabbath and hold to the covenant coming to God's holy mountain. He says the clause "their burnt offerings and sacrifices will be accepted on my altar" is a disjunctive insertion. He compares this with Solomon's prayer in 1 Kings 8, which he says "expresses a nonsacrificial ideology," while noting that the surrounding chapter lists sacrifices he doubts are historical. He reads Isaiah 66:1-3 ("this is the man to whom I will look, he that is humble and contrite in spirit") as rejecting the notion that God needs a house made with hands, and he says the slaughtering-an-ox verse equates sacrifice with murder.

### 6. The Hosea 6:6 "favorite verse" of Jesus
Tabor says Jesus repeatedly quoted Hosea 6:6, "I desire mercy and not sacrifice," and that the passage is consistent with the anti-sacrificial strand he has identified. He says that anyone defending the sacrificial system will say that God "really wanted the sacrifices," and he argues that the Hebrew Bible's prophetic texts pull the other way.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **That the Josephus Testimonium is wholly authentic.** Tabor says it has been "heavily overwritten" by Christian interpolators and that a stripped-down version is probably Josephus's.
- **That the Josephus Testimonium is wholly a forgery.** Tabor rejects the view that Josephus had no part in writing it, arguing for a core text that Christian editors expanded.
- **That Josephus was a Christian.** Tabor says he does not think Josephus was a Christian and that the "he was the Messiah" language is an addition.
- **That the Hebrew Bible unambiguously commands sacrifice throughout.** Tabor argues that prophetic and Psalm texts reject sacrifice and that the pro-sacrifice clauses are later additions.
- **That the sacrificial system was God's clear desire.** Tabor says texts like Jeremiah 7, Hosea 6:6, and Psalm 51 show God does not delight in sacrifice, which he argues was Jesus's position.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. later additions:** Tabor's central claim is that Jesus's "original teaching" in Luke 18 and his quotation of Hosea 6:6 reflect a non-sacrificial position that later scribes overwrote. This is the talk's most direct bearing on the repo's core question, though Tabor does not discuss Paul.
- **Torah, sacrifice, and the temple:** The talk's main substance. Tabor argues that the Priestly and sacrificial material of the Hebrew Bible includes later insertions that conflict with prophetic and Psalm texts, and that the temple was imagined as a "house of prayer for all peoples."
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** The Josephus "he was the Messiah he was the Christ" clause is treated as a later addition, so the passage would not support a Messiah claim from Josephus. Tabor does not discuss Jesus's self-understanding otherwise.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** The talk's method, identifying scribal interpolation in texts, bears on textual transmission generally, though Tabor's examples are from Josephus and the Hebrew Bible rather than the gospels.
- **Resurrection:** The Josephus resurrection clause ("he appeared to them alive again the Third Day") is marked as an interpolation.
