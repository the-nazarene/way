## Summary

Tabor asks why readers who treat the King James Version (1611) as their only Bible can misread it, given that the translation was careful in its own time. His answer is that English has changed since 1611, so words that were accurate then now mislead. He says the KJV, the Geneva Bible (1560), and the American Standard Version were fairly literal and built on the manuscripts available to their translators, but that once the KJV was treated as the inspired word of God, its archaic vocabulary began to be read in modern senses. He gives examples: "help meet" in Genesis 2:18, the Hebrew *sheol* rendered "hell," and "prevent" in the sense of "precede" in 1 Thessalonians 4. For the "hell" examples he uses the Accordance Bible software and an English dictionary to show that *sheol* means the grave or the place of the dead, "literally to be snuffed out," not a place of burning. He says he is not attacking the King James but "redeeming" it, and recommends the New King James Version and the New American Standard Bible as updated options. The talk is a short solo presentation with no guest. It ends with a preview of the next episode in the series, on identifying interpolations in the Bible.

### Key Teachings

### Translations were accurate for their time but the language has drifted
Tabor's core claim is that the KJV, Geneva Bible and American Standard were "quite accurate in its own time and fairly literal and carefully done based on the manuscripts that we had at that time." The problem is that "the English languages changed," so some renderings "can actually mean the opposite" of the original today. He notes that churches built on the KJV treat its wording as the exact word of God, which magnifies the drift.

### "Help meet" and the sense of Genesis 2:18
In Genesis 2:18, the KJV says "I will make him an help meet for him." Tabor explains that "help meet" in 1611 meant something "fitting him like hand in glove," and that the Hebrew literally means something like "one before him, side by side." He ties this to Genesis 1, where God creates "man" as humankind, "male and female," so the phrase points to a partner corresponding to the human, not a subordinate woman. He cites the Revised Standard Version's "a helper fit for him" as a clearer rendering. He calls this a minor point but "worth noting."

### "Hell" translates *sheol*, the grave, not a fiery place
Tabor says that searching the KJV finds "54 uses of the word hell," and that the first, in Deuteronomy, speaks of a fire kindled that "will burn into the lowest hell." The Hebrew word is *sheol*, meaning the grave or death. He reads several passages with Hebrew parallelism, where "the Sorrows of Hell" and "the snares of death" are paired, and argues that a modern rendering would say "the Sorrows of the Grave." He points to "deeper than hell" as "literally deeper than shio the grave," which he describes as a pit into which people fall and from which they do not return. He says that "the wicked shall depart to sheol" concerns the dead generally, and that God could "redeem" his people from *sheol*.

He draws a clear contrast with the New Testament, where he says the Greek word for the Hinnom valley south of Jerusalem (which he calls *gehenna* in the transcript) does refer to an ever-burning fire, a trash dump used as an analogy for the wicked being burned. In the Hebrew Bible, though, he says, "there's none of these right here that have to do with a burning fire." The KJV translates both terms as "hell."

### Why "hell" was used: Old English usage
Tabor argues that in 1611 "hell" meant a hole in the ground, a cellar or pit, and that readers therefore took *sheol* to mean the grave. He supports this with the observation that English speakers in the 1600s stored potatoes "in Hell," a cellar, and that German has a similar usage. He concludes that "grave is actually a fairly good translation" and that transliterating *sheol* as "shio," as some modern versions do, leaves readers asking what it means.

### "Prevent" meant "go before" or "precede"
Tabor says the KJV uses "prevent" in its older sense of "go before" or "meet." He cites a passage, which he attributes to the Psalms, where the KJV reads "oh god of my mercy shall prevent me," and he glosses it as "my God and His steadfast love will meet me, not prevent." His main example is 1 Thessalonians 4, which says the living "shall not prevent them which are asleep." He explains that "prevent" here means "precede": the living will not go before the dead, and the dead "will rise first." He treats this as a theological point, since it bears on how the resurrection of the dead and the living at Christ's coming are ordered.

### Updated options and the scope of the argument
Tabor says he is "not trashing the King James" but "redeeming it." He recommends the New American Standard Bible, which he describes as a literal, modern descendant of the Geneva Bible, and the New King James Version, which keeps the KJV's style but corrects the archaic terms. He answers the common claim that the KJV is "more reliable" by noting that he owns about twenty translations and uses Accordance software, and that for him as an academic, the modern versions and original-language study are the better tools.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Belief:** The KJV is the exact, word-for-word word of God, so its wording should be taken literally in every case. **Correction:** Tabor says the KJV was accurate for its time, but because English has changed, its words can now mean something different from the Hebrew or Greek.
- **Belief:** The "hell" in the Old Testament is a fiery place of torment. **Correction:** The KJV's "hell" usually translates the Hebrew *sheol*, meaning the grave or death, and the fiery sense belongs to the New Testament's Greek and to the Hinnom valley analogy.
- **Belief:** "If the King James was good enough for Paul, it's good enough for me." **Correction:** Tabor treats this as a joke that misses the point, since the KJV's English has changed and some of its wording no longer conveys the original meaning.
- **Belief:** The KJV is "more reliable" than modern translations. **Correction:** Tabor argues that modern translations, read alongside the original languages, give more reliable access to the text.
