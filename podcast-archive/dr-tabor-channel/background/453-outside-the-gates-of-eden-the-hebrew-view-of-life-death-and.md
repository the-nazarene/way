## Summary

Tabor asks what the Hebrew Bible says about human beings: what they are, whether they have a purpose or destiny beyond this world, what happens at death, and what the human future holds. He starts with Genesis 1:1 and argues that the verse describes God beginning to shape a chaotic earth, not creating from nothing and not describing a recreation. He reads Isaiah 45:18 as an explanation of Genesis 1:1. He then follows the account of the human creature (Adam, "earthling") through Genesis 1:26, Genesis 2:7 and Genesis 3:19, and through Psalm 8, Job 25:6 and Psalm 115. His main claim is that the Hebrew texts present a mortal, "disposable" human being rather than an immortal soul. He says that other traditions (Plato, Egyptian, Babylonian, later Greek, Christian, Jewish, Islamic and Hindu traditions in his list) built religions of escape on a soul that leaves the body.

For the afterlife, Tabor argues that the dead "sleep in the dust" in the Hebrew Bible (Psalm 115, Psalm 88, Deuteronomy 29:29), and that Sheol is a shadowy place of silence rather than conscious torment. He reads Isaiah 26:13-19 as a promise that the dead will live again, and Isaiah 25 as a vision of a feast and a removal of death on a mountain that stands for a new creation. The talk closes with his reading of "outside the gates of Eden" as moral maturity: humans shape their character into the moral image of God, and he says this, not an immortal soul, is the purpose of life.

## Key Teachings

### Genesis 1:1 as an ordering of an existing chaos, not creation from nothing

Tabor argues that the Hebrew construct phrase in Genesis 1:1 (rendered "in the beginning") should be read as "when God began," and that verse 2 describes the state of the earth at that start: "without form and void." He supports the grammar by pointing to Jeremiah 26:1 and Jeremiah 27, which use the same construction. He says the earth may have looked something like the moon, with craters from asteroid impacts. He rejects the reading that Genesis 1 describes a recreation after an earlier catastrophe, which he says has been argued "for years" from the Scofield Bible and others. He also rejects the idea that *bara* means creation "out of nothing," calling that a logical problem and tying it to a long Greek philosophical question about why there is something rather than nothing.

### Elohim as "the force of all forces" and the prohibition of worshipping the derivative

Tabor says Elohim takes singular verbs even though the form is plural, and he proposes thinking of this as "the force of all forces." He says the divine name (YHWH) is not used until Genesis 2, so Genesis 1 does not name the force. He links Genesis 1:1 to a warning against idolatry: whatever is "made or created or shaped or formed or engineered" is derivative, and worshipping it means mistaking the creature for the creator. He says this distinction separates the Abrahamic traditions from religions that treat natural forces as deities. He also says the physical forces (electromagnetism, gravity, the strong and weak nuclear forces) are themselves not the force that shapes them.

### Isaiah 45:18 as an explanation of Genesis 1:1, and the mission of "light to the nations"

Tabor reads Isaiah 45:18 as an "exegesis" of Genesis 1:1, using the same three verbs (*asah*, *bara*, *yatsar*). He says the verse begins with Cyrus, whom he calls God's "Messiah," and that the Persian-period return is part of the historical plan. He connects the chapter to the close of the Hebrew Bible in 2 Chronicles ("Let him go up") and to the purpose of Abrahamic faith as he understands it: to teach justice and righteousness to the nations, or, in Isaiah's language, to bring light. He says the historical purpose is not "an empty and waste plan," and that God did not tell Jacob's seed to "seek me in chaos." He notes that Isaiah's text is preserved in the Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran.

### The human as a "disposable" earthling, and the breath of life

Tabor reads Genesis 1:26-27 with Adam understood as a species name with male and female aspects. He says Genesis 1:24 uses "living creature" (*nephesh chayah*) for animals as well, and he does not think humans are distinct in having a soul. He points to Genesis 2:7, in which God forms the human from dust and breathes the breath of life, and he argues that the same breath appears in the flood account (Genesis 6), where "every creature" with "the breath of life" perishes. From this he concludes that the breath is an animating force, not an immortal soul, and that the human is a "temporary disposable version" of the divine image. He presents this as the point where he thinks the world's religions went wrong, following Plato.

### Psalm 8, Job 25, and the weakness of the human

Tabor glosses *enosh* as "weakling," and he argues that Psalm 8 and Job 25:6 use terms like "maggot" and "worm" that are not insults but descriptions of a disposable creature. He says Jeremiah 17:9 should be read as "desperately weak" rather than "desperately wicked." He reads the phrase in Psalm 8:5 as "a little lower than God," and he suggests the Septuagint's rendering may mean both "a little lower" and "a little while." He ties human freedom and intellect to a serious risk, and he says the capacity for self-reflection and moral awareness is what makes the human different from other mammals.

### Death as sleep in the dust, and Sheol as silence

Tabor reads Psalm 115:16-18 as a summary of the cosmos: the heavens belong to Jehovah, the earth is given to the sons of Adam, and the dead "go down into silence." He compares Sheol with Babylonian and Greek ideas of the underworld, which he describes as grim (a "house of dust," a "land of no return," Hades), and says the Hebrew Bible is not grim about the dead. He suggests Sheol may come from *shaah* ("dissolve"), and he uses Psalm 88 as the bleakest example. He describes death as sleep and compares the awakening to waking refreshed in the morning. He says sleep "does not imply non-existence" and calls it an "altered reality." He ties this to Deuteronomy 29:29 ("the secret things belong to God") and says the Hebrew Bible does not offer a full system of the afterlife.

### Resurrection and the new mountain in Isaiah 25-26

Tabor reads Isaiah 26:13-19 as a prayer by a community that has lived under other lords and believes its dead will rise. He reads the passage's phrase "thy dead shall live" as a shift from singular to plural, and he takes it as bodily ("my corpse, they will arise"). He says light falls on the "land of the shades" in the imagery of dew. He mentions Daniel 12 as a passage with problems for scholars, and he refuses to settle how many resurrections there are. He reads Isaiah 25 as a feast on a mountain (Zion, the new creation) where death is swallowed up and tears are wiped away. He quotes Revelation's "blessed are the dead" and the seventh-trumpet judgment of the dead, but he treats the New Testament's version as "blown all out of proportion" and as containing some dualism. He presents the main Hebrew emphasis as how one should live now, not how one gets eternal life.

### The purpose of life as moral maturity: outside the gates of Eden

Tabor reads "outside the gates of Eden" as the state of having "the knowledge of good and bad," which he says every person passes through as they become morally accountable. He points to Deuteronomy 1:39, which he says uses the phrase about children who "have not yet the knowledge of good and evil." He argues that the human is not made in God's image physically (heart, lungs, kidneys are mammalian), but that character can be shaped into that image. He says the dead are preserved in a "restive state" and that the "purpose of life is to become godlike in your character." He describes the human as "Adam 2," someone "forming himself," and calls this art.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Genesis 1:1 means God created from nothing.** Tabor argues the construct phrase and the context describe God ordering an existing chaotic earth, and he calls "creation from nothing" a logical contradiction that comes from Greek philosophy, not Hebrew text.
- **Genesis 1 describes a recreation after an earlier world.** He says this reading, which he attributes to the Scofield Bible and others, is incorrect; Genesis 1:2 describes the earth's state when God began.
- **Humans have immortal souls, as the Bible teaches.** He says the immortal-soul view comes from Plato and later religions, and that Genesis 2:7 and the flood passage give the breath of life to animals as well.
- **Jeremiah 17:9 says the human heart is wicked.** He argues the Hebrew term means "weak," so the text describes human weakness rather than moral corruption.
- **Sheol is a place of torment for the wicked.** He presents Sheol as a silent, dusty, shadowy place of sleep, and contrasts it with Babylonian and Greek underworlds.
- **Isaiah 26 is a passage about eternal life for believers in a heaven.** He says the passage is about the dead rising in the land and light falling on the realm of the dead, and that the Hebrew emphasis is on living now.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **Jesus and the Jewish Hebrew texts:** Tabor's reading of Genesis, Isaiah, and the Psalms is a reading of the Hebrew Bible itself. He does not discuss Jesus' own teaching in this talk, but his claim that later religions built escape-oriented systems on a soul that leaves the body is relevant to any comparison of Jesus' teaching with later Christianity.
- **Resurrection, afterlife and hell:** This is the talk's central topic. Tabor argues for sleep in the dust, Sheol as silence, a bodily resurrection in Isaiah 26 and Daniel 12, and a new creation on a mountain in Isaiah 25. He treats New Testament afterlife language (Revelation) as "blown all out of proportion."
- **Torah:** He reads Genesis 1-3 and Deuteronomy 1:39 as foundational for the human purpose, and treats the Hebrew Bible's account of creation and moral knowledge as the basis for his reading.
- **The Kingdom on earth:** His reading of Isaiah 25 as a feast on a mountain that fills the earth, and of the human task as "in charge of the earth," bears on the idea of a kingdom in this world, although he does not use the word "kingdom" systematically.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Not addressed in this talk.
