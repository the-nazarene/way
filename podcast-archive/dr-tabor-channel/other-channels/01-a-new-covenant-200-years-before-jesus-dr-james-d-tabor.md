## Summary

Tabor opens with his long involvement with the Dead Sea Scrolls, beginning with his first trips to the Judean desert in the early 1990s, and describes the Qumran caves and the 1947 discovery of Cave 1. He then presents the Scroll community as a movement that called itself the "people of the New Covenant": apocalyptic, convinced it was living in the last days, sharing goods in common, preaching judgment, and baptizing by immersion as an initiation. He says this group came roughly a century or more before Jesus and that he would rather use its own name than the label "Essenes."

The central text is 4Q521, which Tabor published with Michael Wise. He reads its opening line, "the heavens and the Earth will obey his Messiah," and points to its claim that the Messiah will heal the sick, preach good news to the poor, and raise the dead. He ties this to the Q passage in which John the Baptist's disciples ask Jesus whether he is the one, and to Jesus's reply listing the same works. He is explicit that Jesus did not copy the scroll; his claim is that both reflect a shared messianism.

Tabor argues that the group is "definitely not" a Pharisee or Sadducee party, that it held beliefs usually treated as distinctively Christian (including resurrection), and that Josephus's description of the Essenes leaves out these features, which he attributes to Josephus writing for Roman readers. He closes by describing his Dead Sea Scrolls course, which reads the Scrolls in English translation over ten lessons with live discussion, and by teasing an archaeological discovery at Qumran that he says relates to the Torah-based holiness of the community and was confirmed scientifically. He does not disclose the find in this talk.

## Key Teachings

### The "people of the New Covenant"
Tabor says the Scroll community called itself the people of the New Covenant, understood itself as the true remnant, and practiced a message of judgment, shared property, and immersion baptism. He presents these as pre-Christian features of the same Jewish stream as John the Baptist and Jesus, and says he prefers the group's own self-designation to "Essenes."

### 4Q521 and messianic healing
Tabor reads 4Q521 as a pre-Christian text in which the Messiah rules the heavens and earth, heals, and raises the dead. He sets it beside Jesus's answer to John's disciples (the Q material) and says the parallel reflects a shared messianic expectation, not copying.

### Josephus's silence
Tabor says Josephus describes the Essenes as a philosophical school and does not mention a New Covenant, a teacher, or a mission "preparing the way in the wilderness." He reads this as Josephus shaping the group for an audience of Roman readers.

### Shared doctrines a century earlier
Tabor says the group held "a set of a dozen doctrines that we associate uniquely with Christianity," which he says were practiced a century before Jesus. He cites the Sadducees' rejection of resurrection as a contrast point.

### Reading the Scrolls directly
Tabor says his course does not lecture about the Scrolls but reads them in English translation, lesson by lesson, with a printed edition in hand, and adds Zoom discussion sessions.

### A teased archaeological find
Tabor says he located a site at Qumran by mapping the Scrolls against the terrain and that an anthropologist confirmed the find scientifically. He links it to a form of holiness and observance of the Torah. The details are reserved for the course.

- **Misconception:** Josephus's account of the Essenes describes the Scroll community fully. **Tabor's correction:** He says Josephus omits the New Covenant identity, the teacher, and the messianic material found in the Scrolls.
- **Misconception:** The Scrolls are a marginal curiosity with no bearing on early Christianity. **Tabor's correction:** He argues they show a pre-Christian movement sharing key ideas with John the Baptist and Jesus, from the same Jewish stream.
- **Misconception (implied):** The Scrolls are a threat to Christianity. **Tabor's correction:** He presents the parallels as background for understanding the period, and as material many readers have not yet read.
