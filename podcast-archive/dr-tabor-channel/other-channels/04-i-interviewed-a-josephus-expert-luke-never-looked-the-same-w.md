## Summary

C. J. Cornthwaite interviews Steve Mason about Josephus, the first-century Jewish historian, and about Mason's book *Josephus in the New Testament*. Mason gives a short biography (born 37 CE into a priestly family in Jerusalem, a stint with a desert teacher, the 66 CE revolt, his surrender at Yodfat, and his later life in Rome writing the *Jewish War*, *Antiquities*, and related works). He argues that Josephus has been "defined" as a traitor or sellout in a way that does not match careful reading of his texts, and points out that Josephus gives two different accounts of why he went to Galilee.

On translation, Mason argues that "Judean" (Greek *Ioudaios*) should usually be rendered "Judean" rather than "Jew," because the ancient category did not separate religion from ethnicity or place. On the "Testimonium" (the Jesus passage in *Antiquities* 18), Mason reviews the long debate from Ernst Gerlach's 1860 Berlin essay onward, and says that Thomas Schmidt's new book makes it "more likely that Josephus wrote this than that he didn't," though not every phrase. He adds that *Antiquities* 20 refers to "the brother of the Jesus known as Christos," James, which he reads as a sign of Josephus's own acquaintance with the name.

The second half is on Luke–Acts. Mason argues that Luke's census under Quirinius, the Judas and Theudas pairing in Gamaliel's speech (Acts 5), the "Egyptian" who led people into the desert (the tribune scene in Acts), Lysanias of Abilene (Luke 3), and the Pharisees as the "most precise" of the schools (Acts 22–23) all match Josephus's wording and details, and that these overlaps are better explained by Luke using Josephus (or someone writing like him) than by shared sources. The talk ends with Mason's advice: clarify your questions, learn the language and context, and use an orientation volume before reading Josephus directly.

## Key Teachings

### Josephus as a source, not a caricature
Mason says Josephus's career has been reduced to labels like traitor, and that careful reading of his two accounts of the Galilean campaign shows a more complicated person. He encourages readers not to put him "in a box of some kind."

### "Judean" as a translation choice
Mason argues that the standard "Jew" hides a category the ancients did not have. He points to the fact that Greek writers list "Egyptians, Syrians, Idumeans" as peoples and switch to "Jews" in one case, and argues the translator should keep the sense of "Judean" (from Judea) and let the content be filled in by context.

### The Testimonium Flavianum
Mason reviews the long debate over the Jesus passage and says the single phrase "he was the Christ" is the main sticking point, since Josephus usually introduces nicknames differently. He says the balance of arguments now points toward Josephus having written much of the passage, but "not every single bit of it."

### Luke and Josephus
Mason argues that Luke–Acts shares vocabulary and details with Josephus that are specific to Josephus: the census under Quirinius and the Judas the Galilean rebellion, the Theudas and Judas order (reversed in Acts 5), the Egyptian prophet and the Sicarii (the tribune scene in Acts), Lysanias of Abilene (Luke 3), and the three philosophical schools with the Pharisees as the "most precise." He says the best explanation is that the author of Luke–Acts knew Josephus, and that the details do not fit Luke's own context.

### Advice for reading Josephus
Mason says historians need languages, a framework of ancient society and rhetoric, and a clear question. He recommends an orientation volume before reading Josephus, so that readers can test their reading against a guide.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Misconception:** Josephus was a traitor or coward. **Mason's correction:** He says this label comes from image-making rather than careful reading, and that the surrender at Yodfat was a practical choice.
- **Misconception:** "Jew" is the correct translation of *Ioudaios* in every context. **Mason's correction:** He argues the ancient term is best rendered "Judean," with the content filled in by context.
- **Misconception:** The Jesus passage in Josephus is wholly authentic. **Mason's correction:** He says the balance now favors Josephus having written much of it, but not every phrase.
- **Misconception:** Luke's census and Acts' historical details are accurate records. **Mason's correction:** He argues the Quirinius census does not fit the Herod timeline in Matthew, and that the details look borrowed from Josephus.
