## Summary

This short sermon by Scott, who introduces himself as Rabbi Scott on the Nazarene Way channel, asks what the Didache teaches about the way of life and about the Eucharist, and why he thinks it matters for a church that calls itself the Nazarene Way. Scott carries nearly the whole talk himself, mostly by reading aloud from the Didache. He says the text is a teaching of "the 12 apostles" given to the Nazarenes and other early Jewish Christians, and he claims it was written "arguably earlier than many of the books in the New Testament." He gives no date, source, or reasoning for that claim.

Scott's main points come from two passages. The second is a eucharistic prayer that he reads over the cup and the bread. He says the prayer has no flesh-and-blood language, and he attributes the later flesh-and-blood idea to "Pauline helenistic Roman ideas."

The talk ends with Scott's closing blessings and a brief reference to his congregation taking the Eucharist "offline." He does not cite any biblical verses or give chapter numbers for the Didache, and he offers no manuscript evidence for his claims about how the Nazarenes celebrated the Eucharist.

## Key Teachings

### The two ways and loving enemies
Scott reads the Didache's opening as "two ways, one of life and one of death." He summarizes the way of life as loving God, loving one's neighbor, and not doing to others what one would not want done to oneself. He then reads the instruction to "bless those who curse you and pray for your enemies and fast for those who persecute you," and he asks what reward there is in loving only those who love you. Scott calls this the "Step Above" that the Nazarenes took, and he says he plans to teach the same to his son.

### Christianity "majors in the minor"
Scott says the love-of-enemies ethic is "so lost within the realm of Christianity," including Catholicism, and he describes this as majoring in minor things. He says Jesus taught people "to major in the major." He connects this to his own experience of Catholic school, where he says the Didache was never mentioned.

### The Eucharist without flesh and blood
Scott's central claim about the Eucharist is that "there is no Pagan ideas of flesh and blood" in the Nazarene practice. He attributes the flesh-and-blood idea to "Pauline helenistic Roman ideas," which implies that Paul introduced it. He does not explain how, or cite any text for either the Pauline origin or the alleged pagan background.

### The eucharistic prayer and the gathering of the church
Scott reads a two-part prayer. Over the cup it gives thanks for "the holy Vine of David" and for the knowledge made known "through Jesus the servant." Over the bread it gives thanks for "the life and knowledge" made known through Jesus, and it compares the bread to scattered grain that is gathered into one. Scott applies the image to his own congregation, asking that "our church the Nazarene way be gathered together from the ends of the Earth ... into your kingdom." He sees this as evidence that the early church was a unified, shared meal.

### Early house churches and synagogue exclusion
Scott says the early Jerusalem community met in homes, which he calls "our very very early days," because "the synagogues didn't allow the nazarin into the synagogues" and "they cursed them actually." He presents this as the reason for home services, but he gives no source for the claim of cursing.
