## Summary

The question is why the first-century movement around Jesus of Nazareth, which the speaker calls "the Nazarenes" and "the Nazarene way," did not survive, while Christianity did. Scott, presenting a short sermon from the Nazarene Way channel, answers that the movement was anti-establishment and opposed Rome. He says it died out after Jerusalem fell, while religions that aligned with Roman power, merging into the Roman religion, survived. His main evidence is a reading from Robert Eisenman's book *James the Brother of Jesus*, which Scott calls a "fascinating" work. Eisenman argues that the Dead Sea Scrolls describe an anti-establishment movement under several self-designations, including "the Nazarene way," "the sons of Zadok," and "the Ebionites." Scott presents Eisenman's thesis that the Teacher of Righteousness in the Scrolls is James the brother of Jesus, while noting that Eisenman's critics dispute it.

Scott also argues that the New Testament was written for a Roman audience to ensure its survival, unlike the Dead Sea Scrolls, which he says lack this defect. He concludes that anyone seeking the historical Jesus should first study James rather than Paul of Tarsus, and he holds that Jesus had biological brothers, a point he says is "hidden in plain sight" in Mark.

The talk is brief (about seven minutes) and almost entirely Scott speaking. It is a reading and commentary on Eisenman, with little direct citation of ancient texts. It ends with a farewell and the words "shabbat shalom."

## Key Teachings

### 1. The movement was defined by opposition to the establishment
Scott reads a passage attributed to Eisenman that calls the authors of the Dead Sea Scrolls a "movement" that "is always at its core anti-establishment." The reading lists possible self-designations: "the Nazarene way," "the sons of Zadok," "the poor," "the Ebionites," "the meek," "the perfect," "the sons of Light," "the Holy One," "the zealots for the day of vengeance," "the poor ones of piety," "the zealots of righteousness," and "perfect of the way." Scott says these groups can be "considered as Allied or related in some way," sharing "common enemies." He tells listeners Eisenman says the name "must be left indeterminate" for now.

### 2. Why the Nazarene sect died out and Christianity survived
Scott argues that the Nazarenes did not survive the fall of Jerusalem because they were opposed to Rome. In his account, the groups that thrived were those that "aligned themselves and ensconced themselves in the power at the time which was the Romans," and so "kind of merged and branched into the Roman religion." He says this is why Christianity survived and "the true teaching in the sect of Jesus of Nazareth died off." He also claims the early Nazarenes were "persecuted and executed" for being anti-Roman.

### 3. The New Testament was shaped for a Roman audience
Scott reads Eisenman's claim that "the most literature at the time pandered to the Romans for survival reasons," and that "the New Testament suffers from the same defects," so that its christology was "written for the Roman audience so that their words can survive and they they won't be executed." Scott contrasts this with the Dead Sea Scrolls, which he says "does not suffer from the same defects." He tells listeners not to read the Bible "at face value" as the inherent word of God, because it was written by humans in specific "socioeconomic" settings. He says it requires study of "deep profound books like this."

### 4. The Teacher of Righteousness is James (Eisenman's thesis)
Scott describes Eisenman as a "fringe scholar" and an "independent scholar" who studied the Dead Sea Scrolls and made them public. Scott says Eisenman has "a compelling thesis and theory that one of the teachers in the Dead Sea Scrolls is actually James the just, James the brother of Jesus." Scott notes that James led the Nazarenes after Jesus' death and was a "core pillar" of the "three pillars of Jerusalem," which he names as James, John the Apostle, and Peter (the transcript reads "sephus," apparently a garble of Cephas). Scott acknowledges that Eisenman "is well respected by many" but also has "many critics," because of the "large claim assertion" about the Teacher of Righteousness. Scott says he thinks the broader point is true even if the identification is contested.

### 5. Seek James, not Paul, and recognize Jesus' brothers
Scott's practical conclusion is that "if you want to understand Jesus you need to First seek not Paul not Paul of Tarsus," who wrote "most of the letters in the New Testament." Instead, listeners should "study James the brother of Jesus." Scott says Jesus "had Brothers," a fact he calls "hidden in plain sight" in the Gospel of Mark, and says that "priests" who claim "cousins" are "hand waving."
