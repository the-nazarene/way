## Summary

Scott, presenting a short sermon for the Nazarene Way congregation, argues that observing the Sabbath as a real day of rest and withdrawal from the world is essential to finding the Kingdom and seeing God. He builds the case from a personal starting point: a modern Orthodox Jewish friend, Sam, who unplugs completely from Friday evening to Saturday evening. Scott says he found this annoying at first, then came to adopt the practice himself, reporting that it reduced burnout and made him value leisure more.

His textual anchor is Gospel of Thomas 27, which he reads from a hand-written copy in the translation of Marvin Meyer, his first college professor. Scott says the saying "likely" preserves sayings of Jesus that came from an early Nazarene or Judean community, while acknowledging the collection was found among Gnostic texts. He glosses "fast from the world" as giving up a desire for one day, and "the kingdom" as both inside and outside a person. He also claims that Sunday observance came from Roman, pagan, and Hellenistic practice, and that Christianity merged with that culture to make Sunday the new Sabbath. He links this to what he presents as a Pauline-influenced distortion of the Pharisees, and he briefly endorses a psychological rationale for sacrifice and fasting drawn from Jordan Peterson, while saying he is not a proponent of animal sacrifice.

The talk ends with Scott repeating the Thomas saying as the bottom line and closing with a Shabbat greeting. It is a devotional sermon, not a scholarly argument: the only textual evidence offered is one Thomas saying and one unspecified Synoptic healing dispute, and the historical claims about Sunday and the Pharisees are asserted without sources.

## Key Teachings

### Sabbath as a full withdrawal from the world
Scott reads Gospel of Thomas 27 as teaching that "if you do not observe the sabbath as a Sabbath you will not see the father." He defines the purpose of the Sabbath as giving up the world for one day, including phone use, Netflix, and watching college football. He frames this as gaining something new rather than simply losing something.

### Timing of the Sabbath
He states that the Sabbath runs "from Friday night till Saturday evening," that this is "what Jesus actually practiced," and that "the seventh day" on which God rested is Saturday. He contrasts this with Sunday observance.

### Sunday as a later, non-Jewish day of rest
Scott argues that the days of the week were named starting from Sunday because "the Romans and pagans and helenistic Greeks" already treated Sunday as a day off. He says Christianity "merged with Pagan Greek religion" and that Sunday "became the new Sabbath," especially after the Bar Kokhba revolt of 130 CE left Jews "pretty much outcasted." He attributes some of this shift to "Paul's Christianity," though he does not develop the argument with textual evidence.

### The Pharisees and the Pauline writings
He says the Pharisees "were given a horrible reputation" that came from "the writings of a lot of the Pauline pagans," and that they became a scapegoat in place of the actual Jews. He says this is a topic for a separate sermon, so it is only sketched here. He points to Synoptic Gospel stories in which the Pharisees object to Jesus healing on the Sabbath, and states that Jesus "made it a point that the Sabbath is a day to practice spirituality."

### Gospel of Thomas as a source for Jesus' sayings
Scott describes the Gospel of Thomas as "found amongst other Gnostic texts" but says that "a lot of the core teachings stem from what likely are the sayings of Jesus of Nazareth" from an early Nazarene or Judean community. He notes early translations and says the language "close to the root" of Jesus' language was Aramaic. He presents this as his own understanding, not a demonstrated conclusion.

### Sacrifice, fasting, and psychology
Scott says he is "not a proponent" of animal sacrifice, but argues that the principle of sacrifice "works" psychologically, and that this explains why Orthodox Jews are strict and why people keep fasting, turning off electronics, and giving up TV for a day. He credits Jordan Peterson with this point, without giving a source.
