## Summary

Scott asks whether the New Testament can be called "heretical," and he answers yes, on the grounds that Jesus and his earliest followers, including James the Just, did not read or abide by it. He says the term "New Testament" was first introduced by Marcion, citing Marcus Vincent's book *resetting the origins of Christianity* (page 163), and that some scholars contend the concept goes back to Marcion, who assigned it to his own gospel. Scott notes that Marcion was later declared a heretic by Epiphanius, whose *Panarion* he describes as listing about 80 heresies, and he says Marcion was also treated as a heretic earlier by some churches in Rome in the second century. He stresses that the first listing of the 27 New Testament books comes in 367 CE, roughly 337 years after Jesus's death, and he argues that the canon was assembled by fourth-century church fathers rather than by Jesus. He compares this to judging the Shakespeare literature (about 2,000 books) by a few dozen chosen works, and he points out that many New Testament books are pseudonymous, while other texts, such as the Gospel of Thomas and the pseudo-Clementines, were labeled apocryphal.

The talk ends as a practical sermon. Scott recommends reading the texts Jesus read (Isaiah, the prophets, the Hebrew Bible, and the Book of Enoch, which he says was found among the Dead Sea Scrolls), setting aside the New Testament as an inherent word of God, and treating wisdom as "action times knowledge," which for him means feeding the poor and serving the ebionites. He closes with a call to an open, curious and loving intellectual quest.

## Key Teachings

### Marcion as the origin of the term "New Testament"
Scott says the term "New Testament" and its concept were first introduced by Marcion, whom he cites from Vincent's book (page 163). He says "some Scholars contend" that the concept goes back to Marcion, who applied it to his own gospel and did not accept the four gospels, choosing only one. Scott also says Epiphanius counted Marcion among his heresies, and that Marcion had earlier been declared a heretic by some churches in Rome in the second century. The point Scott draws is ironic: the coiner of the term was himself later condemned as a heretic.

### The 27-book canon came late and was assembled by later churches
Scott gives 367 CE as the date of "the first actual listing that we have the 27 books of the New Testament," and says acceptance and canonization followed in the fourth century. He compares this to judging the life of someone who lived in the 1600s, and he argues that the canon was chosen by "a bunch of patristic helenistic Greek Roman Catholic Church fathers and chapers," who used the Holy Spirit as a "crutch" to decide which texts were the word of God. He states that "it wasn't Jesus that decided it was the word of God."

### Pseudonymity and the treatment of rejected texts
Scott argues that the New Testament is not uniquely authoritative in its authorship, saying that "every single freaking book in the New Testament is all like almost every one of them is pseudonymous," with the exception of uncertain cases such as Luke. He contrasts this with the labeling of the pseudo-Clementines, which he reads as a way to make them seem less authoritative, and he says the Gospel of Thomas and other texts were labeled Apocrypha. He calls this labeling "absurd" and says it hinders people from finding "the true Spirit" of Jesus. The Shakespeare analogy supports his argument that the New Testament is one selection among many works about a figure.

### Read what Jesus read: the Hebrew Bible, Enoch, and Torah observance
Scott instructs listeners to "read the same texts that Jesus of Nazareth read," naming Isaiah and the prophets, and the Book of Enoch, which he says "was found in the Dead Sea Scrolls" and was cited by Jesus and his brother Jude. He recommends the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh) in a translation by Robert Alter, which he describes as one with the footnotes of the original Hebrew. He also argues that Jesus and early believers wore tzitzit ("zet seats" in the captions), followed the Torah, and remained in the temple. He cites Acts 2:46 for the claim that new converts in Jerusalem "remained with one Accord in the temple day after day," and he says early believers called their gathering place a Temple, not a church, a later "medieval construct."

### Wisdom as action: feeding the poor and serving the ebionites
Scott says "wisdom is more important than Faith," and he defines wisdom as "action times knowledge." He contrasts this with modern megachurch Sunday attendance and says Jesus and James "fought for Action," naming feeding the poor and "serving the ebionites which are the poor ones." He describes his own study of Vincent's book as the basis for this sermon.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Bible is the inherent word of God, which is what modern Christians believe.** Scott says this is a mistaken view, since "they have no idea what that even means," and the canon was chosen by later churches, not by God or Jesus.
- **The New Testament has always been the accepted canon.** Scott says the 27-book list first appears in 367 CE, about 337 years after Jesus's death, and that the concept of a "New Testament" has a contested origin in Marcion.
- **Books outside the New Testament are Apocrypha and therefore false.** Scott argues that labeling rejected texts this way is "absurd" and that many New Testament books are themselves pseudonymous.
- **The Old Testament is the outdated part of Scripture.** Scott calls the term "Old Testament" "a horrific term" that leads Christians to skip it, and he recommends reading it.
- **Early believers met in a "church."** Scott says early Jerusalem believers called their gathering place a Temple, and that "church" was a later "medieval construct" (in his words, emerging perhaps in the third or fourth century).
