## Summary

Scott asks why the fact that Jesus had brothers and sisters is "almost completely unknown and overlooked today." He argues that the New Testament plainly names four brothers (James, Joses, Judas/Jude, Simon) and refers to unnamed sisters, starting with Mark 6:3 and repeating the point in Matthew, Luke and John. His explanation is that a later doctrine of Mary's perpetual virginity, which he dates to the second century and says became Catholic dogma at a sixth-century council, made the siblings an embarrassment. He says the doctrine came from the Proto-evangelium of James, a non-canonical text, and was then defended by church fathers (Irenaeus, Clement, Origen, Jerome). Scott also quotes Reza Aslan's *Zealot* on the "brother" = "cousin" explanations, and cites Eusebius's account of Jude's descendants to argue that the brothers taught action over faith, in contrast to Paul.

The talk then moves to Jude and James as witnesses for a non-Pauline Jesus. Scott credits Robert Eisenman for the Jude argument and presents the Epistles of James and Jude as the brothers' own writings, though he admits they are pseudonymous. He claims that Paul invented "Christ Jesus" and an emphasis on "flesh" and "faith." He offers three "tells" for recognizing a Pauline gospel, and he closes by recounting that his first purchase for the church was a knife for protection while feeding the poor.

Most of the argument is Scott's own reading of the texts, and several claims rest on secondhand sources or on his paraphrase of Eusebius. Where he attributes a position to another author, the notes below say so. The talk does not engage with any counter-readings beyond waving them off.

## Key Teachings

### 1. Mark 6:3 names the brothers and sisters
Scott reads Mark 6:3 from the NRSV (which he says Catholics use; he dismisses the NLT and NIV as "colloquial"). He stresses that the Greek *tektōn* does not mean a furniture-building carpenter, and he takes the passage's list of four brothers and "sisters here with us" as a plain statement of Jesus's siblings. He notes that the name Judas (Jude) appears on the list and that the Epistle of Jude is traditionally linked to him, though he says the New Testament letters are pseudonymous. He says Scholars "are not fully sure who Simon was."

### 2. Matthew, Luke and John repeat the point
Scott cites Matthew 13:55-56 as taking "a lot of Mark" and adding to it, Luke 8:19-21 and John 7:3-5 (transcribed by the captioner as "7: 3:5") as referring to Jesus's "brothers" in a way that treats them as kin and "not as you know oh what's up brother my disciple." He argues the narrative context makes this clear, and that the sisters are simply unnamed, which he says was common for women of that era.

### 3. The perpetual virginity doctrine as a later invention
Scott's central historical claim is that Mary's perpetual virginity, including the idea that her hymen remained intact, was not taught by Jesus, Paul, the New Testament, or the Gospel of Thomas. He says it "came from and was invented in the 2 Century around the year 150 CE," citing the Proto-evangelium of James as its source. He gives a sequence of supporting figures without quoting them:
- Irenaeus (180 CE), who he says called Mary the "new Eve"
- Clement of Alexandria (200 CE), who asserted her lifelong virginity
- Origen (248 CE), who argued against Jesus having brothers (Scott also makes an unsourced anecdote about Origen's self-castration)
- Jerome (383 CE), writing against Helvidius
- The Second Council of Constantinople (553 CE), where he says the doctrine became dogma

He concludes that the siblings were covered up because they "invalidated" the doctrine. He argues that Joseph's continued abstinence is implausible, and he cites Isaiah 7:14 as a mistranslation of "young woman" as "virgin."

### 4. The "cousin" and "stepbrother" explanations
Scott reads a passage that he attributes to Reza Aslan's *Zealot* (he transcribes the author as "Reza aen"). It says that by the third and fourth centuries, Jesus's brotherhood with James became "an obstacle" to the perpetual virginity doctrine, and that "overly clever Solutions" were built to explain it, such as Joseph's children from an earlier marriage or *adelphos* meaning cousin. The passage argues that *adelphos* appears roughly 340 times in the New Testament without meaning cousin or stepbrother, and that Mark 6:17 uses it for a half-brother only in a way that still "implies physical brother." Scott treats this as decisive.

### 5. Jude, James and Eusebius: works over faith
Scott attributes to Robert Eisenman the argument that Jude and James are better witnesses to Jesus's teaching than Paul, who "never met Jesus." He says Eisenman's argument is "very valid." He builds on the Epistle of James 2:14-17 ("faith without works is dead") as the core of James's teaching. He then describes Eusebius's account (book 3, chapters 19-20; he transcribes the author as "usus") in which an emperor (transcribed as "Damian"; this is likely Domitian) investigated Jude's descendants. According to Scott, the descendants said Christ's kingdom "was not of this world or Earthly but Angelic and Heavenly" and would be established at the end, when people would be rewarded "according to their deeds." Scott reports that they were poor farmers with calloused hands and were released. He places this around "the late '90s," and he says Eusebius admits Jesus had a brother named Jude while the "Pauline tactic" calls him a brother "in the flesh not in the spirit." Note: Scott paraphrases this passage rather than reading it verbatim, and the talk does not quote Eusebius's text.

### 6. Three "tells" of a Pauline gospel
Scott closes with three checks for whether a speaker is preaching "Paul" rather than Jesus:
1. **"Christ Jesus."** He says "Christ" is a Greek term, that "Jesus" derives from Greek, and that "Christ Jesus" is "an invented term by Paul."
2. **"Flesh."** He says Paul uses the word constantly, and that the Eucharist's "flesh" was "burgled" in from Paul. He claims that James and perhaps Jesus were vegetarian, pointing viewers to the Christspiracy documentary.
3. **"Faith."** He says Paul makes faith more important than deeds, while the brothers believed in "action over faith."

He ends by saying the church's first purchase was a knife, justified by feeding the poor and by a line he attributes to Jesus ("I came to bring the sword not peace").

- **Paul is the authority on Jesus.** Scott argues that Paul never met Jesus and that the brothers are more reliable witnesses.
