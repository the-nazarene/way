## Summary

The talk asks when the virgin birth of Jesus entered Christian belief, and whether it was part of the original teaching. Scott, presenting a sermon for Nazarene Way Church, builds the case mainly from a reading of page 294 of Marcus Vincent's *Resetting the Origins of Christianity*, which he says he recently finished. Vincent's book is said to trace Catholic sources backward from the medieval period. Scott's own argument leans on the letters of Paul. He says Paul claims only a spiritual encounter with Jesus, and that Paul's letters (Galatians, Romans) describe Jesus as "born of a Jewish woman" and descended from David "according to the flesh," with no virgin birth. He sets this against a dating sequence he gives: Galatians around 48 CE, Romans around 56 CE, Mark around 55 CE, and Matthew, the first gospel with a birth narrative, around 80 CE. On that sequence, he argues, the virgin birth was a later addition, prompted partly by objections to Jesus's Davidic lineage and partly by a reading of Isaiah 7:14 that he calls a mistranslation.

He says that teaching centered on loving God and neighbor and on action, especially caring for the poor, rather than on faith in Christ. He treats Paul as a figure the Ebionites considered an apostate, and he names James the Just and Jude as the followers whose example should guide readers. ## Key Teachings

### Paul's letters do not mention a virgin birth
Scott's first argument is that Paul never describes Jesus as born of a virgin. He cites Galatians 4:4, where Jesus is "born of a woman, born under the law," and Romans 1:3, where Jesus is "descended from David according to the flesh." Galatians 1:22 is used to show that Paul "remained personally unknown" to the Judean churches, which Scott takes as evidence that Paul had no physical acquaintance with the historical Jesus. He says Paul's claimed encounter with Jesus came as a visionary experience on the road to Damascus, which he dates to around 37 CE.

### The dating sequence
Scott argues from the order of composition. He places Galatians around 48 CE, Romans around 56 CE and Mark around 55 CE, calling Mark "the earliest written gospel." Mark, he notes, begins with the baptism of Jesus by John, with no birth account. He places Matthew around 80 CE and says Matthew added "two little chapters" before the baptism narrative, which contain the virgin birth and a genealogy tracing Joseph's descent from David. Scott says the early letters and Mark therefore predate the virgin birth tradition.

### The Davidic-lineage objection and the invented genealogy
Scott proposes a motive for the later addition. He suggests that in the 40s and 50s, Paul or Peter faced objections from Jewish believers who held that the Messiah must come from David's line. In his account, a family tree was then created to answer that objection, and the same narrative was prefaced onto Matthew. He links this to the word *netzer* ("branch"), which he connects to the words Nazarene and Nazarite, to argue that Davidic descent was central to Jesus's messianic claim.

### Isaiah 7:14 as a mistranslation
Scott says the claim that the Messiah "will be born of a virgin" in Isaiah 7:14 is a misreading. He argues the underlying term means "young woman" or "young maiden," and that the error gave rise to a further invented narrative to fulfill the verse. The talk gives no Hebrew text or lexical evidence.

### Davidic descent versus virgin birth
Scott argues that Romans 1:3 and Galatians 4 are in tension with the later doctrine. If Jesus was "descended from David according to the flesh," he says, the belief that Jesus was conceived without Joseph contradicts it. He says that if Paul had known of a virgin birth, he would not have described the Davidic and fleshly lineage as he does.

### The Ebionites and Nazarenes and the rejection of the virgin birth
Scott describes the Ebionites as a sect of the first to third centuries, outlined by Epiphanius, that likely split from the Nazarenes, "the earliest people who knew and followed Jesus." He says they rejected the virgin birth as a pagan, cultic invention. He presents this as a fact about the Ebionites but does not quote Epiphanius or cite the passage.

### Action over faith, and the rejection of Paul
Scott says the Ebionites and early Nazarenes regarded Paul as an apostate and looked instead to James the brother of Jesus and to Jude. He presents the core of Jesus's teaching as loving God with heart, soul and mind, and loving one's neighbor, and he insists that action outweighs faith. He contrasts this with Paul's "pagan invented deity called Christ Jesus," in which belief alone is required. He also says Jesus was anti-accommodationist, spoke Aramaic and not Greek, and was focused on a kingdom above Rome. He cites the Clementine literature and the Gospel of Thomas as texts to be read alongside the Bible, though the talk does not say what in them supports his points.

### Scripture as human, not inerrant
Scott warns against treating the Bible as inerrant. He says it is "inspired by God" but "written by imperfect humans," with copying errors. He tells listeners to "look deeper than the text" to find the "true Spirit" of Jesus.
