## Summary

Scott asks why Jesus of Nazareth became, in his words, "hands down the most famous and wellknown human being" in history, when several other first-century figures who claimed to be the Messiah are now largely forgotten. He first notes that messianic claimants were not unique to Jesus and that Josephus viewed them as dangerous charlatans. He then surveys five "failed Messiahs": Hezekiah the Bandit Chief, Judas the Galilean, Menahem, Simon son of Giora, and Simon bar Kokhba. For each he gives a date, a summary of the movement, and a reason for its failure. Scott's main argument is that these movements were political and violent, focused on Roman rule and on Israel as the only people God cares about, and that they lacked a "deep theological" or transcendent message.

Scott contrasts this with Jesus's teaching, which he presents as a "mandate from Heaven" centered on the Kingdom of Heaven, the greatest commandments (love God, love neighbor), and love of enemies. He ties the Nazarene movement's decline to the Bar Kokhba revolt and the banishment of Jews from Jerusalem, and says Paul's work among Greeks let a Gentile Christianity flourish in that gap. He closes by urging viewers to live out love and care for the poor, and to wear tzitzit as a reminder of the commandments.

The talk is a single-speaker sermon. Scott gives no sources for many of his claims, including family details, the Herodian-era history, and the claim that the Catholic canon was "invented" in the late fourth century. The historical sequence is presented as his own reading rather than as settled scholarship.

## Key Teachings

### The five failed Messiahs and why they failed
Scott's list, with his stated dates and explanations:
- **Hezekiah the Bandit Chief** (late first century BCE): resisted foreign rule, was captured and executed by Herod the Great, and had no "deep theological roots."
- **Judas the Galilean** (around 6 CE): led a tax revolt against the Romans after the census of Quirinius and founded the Zealot movement. Scott says the Romans crushed the revolt and burned Sepphoris, and that his message was "highly nationalistic and lacked a universal appeal."
- **Menahem** (66–70 CE): a Zealot leader and descendant of Judas, who briefly seized Jerusalem and claimed messianic authority. Scott says his brutal tactics alienated fellow rebels and he was assassinated by rival factions.
- **Simon son of Giora** (66–70 CE): a military leader who was captured and executed after the fall of Jerusalem, with "a lack of a transcendent spiritual message."
- **Simon bar Kokhba** (132–135 CE): led the revolt, minted his own coins, and was seen as a warrior Messiah by many Jews, including Rabbi Akiva. Scott says his defeat discredited his messianic claim.

Scott's common thread is that all five "declared themselves King of the Jews," claimed Davidic descent, and tied messiahship to violence and political victory.

### Jesus's message as "above the flesh" and political
Scott argues Jesus's teaching was "a mandate from Heaven" and "above the political realm," in contrast to these movements. He describes the Kingdom as "a kingdom of Heaven, not a kingdom of Earth, not a Jerusalem Kingdom." He says Jesus was an apocalyptic, eschatological figure who expected the end times, and that it is "an argument" that Jesus believed they would come in his lifetime. He adds that the Kingdom "started" in some sense, though the end did not come as expected.

### The greatest commandments (Matthew 22:34)
Scott reads Matthew 22:34 onward, in which a lawyer asks Jesus the greatest commandment. Jesus answers with love of God "with all your heart... soul... and... mind," and love of neighbor "as yourself," and says "on these two Commandments hang all the law and the prophets." Scott connects this to the four tzitzit (fringes) worn by Jews, which he uses as a reminder of these commandments and of the 613 laws in the Torah.

### Love of enemies and nonviolence
Scott presents Jesus's core ethic as nonviolent love of enemies. He tells the story (without giving a verse) of a Roman's right to make a Jew carry his gear a mile, and says Jesus's teaching was to go an extra mile: "we do not commit violence towards our enemy we commit love towards our enemy." He calls this "the most gangster move" and compares Jesus to Gandhi as an example of nonviolent power. He says Jesus "did not care about restoring or taking back Jerusalem from Roman rule."

### The Nazarenes after Jesus: James, the Jerusalem pillars, and Bar Kokhba
Scott says Jesus's followers were called "nazarenes" by outsiders and called themselves "people of the Way," which he finds in Acts. After Jesus, James the Just led the Jerusalem community, with Peter and John as the other two "pillars" (Scott calls Peter "Kephas"). After the Bar Kokhba revolt, Jews were banished from Jerusalem, and only Gentile-circumcised teachers were allowed to teach in the Jerusalem community, leaving Jewish followers of Jesus without a home base. Scott says Romans, Greeks, and Hellenists could not distinguish Nazarenes from other Jews, so the sect was lumped in with them. He says this, plus Paul's work among scattered Greeks, created a "perfect storm" for the Hellenistic movement called Christianity.

### Scott's account of the Jewish groups and the Gospels
Scott describes four Jewish "schools" (Sadducees, Pharisees, Essenes, and the Zealot "fourth philosophy," per Josephus). He says the Pharisees were unfairly portrayed in the Gospels by "helenistic Pauline writers," and that the Sadducees were the wealthy, corrupt Herodian party. He suggests John the Baptist may have been influenced by the Essenes, and that Jesus was close to the Zealots, citing Reza Aslan's book *Zealot*. He calls Jesus the "Son of man" (Ben Adam).

### James on faith and works
Scott quotes James's view that "a person is justified by works and not by faith alone," and presents it as Jesus's emphasis on action. He says the Epistle of James was moved to the end of the Catholic canon, which he says was "essentially invented in the late um fourth Century."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus was the first would-be Messiah.** Scott says messianic claimants were common around Jesus's time and that Josephus regarded them as dangerous.
- **The Pharisees were the villains.** Scott says the Pharisees were not "horrible people," that the Gospels' negative portrait came from Pauline writers, and that the Sadducees were the more corrupt party.
- **Jesus was simply a "Jew."** Scott argues Jesus was a Nazarene, and that outsiders lumped Nazarenes in with Jews for simplicity.
- **Christianity is the direct continuation of Jesus's first followers.** Scott argues Christianity grew from Paul's Gentile mission and the Jerusalem community's exclusion, and that the Nazarenes died out.
- **The biblical canon was fixed early.** Scott says the Catholic canon was "essentially invented" in the late fourth century.
