## Key Teachings

### Five causes of the Nazarenes' disappearance

Sheeper lists the causes in sequence: (1) Catholic persecution in the third through fifth centuries, when Epiphanius declared the Nazarenes and Ebionites heretics; (2) Jewish persecution after the first revolt, when the surviving Pharisees treated other sects as heretics; (3) Roman persecution, especially after the Jerusalem siege and the Bar Kokhba revolt; (4) the loss of a charismatic leader after Jesus died and after James was killed; and (5) Paul's success with the Romans. He presents these as reasons "why the nazarin died," and he does not weigh them against other possible explanations.

### Jewish sectarian context

Sheeper describes first-century Judaism as having several sects in conflict: the Essenes (living around Qumran and the Dead Sea Scrolls), the Sadducees (who, he says, did not believe in resurrection and were effectively "herodians"), the Pharisees (whom he links to later rabbinic Judaism and "Orthodox Jews"), and the Zealots. He says Josephus describes these groups. He adds that the Nazarene sect was a fifth branch, and that after the wars the Pharisees became dominant and declared all the others heretics. He notes that scholars disagree about whether the Zealot Simon and Jesus's brother Simon or cousin Simeon were the same person.

### James the Just and the loss of leadership

Sheeper presents James as Jesus's successor and leader of the Jerusalem church. He describes James as "stoic" and "disciplined," not "fiery," and he cites Clementine literature for the story that James's knees were damaged from long periods of prayer in the Temple. He claims James was killed in 68 CE, before the Temple's destruction, and that leadership then passed to other relatives of Jesus who scattered to places such as Pella. He argues this lack of a strong leader was one reason the movement weakened, and he contrasts James with Paul, whom he describes as a "very very charismatic leader."

### Paul versus Jesus

Sheeper argues Paul "never even knew Jesus of Nazareth" and taught ideas he calls "completely contradictory" to Jesus's teaching. He calls the title "Christ Jesus" an invention of Paul's ("any time you hear Christ Jesus just run"). He describes Paul's conversion as a supposed revelation of white light, comparing it to Joseph Smith's. He also says Paul's mission was to the Gentiles, "which really means" to the Romans and those in power, and that Paul's version of Christology "latched onto" the Roman political entity, while Jesus's teaching "latched onto" the Nazarene community, which was eventually persecuted.

### Nonviolence and the Sermon on the Mount

Sheeper reads Matthew 5 and focuses on "if anyone forces you to go one mile go also the Second Mile." He explains that, in his account, Roman law allowed soldiers to make Jews carry their gear for a mile, which Jews resented. He contrasts Jesus with the other would-be messiahs, including Bar Kokhba and Judas the Galilean, who in his telling used violent resistance. He says Jesus instead "gave himself the power," offering more than was demanded. He describes Jesus as "not fighting for a physical Kingdom" but for "the Kingdom of Heaven," which he takes to be the metaphysical, spiritual realm.

### Torah purism and criticism of Catholic practice

Sheeper says Jesus was "actually a purist" who appealed to the Five Books of Moses, Isaiah and Daniel rather than to the oral law of the Pharisees. He applies this to modern Catholicism, arguing that Jesus would reject its "doctrinaire dogmatic" practices such as confession and incense. He criticizes the perpetual virginity of Mary as a "weird apocryphal" doctrine from the third to fifth centuries, and he says Jesus called himself a rabbi and teacher, which Sheeper claims for his own role.

### Canon and non-canonical texts

Sheeper argues that the 27 canonical books of the New Testament are not the only useful source for Jesus's character. He says Clementine literature, which early Jewish Christians read, and the Gospel of Thomas contain "just as much if not more wisdom." He presents Paul's letters as less reliable for knowing Jesus, because Paul did not know him in person. He also says "the canonical word of God" alone "does Faith disservice" if read without these other texts.

- **The New Testament is the only reliable source on Jesus.** He argues that non-canonical texts such as Clementine literature and the Gospel of Thomas offer valuable material.
- **Paul is an authority on Jesus's teaching.** He argues Paul "never even knew Jesus" and so is a weaker source than the family and disciples who did.
