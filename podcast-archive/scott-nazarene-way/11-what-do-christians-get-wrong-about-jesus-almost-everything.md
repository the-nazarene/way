## Summary

Scott, who presents himself as the "Rabbi" of Nazarene Way Church, asks what Christians get wrong about Jesus and answers that much of Christianity rests on Paul's interpretation of a figure Paul never met. He presents the talk as a sermon built on a "common thread" he found in Daniel Boyarin's *The Jewish Gospels*. From that book he draws three claims: that Jesus's followers called themselves Israelites and Judeans rather than Christians, that Mark and Paul present a christology in which Jesus became God at his baptism rather than through a virgin birth, and that the Mark handwashing dispute was an argument over how to observe the Torah, not an attack on it. Scott also argues that Jesus's core religion is love of God and neighbor plus faith expressed through works for the poor, drawing on the epistle attributed to James. He contrasts this with a "Pauline" Christianity centered on belief in Jesus's death and resurrection.

The talk moves into a practical close. Scott describes buying a knife with the church's first funds for self-defense while feeding homeless people at a 7-Eleven, and he reads Matthew 10:34 ("I have not come to bring peace but a sword") as literal. He links this to the Zealot movement and to Simon the Zealot. The talk is mostly one speaker's monologue. It digresses often, includes attacks on Catholicism and Mormonism, and ends with a call to subscribe and the words "welcome to the way peace and love." Most claims are unsourced beyond the Boyarin book, and Scott cites page numbers without quotation, so the group should check them against the book before relying on them.

## Key Teachings

### Paul as the source of later Christianity
Scott says that "Christianity is based on Paul's interpretation of a figure that he never met," and that Paul was a Hellenistic Jew. He gives a rough estimate that Christianity has "maybe 20% to 50%" right. He argues Paul's account of Jesus contains a "white light" vision and that the phrase "in the flesh" is Paul's way of deriding the idea of meeting a real person. He also says "Christ Jesus" is a Pauline phrase and that Jesus never used the term "Christ." He presents these as the core of the talk's thesis rather than as conclusions from a detailed textual analysis.

### Jesus's followers as Israelites and Judeans, not Christians
Scott cites Boyarin on page 20, which he paraphrases as saying all Nazarenes "considered themselves Israelites," and on page 30 (and page 33 in his paraphrase), which says followers of Jesus considered themselves Judeans. He notes that "Jew" comes later from the Greek and prefers "Judean." He adds that followers called themselves "people of the Way," not Christians or Catholics, and he presents the Nazarenes as a distinct Judean sect. He also gives a short etymology of "Israel" from El and Yahweh, drawing on Genesis and Exodus.

### Christology: Jesus becomes God at baptism
Scott says that according to Boyarin (page reference not given in the talk), "Mark and Paul display a christology in which Jesus became God at baptism of John not the Virgin birth." He adds that this view "was declared heresy and was quote unquote not stamped out until the Middle Ages." He presents this as a point the Gentile church has suppressed. He also says the perpetual virginity of Mary rests on the apocryphal Ascension of James and that Jesus and Paul never taught it.

### The Mark handwashing dispute as Torah interpretation
Scott's "key to the sermon" is Boyarin's reading (page 111) of the Mark passage where Jesus argues with the Pharisees. In Scott's account, the dispute was not an attack on the Torah but a disagreement over the correct observance of it, specifically a handwashing ritual the Pharisees had invented. He says Gentile Christianity has built on this one example to claim that Jesus's message is simply belief in him.

### Against salvation by belief in the death and resurrection
Scott argues that "Jesus never said... the way to eternal life is by believing in my death and Resurrection." He says these ideas, including the Nicene-style creedal formula that Catholics recite, came from Paul. He contrasts this with Jesus's citation of the Torah's great commandments: love of God "with all your heart, with all your soul, with all your mind," and love of neighbor "as yourself." He attributes the second to the Hebrew Bible and says these are the words of Jesus. He also states that Catholics "declare Jesus's religion and nazarin as Heretics," which he presents without evidence.

### Faith through works, and feeding the poor
Scott names three pillars: the two great commandments and "to do as James the just... said," that "it is not through faith alone but through acts" that one walks with Jesus. He defines the "ebionites" as "the poor ones" and says following the Way means serving and feeding them. He describes his own outreach, buying about $20 of goods for homeless people at a 7-Eleven at 1 a.m. and speaking with them "as actual human beings."

### The sword and Zealot self-defense
Scott reads Matthew 10:34 ("I have not come to bring peace but a sword") as literal rather than figurative. He ties the knife he bought to the Sicarii (which he calls "Sakari"), a branch of the Zealot movement, and suggests Jesus may have been connected to the Zealots. He says Simon the Zealot "had a knife" and that Jesus "would be bringing the sword." He also contrasts modern megachurch Christianity, which he says supports the Sadducees, the Herodians, and the elite priests, with Jesus. This is the talk's most contested claim, and it is largely Scott's own interpretation.
