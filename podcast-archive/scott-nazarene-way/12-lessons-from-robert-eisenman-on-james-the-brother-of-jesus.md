## Summary

Scott, presenting on the Nazarene Way channel, asks what Jesus taught and what happened to his first followers, especially his brother James "the Just" and the Jerusalem movement, compared with the Christianity that grew from Paul. He presents his talk as a reading of Robert Eisenman's *James the Brother of Jesus* and says he is still working through the book. His core position is that Jesus was a Jewish teacher whose message was summed up in the two great commandments (love God; love your neighbor), that James was the real leader of the movement after Jesus's death, and that Paul's faith-centred message departs from both.

His evidence is mostly textual and drawn from the book. He cites Acts 12:17 for James as principal leader of the church, Paul's Galatians references to his authority coming from James, the Dead Sea Scrolls (with a stated hedge about Eisenman's identifications), the claim that the New Testament Gospels are pseudonymous and were shaped for Roman audiences, the observation that Paul's letters never mention the virgin birth, and the *Didache* (the "Teaching of the Twelve Apostles") as a surviving statement of early Nazarene ethics. Much of the reasoning is presented as the speaker's own inference rather than established scholarship.

The talk ends with an appeal for practical charity: "feed the ebionites," give to beggars without judging them, and "practice action over faith." Scott also promotes his own channel and the other Nazarene channels listed in the description. Throughout, he speaks in an informal, polemical register and frequently attacks Catholicism and Mormonism.

## Key Teachings

### Paul versus James and the Jerusalem leadership
Scott says Paul's teaching ("Pauline Doctrine") is an invented message, and that Paul never met Jesus in the flesh. He reports that Paul's authority came from James, citing Galatians for Paul's subordinate status, and that Acts 12:17 names James as the principal leader of the church. He presents this as a "great big Paradox in cover up," saying the New Testament later pushed James's influence into the background. He also says the Epistle of James was among the disputed books of the canon and sits after Paul's letters.

### Jesus's core ethic and the "anti-accommodationist" reading
Scott summarizes Jesus's teaching as the two greatest commandments from the Pharisees' question: love the Lord God with all heart, soul and mind, and love your neighbor as yourself. He argues that "neighbor" meant a fellow Israelite. He describes Jesus as anti-Roman but opposed to military rebellion, contrasting him with Judas the Galilean (whom he calls the founder of the Zealot movement), the 66–70 CE war, and Bar Kokhba (130 CE). Scott links the nonviolent love-of-enemy ethic to the *Didache* and to Jesus's example. He claims Jesus was fighting for "the Kingdom of Heaven" rather than an earthly kingdom in Jerusalem.

### Dead Sea Scrolls and the Qumran–Essene–Nazarene connection
Scott reports Eisenman's argument linking figures in the Scrolls (the "Righteous Teacher" and the "Wicked Priest") to James. He is explicit that this identification is weak: he puts it at "probably 80% unlikely." He says Eisenman's broader point, that the Scrolls show a community ripe with reform ideas, is more persuasive. He notes that the Scrolls do not identify their authors as Essenes, even though Josephus describes the Essenes and scholars often assume the link. He also says the Essenes, Nazarenes and Ebionites shared features, including baptism-by-immersion language and anti-establishment attitudes (Eisenman, pages 36–37 according to Scott).

### Gospels as pseudonymous, Greek-oriented texts
Scott argues that the Gospels are pseudonymous (he says "we don't know if Mark actually wrote the Gospel of Mark") and that the New Testament was written in Greek for a Greek-Roman audience. He uses this to explain why Pilate is portrayed as reluctant to execute Jesus, which he calls "absolutely Preposterous and completely fabricated." He says Jesus called himself *ben adam* ("son of man") rather than "Son of God." He claims the New Testament's order was arranged by later Church Fathers. He points to the pseudo-Clementines and says many Pauline letters are also pseudonymous.

### Chronology: Paul first, then James, then the Gospels
Scott asserts that the chronologically earliest material is Paul's letters, then James, then the Gospels, with Revelation later. He then asks why Paul never mentions the virgin birth. He notes that Mark begins at John the Baptist's baptism of Jesus (the Jordan River, which he says is depicted in his own church). He treats this silence as evidence that the virgin birth was a later development. He does not cite specific dates for each text beyond saying Mark was written around the 60s or 70s CE.

### The Didache as a surviving Nazarene ethic
Scott reads the opening of the *Didache* ("two ways," love of God and neighbor, "bless those who curse you," "love those who hate you"). He describes it as a central teaching of the Nazarenes and says it was lost until it was found in a library in the mid-1800s (his claim, not given with a source). He then applies it to giving to beggars: give to anyone who asks, without judging how the money will be used.

### Works over faith
Scott contrasts Paul's supposed message (proclaim faith in Christ and be saved) with James's emphasis on action. He claims James "admonished" Paul to remember the poor, and he presents this as the central practical teaching of the Nazarene movement.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Paul as the authoritative continuation of Jesus's teaching.** Scott argues Paul never met Jesus in the flesh and that James, not Paul, was the legitimate leader of the Jerusalem movement.
- **The Pilate story of handwashing.** Scott says the Gospels' portrayal of Pilate as reluctant to kill Jesus is fabricated, and that Pilate was in fact ruthless.
- **Jesus as "Son of God" in his own words.** Scott says Jesus called himself *ben adam* ("son of man") in Aramaic and Hebrew.
- **The Gospels as eyewitness accounts.** Scott says they are pseudonymous and written for Greek and Roman readers.
- **The Essenes as authors of the Dead Sea Scrolls.** Scott notes the Scrolls do not identify their authors as Essenes.
- **"Nazarene" as a later Baptist denomination.** Scott says the term refers to the first-century Jewish sect, from the Hebrew *netser* ("branch"), and that the Baptist usage is a 19th-century innovation.
- **Faith alone as sufficient for salvation.** Scott says Paul's message reduces to saying "I have faith in you" and that James and Jesus stressed action.
