## Summary

Scott, presenting for the Nazarene Way Church channel, uses this second part of his series on Robert Eisenman's *James the Brother of Jesus* to ask what the first movement around Jesus and James actually was, and why it disappeared while Paul's Christianity and rabbinic Judaism survived. He opens with a reading from the Didache, chapter 1, on giving to everyone who asks, and treats that as a core Nazarene teaching. He then reports points he takes from Eisenman's book, with page numbers: the Maccabean and Hanukkah background; Pauline Christianity and rabbinic Judaism as two accommodationist, pro-Roman survivors; the Sadducees as Herodian puppets; "Christ Jesus" as a Pauline invention; Peter's subordination to James according to Galatians 2:11-12; and the Romans lumping several Judean sects together as "Jews."

Scott's own argument is more devotional and polemical than evidentiary. Much of what he says is his own assertion, with little sourcing in the talk: that Jesus was not a Jew, that the Gospel of John was written around Alexandria about a century after Jesus, and that the New Testament was assembled hundreds of years later. The talk is essentially one speaker reading and summarizing a book, not a sourced debate, so the claims should be checked against Eisenman's text and the primary sources.

The talk ends with an exhortation to give money directly to poor people rather than to churches, plus a request to like, subscribe and share the channel.

## Key Teachings

### Give to the poor, not to churches (Didache 1)
Scott reads Didache 1, "give to everyone who asks you and ask it not back," and presents it as an early Nazarene text. He admits we don't know how early it is. He says it was rediscovered "in some uh library in the 1800s," and that early church writers referred to it. He draws a practical rule from it: give money, even generously, to homeless people on the freeway or outside a 7-Eleven, and treat them as human beings regardless of what they do with it. He contrasts this with giving to churches, which he claims send "less than 4%" to the poor. These figures and the mega-church examples he gives are his own assertions, with no source given in the talk.

### Maccabean background and anti-foreign zeal
Scott reports Eisenman's (page 38) description of Hanukkah as a celebration of Maccabean "anti-foreign Priestly Zeal," winning back the Temple. He stresses that the Judeans of the period were anti-foreign and anti-establishment, and that Roman, Persian and Greek domination formed the background to Jesus's upbringing.

### Pauline Christianity and rabbinic Judaism as accommodationist
On page 39, Scott says, Eisenman argues that Pauline Christianity and rabbinic Judaism are "two sides of the same coin," both pro-Rome, and that this is why they survived, while the Nazarenes, who were anti-Roman but pacifist, died out. Scott also reports Eisenman's claim that the Pharisees rebranded into rabbinic Judaism after persecution. He says the Gospels were written in Greek and that Pilate's washing of his hands was a pro-Roman invention that blamed "the evil Jews the high priests."

### Sadducees as Herodian puppets
On page 50, Scott says the Sadducees, who ran the Temple and its sacrifices, were "puppets of Herod." He tells listeners to replace "high priests" and "Sadducees" in their minds with "Herodians," and suggests that the Pharisees were probably the most earnest Jewish sect and got a "very bad rap." He also says Caiaphas and Pilate were responsible for the killing of Jesus, a point he presents as Eisenman's and his own.

### "Christ Jesus" as Paul's invention; Jesus as Son of Man
On page 51, Scott reports Eisenman's observation that the figure Paul calls "Christ Jesus" often looks like a copy of Paul himself. Scott adds that "Christ Jesus" is a Pauline fabrication, and that Jesus used the Aramaic "benadam," "son of man," and never called himself "Christ Jesus" or openly "Son of God." Scott tells listeners that a church preaching "Christ Jesus" is "not teaching the true way."

### Peter subordinate to James (Galatians 2:11-12)
On page 53, Scott notes that Peter, the alleged first pope, had a wife, which he says Paul complained about. He then argues from Galatians 2:11-12 that Peter was "subservient to James," the biological brother of Jesus whom Paul never met. He presents this as "hidden in plain sight" in the Pauline letter.

### Four or five Judean sects and the Roman "lumping"
On page 67 and after, Scott describes Eisenman's account of the Sadducees, Pharisees, Essenes (Josephus's term; Scott notes the sect never self-identified as Essenes), and the Zealots or "fourth philosophy" (Josephus's term). Scott suggests Nazarenes may have been a fifth group, possibly branching from the Essenes or Zealots, though he says "we don't know for sure." He says Romans lumped all of these into one category, "Jews," before 70 CE, and that after Jerusalem fell, Paul's branch spread beyond Judea, leaving "Christians" and "Jews" as two categories. He compares this to the two-party system in the United States.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus was a Christian or founded Christianity.** Scott says Jesus "was not a Christian either"; he was a sect of Judean religion trying to restore true Hebrew religion. He also says "Christianity is a Pauline invention."
- **"Christ Jesus" is how Jesus named himself.** Scott says this is Paul's fabrication, and Jesus used "son of man."
- **Pontius Pilate washed his hands and the Jews were to blame.** Scott says the Gospels' blame-shifting to "the evil Jews" was a pro-Roman invention.
- **The Sadducees and high priests were the godly Jewish leaders, and the Pharisees were the villains.** Scott argues the reverse: Sadducees were Herodian puppets, and Pharisees were the most earnest group, with an unfair reputation.
- **Peter was the leader of early Christianity.** Scott argues Peter was subordinate to James.
- **Christians and Jews were always distinct religions.** Scott argues the Romans did not distinguish them before the fall of Jerusalem and that the distinction emerged later.
