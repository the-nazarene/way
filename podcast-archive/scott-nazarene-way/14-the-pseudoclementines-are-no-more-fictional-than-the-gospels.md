## Summary

Scott, presenting on the Nazarene Way channel, works through pages 76 to 129 of Robert Eisenman's *James the Brother of Jesus*. His starting point is the objection that the Pseudo-Clementine literature is fiction. He argues that the objection applies equally to the Gospels: Matthew, Mark and John were probably not written by the authors whose names they carry, Luke is the one exception he allows, and the Gospels contain miracles such as the virgin birth and walking on water. He also says Josephus, the main first-century historical source, wrote with an eye to Greek and Roman readers, so it needs critical reading too.

From there Scott moves to his own account of the "true religion" of Jesus and James: love of God and neighbour, the Torah kept rather than abolished, and "action matters over faith," which he takes from the Epistle of James. He contrasts this with Paul, whom he says invented a figure called "Christ Jesus," and whose letters he calls not sacred. He also argues that the Nazarites (including John the Baptist and James) took a vow of abstinence, and he describes his own practice of the Nazarite vow and vegetarianism. He presents much of this as his own reading of Eisenman, and he says he is not yet convinced by Eisenman's identification of James with the Teacher of Righteousness in the Dead Sea Scrolls.

The talk ends as a sermon: Scott urges viewers to read the Epistle of James, study Jesus's words, keep the Sabbath, and "open your eyes." Most of the claims are presented as Scott's conclusions from Eisenman, and several (the Sunday/sun-god link, the Nazarite etymology, the claim that all scholars agree on Gospel authorship) are stated without supporting argument in the talk.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The Pseudo-Clementines are no more fiction than the Gospels
Scott reports Eisenman's point (p. 76) that the Pseudo-Clementines, important to Jewish Christians and Ebionites, get dismissed as fiction and as "pseudonymous," even though the Gospels are also pseudonymous and contain miracles. He says the Pseudo-Clementines are closer to a shared source that Acts also drew on, and that the text includes a story about Paul attacking James. He admits he has not studied that passage closely and has only read about it across several books.

### 2. The Gospels are not firsthand accounts
Scott argues that the Gospel titles ("according to Matthew," "according to Mark," and so on) are later attributions. In his view, the writers were passing on oral tradition they had heard from others. He says Luke is the exception, with Luke presented as Paul's assistant who never met Jesus. He also repeats the claim that Tertullian or Marcion coined the term "New Testament," though he hedges with "I believe."

### 3. Josephus is not a neutral source
Scott calls Josephus "the only quote unquote historical work" from first-century Judea and Galilee that survives. He says Josephus overexaggerated and wrote to please Greeks and Romans, so that he would not be executed. Scott says the Gospel writers did the same, including making Pontius Pilate look good. He tells critical readers to "read through that" to reach the underlying religion of Jesus.

### 4. Jesus kept the Torah; Paul invented "Christ Jesus"
Scott gives Jesus's core teaching as loving "the Lord God with all your heart with all your soul with all your mind" and loving one's neighbour as oneself. He says Jesus came to fulfil the Torah, not abolish it, and that the Torah (which he calls the Tanakh) should still be observed. He contrasts this with Paul's "faith in Christ Jesus" as the only thing that matters. He says Paul's "Christ Jesus" is "a carbon copy of Paul himself" (summarising Eisenman's argument), and that Jesus called himself Yeshua, probably "the Messiah," or "the son of man" (Ben Adam). Scott points out that this is his reading and that Jesus did not use the name "Christ Jesus."

### 5. Action over faith: James against Paul
Scott says the "core tenant" of James, in direct contrast to Paul, is that "action matters over faith." He says James's concern was the ebionites (from the Hebrew for "poor ones"), and that viewers should read the Epistle of James, which he says has been pushed to the back of the New Testament behind Paul's letters. He says Jude, another brother, was similarly put at the back. He also claims that Sunday observance was linked to sun-god worship, and that the Sabbath (which he says Jesus kept from Friday night to Saturday night) was replaced when "Gentile Christianity came and Paul took over."

### 6. The Nazarite vow and James's diet
Scott says the words "Nazarene" and "Nazarite" share a root meaning "to branch off" or "consecrate oneself." He cites Numbers for the vow: uncut hair, no wine or grapes, and no contact with dead bodies. He says Samson, John the Baptist and James were Nazarites, and that James was a vegetarian. Scott says he has taken the Nazarite vow himself, that he is also vegetarian, and that the vow is "a recommendation," which he suggests people try for a month ("Nazarite January").

### 7. Paul's letters are not sacred scripture
Scott says the letters of Paul "are not the inherent word of God," because they were written by a person, contain errors and contradictions, and should be seen as external context rather than scripture. He also criticises literalist Christians in the "Bible Belt" who oppose homosexuality, using Paul's letters to justify their views. He describes Paul as having "preached his own concoction of a religion" that later "snowballed," and says that Irenaeus, a church father whom he calls "rabidly anti-Semitic" (citing Eisenman, p. 113), contributed to this.

- **Miracle stories make a text fiction.** Scott says the Gospels contain miracles too (virgin birth, walking on water), so miracles alone do not separate fiction from history.
- **The Gospels are firsthand eyewitness accounts.** Scott says Matthew, Mark and John were probably not their named authors, and that Luke was not an eyewitness.
- **Paul's letters are the inspired word of God.** Scott says they are human writings with errors and contradictions.
- **Faith in Christ saves.** Scott says James taught that action matters more than faith, and that Paul's "Christ Jesus" faith is not what saves.
- **Sunday is the Christian day of worship, and the Sabbath is outdated.** Scott says Sunday observance came from pagan sun-worship and the Sabbath should be kept.
- **Jesus called himself "Christ Jesus."** Scott says Jesus spoke Aramaic and called himself Yeshua, probably "the Messiah," or "the son of man."
