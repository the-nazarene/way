## Summary

Scott, presenting for the Nazarene Way church, asks whom Paul's letters should be measured against: the people who walked with Jesus, including his brother James and the Jerusalem community. He argues that those followers would not have accepted Paul's letters or the later New Testament canon, which he says was selected by Hellenistic Greeks in the second and third centuries. In his view, Jesus would have read the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), and the Dead Sea Scrolls are the closest surviving witness to that text. Beyond that, he proposes that the Nazarenes used a Gospel of the Hebrews, which he describes through Epiphanius as roughly Matthew without its first two chapters. He argues those chapters, with the Davidic genealogy and the Nativity story, were added later to answer objections about the Messiah's birthplace and lineage. He also says Jesus' message centered on "the Kingdom of Heaven is near" and repentance by water baptism, and that James taught that action matters more than faith.

Scott's main evidence is a reading of Robert Eisenman's *James the Brother of Jesus*, which he cites by page (146 and 187). He also relies on Acts and Paul's own letters, which he says show conflict with Peter and with James and the "three pillars," and on Epiphanius's account of the Nazarenes. He points to Josephus's silence on Nazareth and to a Hebrew Matthew he says he owns, though he gives no edition or date beyond "like in the 1800s." He presents several of these points as established, and several are his own inferences or attributions to sources he does not quote directly.

The talk ends with an ethical appeal: Scott tells viewers to treat poor and homeless people with love and to give to them, without judging how the money will be used, and he presents this as the core of Jesus' and James's teaching. He closes by saying his faith-affirming message should strengthen rather than shatter viewers' faith.

## Key Teachings

### The canon Jesus and his followers would have used
Scott argues that the "New Testament" did not exist in Jesus' time and was assembled in the second and third centuries by Hellenistic Greeks, "formerly pagans." He says the closest canon to Jesus is the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh) and, for an even closer witness, the Dead Sea Scrolls. He recommends the Hebrew Bible over the "Catholic flavored version" of the Old Testament.

### Epiphanius and the Gospel of the Hebrews
Scott cites the fourth-century Church Father Epiphanius, whom he describes as calling the Nazarenes "heretics." He says Epiphanius reported that the Nazarenes had a gospel, the Gospel of the Hebrews, which he describes as "essentially the gospel of Matthew minus the first two chapters." He infers from this that Jesus' closest relatives and followers used this text. He notes Epiphanius's account is unclear about how the group branched into a heretical sect.

### Matthew's first two chapters as later additions
Scott dates the Greek Matthew "around 80 to 90 CE," about 50 to 60 years after Jesus' death, and argues this gave time for objections about the Messiah. In his reading, one objection was that the Messiah had to be born in Bethlehem, and another was that the Messiah had to be of David's royal line. He says the Davidic genealogy and the Nativity story (the census, the Magi, the Bethlehem birth, and the Immaculate Conception) were invented to answer these objections. He also says there is no evidence of the census he describes and that Matthew and Luke contradict each other on the Nativity.

### Jesus' message: the Kingdom and water baptism
Scott contends that Jesus' message was not a mission to the Gentiles. He says the Hebrew Gospel of Matthew lacks the Great Commission-type passage about going into the world, which he calls a "Gentile Pauline idea" added later. In his account, Jesus' message was "the Kingdom of Heaven is near, repent," with repentance expressed through water baptism, continuing John the Baptist's message at the Jordan River.

### Paul versus James, Peter, and the Jerusalem leadership
Scott says Paul's own letters and Acts show conflict with the apostles: Paul "gets into a fight with Peter," "butts heads" with them, and "gripes about James" and the "three pillars" of the Jerusalem church. He draws on Eisenman (p. 146) for the claim that Paul was mocked inside the church as a "man of dreams, lies or the enemy." He draws on Eisenman (p. 187) for the claim that James the Just was chosen as leader of the Jerusalem church over Peter and John.

### Paul's conversion and the Judas Thomas account
Scott describes a lost or non-canonical account, which he does not name, in which Paul's conversion came through the encounter with Jude, "James's brother" also called Judas Thomas. He says this account conflicts with Paul's own claim to have met Jesus on the road to Damascus about seven years after the crucifixion. He says Paul's "encounter" included a white light, blindness, companions, and a voice that others did not hear. Scott gives no source for the Judas Thomas account beyond the mention of other gospels.

### James: works over faith, and serving the poor
Scott says James taught that "action matters more than faith," and he contrasts this with a "Faith virus" he attributes to Paul's "Christ Jesus" teaching. He says the core teaching of Jesus and James centered on the *ebionim*, "the poor ones," and concludes with the practical appeal to give to poor and needy people and to treat them as human beings.

- **Paul's gospel is the message of Jesus.** Scott argues the Gentile mission, "faith in Christ Jesus," and the Great Commission-type passage were Pauline additions, not Jesus' teaching. He says Jesus' message was the Kingdom near and repentance by water baptism.
- **Faith alone saves.** Scott contrasts Paul's faith teaching with James's statement that action matters more than faith.
- **The Nativity stories are historical.** Scott argues the first two chapters of Matthew were added later to answer objections about the Messiah's birthplace and lineage, and he says there is no evidence for the census described.
- **Peter is the rock of the church's papal succession.** Scott says that James, not Peter, was the Jerusalem leader and that the Catholic line traced to Peter erases James.
