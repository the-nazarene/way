## Summary

Scott asks whether Paul of Tarsus, not Jesus, was responsible for the teaching that became Christianity, and whether Paul was involved in the death of James, the brother of Jesus and leader of the Jerusalem community. He frames the talk as a continuation of his reading of Robert Eisenman's *James the Brother of Jesus*, and cites page 210 of that book. His main evidence is a passage from the Pseudo-Clementine literature (14:03 to 17:14 in the video), which he reads aloud. In it, an unnamed enemy of James disrupts a gathering at the Temple, incites a riot, and throws James down the steps. Scott identifies that enemy as Paul, using the name "Saul" that appears in the text he reads. He argues the Clementine account is at least as historical-sounding as the Gospels, and that its version of James's death may be more accurate than the accounts of Hegesippus and Josephus. He concludes with "where there's smoke there's fire": he believes the story did not come from nowhere, though he offers no independent dating or corroboration beyond his reading.

Scott also argues that Paul preached a different gospel from the one taught by Jesus and by those who knew him, and that the New Testament is "the Bible of Paul" rather than the Bible of Jesus. He ties this to the Virgin birth, which he calls a later invention, and to the claim that action matters more than faith, which he takes from the letter of James.

The talk ends with an exhortation to live as Jesus, James and the apostles did, caring for "the ebionites, the poor ones." The sermon is delivered entirely by Scott, with no guest or host.

## Key Teachings

### Paul as the enemy of James (Pseudo-Clementine reading)
Scott's central argument rests on a passage he reads from the Pseudo-Clementine literature. In it, an unnamed opponent interrupts a baptismal gathering at the Temple, stirs a crowd, and attacks James, who is refuting him. The enemy seizes a brand from the altar, a tumult breaks out, and the enemy "threw him headlong from the top of the steps and supposing him to be dead he cared not to inflict further violence upon him." Scott says the enemy is Paul, here called Saul. He adds that Galatians and Acts record conflict between Paul and James, and that Paul was reprimanded. Scott's reasoning from the text is that "where there's smoke there's fire."

### Why the Clementine account might be preferred
Scott says Eisenman observes that Acts of the Apostles and the Pseudo-Clementines draw on overlapping material, possibly a shared source, and that the Clementine version looks more accurately preserved. Scott describes the Clementines as probably written around the third century ("like the 200s"), and he suggests that a later Church may have tried to set them aside as historical sources. He says Eisenman places the account of James's attack and death in the Clementines above the accounts of Hegesippus and Josephus. Scott also says there are "external sources" saying James was thrown from the Temple, and he names Josephus for this. The transcript garbles the names here, but the context identifies them as Hegesippus and Josephus.

### Paul versus Jesus
Scott argues that Paul's gospel differed from Jesus's teaching. He says Paul "never met" Jesus, despite claiming a vision of him "seven years after Jesus's death," and that Paul "invented" Gentile Christianity. He presents the "two most important Commandments" (to love God with all one's heart, soul and mind, and to love one's neighbour as oneself) as Jesus's core teaching, and says Jesus upheld the Ten Commandments and the Torah. He refers to an earlier sermon in which he lists "the great contradictions" between Paul and Jesus. He does not quote Paul's letters in this talk.

### The Virgin birth and Mary as late developments
Scott argues that the Virgin birth is absent from Mark, from Paul's letters and from John, and that it was "a later incantation to try to fulfill incorrectly a reading of Isaiah 7:14." He says the doctrine of Mary's perpetual virginity comes from the apocryphal Ascent of James, and he asks why Catholics treat a non-canonical book as the source of a core belief. He also notes that the Gospel of the Hebrews, which he describes as Matthew without its first two chapters, lacked the genealogy and the Nativity story. He reads that as evidence that the Virgin birth is a later invention.

### Action over faith
Scott says that the Nazarene Way holds that "action matters over faith," and he draws on the letter of James to support this. He contrasts this with a view he attributes to contemporary Christians, that one can sin and then be forgiven on the strength of a confession of faith. He does not quote the verses of James in this talk.

### Matthew and the Torah
Scott says that Jesus "did not come to abolish the Torah" but to fulfil it, citing Matthew. He presents this as evidence that Jesus kept the law, and he contrasts Jesus with Paul on the same point. He also asserts that the yarmulke (kippah) was created in the third to fifth centuries, after Jesus's death.
