## Summary

Scott, presenting a sermon-style video for the Nazarene Way channel, argues that James the Just, the brother of Jesus, not Peter, was the first leader of Jesus's movement and so should be called the "original pope." He also argues that the Hebrew Bible was Jesus's only scripture, and that Paul's teaching on faith departs from the message of James. He draws mainly on Robert Eisenman's *James the Brother of Jesus*, which he says he is working through, along with Eusebius's *Ecclesiastical History*, Epiphanius's heresy catalogue, and the Gospel of Thomas. The talk opens with a reading of the parable of the mustard seed (Matthew 13:31) as a picture of humble beginnings, and Scott connects this to his small congregation. He also reads a passage from Matthew and discusses the Gospel of the Hebrews as a source for the earliest Nazarenes and Ebionites.

Scott's case rests on what the church writers say about James: that Eusebius calls him "the brother of the Lord," places him over Peter and John in Jerusalem, and that Acts names James, Peter and John as the Jerusalem pillars. He also reads the Galatians and Acts accounts of Paul's dispute with James as evidence that James had authority over Paul. The talk is mostly Scott speaking; no guest appears. It ends with an exhortation to read the New Testament as a "helpful literary text" and to take the Hebrew Bible as the canon. Several of Scott's claims, including the dating of the yarmulke, the Nazirite vow imposed on Paul, and the claim that Jesus never called himself "Christ Jesus," are presented without citation.

## Key Teachings

### James as the first leader, not Peter
Scott argues that James the Just was the leader of the Jerusalem church and of the early followers of Jesus. He cites Eisenman (p. 156) for the claim that "there can be little doubt" James led the Ebionites, and he says Eusebius records James being chosen as bishop of Jerusalem. He cites Eisenman (p. 187) for the claim that Eusebius says James was chosen over Peter and John. Scott describes Peter as James's "right-hand man" and John as his "left-hand man." From this he concludes that "Peter was not the first pope," and that Catholic claims of apostolic lineage through Peter trace back to "the wrong Peter."

### "Brother of the Lord" as a title for James alone
Scott notes that Eusebius and "others" call James "the brother of the Lord" and, in his view, apply this title to no one else, not Peter, John, or even Jude (also a brother of Jesus in Scott's reading). He acknowledges that the New Testament uses "my brothers and my sisters" in other senses. He presents the title's exclusivity as evidence that James's status was specific and historically recognized.

### Acts, Galatians and Paul's submission to James
Scott says Acts describes "the three pillars of Jerusalem" as James, Peter and John. He reads Galatians and Acts as showing Paul summoned back to Jerusalem to answer to James. In Scott's telling, James required Paul to take a Nazirite vow (shaving the head, letting the hair grow, abstaining from wine, avoiding contact with the dead) as the way to repent. Scott says this vow was also taken by Samson, James the Just, and John the Baptist. He gives no verse for the vow or for Samson, and the account of Paul's Nazirite vow is his own reading, not a passage he quotes.

### Faith versus action in the letter of James
Scott contrasts the Epistle of James with Paul. He says Paul's message centers on faith in "Christ Jesus" as the only route to salvation, while James teaches that "faith is inferior to one thing and that is action." He presents this contrast as the reason later readers should prefer James, and he says that "psychopaths" who use Paul's faith-alone message to justify their views have misread the New Testament.

### The Hebrew Bible as Jesus's canon, and the New Testament as Hellenized
Scott argues that Jesus's canon was the Tanakh, the Hebrew Bible. He says the New Testament is Greek, that 13 of its 27 books are from Paul, and that the texts show anti-Jewish bias (he cites Pilate's handwashing as an example). He asks: "What was Jesus's canon? What was his Bible? What was his text? It's called the Tanakh." He recommends reading the New Testament "as if it's a helpful literary text," with historical and critical method.

### Matthew, the Gospel of the Hebrews, and the Nazarenes and Ebionites
Scott says Matthew is "the most Jewish" of the four canonical gospels and therefore closest to Jesus's followers. He describes Epiphanius (4th century, in his *Panarion*, which he calls "80 heresies") as calling the Nazarenes heretics, and he says Epiphanius's attacks are the reason we know about these groups at all. He says the earliest Jewish believers used a Gospel of the Hebrews resembling Matthew but without its first two chapters, which he calls invented to answer objections about Bethlehem. He also says the Nazarenes and Ebionites shared this text, and that it lacked a passage at the end of Matthew found in a later Hebrew version. He does not name that passage.

### Humble beginnings (the mustard seed)
Scott opens with the parable of the mustard seed from Matthew 13:31. He reads it as a lesson that great things start small. He applies this to his own channel, saying he is not promoting it through his larger channels and is growing it slowly. He contrasts this with the modern demand to "launch fast."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Peter as the first pope or leader of the church.** Scott says the first leader was James, and that Catholic claims of apostolic lineage through Peter rest on a mistake.
- **Jesus had no brothers, or James was just one disciple.** Scott says James was the "brother of the Lord" in Eusebius's account, and that this title is used of no one else.
- **Faith alone is what matters.** Scott says the Epistle of James teaches that action matters more than faith, and that Paul's faith-alone teaching has been misused.
- **The New Testament is Jesus's Bible.** Scott says Jesus's Bible was the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), and that the New Testament is a Greek, partly anti-Jewish text to be read critically.
- **Yarmulkes and clerical dress were part of early practice.** Scott says covering the head in prayer was the practice of James and Jesus, but that the yarmulke did not appear until a later date, which he gives as between about 300 and 500 CE. He presents this without citation.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Scott's main argument is that Paul's faith-centered message conflicts with the action-centered teaching of James, which he treats as closer to Jesus.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Scott argues that James led the Jerusalem church, was recognized over Peter and John, and had authority over Paul. The evidence he cites is Eusebius, Eisenman and Acts/Galatians.
- **The Nazarenes/Ebionites/Essenes:** Scott says the earliest Jewish believers were called Nazarenes and Ebionites and used a Gospel of the Hebrews. His source for this is Epiphanius, whom he presents as a hostile witness.
- **Torah, sacrifice, diet and nonviolence:** Scott mentions the Nazirite vow and Paul being told not to be circumcised or follow the laws of Moses, but does not develop a position on Torah observance beyond that.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Scott says Matthew is closest to Jesus's followers, that the Gospel of the Hebrews lacked the first two chapters of Matthew, and that the New Testament canon, in his view, reflects Greek and anti-Jewish influence.
