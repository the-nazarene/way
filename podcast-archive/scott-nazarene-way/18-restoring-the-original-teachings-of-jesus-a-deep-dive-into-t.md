## Summary

Scott, presenting a sermon for Nazarene Way Church, asks what Bible and text a person should follow to "restore" the teachings of Jesus the Nazarene. He argues that Jesus and his first followers used the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh) and a Hebrew Gospel of Matthew that is closer to a Hebrew or Aramaic source than the Greek Gospel. His main evidence is a Hebrew Matthew said to have been found in the library of the 14th-century rabbi Shem Tob, which he says shows signs of a Semitic source rather than being a simple translation from Greek. He also claims that the Gospel of the Hebrews, used by James and the early community, was Matthew minus its first two chapters, citing the church father Epiphanius for this.

The main text under study is the parable of the lost sheep (Matthew 18), read from the Hebrew Matthew. Scott contrasts God's pursuit of the one lost sheep with utilitarian ethics (the trolley problem) and a Costco-lost-child analogy, and reads the parable as meaning that the lost person must "let God find you." He presents the Sabbath on Saturday as Jesus' practice, says Paul and later Greeks shifted it to Sunday, and treats the virgin birth as a later addition based on a mistranslation of Isaiah 7:14. The talk ends with practical advice: buy George Howard's translation, cut out the first two chapters of Matthew, and pair it with Robert Alter's Tanakh. Scott closes with "peace and love" and a Shabbat greeting. Throughout, he presents his dating and textual claims as his own reading, and he gives little detailed evidence for them within the talk.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The Hebrew Gospel of Matthew as a witness to an earlier source
Scott says a Hebrew Matthew was discovered in the 14th century in the library of Shem Tob, a Jewish rabbi. He reports that scholars found "signs and interesting indications that perhaps this Hebrew Gospel of Matthew relied on a Hebrew or Aramaic source" and was not merely a Hebrew translation of the Greek. He points to differences in the ending: the Hebrew text lacks the "you must go and baptize and make all you know nations Christian" language found in the canonical Greek Matthew. He treats this as evidence of a less evangelistic original. He gives no further detail on the manuscript's dating or transmission.

### 2. The Gospel of the Hebrews as Matthew minus the first two chapters
Scott states that "the gospel of the Hebrews is essentially the gospel of Matthew" without its first two chapters. He says this is "what we believe," citing the church father Epiphanius. He demonstrates his method by saying he cut out those chapters "with a essentially a razor blade," leaving the Hebrew Matthew joined to the Tanakh as the group's working canon. He says the Gospel of the Hebrews was used by James and the disciples, and he also lists the Gospels of the Ebionites and Nazarenes as related texts. He presents these identifications as the group's position.

### 3. Torah, the Sabbath, and Paul
Scott argues that Jesus said he "did not come to abolish the Torah" but to fulfill it, and that Jesus continued to observe the Sabbath on Saturday. He says the Sabbath was on the seventh day, "which is Saturday," and that Sunday was adopted after "an individual named Paul" and "the early Greeks" merged the traditions. He also states that James the Just observed a head covering, which he ties to Leviticus, and that the yarmulke was a later invention "between the 3rd and fifth centuries." He gives no sources for the Sabbath-to-Sunday shift in this talk.

### 4. "Son of man" versus "son of God"
Using the Hebrew Matthew 18 reading, Scott says the phrase "son of man" appears where many Greek versions have none. He says Jesus "referred to himself as son of man" and that the idea that Jesus repeatedly called himself the Son of God is a popular misunderstanding. He ties this to his claim that the Hebrew text is "truer to the source of the Semitic source."

### 5. The parable of the lost sheep: God's love beyond utilitarian logic
Scott reads Matthew 18 (the Hebrew version): God leaves the 99 to seek the one that strays, and "rejoice[s] over it more than the 99." He argues that this contradicts utilitarian reasoning, illustrated by the trolley problem (one death is better than 99). He says "God is beyond any logical thought experiment that you want to throw at him," and gives the Costco example of a parent who searches for a lost child rather than leaving. He notes that Jesus uses sheep, not warriors or David, because sheep are "docile," "helpless," and "lost." His reading is that "it's not that you need to find God. It's that you need to let God find you."

### 6. Virgin birth as a mistranslation
Scott says the Greek version of Isaiah 7:14 mistranslated the word for a young woman as "virgin," which he says led to a later story being "prepended" to Matthew 1-2. He argues that the virgin birth is absent from Paul's letters, from Mark (which he calls the earliest written gospel), and from John, and that it appears only in the Greek Matthew and in Luke. He says he thinks the Gospel of the Hebrews and "a list of sayings" were written earlier than Mark, but he gives no evidence for that date.

### 7. The Old Testament and the shape of the canon
Scott claims the Old Testament, the New Testament, and the Catholic canon were largely "invented" around the 3rd to 5th centuries by "helanized" Greeks and Romans. He presents the Tanakh (Torah, Prophets, Writings) as the only canon Jesus used. He also dismisses Luke as "essentially the secretary and fanboy of Paul," without elaborating.
