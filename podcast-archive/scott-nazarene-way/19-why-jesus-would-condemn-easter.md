## Summary

Rabbi Scott asks why Jesus, if he were alive today, would not celebrate Easter and would probably condemn it. He argues that Easter has pagan roots and was created at the Council of Nicaea in the fourth century to separate Christians from Jewish practice. He contrasts it with Passover, which he says Jesus observed, including at the Last Supper. The second half of the sermon argues that the bodily resurrection of Jesus was a later myth. His evidence is that Paul describes a "spiritual body" in 1 Corinthians 15:44, and that the earliest manuscripts of Mark end at 16:8 with no appearance of Jesus. Scott also says that the Nazarenes, James, and the Ebionites held a spiritual rather than physical resurrection, though he gives no source for the claim about James.

Scott presents these positions as his own reading. Several are stated with more certainty than the talk supports: he says "all scholars" agree on Mark's ending and that Easter comes from the goddess Eostre, but names no scholar or source for either claim. The sermon closes with a call to celebrate Passover instead of Easter, to stop teaching children that Easter is a Christian holiday, and to follow "action over faith" in the manner he attributes to James. He invites viewers to subscribe and join the church community.

## Key Teachings

### Easter as a later, anti-Jewish invention
Scott says Easter was "created in the 4th century at the council of Nika" (his spelling for Nicaea) by the church fathers, whom he calls "petristics" (patristics). In his account, they wanted to separate from Judean and Jewish practice, so they replaced Passover with a pagan holiday he calls "eostre." He describes this as the "early days of the anti-semitism of the Catholic Church." He gives no source for the Council of Nicaea's motives or for the Eostre derivation.

### Idolatry and the Ten Commandments
Scott argues that Easter's symbols, especially the Easter bunny, are idols, and that worshipping them breaks the commandment against idols. He says he is "all for having fun and... Easter egg hunts," but objects to presenting Easter as a Christian holiday that Jesus would have kept. He calls Easter "an American holiday" and "a Roman holiday."

### Passover as the festival Jesus kept
Scott says Passover commemorates liberation from Egyptian slavery and a return to God's law (Torah). He says Jesus and his earliest followers kept Passover, and that the Last Supper was a Passover meal. He reads the Last Supper and Jesus's return to Jerusalem on a donkey as deliberate symbolism of freedom from Rome and a return to God, though he does not cite a text for the donkey detail. He says Jesus would want people to "break away from the American Easter holiday" and return to the Torah.

### The Nazarene canon
Scott says the Nazarenes use the Tanakh (Torah, Prophets, and Writings) as their Bible, plus the Gospel of the Nazarenes or Hebrews. He says they use the Hebrew Gospel of Matthew, minus its first two chapters, because those chapters are missing from Mark, which he treats as the earliest gospel. He says Matthew 1-2 "are likely... mythologized prepended things." He says Matthew's Gospel has possible problems at its ending, which he plans to address in a later sermon. He describes Paul's letters as "an interesting historical document" rather than scripture, and names the "pillars of Jerusalem" as James, Peter, and John, reading Paul's remarks about them as critical.

### Spiritual, not bodily, resurrection
Scott's main argument is that the bodily resurrection was a later myth. He makes four points:
- Paul's 1 Corinthians 15:44 says the body is "sown" as a physical body and "raised a spiritual body," and Paul's encounter on the road to Damascus was a light or apparition, not a touch of the physical Jesus.
- Mark's earliest manuscripts end at 16:8 with no appearance of Jesus, and he says all scholars agree on this (no citation given).
- Only eyewitnesses such as Mary, James, and the disciples could know, and they are dead, so everyone else is "agnostic" in the literal sense of "without knowledge."
- Scott says that Jesus, Paul, James, the Nazarenes, and the Ebionites all held a spiritual resurrection, and that the teachings of Jesus "live on through us spiritually."

### Rejecting salvation by belief in the physical resurrection
Scott rejects the idea that salvation depends on believing in a physical resurrection, which he says makes believers threaten others with hell. He says "Jesus never preached that" and that neither Paul nor the disciples preached it. He attributes the doctrine to later church fathers.
