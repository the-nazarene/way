## Summary

Scott, presenting a sermon for the Nazarene Way Church channel, argues that John the Baptist, Jesus, and James the Just were Nazirites and vegetarians, and that the standard account of John eating "locusts and honey" is a mistranslation. He presents the Nazarene way as the original religion of Jesus, to be restored in place of what he calls a Gentile, Roman-shaped Christianity. The main evidence is a quotation from Robert Eisenman's *James the Brother of Jesus* (p. 332) calling the locust diet "improbable and even insulting," the claim that the Ebionites held John ate honey cakes, and a Greek-spelling argument that the words for "locusts" and "honey cakes" are close enough to be confused. He also cites Epiphanius's fourth-century *Panarion* as the source for the Nazarene and Ebionite groups, and the lost Gospel of the Hebrews, Gospel of the Nazarenes, and Gospel of the Ebionites, which survive only in fragments.

Scott's conclusions are practical as well as historical. He says "action matters more than faith," drawing on the letter of James; calls for material help to the poor; and says he has adopted a vegetarian diet and the Nazirite vow (no wine or strong drink, no funerals, uncut hair) for about nine months and six months respectively. He recommends that viewers start small and adopt one practice. He also says the Nazarenes reject violent resistance and practice "anti-accommodationism" through love, and that they observe the Sabbath from Friday sundown to Saturday sundown.

The talk ends with a "call to arms" to subscribe and join a "revolution to restore the way of the Nazarines." Scott himself carries the whole talk, with no guest. Many of his claims are his own readings, offered without citing the primary text for most of them. The talk also contains several factual assertions (for example, that the Jewish label came from the Middle Ages, and that the yarmulke was not invented until the third to fifth centuries) that he does not source.

## Key Teachings

### 1. John the Baptist was a lifelong Nazirite and likely a vegetarian
Scott describes a lifelong Nazirite as someone who never cuts their hair, never drinks wine or strong drink, and never goes near dead bodies. He says James the Just was also a lifelong Nazirite and a lifelong virgin, "and took it to the extreme," and that James, like many Nazirites of the day, was a vegetarian. From this he argues that John, and probably Jesus, were also vegetarians. He reasons that if John, Jesus, and James are the first three leaders of the movement (in his order: John, then Jesus, then James), a shared Nazirite and vegetarian practice is probable. He says "I'll let you decide that."

### 2. The "locusts and honey" diet is a mistranslation
Scott cites Eisenman (p. 332) calling the locust-and-honey diet "improbable and even insulting." He reports that the Ebionites held that John ate honey cakes, not insects. His linguistic argument is that the Greek words for locust and for honey cake are phonetically very similar, so a scribe taking dictation could have confused them. He illustrates this with a joke about the film *Big Daddy*. He also says James Tabor has discussed certain pea plants and manna-like honey cakes as a possible source for the phrase. He treats the honey-cake reading as "approximately correct," not as proven.

### 3. Action over faith, drawn from the letter of James
Scott says the third "tenant" of the Nazarene way is James's teaching that action matters more than faith. He reads James's focus on "the poor ones" (which he equates with the Ebionites) as a call for concrete help. He says the Nazarene Way Church gives food, cash, and care to the poor, and he tells a story of refusing to judge a homeless man asking for money and giving him "money and a hug" instead. He also says that the early church fathers, in the third and fourth centuries, argued that James should not be in the New Testament because it was "so Jewish in nature."

### 4. The lost Gospel of the Nazarenes and a stripped Hebrew Matthew as the working canon
Scott says the earliest Nazarene gospel was a version of Matthew without its first two chapters, and that the Gospel of the Hebrews, the Gospel of the Nazarenes, and the Gospel of the Ebionites are now lost, surviving only in fragments and in quotations by critics such as Epiphanius. He says he has physically cut the first two chapters out of a Hebrew Matthew, and that the group's working canon is the Tanakh plus that Hebrew Matthew. He calls this "an index fund" (borrowing a Warren Buffett comparison) and says it is not a fixed canon. He also says the group reads apocryphal and pseudo-Clementine material, and Gnostic-tinged texts such as the Gospel of Thomas, with discernment.

### 5. Reading Paul critically
Scott says Paul's letters, which he dates to the 50s CE, are useful not as authoritative word but as evidence, read "between the lines." He cites Galatians and its reference to the "so-called pillars of Jerusalem" (James, Peter, and John) to argue that Paul and James were in conflict. He says Paul "invented his own helanized pagan influenced religion" and that the figure of Christ Jesus is "a carbon copy of Paul himself," attributing this view to Robert Eisenman. He says Paul was wrongly thought by the Romans to be the ringleader of the Nazarenes.

### 6. Nonviolent, "anti-accommodationist" resistance and Saturday Sabbath
Scott says Jesus' stance was anti-accommodationist, meaning resistance to Rome and to compromise with power, but not through militant force. He contrasts this with figures he calls "failed messiahs," naming Bar Kokba. He says the group fights through "walking the extra mile in their armor," showing love. He also says Jesus and the Nazarenes observed the Sabbath from Friday sundown to Saturday sundown, not on Sunday.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **John the Baptist ate locusts and honey.** Scott says this is probably a textual error for "honey cakes," based on Eisenman and the Ebionite tradition as Epiphanius reports it.
- **The Nazarenes are just another heretical sect.** Scott argues that Epiphanius's label of "heresy" was propaganda, and that the Nazarenes and Ebionites were the original followers of Jesus.
- **The New Testament's Paul speaks for Jesus.** Scott says Paul's letters should be read critically, and that Paul invented a Gentile religion distinct from Jesus' and James's.
- **The yarmulke and the term "Jew" have ancient roots.** Scott says the yarmulke was not invented until roughly the third to fifth centuries, and that "Jew" (short for Judean) emerged in the Middle Ages. He hedges on the etymology of the head-covering.
- **Sunday is the Christian Sabbath.** Scott says Jesus observed the Sabbath from Friday to Saturday sundown, "not on some pagan Roman Sunday."
- **Nazirite and vegetarian practices are unrelated to Jesus.** Scott argues that Jesus, like John and James, probably kept the Nazirite vow and a vegetarian diet.
