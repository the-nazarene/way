## Summary

Scott, presenting a sermon for Nazarene Way Church, asks what Jesus actually taught and how his message should be lived today. His main text is Matthew 10:34-36, where Jesus says he came "not to bring peace... but a sword." Scott reads the "sword" as metaphorical, a moral and spiritual division that can set a person against their own family, and he ties it to the claim that Jesus's brothers, including James the Just, did not accept him at first. He presents the Hebrew Bible (the Tanakh, which he equates with the Torah) as the scripture Jesus used, and he recommends George Howard's *Hebrew Gospel of Matthew*, which he says he modified by cutting out its first two chapters, as a starting point for study.

Scott's broader argument is that later Christianity, especially what he calls "Pauline Christianity," departed from Jesus's teaching. He contrasts Jesus's emphasis on action with Paul's emphasis on faith, presents God as a being to be feared as well as loved ("God is not love"), and says that Jesus's "sword" was practical care for the marginalized rather than violence. He also argues that the doctrine of the Trinity was coined by Tertullian in the second century, and that Catholic practice has drifted from the one God. Scott offers no manuscripts or scholarly sources for the Tertullian claim or for his account of the Gospel of the Ebionites within the talk.

The talk ends with practical application: Scott says that following "the Way" may bring division, such as rejecting Sunday traditions for Sabbath observance, leaving churches that "preach Paul over Jesus," or changing diet and way of life. The sermon closes with a Sabbath greeting. The talk is entirely Scott speaking; there is no guest or second voice.

## Key Teachings

### Jesus's "sword" is moral and spiritual division (Matthew 10:34-36)
Scott reads Matthew 10:34-36 as Jesus describing family division, with a man set against his father and a daughter against her mother. He says the sword is "metaphorical," not literal, and links it to a Hebrew word (*netzer*, the root of "Nazarene") that he glosses as splitting off. He suggests Jesus's biological brothers rejected his messianic claim, and that the division Jesus describes comes from following God's principles over family loyalty.

### Jesus's weapon was action, not force
Scott contrasts Jesus with failed messiahs of the first and second centuries (he names Judas the Galilean, Simon, and Bar Kokhba) who "fought with a physical sword." Jesus, he says, fought "by healing and touching the people that no one else wanted to touch," washing feet, and caring for lepers, the homeless, and prostitutes. He criticizes "the Catholics" as modern Pharisees who give "lip service to the poor."

### Torah and the Hebrew Bible as the true canon
Scott says Jesus's Bible was the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), which he calls "the canonical Bible that Jesus himself followed," and he says Jesus read it "with a critical eye." He glosses the Torah as the first five books of Moses. He cites Jesus's statement in Matthew that he came "to fulfill the Torah," though the talk gives no chapter or verse for it.

### Action over faith: James against Pauline Christianity
Scott names James the Just, Jesus's biological brother, as having said that action matters more than faith, and he says "Pauline Christianity puts faith on a pedestal." He claims that Paul's words, lined up against the "red letters" of Jesus, contradict Jesus. No specific Pauline or Jesus verses are cited for this comparison.

### God is to be feared, not reduced to love
In the sermon's longest theological passage, Scott argues that modern Christians "anthropomorphize" God into "a lovey lovey deity," and that reading the Hebrew Bible shows God as "love and fire" who brings justice. He says "God is not love" and "you cannot reduce him to love." He ties this to the first two commandments and attacks veneration of Mary, saints, and popes.

### The Trinity and Jesus's identity
Scott says Jesus called himself "Benadam," the son of man, and "did not refer to himself as God himself." He says the Trinity was "actually invented by a guy named Tertullian in the second century," who coined the Latin term *trinitas*, and he claims Tertullian later had a "deathbed conversion" back toward "the Nazarene way." The talk offers no source for the Tertullian claims.

### Prophetic opposition to sacrifice and temple corruption
Scott places Jesus in the prophetic tradition of Elijah and Jeremiah. He says Jeremiah fought against how the temple system turned God's law into animal sacrifice "and making money off of that," and he says Jesus "overturn[ed] the tables" and did not attend sacrificial rituals. Scott gives no text reference for the sacrifice claims beyond these prophets' names.

- **Jesus as the "prince of peace" and pacifist:** Scott says Matthew 10:34 is misread if Jesus is taken as a simple man of peace. He reads the sword as moral division.
- **"God is love" as the whole picture of God:** Scott says reducing God to love is a modern Christian distortion; the Hebrew Bible shows God as fearsome and just.
- **The Trinity as original to Jesus:** Scott says the Trinity was coined by Tertullian in the second century, and that Jesus called himself son of man, not God.
- **"Jew" as the original name for Jesus's people:** Scott claims "Judean" is the original term, and that "Jew" emerged in the Middle Ages.
- **Jesus as a Pharisee or Sadducee:** Scott says Jesus was neither; he was a Nazarene Judean who overturned temple tables.
