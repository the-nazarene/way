## Summary

Scott, who leads the Nazarene Way Church, asks what Jesus of Nazareth actually taught and argues that the teaching was lost when a Paul-based, Gentile Christianity took shape and was later absorbed into the Roman Empire. He presents the Tanakh as the only scripture Jesus used and treats the Hebrew Gospel of Matthew, with its first two chapters removed, as the closest surviving text to the Nazarene and Ebionite gospel. He relies mainly on Epiphanius's *Panarion* for knowledge of the Nazarenes and Ebionites, on Robert Eisenman's work on the Dead Sea Scrolls, and on his own reading of Matthew 5-7 and Matthew 7:13-14, with support from James 2:17.

Scott's central claim is that the Sermon on the Mount, read through an anti-Pauline lens, has three pillars: blessing the poor, the Torah is not abolished but fulfilled and still binding, and a righteousness that exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees. He reads "faith without works" in James and Matthew 7:21 as a direct rebuke to Paul's teaching on faith, and he ties the "narrow way" in Matthew 7:13-14 to the "way" language in the Dead Sea Scrolls and in Acts. He presents the nativity story as a later addition meant to answer Jewish objections, and he argues that Jesus would be a Nazarene, not a Christian, if he were alive today.

The talk ends with an appeal to his viewers to join a "Nazarene revival," to watch other channels and documentaries, and to read Eisenman and James Tabor. Scott offers almost no direct textual argument for many of his strongest claims (for example, the claim that Christianity's leaders deliberately burned Nazarene writings, and the figure of "1.2 billion" Christians). Those claims are his, not established results.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The Tanakh as the only scripture Jesus used, and the Nazarene gospel reconstructed from Matthew
Scott says the books Jesus used were the Tanakh (Torah, Prophets, Writings), and that other texts, including the New Testament, the Clementine literature, and the Gospel of Thomas, must be read "with a critical eye." Because the Gospel of the Hebrews, Gospel of the Nazarenes, and Gospel of the Ebionites survive only in fragments, he proposes working from the Hebrew Gospel of Matthew with its first two chapters cut out. He says he has done this himself with a razor blade. He treats the three named Nazarene and Ebionite gospels as very similar, possibly the same text, and mentions a scholar he calls "Bartman" who finds distinctions among them. (The name is garbled in the captions and is not identified here.)

### 2. Nativity and Davidic genealogy as later additions
Scott says the Hebrew gospel lacked the first two chapters, which contain the Davidic genealogy and the nativity. He argues that "most scholars agree" the nativity is absent from Mark and John, and so was probably a later addition meant to answer Pharisaic and Jewish objections. He says the virgin birth rests on a misreading of Isaiah 7:14, which in his view says "a young woman" and is "taken out of context." He states this without naming the scholars he has in mind.

### 3. "Narrow way" and the way of the Dead Sea community
Scott reads Matthew 7:13-14 ("enter through the narrow gate...") as a call to the "way," which he says appears in Acts, in Nazarene self-identification ("people of the way"), and in 1QS 4:20-23 from the Dead Sea Scrolls (cited as "1 QS 4 20-23"). He says Jesus's family, including his brother James the Just, shared Essene-style language about "the righteous." He also says the Dead Sea community was "anti-accommodationist" and opposed the temple as then administered. He states that the Galilean and zealot background of Jesus's family (the Maccabees, Judas the Galilean, and "Jude" as a form of "Judas") shaped Jesus's movement, though he adds that Jesus used love rather than militancy.

### 4. Three pillars of the Sermon on the Mount
Scott names three pillars from Matthew 5-7: (a) "Blessed are the poor," which he links to the Ebionites, "the poor ones"; (b) "I have not come to abolish the Torah. I have come to fulfill it," which he reads as meaning the Torah still binds; and (c) righteousness that must "exceed that of the scribes and Pharisees" (Matthew 5:20). He argues that the Pharisees were in fact righteous and observant but showy, and that the Sadducees were Herodian bureaucrats who took bribes and ran the temple.

### 5. Faith versus works: a direct challenge to Paul
Scott says Matthew 7:21 ("only the one who does the will of my father") and James 2:17 ("Faith without works is dead") reject a gospel of faith alone. He presents Paul as teaching that Jesus's blood sacrifice made salvation automatic through belief, and he says that James, "the brother of the Lord," opposed this in the "so-called pillars" passage in Galatians. He calls for concrete action toward the poor, including touching and feeding homeless people, and he describes this as a "radical action" in his day.

### 6. Christianity as a later invention; Epiphanius and the sources
Scott dates the start of Gentile Christianity to about 100 CE, with Ignatius and Acts as early evidence. He says the term *Christianos* first appears in Antioch. He says the figure of "Christ Jesus" was deified by the fourth century, and that Robert Eisenman showed this figure was modeled on Paul. He relies on Epiphanius's *Panarion* (which he calls the "Penarian") and its 80 heresies as the main source for knowledge of the Nazarenes and Ebionites, and he says Rome burned their writings. He also says Marcion, not an orthodox council, compiled the New Testament canon in the second century.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Christianity as the direct continuation of Jesus's teaching.** Scott says Jesus's teaching was replaced by a Paul-based religion and later a Roman pagan cult.
- **Salvation by faith alone.** Scott says Jesus and James taught that action, not belief alone, is required, and he calls Paul's faith-alone teaching a departure.
- **The virgin birth and Davidic lineage as original.** Scott says both are later additions and that Isaiah 7:14 is misread.
- **Jesus as a Christian.** Scott argues Jesus was a Nazarene Judean whose movement differed from later Christianity.
- **The Pharisees as wicked hypocrites.** Scott says the Pharisees were more righteous and observant than the Sadducees, though he also says they were "showy." He presents this as Jesus's view.
