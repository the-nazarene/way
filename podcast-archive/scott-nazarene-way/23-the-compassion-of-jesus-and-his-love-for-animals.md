## Key Teachings

### 1. Eden and the seed-and-fruit diet (Genesis 1:29)
Scott reads Genesis 1:29 ("every plant yielding seed... and every tree with seed in its fruit, you shall have them for food") and argues that God's original arrangement for humanity was plant-based, "no death, no bloodshed." He reasons that a creator who intended peace among his creatures would not have designed an ecosystem of animals slaughtering one another. He also argues that humans are animals and that the Eden ideal is what Jesus came to restore, not to abolish Torah.

### 2. James the Just as a Nazirite who ate no flesh (Hegesippus via Eusebius)
Scott cites the ancient historian Hegesippus, quoted by Eusebius, as saying James was called "the Just" for his righteousness. He reports that Hegesippus wrote James "did not eat flesh" and was a Nazirite, meaning he did not cut his hair, avoid dead bodies, or drink wine or strong drink. Scott calls James's vegetarianism something "we know... for an absolute fact," then argues that, if James lived this way, Jesus probably did too. The transcript does not show Scott quoting Hegesippus directly, so the words attributed to Hegesippus are Scott's paraphrase and should be checked in Eusebius.

### 3. The Gospel of the Ebionites and Passover meat (Epiphanius)
Scott says the Gospel of the Ebionites survives only in fragments, excerpted by Epiphanius, who then opposed it. He reports that these fragments say Jesus was offered Passover meat and refused it, stating "I have no desire to eat flesh." Scott concedes: "Whether or not this exact statement can be confirmed, it fits the pattern of Yeshua's teachings." He treats this as supporting evidence for a gentle, restorative Jesus, not as proof.

### 4. John the Baptist and "locusts and honey" as a mistranslation
Scott argues that John the Baptist, a Nazirite, did not eat locusts and honey. He proposes that the Greek phrase was a scribal error for "manna cakes" (manna and honey bread), a reading that differs by "just one letter" in his account. He offers no manuscript evidence in the talk. He also says the John the Baptist material appears in the Ebionite gospel, a claim the transcript does not substantiate.

### 5. Pauline Christianity as the real departure
Scott claims Epiphanius labeled Jesus's earliest followers heretical, and says the true "heresy" is the religion built on Paul of Tarsus, which he says Catholicism relies on. He also says the Trinity was invented by Tertullian, who later distanced himself from the church. He describes Catholic ritual as cultic. These claims are polemical, and the transcript provides no citations for the Tertullian story. He attributes the Trinity to Tertullian without evidence in the talk.

### 6. The canon: a Hebrew Matthew and the Tanakh read critically
Scott holds up a Hebrew Gospel of Matthew from which he has cut the first two chapters, saying the early Nazarenes and Ebionites used a version lacking the nativity narrative. He presents the Tanakh as the canon Jesus read, with the Torah, Prophets and Writings, but read with "a critical eye" because scribes and priests had interpolated their own agendas. He uses the two great commandments as the test for what to keep. He also says he expects the lost Gospel of the Hebrews to be found one day and to undermine Petrine claims for Rome, which he thinks are "hidden in plain sight" in favor of James.
