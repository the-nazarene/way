## Key Teachings

### Two ways and the narrow gate
Scott says that Jesus taught two ways, "the narrow way" echoed in Matthew, and the same two-way framework he attributes to the Didache, which he calls "the teachings of the 12 disciples" and "likely an early text." He says finding the gate or learning the way is not enough, and that one must act on Jesus's words. He tells listeners to research the Didache themselves.

### Matthew 7:24 and the house on rock: action over faith
Scott reads Matthew 7:24 ("Everyone then who hears these words of mine and acts on them...will be like a wise man who built his house on rock") and contrasts it with the foolish man who "hears, but he does not do." He argues the passage contains no mention of salvation through faith or through Christ's blood sacrifice. He says the storms (trials and persecutions) will come regardless, so the only protection is acting on Jesus's words. He reads James 1:22 as an implicit rebuke of Paul: "be doers of the word," not "believers." He suggests the book of James was "hidden" in the New Testament and that Martin Luther tried to remove it, though he gives no source for either claim.

### The Nazarenes, Ebionites and the name "Nazarene"
Scott argues that Jesus's followers were called "Nazarenes" externally and "people of the Way" internally. He derives "Nazarene" from the Hebrew root netzer ("branch") and links it to the Nazirite vow in Numbers, rather than to the town of Nazareth. His evidence is that Josephus lists the towns of Galilee and omits Nazareth, which he takes to mean the town was invented later, "maybe around...100, 200 to 300 years after the death of Jesus." He claims the Nazarenes later became or merged into the Ebionites, whom he glosses as "the poor ones." He credits Epiphanius with preserving knowledge of both groups, even though Epiphanius called them heretics.

### Gospel of the Hebrews and the rejection of the birth narratives
Scott says the Nazarenes and Ebionites used a "Gospel of the Hebrews," which he describes as a Hebrew version of Matthew lacking its first two chapters (the genealogy and the nativity story). He argues the virgin birth is a later invention based on mistranslating Isaiah 7:14, and says the earliest gospel (which he identifies as Mark) lists Jesus's brothers and two unnamed sisters. He claims Luke was written by "a Paul of Tarsus fanboy." Scott does not give manuscript evidence for the Gospel of the Hebrews's contents in this talk; he presents its contents as known.

### Canon: Torah, First Enoch and Jude
Scott says Jesus's canon was the Hebrew Scriptures, with the Torah first, and that Jesus quoted First Enoch, which he says is echoed by Jude, Jesus's brother. He states that the group's canon is determined by asking "what canon did Jesus use?" He says the New Testament contains errors and contradictions and is not, in his view, the inherent word of God.

### Vegetarianism and charity to the poor
Scott asserts that the Nazarenes and Ebionites were vegetarian "just like in the Garden of Eden," and that James the Just was a lifelong Nazirite who abstained from alcohol. He says "we know for a fact" James was vegetarian, citing Epiphanius for the Nazarene and Ebionite groups; the talk does not give a source for James's personal diet. He challenges listeners to try a meat-free week and points to Matthew 23:23 (mercy, justice, faithfulness) and to charity to the poor as core practices.

### James the Just, Acts and the house church
Scott presents James the Just as leader of the Jerusalem church, which met in houses, and as the one who told Paul "not to forget the poor" (he cites Acts). He describes his own home as the same kind of house assembly. He also says Jesus's brother James and Jude carried the movement forward, and he identifies the brothers as those listed in Mark 6.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Faith alone saves.** Scott says Jesus taught action over faith and that Paul's gospel of salvation through faith and Christ's blood sacrifice is a later invention; he rests this mainly on Matthew 7:24 and James 1:22.
- **"Nazarene" means "from Nazareth."** Scott argues the title comes from the root netzer and the Nazirite vow, and that Nazareth may not have existed in Jesus's lifetime (based on Josephus's silence).
- **The virgin birth and nativity stories were original.** Scott says they were added later, and that the Jewish Christian gospels lacked them; the Isaiah 7:14 mistranslation argument is his.
- **Christianity follows the Bible as the inherent word of God.** Scott says the New Testament contains errors and contradictions, so the Nazarene canon is instead what Jesus himself used (the Hebrew Scriptures plus First Enoch).
- **Christians hold a legitimate Trinitarian faith.** Scott calls belief in the Father, Son and Holy Spirit idolatry, which he presents as a violation of the first commandment.
