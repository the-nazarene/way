## Summary

Scott, preaching at Nazarene Way Church, asks what the "true message of Jesus" and of his brother James the Just was, and how it differs from what he calls "Pauline Christianity." He argues that James taught action over faith, and he builds the case on a fragment of the Gospel of the Nazarenes, transmitted by Jerome, in which James fasts after Jesus's death until he sees the risen Messiah. Scott presents James as a Torah-observant Nazirite and (citing Epiphanius and Eusebius) a vegetarian, and he uses the Epistle of James to contrast James's emphasis on works with Paul's teaching on faith.

Scott's central move is the resurrection appearance. He says Jesus came to James first, broke bread with him, and said "Eat your bread, for the Son of Man is risen," which he reads as a call to end the fast, rejoin life, and take up a purpose. He also says the early Nazarenes and Ebionites may have understood this as a spiritual appearance rather than a bodily resurrection, and that a flesh-and-bones account may be a later addition. Scott's evidence is mostly church-father reports and the pseudo-Clementine literature, which he dates to the second and third centuries. He presents these as his reading, not as settled history.

The talk ends as a practical exhortation. Scott tells listeners to fast, pray, and act, even in small ways, and to "stay faithful just like James the Just." He closes with "Shabbat shalom." The talk is a single sermon by Scott; no guest or co-host speaks.

## Key Teachings

### The Nazarene canon and the Gospel of the Hebrews
Scott says the Nazarenes kept their own gospel, the Gospel of the Hebrews, which survives only in fragments quoted by church fathers who called the group heretical. He describes this lost gospel as "probably written first and maybe even before Mark." For now, he says, his working canon is the Hebrew Bible (Torah, Prophets, Writings) plus the Hebrew Gospel of Matthew "minus the first two chapters," which he says covered the Davidic line and nativity. He also draws on the Clementine literature, the Homilies, the Didascalia (transcribed in the captions as "dedicay"), the Gospel of Thomas, and, "with a careful eye," the canonical Acts. Scott presents this as a provisional method, not a finished text.

### James the Just as leader of Jerusalem
Scott identifies James as Jesus's eldest brother, citing Mark 6:3 for the list of siblings and calling him "James the brother of the Lord" in Eusebius's phrasing. He says James was not one of the Twelve but became leader of the church after Jesus's death, and he names the three "pillars" of Jerusalem as James, Cephas/Simon Peter, and John, referring to Acts and Galatians. Scott explains the "Just" or "Righteous" title by saying James was Torah-observant and humble. He presents the Jerusalem house church as the model for his own congregation.

### Faith versus action: James against Paul
Scott says the Epistle of James, which he describes as tucked into the back of the Catholic New Testament, "said the exact opposite" of Paul. In his reading, Paul taught that one could "just fart around and you know have faith and be saved," while James said faith matters less than works, and works means real Torah practice and concrete help for the poor. He illustrates with his own story of buying items for homeless people outside a 7-Eleven in San Diego, and he says giving clothes and food is better than giving money. Scott's contrast is rhetorical. He does not quote Galatians or Romans on faith and works.

### James's vow and fast
Scott cites the Gospel of the Nazarenes, through Jerome, for the claim that after Jesus died James "swore not to eat until he saw the risen Messiah." He says James was a Nazirite, a status he links to the no-haircut, no-wine, no-corpse rules, and he compares it to Samson's vow. He also says that Epiphanius and Eusebius report James was a vegetarian. Scott describes the fast as a "vow of mourning" and as James waiting "not for emotion, but for real evidence."

### The appearance to James and "Eat your bread"
Scott says the Gospel of the Hebrews reports that Jesus came to James first, not Peter. Jesus took bread, blessed it, broke it, and gave it to James, which Scott links to the Passover and the upper room, though he notes "there was no crowd." He quotes the line from the pseudo-Clementine literature: "Eat your bread for the Son of Man is risen." Scott reads this as a call to "break the fast, rejoin life, step into your purpose," and he says the Clementine text describes James's knees as calloused from prayer.

### Physical or spiritual resurrection?
Scott says the question is "not as cut and dry" as a bodily resurrection. He claims Paul reports James's experience as a spiritual appearance, and that the early Nazarenes and Ebionites "believed that Jesus did not necessarily rise from the dead," seeing him as a great prophet and "son of man" who appeared to a fasting James. He suggests the flesh-and-wounds account may be "a later on mythology tacked on" decades or centuries after Jesus's death. These are Scott's proposals, offered as possibilities.

### Application: fast, pray, act
Scott closes by urging listeners to fast, even briefly ("a three-hour fast" or skipping a meal), pray, and act. He says "resurrection looks like getting up and just simply eating again," and he ends with "Action over faith."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Paul and James taught the same gospel.** Scott says James's teaching ("the exact opposite" of Paul's) puts works above faith, and that mainstream Christianity has followed Paul's faith-alone message.
- **Jesus's resurrection was necessarily a bodily, flesh-and-wounds event.** Scott suggests the earliest Nazarene and Ebionite view was a spiritual appearance, and that the bodily account may be a later addition.
- **Jesus had no brothers.** Scott argues from Mark 6:3 and Eusebius that James was Jesus's biological brother.
- **Modern Christians act on faith.** Scott claims most modern Christians give little beyond sending a few members on missions, and that James's example demands personal, concrete action.
