## Summary

This sermon by "Scott" of Nazarene Way Church asks what the original religion of Jesus (called Yeshua, "the Nazarene") and his first followers actually taught and practiced, and how that differs from the Christianity that grew out of Paul. Scott's answer is that the earliest community (James the Just, the Nazarenes and the later Ebionites) followed Torah, valued action over faith, rejected animal sacrifice, and lived in peace with animals. He supports this mainly by citing fragments of lost gospels (the Gospel according to the Hebrews, the Gospel of the Ebionites, the Gospel of the Nazarenes, and a Hebrew Gospel of Matthew) that he says survive only as quotations in Origen, Epiphanius and Jerome. He also relies on Jesus' quotation of Hosea 6:6 ("I desire mercy not sacrifice"), the Letter of James, and his reading of the "my mother, the Holy Spirit" saying.

Much of the talk is polemical rather than documentary. Scott attacks Paul as an apparition-based founder, calls Epiphanius a "psychopath," claims Origen mutilated himself, and makes claims about Jesus' diet and Nazirite practice with little or no sourcing. He describes Jesus as a Galilean and Nazarene wilderness figure, not a Christian or (in the modern rabbinic sense) a Jew.

The talk ends with a call to action: read the Letter of James as "homework," choose the "narrow way" of mercy and feeding the poor over faith alone, and reject Pauline teaching. Scott mentions a planned outreach with a vegan pizza giveaway in Venice Beach, and signs off with "Shabbat shalom."

## Key Teachings

### Jesus as a Nazarene, Not a Christian or a Jew
Scott argues that Jesus "was not a Christian" and "wasn't a Jew." He describes him as a Galilean from the wilderness, of the house of David, and a member of "a Judean sect" called the Nazarenes, comparing him to John the Baptist. He contrasts this with "what we think of Jews today," which he says is later medieval rabbinic Judaism, and says that this later form "got a big rebrand" after persecution. He gives no sources for these historical claims.

### Torah kept, not abolished
Scott quotes Jesus from Matthew: "I did not come to abolish the law. I did not come to abolish the Torah. I came to fulfill it." He reads "fulfill" as meaning continued practice of Torah, not its replacement. The Nazarenes, he says, "clung to both the Torah and the teachings of Yeshua," and called their path "the way."

### Action over faith
Scott says the Nazarenes "acted by giving to the Ebionim, the poor ones," and that "What matters more than faith is action." He ties this to the Letter of James, which he says is "hidden and absconded into the back of the New Testament," and summarizes James as saying that "action and works matter more than faith." This is set against Paul, whom Scott treats as a rival teacher.

### Mercy, not sacrifice
Scott's central teaching claim is that Jesus rejected animal sacrifice. He cites a Gospel of the Ebionites fragment, quoted through Epiphanius, in which Jesus says "I have come to abolish the sacrifices." He also points to Hosea 6:6 as echoed in Matthew 9:13 ("I desire mercy not sacrifice"). He reads the Temple's "den of thieves" as "den of violent ones" and says sacrifice was a profitable trade. He also cites a Gospel of the Nazarenes saying, quoted by Jerome, that Yeshua "rebuked the altar as a house of slaughter."

### Diet and the Garden vision
Scott argues that Jesus and James were vegetarians and that people in Jesus' time "rarely ate animals," eating them only on special occasions such as Passover as part of sacrifice. He says Jesus was probably a Nazirite, abstaining from grapes, wine, hair-cutting and contact with the dead. He presents this as part of a "kingdom of heaven" in the Garden of Eden vision from Genesis, in which humans live with animals rather than slaughter them. The talk offers no sources for the claims about James's and Jesus's diet or for the Nazirite practice.

### Holy Spirit as "mother" and the rejection of the virgin birth
Scott reads the Gospel according to the Hebrews fragment ("my mother, the Holy Spirit took me by one of my hairs and carried me to the great mountain Tabor") as evidence that the Spirit was understood as feminine in Aramaic. He argues Origen wrongly used it as proof of a virgin birth. He reads the baptism, when the Spirit came down "in the form of a dove," as the moment Yeshua became the son of man, and says the virgin birth was "a misreading of Isaiah 7:14." He presents the Spirit's guidance as humble, inward obedience rather than doctrine.

### The fragment-based method and the lost Hebrew gospel
Scott's method throughout is to rely on fragments transmitted by hostile church writers (Origen, Epiphanius, Jerome). He says "thanks to them because their texts are saved we actually have fragments of the lost texts of Jesus's original disciples." He mentions the Hebrew Gospel of Matthew as an object in front of him and says the Gospel of the Hebrews was probably lost "maybe they've been burned," though he gives no source for that suggestion. He does not discuss the manuscripts or editions he is using.
