## Key Teachings

### 1. "Son of man" at baptism, not "Son of God" at birth
Scott's central claim is that Jesus was never called "Son of God" by himself, and that his identity as Messiah came from the baptism. He cites the Aramaic *Ben Adam* and says it "comes from Adam." He reads the phrase "this day I have begotten you" as God's declaration at the baptism, when the dove (the Holy Spirit) transforms Jesus into the son of man. He argues that Jesus "became the Messiah through his actions, through his righteousness." The main support is a fragment from the Gospel of the Hebrews, which he says Jerome preserves, in which the Holy Spirit rests on Jesus at the baptism. He also cites a fragment in which Jesus asks, "what sin have I committed?" Scott reads this as Jesus choosing baptism "to fulfill righteousness" rather than for forgiveness.

### 2. The virgin birth as a later invention
Scott claims the virgin birth was "concocted way after the fact." His reasons are: (a) a supposed mistranslation of the Hebrew word in Isaiah 7:14, which he says means "a young maiden, a young woman," not "virgin"; (b) only Matthew and Luke mention the birth among the New Testament books; (c) Paul never mentions it; and (d) the two Gospel birth narratives differ from each other. He dates Paul's letters to about 50 CE and the birth narrative to about 80 to 90 CE. Scott gives no manuscript evidence or scholarly source for either date, and he does not discuss the Isaiah passage's textual history.

### 3. Action over faith: James versus Paul
Scott argues that James, "the Jesus's actual biological brother," taught that "action matters more than faith," and that the Letter of James shows this. He presents Paul's teaching as "faith matters more than anything else," including "drink the blood of Christ symbolized by wine," and says Paul's letters are numerous ("13 I think") though "some of the letters aren't actually authentically Paul." He notes this uncertainty but does not explain it. He sums up his view: Jesus "never said that in order for you to be saved... you have to profess your faith in Jesus Christ," and "That was Paul."

### 4. John the Baptist and "the Way"
Scott presents John the Baptist as "outside the system," in contrast to the temple priesthood and the modern Vatican, which he criticizes in harsh personal terms (those remarks are not evidence-based and are not repeated here). He says John "rejected the corrupt priesthood" and "prepared the way in the desert." He points to Acts, where followers are called "people of the way," and to Jeremiah 6:16 as describing "the ancient paths." He says John's message "was about purity of heart... about a life aligned with the Torah." Scott also cites a fragment in which John ate "honey cakes" or "mana bread," not locusts dipped in honey. He calls the locust reading a mistranslation by a scribe, but he gives no textual evidence for this claim.

### 5. Scripture, Torah and the canon
Scott says the only Bible his group follows is "Jesus's Bible," the Hebrew Bible (Torah, Prophets and Writings), read "with a critical eye," with the "bloodlusting... Canaanite blood sacrifice stuff" set aside as later scribal insertions. Since the Gospel of the Hebrews survives only in fragments, he tells listeners to use the Hebrew Gospel of Matthew and "cut out the first two chapters," which he says the Nazarenes and Ebionites did not have. He also states that the Nazarene movement grew from a house assembly, like the early Jerusalem church under James. Scott provides no evidence that the Ebionites lacked these chapters, and the claim rests on his own reading.

### 6. Vegetarianism as Nazarene practice
Scott says "we know for a fact that the Ebianites were vegetarian," and he ties this to the Garden of Eden, where people ate "fruits, the plants, the seeds." He frames the practice as "highly recommended" but not required, and presents his own vegetarianism as a personal example. He says that avoiding meat avoids participating in "a mass genocide of 90 billion animals," a figure given without a source. The talk does not cite any ancient text for the Ebionite diet.
