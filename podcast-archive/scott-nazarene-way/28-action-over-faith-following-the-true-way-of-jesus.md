## Key Teachings

### Jesus as "the Nazarene," a practitioner of the true Torah
Scott says the title "Jesus the Nazarene," not "of Nazareth," is the correct one, and he links it to the Hebrew root *netzer* (he says it means to consecrate oneself and branch off, "like the branch of David"). He describes Jesus as a revolutionary who restored "the way" of acting on "the true Torah, not the perverted Torah of uh the Leviticus high priests." He summarizes Jesus's core teaching as love of the Lord God with heart, soul and mind, love of neighbor as oneself, and care for the poor. He adds that Jesus "came not to... abolish the Torah... but to fulfill it." The talk gives no verse references for these claims.

### Works over faith
Scott cites James's letter ("What good is faith without works?") and argues that church attendance, worship music, and altar-call conversion are not what saves. He says "we need to actually choose action over faith" and treats feeding and clothing the poor as the measure of discipleship. He contrasts this with what he calls "modern-day Pauline Gentile Christianity."

### The Venice Beach outreach as a model
Scott describes an outreach using about 55 vegan pizzas, bought with roughly $1,000, given to a single mother with three children, Vietnam veterans, and a man with a missing leg. He says the group chose vegan food because "Jesus would not be a fan of" mass animal slaughter, and he cites a figure of "90 billion animals" killed each year. He presents the outreach as "following the way" of Jesus, and his description is anecdotal.

### Paul as the opponent of the original disciples
Scott's main argument is that Paul taught the opposite of Jesus and of the Jerusalem disciples (he names James the Just and Simon Peter). He reads from Schonfield, page 161, which says the Nazarenes made a "spirited but belated" attempt at counter-propaganda, and he says the Roman church absorbed Paul's message by "the end of the second century." Scott estimates that this was "around 170 CE," about 140 years after Jesus's death. He also says that Irenaeus recorded that Paul was called "an apostate from the law" by the Nazarenes and Ebionites, and he says Epiphanius recorded that these groups used Clementine literature, including "the acts of the Nazarenes" and "the travels and writings of Peter."

### The Clementine Homilies and the vision argument
Scott reads a passage that he says describes Paul. The passage says that if Jesus "appeared to you in a vision," it was "as one who is enraged with an adversary," and that visions and revelations from outside are not the same as being taught over time. Peter's rebuttal, as Scott reads it, asks why Jesus would "abide in discourse a whole year" with the disciples if he then overturned their teaching through a later vision. Scott says this is the "Paul" figure, and he compares visionary founders to Joseph Smith. He ends the passage by saying Paul's religion "bears Jesus's name" while opposing Jesus's teachings.

### "Pseudo" labels and New Testament authorship
Scott argues that the "pseudo" label on the Clementine literature is applied selectively. He says scholarly consensus holds that "most of the New Testament books are also all pseudonymous," naming Mark, Matthew and John as examples and noting that Luke and Acts are the exception he says scholars accept as by the same author. He claims the "pseudo" label is a way to discredit texts. He recommends looking "beyond the Wikipedia entry" and "what crap GPT says."
