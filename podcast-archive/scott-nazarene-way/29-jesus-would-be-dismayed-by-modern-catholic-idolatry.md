## Summary

Scott, speaking as pastor of the house church Nazarene Way Church, asks why the Second Commandment (no graven images, no bowing to them) is not kept in Catholic practice, and whether the faith Jesus taught has been replaced by a later "Roman pagan" religion. He sets the Second Commandment against rosaries, Marian devotion, crucifixes and the Eucharist, and argues that idolatry is the central problem. He presents the Nazarenes and Ebionites as the original community of Jesus and James, holding that Jesus was a righteous, anointed man (Messiah) and not God, with no virgin birth. He offers three main supports: Mark has no birth narrative, Paul and John never mention a virgin birth, and the idea came later from the Protoevangelium of James. He also cites Hugh Schofield's *Those Incredible Christians*, Epiphanius's *Panarion*, and Jerome to argue that the Nazarenes were wrongly labeled heretics, and he dates the divinization of Jesus to the Council of Nicaea in 325 CE.

The talk closes with practical instructions rather than further argument. Scott describes a food-distribution outing in Venice Beach with vegetarian pizzas, says Jesus would oppose animal slaughter, and tells viewers to audit their homes for idols, discard crucifixes and statues, and give clothing to the poor. He ends by calling Jesus "not a Christian" and "not Jewish" but a Nazarene, and invites viewers to the next week's sermon. Much of the case rests on the speaker's own reading and assertion, and several claims (the animal figure, Tertullian's role, the "Jew" term's date) are given without sources in the talk.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The Second Commandment as the test of Catholic practice
Scott reads Exodus 20:4-5 ("you shall not make for yourself an idol... You shall not bow down to them or worship them") and argues it forbids making or venerating any physical or "metaphysical" image of God. He applies this to rosaries, statues of Mary, crucifixes, the saints, and the Trinity, which he calls "a madeup notion" that he attributes to Tertullian in the second or third century. He says that a Catholic who breaks the Second Commandment "has a worm at the core," and that the Second Commandment matters more than murder.

### 2. No virgin birth in the earliest sources
Scott argues that the virgin birth is a later development. He says Paul's earliest writings never mention it, that the Gospel of John does not either, and that Mark (which he calls the earliest written gospel) has no birth narrative, opening instead with Jesus anointed at John the Baptist's baptism. He traces the idea to the *Protoevangelium of James*, which he calls an apocryphal gospel, and says the traditional reading of Isaiah 7:14 is a misreading.

### 3. Nazarene and Ebionite christology: an anointed man, not God
Scott says the Nazarenes and Ebionites believed Jesus was born of Joseph and Mary, was righteous because he lived by the law, was anointed by God at his baptism, and was the Messiah. "The Nazarenes never deified Yeshua," he says, and "He was not the son of God. He was not a deity. That came later on." He says Jerome made a mistake when he later claimed some Nazarenes believed in the virgin birth, which Scott rejects.

### 4. Heresy labels and the Council of Nicaea
Scott reads from Schofield's *Those Incredible Christians* (p. 158), which says Jewish Christianity such as the Nazarenes did not debase Jesus, and that it was "the church in general, which was misled into deifying him." He describes Epiphanius's *Panarion* as a "medicine box" listing 80 heresies, including the Nazarenes and Ebionites. He dates the formal divinization of Jesus to the Council of Nicaea in 325 CE, which he says declared anyone who denied it a heretic, and he draws a parallel to Roman claims that Caesar was a son of god.

### 5. The canon and the priestly accretions
Scott says the Nazarenes' canon is the Hebrew Bible, which Jesus "read very specifically." He says Jesus rejected the temple sacrificial system and "the overdoing of Leviticus," which he attributes to "crazy scribes." He gives an example of textual growth: the Ten Commandments, which later became 613 commandments in Orthodox Judaism. He also says he holds up a "Hebrew Gospel of Matthew" on camera, though he does not discuss its content or date.

### 6. Care for the poor and against animal slaughter
Scott's central ethical claim is that feeding the poor is the only religious practice that matters and that everything else is "a distraction." He describes buying pizza with church funds and handing boxes to homeless people on Venice Beach, and says the pizzas were vegetarian. He claims that John the Baptist, James the Just, and probably Jesus were vegetarians, and that Jesus would oppose the slaughter of "90 billion animals per year," which he says is mostly factory farmed. He reads the temple episode as a rebuke of people "destroying and slaughtering animals for money."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The virgin birth is original to Jesus's story.** Scott says it is a later addition, first found in the *Protoevangelium of James*, and absent from Mark, Paul and John.
- **Jesus was divine from the start.** Scott says the Nazarenes and Ebionites held Jesus to be an anointed human Messiah, and that divinization came with Nicaea (325 CE).
- **The Nazarenes and Ebionites were heretics.** Scott says they were labeled heretical by Epiphanius and others, and that they were persecuted by the church.
- **Catholic devotions to Mary, saints, the crucifix and the Eucharist are acceptable.** Scott says they violate the Second Commandment.
- **Jesus was a Christian or a Jew.** Scott says he was a Hebrew and Nazarene, and that the term "Jew" was not used in the sense of Orthodox Jews until the Middle Ages.
