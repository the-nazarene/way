## Summary

Scott, presenting a sermon for Nazarene Way Church, asks what happened to the Hebrew and Judean roots of Jesus's religion and why they were removed from Christianity. He says Jesus's first followers, the Nazarenes, were led in Jerusalem by James "the Just," Jesus's brother, and that Catholic and Christian tradition later declared them heretical. He presents Jesus as a teacher of Torah observance who wanted his followers to practice Jewish traditions such as Sabbath and Passover, and to focus on loving God and caring for the poor. Scott contrasts this with what he calls the religion of Paul, which he describes as faith-based and easy to adopt.

His main textual evidence is Matthew 5:17-19 ("Do not think that I have come to abolish the law or the prophets"), Matthew 10:5-6 (go "to the lost sheep of the house of Israel"), and the claim that the Hebrew Gospel of Matthew lacks the universal mission found in the Greek text. He also relies on Hugh Schonfield's book *Those Incredible Christians* (p. 184) for the idea that the Nazarene heritage was "worked over," and on his reading of Tertullian as the inventor of the Trinity. He argues that Rome wanted a universal religion less tied to Israel, especially after the Jewish revolts, and so downplayed Jesus's Jewishness and elevated his divinity.

The talk ends with practical advice: stop observing Christmas and Easter, keep the Sabbath and Jewish festivals, and, as a quick check, ask an AI chatbot which holidays Jesus observed. Many of Scott's historical claims are stated without sources or are hedged ("I believe"), so the study group should check the dates and attributions he gives (for example, the coinage of "Old Testament" and the Trinity's origin) against primary and scholarly sources.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The Nazarenes as Jesus's original community
Scott says Jesus and his disciples were called "Nazarines" by outsiders, while they called themselves "followers of the Way." He says the Jerusalem church began under James the Just, whom he links to Eusebius's history. He cites Epiphanius's *Panarion* ("Paneran" in the captions) and says the Nazarenes were declared heretical only about 400 years after Jesus. He also says that the Nazarenes later became known as the Ebionim or Ebionites, "the poor ones."

### 2. Matthew 5:17-19 and the Hebrew Bible
Scott reads Matthew 5:17-19 as Jesus telling followers to keep the law and prophets, not abolish them. He argues that the name "Old Testament" is a demeaning label, and he says it was coined by Marcion in the first or second century (introduced with "I believe"). He prefers "Hebrew Bible" or "Tanakh" and describes its three parts: Torah, Prophets, and Writings. He says Jesus meant the Writings too, though he did not mention them. He says Jesus's practice included Shabbat from Friday evening to Saturday evening, Passover, and Hanukkah.

### 3. Paul versus Jesus
Scott sets Paul's teaching against Jesus's. He describes Paul's message as "faith matters above all else," and says it is easy to adopt by walking into church and professing faith. He says Paul "had never even met Jesus" and that Paul's authority rests on a vision seven years after Jesus's death, which he compares to Joseph Smith's claims. He argues that if one accepts Paul's authority, there is no logical reason to reject Smith's. Scott says he prefers "those who walked with Jesus" and "the actual words of Jesus himself."

### 4. The Trinity and "Son of Man"
Scott says the Trinity (Latin *trinitas*) was invented by Tertullian about 100 to 200 years after Jesus. He argues that a three-god system violates the first of the Ten Commandments. He says Jesus called himself "the son of man" (Ben Adam), not the son of God, and that making Jesus divine turned the Jewish message into a false gospel. He reads Matthew 10:5-6 as Jesus excluding the Gentiles, whom he equates with the Trinitarian Christians of his own day.

### 5. Caring for the poor versus "majoring in the minor"
Scott says Jesus's core instruction is to love God with all your heart, soul, and mind, love your neighbor, and care for the poor. He says the "613 laws" of later rabbinic tradition, such as the rule against shellfish, were added by scribes and that his community should not "major in the minor." He says the Beatitudes and rosary are distractions from this purpose. He cites Schonfield's book to say that the Nazarene heritage was not omitted from the New Testament but "worked over" to fit Roman doctrine.

### 6. The Hebrew Gospel of Matthew and the universal mission
Scott holds up a Hebrew Gospel of Matthew, which he says was "discovered accidentally," and says it lacks the command to make disciples of all nations. He argues that this command was added later by scribes seeking to convert the world. He concludes that the Nazarene mission was a restoration movement within Israel, not a universal religion.

### 7. Rome's motives
Scott argues that Rome wanted a universal religion that would not look too Jewish, because Rome had burned Jerusalem around 60-70 CE and fought the Bar Kokhba uprising around 130 CE, after which Jews were banned from Jerusalem. He says Rome competed with another religion (garbled in the captions as "myth Mithrael," presumably Mithraism) and that Paul's gentile Christianity was "too Jewish" to spread. Scott says Constantine merged Rome with Catholicism, and that the "great contradictions" between Jesus's sayings and Paul's teaching are worth researching.

- **The Trinity is the orthodox doctrine of one God.** Scott says it is a three-god system that violates the first commandment.
- **"Israel" in Matthew 10:6 refers to the modern nation-state.** Scott says it means the people who believe in one God.
