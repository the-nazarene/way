## Key Teachings

### One God, not the Trinity
Scott says the Nazarenes keep the first commandment of believing in one God, and that the Trinity is a later idea. He says the doctrine was "invented" in the second century by Tertullian, a figure he calls a heretic who later left the Catholic Church, and that Jesus never spoke of it. He grounds the point in the Hebrew Bible ("the Torah, the prophets, and the writings"), which he calls the only scripture Jesus used, and says the Trinity does not appear in it.

### Jesus as Messiah and "son of man," and faith versus action
Scott says Jesus called himself "Ben Adam," the son of man, and the Messiah, not the son of God. He argues from Mark, which he calls the earliest gospel, that Jesus was anointed at his baptism by John the Baptist, when a dove descended. He sets this against what he calls Paul's idea that salvation comes by professing belief in Jesus as God's son. He says salvation comes through "walking the way," and that "action matters more than faith." He ties this to the Gospel of Thomas, which he says places the kingdom of heaven "within you and without you."

### Care for the poor as the highest value
Scott makes caring for the poor the central Nazarene practice. He reads Galatians 2:10 as showing that James, Peter and John, the Jerusalem "pillars," asked Paul to remember the poor, and that Paul's reply ("I was eager to do that") was self-serving. He describes the Ebionites as "the poor ones" who adopted a modest lifestyle. He says practice means "boots on the ground": going out, learning names, and handing out clothes, supplies and pizza. He reports that the church spent $1,000 on vegetarian pizzas for homeless people. He contrasts this with what he calls idol worship of Jesus as a god.

### The canon: the Tanakh, read critically
Scott says the Nazarenes' Bible is the Tanakh, read "with a very careful eye" because of later scribal and orthodox changes. He names the Gospel of the Hebrews, which he describes as a Hebrew Matthew without its first two chapters, and says it survives only in fragments. He also cites the Clementine literature, the Gospel of Thomas, a text the captions render as "dedicay," and other apocryphal sources. He defends this with the claim that most New Testament books are also pseudonymous, and recommends Bart Ehrman's *Misquoting Jesus* on scribal error. He says the "best canon" is "reading all canons" critically.

### Vegetarianism, with the Ebionites and James as witnesses
Scott says the Nazarenes and Ebionites were vegetarian and read "thou shalt not kill" as covering animals. He cites Hegesippus, preserved by Eusebius, as saying James the Just drank no wine or strong drink, ate no meat, did not cut his hair, and wore linen. He reports from Mark 1:6 that John the Baptist's "locusts" were actually a honey cake, a mistranslation through Aramaic, Hebrew, Greek, Latin and English. He cites Epiphanius's account of an Ebionite last supper in which Jesus says he came "to abolish the sacrifices." He argues that the "fish" of the feeding stories was really "relish," and that Jesus's "bread only" mention shows he was likely vegetarian too. He encourages listeners to begin gradually.

### The Nazarite vow
Scott describes the Nazarite vow, which he finds in Numbers, as optional but his own practice. It includes no wine, no grapes, no strong drink, no cutting of the hair, and no contact with the dead. He says he gave up whiskey after arguing with ChatGPT and translators, and has been alcohol-free for at least six months. He cites Leviticus 21:5 for the ban on shaving the head and beard. He says James the Just took the vow, and that Jesus and John the Baptist probably did too.

### Water immersion and holidays
Scott describes regular immersion in running water as a symbol of being "born anew" and a core practice. On holidays, he says the Nazarenes observe only Hebrew and Judean festivals, which he says Jesus observed, and reject Easter and Christmas as pagan. He suggests listeners check which holidays Jesus practiced, using ChatGPT.
