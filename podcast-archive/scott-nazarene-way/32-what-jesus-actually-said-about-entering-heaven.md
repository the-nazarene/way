## Key Teachings

### Faith-based salvation is Paul's teaching, not Jesus's
Scott's central claim is that Romans 10:9 ("if you confess with your lips that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved") is "Paul of Tarsus" speaking, and that Jesus "said the exact opposite." He treats the Matthew 7:21-23 saying, that not everyone who says "Lord, Lord" enters the kingdom but only the one who does the Father's will, as the contrast. He notes that Jesus "makes no mention of faith and having faith in him" in that passage. He also compares Paul to Joseph Smith, saying he claimed a revelation and asked for trust on his word alone.

### Walking "the Way" and the two-ways theme
Scott says the earliest followers called themselves "disciples of the way" or "people of the way," which he traces in Acts and to the Hebrew prophets, especially Isaiah. He connects this to the Hebrew Bible's contrast between the way of life and the way of death. He says Jesus's message "centered on... walking the way," not on a Pauline gospel.

### Torah, the 613 commandments, and sacrifice
Scott says the Torah began with ten laws, but that scribes and high priests later expanded it to 613, creating a "majoring in the minor" legalism that Jesus opposed. He says Jesus "did not come to abolish the Torah" but to restore it, and that Jesus summarized the law as loving God with heart, soul, and mind and loving one's neighbor. He says the priestly laws about animal sacrifice and slaughter were not central to Jesus's message.

### Virgin birth as a later invention
Scott argues from silence. He says Mark (which he calls the earliest gospel) omits the virgin birth, as does John, and that Paul's letters from the 50s CE do not mention it either. He says only Matthew and Luke in Greek include it. He says the idea was drawn from the *Proto-Evangelium of James*, a non-canonical text. He also reports that Jerome contradicted himself on whether the Nazarenes accepted the virgin birth (in 398 and again in 404), and he treats Epiphanius's account of the Ebionites as evidence that they rejected it.

### Nazarenes and Ebionites as the original Jewish movement
Scott argues that "Nazarene" was the outsiders' name for Jesus's first followers (he says Romans asked Paul whether he was "the ring leader of the Nazarenes"), and that the sect later became known as the Ebionites ("the poor ones"). He says the family of Jesus, including the brothers named in Mark 6:3, was part of this movement. He says the church fathers Eusebius, Epiphanius, Jerome, and Ignatius shaped later doctrine and declared these groups heretical.

### Diet and the Genesis ideal
Scott says the first diet God prescribed was vegetarian, so that "Jesus came to... restore us" to the Genesis state before the fall. He says John the Baptist, James the brother of Jesus, and probably Jesus himself were vegetarians. He argues that the fish-eating stories in Luke are a mistranslation or interpolation, since the Greek term he says means "relish," a garnish for bread.

### Christology: Son of Man, Messiah, and one God
Scott says Jesus never called himself "the son of God" but "the Ben Adam, the son of man, the Messiah." He reports that the Nazarene literature in Epiphanius shows they did not believe Jesus was God. He attributes the Trinity to Tertullian in the second century and calls it a "three god system" that departs from the Shema.
