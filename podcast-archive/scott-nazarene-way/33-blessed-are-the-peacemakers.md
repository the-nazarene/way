## Summary

Rabbi Scott, preaching at Nazarene Way Church (a house church, by his account), uses Matthew 5:9, "Blessed are the peacemakers, for they will be called children of God," to ask what the Sermon on the Mount asks of ordinary people. He argues that "peacemaker" means active repair and healing rather than passive avoidance, and that Jesus broke a cycle of retaliation that he says the "militant messiahs" of the period, such as Judas the Galilean and Bar Kokhba, did not break. He presents this as a model of nonviolence drawn from the Hebrew Bible, which he says Jesus read as his only scripture.

The talk also makes a set of wider historical claims. Scott argues that the New Testament did not exist in Jesus's time, that Matthew's first two chapters were later additions to a Nazarene gospel (the Gospel according to the Hebrews), that Paul was not an apostle and built a different religion, and that Jesus's brothers included James and Jude. He offers the Church fathers (named as Jerome, Eusebius and Epiphanius) and the Protoevangelium of James as sources for some of these points, but he gives few verse-level or manuscript references, and he treats several claims as settled.

The sermon closes with three applications (at home, in the community, and in the heart), a rule of waiting 24 hours before retaliating, and a call to follow Jesus's "way" by action rather than by faith alone, which he contrasts with Paul. He signs off by inviting listeners to read the Hebrew Bible as Jesus did.

## Key Teachings

### Matthew 5:9 as a call to active peacemaking
Scott reads Matthew 5:9 as a call to address the "pendulum" of retaliation ("they did this to me, I'm going to do this back to them"). He says a peacemaker "doesn't mean that you just passively step away," and that the word means "active repairing and healing." He ties this to justice, mercy and simplicity in the Torah and the prophets, read "with a critical eye" to separate later additions from the core. He also claims that John the Baptist, James the Just and the Nazarenes and Ebionites lived as a separated community in the wilderness and in aesthetic communities, which he presents as the setting in which this teaching took shape.

### The Hebrew Bible as Jesus's scripture, and the Gospel according to the Hebrews
Scott states that the Hebrew Bible (Torah, Prophets, Writings) was the only scripture of Jesus and his first followers, and that "there was no such thing as the New Testament" in Jesus's lifetime. He attributes the New Testament canon to Marcion (whom he calls "Marcian") in the second century and to Catholic decisions around 400 to 500 years after Jesus. He says that the Nazarenes had their own gospel, the Gospel according to the Hebrews, which he describes, citing the Church fathers, as "basically... the gospel of Matthew minus the first two chapters." From this he argues that the birth narratives in Matthew 1–2 (and the virgin birth narrative in Luke) were added later. He holds up a copy of George Howard's Hebrew Gospel of Matthew and suggests listeners cut out the first two chapters, a practical step he describes as using "what we use from our canon." He admits the Gospel according to the Hebrews survives only in fragments and is "lost in its full form," but he states its contents with confidence.

### Paul versus James: action over faith
Scott contrasts Paul, whom he calls a "self-proclaimed apostle" who "never knew Jesus," with James the Just, the leader of the Jerusalem community. He says Paul's teaching lets people be saved by professing belief that Jesus "resurrected from the dead," while Jesus "stood for taking action." He cites the Book of James for the claim that "faith alone isn't what gains salvation." He also says Acts and Galatians show Paul answering to James, Peter and John. These are presented as the basis for his view that Paul's gospel departs from Jesus's.

### Failed messiahs and the contrast with Jesus
Scott describes the first-century "militant messiahs" (he names Judas the Galilean and Bar Kokhba, the latter about a century after Jesus) who "went to war" against Rome, refused its taxes and were crushed. He says that most of the world does not know these figures because they did "nothing extraordinary" and only retaliated with force. He claims that Jesus "took the Romans essentially equipment, wore it, and walked the extra mile than was allowed by the law," which he presents as a deliberate act of non-retaliation. He draws a parallel to modern conflict, saying Hamas attacks and Israeli responses follow the same retaliatory pattern, and that Jesus's way breaks it.

### Jesus's brothers and family
Scott lists Jesus's siblings from Mark and John as James, Jude, Joseph and Simon, plus two sisters, and says Jude was named after Judas the Galilean to avoid confusion with Judas Iscariot. He argues that the Catholic position on Mary's perpetual virginity, which he attributes to the Protoevangelium of James, is contradicted by the sibling list. He describes the Protoevangelium as "apocryphal non-canonical," and he says that the sibling listing is "hidden in plain sight."

### Practical application: home, community and heart
Scott gives three applications. At home, be "open, curious, and committed to learning" rather than "closed, defensive, and committed to being right," and forgive. In the community, care for the homeless, with the example of giving a sleeping bag and clothing rather than cash. In the heart, "cast off envy," "cast off wrath," and wait 24 hours before responding in anger. He frames this as the "way" (capitalized) that Jesus followed, as opposed to looking to Rome, priests or pastors for guidance.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The Church of the Nazarene owns the name "Nazarene."** Scott says it "hijacked" the term, that it was founded in the 18th or 19th century, and that the term "can't be trademarked" because it is ancient.
- **The New Testament was available in Jesus's time.** Scott says no such canon existed, and that the canon was assembled in the fourth century.
- **Paul was an apostle.** Scott says Paul was "self-proclaimed" and "never an apostle," and that he never knew Jesus.
- **Jesus was God; the Trinity is biblical.** Scott says Jesus never taught a Trinity and that he was not God.
- **Jude was a woman, or a saint of a different identity.** Scott says Jude was Jesus's brother, and he attributes the popular confusion to the Beatles song "Hey Jude" and to Catholic tradition.
- **Mary was a perpetual virgin.** Scott attributes this to the Protoevangelium of James and argues that Jesus's siblings contradict it.
