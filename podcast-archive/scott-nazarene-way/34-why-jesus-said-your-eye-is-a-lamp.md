## Summary

Scott, presenting a sermon for the Nazarene Way church, asks what the original followers of Jesus, whom he calls the Nazarenes, actually practiced and believed, and how their teaching differs from the Christianity that grew from Paul. He frames the talk around Matthew 6:22-23, the saying "the eye is the lamp of the body," and treats it as the core of Jesus's teaching on what a person should focus on.

His case rests on a few kinds of claim. He says the Nazarenes took their name from the Nazirite vow rather than from Nazareth, and he cites James the Just, John the Baptist and Samson as Nazirites. He says the Nazarenes read the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh) as their scripture, observed Jewish customs, and used a "Gospel According to the Hebrews," which he describes as an early version of Matthew missing its first two chapters. He attributes knowledge of the group to the church fathers Epiphanius and Eusebius, and to the Gospel of Thomas, whose saying 24 he uses as independent confirmation that Jesus taught the "light" idea. He also argues that Paul, who he says never met Jesus, is the source of belief-based salvation, the virgin birth and a physical resurrection, and that Paul's letters (dated by him to the 50s CE) never describe a physical resurrection.

Scott applies the verse practically: what the senses take in (he extends "eye" to all the senses) shapes a person, so one should limit media consumption, choose simplicity over status and material desire, and let the "light" shine outward through compassion, justice and charity. The talk ends with a Shabbat blessing and an invitation to other Nazarene channels. Much of the historical argument is stated as Scott's own conclusion, and several claims (about Paul, Marcion, Tertullian, and the origin of the Trinity and New Testament) are asserted without sources given in the talk.

## Key Teachings

### The Nazarenes as a distinct, Hebraic group
Scott says the name "Nazarene" comes from the same root as "Nazirite," not from the town of Nazareth. He cites Samson, James the Just and John the Baptist as Nazirites who did not cut their hair, drank no wine or strong drink, and avoided dead bodies. He says the Nazarenes called themselves "disciples of the Way" or "children of the Way." He distinguishes them from the 19th-century Church of the Nazarene, which he describes as ordinary Christians. He says the Nazarenes studied the Torah and Tanakh, kept Jewish customs, and did not follow the later rabbinic or "Orthodox" Judaism, which he links to the Pharisees.

### Paul versus the Hebrew Bible and the "Christian" Testament
Scott contrasts the Hebrew Bible, "the same Bible that Jesus used," with the New Testament, which he says was first assembled by Marcion in the second century. He argues the Hebrew Bible shows a conflict between priests, scribes and prophets, and that the "613 laws" came from scribes rather than Moses, while the original core was the Ten Commandments. He says Jesus called himself "Ben Adam" (son of man) and never "son of God," and that the Trinity was a later invention, attributed to Tertullian. He casts Paul as the origin of belief-based salvation and of a religion centred on Jesus as God. He admits Paul was sent out by the Jerusalem leadership (James, Peter and John) but says he was taken to be the ringleader of the Nazarenes. These are presented as the speaker's conclusions and are not supported by sourced argument in the talk.

### Gospel of the Hebrews, virgin birth and resurrection
Scott says the Nazarenes had a gospel, the Gospel According to the Hebrews, which he calls an early Matthew missing the first two chapters. He reasons that Mark was also missing its first two chapters, so the infancy material may be later. He rejects the virgin birth, which he says rests on a misreading of Isaiah 7:14, and says the Nazarenes did not necessarily believe in a physical resurrection. He notes that the Gospel of the Hebrews has Jesus appearing first to James, and asks whether that appearance was physical or spiritual. He says Paul's letters are the earliest Christian writings and describe no physical resurrection, which he presents as a puzzle.

### Salvation through walking the Way, not through belief
Scott says the notion that salvation requires professing Jesus as God's son and believing he died and rose for sins is not from Jesus. He says Jesus said "It's not about faith at all. It's not about belief at all," and that those who gain salvation are those who walk the Way and live by the true spirit of the Torah. He attributes the faith-based message to Paul.

### Matthew 6:22-23 and the sensory "lamp"
Scott reads Matthew 6:22-23 ("the eye is the lamp of the body... if the light in you is darkness, how great is the darkness") and places it in the Sermon on the Mount, just after warnings about storing up treasure and before the line that one cannot serve God and wealth. He reads "eye" broadly as "all six senses," counting a sense of balance, and argues that what one focuses on becomes one's character. He cites Gospel of Thomas saying 24 as a parallel. He says the two texts "were probably talking about the same thing that Jesus said," recorded by different people, and that this gives confidence Jesus said it. He does not discuss the Greek or Aramaic wording of the verse.

### Practical application: simplicity, media and the Kingdom of the good life
Scott's application has three parts. First, choose simplicity and reject status goods, which he links to John the Baptist and Jesus as "poor Galileans." He describes Orange County car culture as a "hedonic treadmill." Second, he urges the listener to be careful what they consume (YouTube, Netflix, podcasts) and to replace it with single audiobooks at 1.5x or 2x speed. Third, he urges living "light outward" through compassion, justice and charity, and guarding against greed, envy and resentment. He ends by urging listeners to love God and neighbour and to give to the poor, and he criticizes services that focus on other things.
