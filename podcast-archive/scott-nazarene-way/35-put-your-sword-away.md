## Summary

Scott, pastor of Nazarene Way Church, delivers a Sabbath sermon that asks what Jesus actually taught and how that should shape a response to violence. He presents Jesus as a Hebraic prophet and Messiah who taught one God, not a Trinity or a deity, and he identifies his church with the Nazarenes and Ebionim. He says the group rejects the authority of Paul of Tarsus and follows only the words of Jesus. The sermon's central text is Matthew 26:52, where Jesus tells Peter to put his sword back in its place. Scott reads this as a rejection of armed defense even at the moment of Jesus' arrest, and he backs it with a line from the Clementine literature (Homily 12, chapter 62) that the true prophet "leads to peace, not bloodshed."

Scott then connects the verse to the 2025 shooting of Charlie Kirk, which he describes as a tragedy above politics. He quotes Kirk as saying that gun deaths each year are "worth the cost" of the God-given right to carry guns, and argues that the right to bear arms is a societal construct rather than a divine one. He also turns to animal sacrifice and diet, arguing from Genesis 1:29 that the Edenic ideal was seeds and fruit, and that the temple sacrificial system was a corruption Jesus opposed.

The sermon ends with a restatement of Matthew 26:52 applied to guns: "for all who take the gun will perish by the gun." Scott's case rests almost entirely on his reading of the verse and on his own assertions. The talk shows little argument about the textual history of Matthew, the Clementines, or the Pauline letters.

## Key Teachings

### Jesus as prophet and Messiah, not deity

Scott argues that Jesus was a prophet and Messiah who restored the "true religion" of Judaism, and that he taught the one God. He says there are "not three gods" and rejects the Trinity and the idea that Jesus was a "Roman pagan son of God." He links the deity doctrine to the second commandment, which he says it breaks. Scott also says "Christian" was a Greek term "invented 100 years after Jesus lived," so Jesus himself was a Nazarene, not a Christian. He presents the Nazarenes and Ebionim as the earliest continuation of this movement and says Nazarene Way is "restoring" it, disclaiming any link to the Church of the Nazarene.

### Rejecting Paul and the Pauline letters

Scott's main argument against Paul is about authority. He says Paul "never met Jesus" and "never walked with him," and that Paul's claim to have received a vision of Jesus, seven years after Jesus' death (as Scott puts it), is the same kind of claim Joseph Smith made. He asserts that Paul wrote 13 of the 27 New Testament books and calls him an apostate of the Torah, which he says the Nazarenes and Ebionim also held. Scott says his group therefore reads "nothing that Paul wrote" and instead looks to "the people that walked with Jesus." He does not discuss the content of any Pauline letter in this talk.

### Matthew 26:52 and refusal of armed defense

This is the sermon's core text. Scott quotes Jesus: "Put your sword back into its place, for all who take the sword will perish by the sword." He argues that the moment is significant because Jesus was "wrongfully arrested" for overturning the tables of the sacrificial trade, so violence "might seem justified." Scott reads the verse as a rejection of armed self-defense: Jesus "rejects armed defense even for his own life." He says this is the same principle found in other early literature, which he describes as "one resounding principle" across texts, "canonical or not."

### The Clementine literature and the "true prophet"

Scott supports his nonviolence reading by citing the Clementine literature, which he says the early Jewish Christians, Nazarenes and Ebionites used. He gives the reference as Homily 12, chapter 62, and states that the literature says the true prophet, Jesus, "leads to peace, not bloodshed," and calls Paul "the apostate of the law." The talk does not quote the passage itself, so listeners should check the text directly before relying on this.

### Animal sacrifice, the temple, and diet

Scott says the temple cult of the Sadducees and Pharisees had turned sacrifice into a "huge money market," and claims that "God didn't say" buying a cow and slaughtering it brings heavenly merit. He says Jesus saw the temple as a slaughterhouse and overturned it. He points to Genesis 1:29 as the Edenic ideal, saying humans were instructed "to eat seeds, to eat fruit, not to slaughter and kill animals." He also says that "the Ebionim, the Nazarenes... they're all vegetarians," but he gives no source for this claim in the talk.

### Charlie Kirk and "God-given rights"

Scott opens the Kirk discussion by calling the shooting tragic and saying it should be viewed "above politics." He quotes Kirk: "It is worth the cost of some deaths per year from gun violence in order to have our God-given right of carrying guns." Scott calls this a paradox and argues that "God-given" rights do not exist: "The fact is that having a gun is not a God-given right." He says the right to bear arms is a "societal right" created by governments, and that the tragedy "should awaken us not to bear more arms, but to repent of violence altogether." He also ties Kirk's support for Second Amendment advocacy to his own death, which he presents as an irony. The talk gives no source for the quotation and does not discuss the surrounding circumstances beyond the basic facts Scott states.
