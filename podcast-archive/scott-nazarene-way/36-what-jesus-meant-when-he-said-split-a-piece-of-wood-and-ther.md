## Summary

Rabbi Scott's sermon asks what Jesus meant by Gospel of Thomas saying 77: "Split a piece of wood. I am there. Lift up the stone and you will find me there." He reads the saying as a claim that God is found in ordinary, embodied action, not in temples, hierarchy, or sacrifice. He ties this to his own recent experience of rebuilding skateboards by hand, which he describes as a state of full presence.

The sermon also sets out the channel's wider framework. Scott argues that the movement of Jesus, carried on by his brother James and the Nazarenes and Ebionites, was distinct from the Christianity shaped by Paul, which he calls "the religion of Paul." He says these first followers kept the Torah and Hebrew Bible, used the Gospel of the Hebrews, the Gospel of Thomas, the Clementine literature and the Didache, and rejected blood sacrifice, animal killing and the view that Jesus was God. He presents Thomas as a non-gnostic witness to a sayings source (Q) behind the synoptic gospels. Most of these claims are stated without citation to primary texts or scholarship beyond a brief mention of Q, and Scott does not explain how he dates or weighs the sources.

The talk ends with Scott's reflections on finding God in the "kitchen sink," the morning commute and the way he now sees a spider outside his home. He closes with the saying "God is within you and he is among you" and a Shabbat greeting. Much of the second half is personal reflection rather than textual argument.

## Key Teachings

### Saying 77: God is found in the ordinary and in embodied action
Scott's central reading of Gospel of Thomas 77 is that Jesus locates the divine presence in everyday work done with the hands. He notes that Jesus was a tekton (craftsman or day laborer) and says that "Split a piece of wood, work on something with your physical hands, and I am there" means that one is "one with God through through action, through being fully present." He contrasts this with places where people expect to find God: "the golden halls of the Vatican," Bible study, or the synagogue and temple. His summary is that "God is in the ordinary" and that "The Nazarene message wasn't just about a new religion or a new way. It was a return to seeing God clearly."

### The Gospel of Thomas is not gnostic, and may preserve early sayings
Scott argues that Thomas is often dismissed as "gnostic" by people who "just call it gnostic heresy," and says that "if you read the gospel of Thomas closely it is not a gnostic text." He links Thomas to the scholarly hypothesis of a Q source (he glosses "Q" as the German word for source) behind Matthew, Mark and Luke. He suggests Thomas might be "a late instantiation of all the original sayings of Jesus." He gives no manuscript dates or specific scholarly arguments for this.

### Jesus as "son of man," not God, and the rejection of Paul's religion
Scott states that "we know from all of the texts that we have left left over of the Nazarenes and the Ibanim that they did not believe Jesus was God," that they believed he was the "son of man" (Ben Adam), and that "Jesus never called himself the son of God." He ascribes the "son of God" title to later Greek, Roman and pagan developments that "latched on to Paul's religion." He also argues that the New Testament is mostly Pauline, saying that 13 of its 27 books were written by Paul. Scott says the claim that Jesus was God contradicts the first commandment's prohibition on idols.

### Texts the community follows, and the Matthew manuscript he shows
Scott describes the Nazarene canon as the Hebrew Bible (Torah, Prophets, Writings) read "with a very critical and scrutinizing eye," plus the Gospel of Thomas, the Clementine literature and the Didache. He says the Nazarenes and Ebionites used the Gospel of the Hebrews, "a version of the Gospel of Matthew written in Hebrew" that lacked Matthew 1-2. He holds up a copy of a Hebrew Matthew, says he "took a razor to it" and removed the first two chapters, and claims the birth narrative was "prepended later on to meet and fulfill a prophecy which was a misreading of Isaiah 7:14." He presents this as established, but the talk does not show the manuscript or cite the scholarly basis for these claims.

### Against blood sacrifice and the killing of animals
Scott argues that Jesus was a vegetarian who "did not kill animals," and that "Thou shalt not kill" applies to all conscious animals. He questions the Hebrew Bible's sacrificial commands, asking why God would command "slit the throat of these animals to atone for a sin," and concludes: "That is a human being idea. Jesus came to help us see God more clearly and throw that away. And he was killed for it." He says the Didache teaches simplicity and ethical action. He does not cite a text for Jesus's diet.

### Works over faith (James vs. Paul)
Scott says the Didache and the book of James emphasize "deeds, not... hierarchy," and that the Pauline view, that one must believe in Christ's blood sacrifice and in his resurrection, is wrong. He attributes to James, "the brother of Jesus," the position "Works over faith. Works matter over faith." He does not quote the book of James, and he presents this as the view of Jesus too ("Jesus said the exact opposite").

### God is near and within
Scott concludes that "God is not far. He is already present. The kingdom isn't coming over here or over there." He cites a saying "Jesus said God is within you and he is among you," without giving a source, and reads the Gospel of the Hebrews as saying "God is near the humble."
