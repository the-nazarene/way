## Key Teachings

### 1. The verse as a test of true prophecy
Scott reads the line from the Clementine Homilies as a discernment rule. He breaks it into three parts. "The true prophet" refers to Jesus, which he says the context of the quotation makes clear. "Never brings about strife" means true spiritual authority does not fuel division or violence. "Ever peace" means *shalom* as "wholeness, justice, reconciliation," not just the absence of war. He cites Matthew 5:9 ("blessed are the peacemakers") as a parallel.

### 2. Gospel of Thomas 16 and "casting conflict"
Scott raises Gospel of Thomas saying 16, in which Jesus says people think he came to cast peace on the world but he came to cast conflicts. He says this seems to contradict the peacemaker reading, and that many people misread it. His reading is that Jesus was "dismantling false peace to reveal true peace." He says Jesus never harmed anyone with a weapon but created a great deal of conflict against a status quo he regarded as false.

### 3. The temple, sacrifice, and the overturned tables
Scott describes the Jerusalem temple as a system of animal slaughter and blood-letting that he says drew on Roman pagan and Canaanite practices and was profitable. He says Jesus entered the temple, overturned the money-changers' tables, freed the animals, and declared the practice the opposite of peace. He presents this as a model for protesting animal sacrifice and mistreatment today, "with love" and without violence. He does not give a source for the temple scene in this talk. He also cites Jeremiah's warning about "the hands of the lying scribes" as a reason to read the Hebrew Bible critically, linking it to the sacrificial cult.

### 4. Nazarene anti-accommodationism and the family names
Scott says the Nazarenes were anti-accommodationist (a point he attributes to Robert Eisenman). They resisted Rome but were not a militant messianic group, though they had learned the dead ends of militancy. As evidence he points to the names of Jesus' siblings. He says James the Just, Simeon, and Judas were named after figures such as Judas the Galilean and his son Simon, a militant messianic figure from Galilee, and that the brother later called Jude was rebranded by the Roman church.

### 5. Paul versus Jesus
Scott says most New Testament books are "not by Jesus or not about Jesus" and that Christianity follows Paul. He contrasts the Pauline formula of professing and believing in Christ's resurrection for salvation and entering the kingdom with Jesus' own teaching, which he says Jesus never stated that way. He notes that Paul "never walked with Jesus, never knew him," and that Paul wrote decades after Jesus' death.

### 6. The Clementine literature and the Nazarene sources
Scott rejects the label "pseudo-Clementine" as a derogatory invention of the church. He argues it is no different from the anonymous Gospels, whose authors are not known ("Mark didn't write Mark"). He cites Epiphanius, a fourth-century church father who catalogued 80 heresies, for the claim that the Nazarenes and Ebionites used a Gospel of the Hebrews (Matthew minus its first two chapters) and a Nazarene Acts. He says the Nazarene Acts likely corresponds to the Clementine Recognitions and the Sermons of Peter to the Homilies, or that both drew on a common source. Scott gives no independent evidence for these identifications in the talk.

### 7. The discernment test and animal ethics
Scott gives three questions for testing any teacher or movement: does it produce peace or strife, does it reconcile or divide, does it invite humility or domination. He applies these to family life, communities, online arguments, and to the Catholic requirement of baptism and church membership, which he calls a sign of domination. He ties the Nazarene practice to the original diet in Genesis 1, arguing that the "thou shalt not kill" principle extends to animals, and that "Humans are animals." He calls the Nazarene practice "active, courageous, costly, and radical" peace.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Gospel of Thomas 16 contradicts Jesus' peacemaking.** Scott says a careful reading shows Jesus was dismantling false peace, not endorsing violence.
- **"Pseudo-Clementine" means the text is worthless.** Scott argues that the Gospels are also anonymous and that the label is a derogatory church invention.
- **The Christian faith is Jesus' own teaching.** Scott argues it follows Paul, and that the resurrection-faith formula for salvation is Paul's, not Jesus'.
- **Jesus' violence-free ministry was passive.** Scott says Jesus "did bring about a ton of conflict" against the temple status quo, so nonviolence is not the same as inoffensiveness.
