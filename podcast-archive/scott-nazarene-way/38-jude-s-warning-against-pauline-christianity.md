## Summary

Scott asks what the letter of Jude, read through Jude 1:4, actually warns against. He argues that the "intruders" who turned God's grace into "debauchery" were not Paul himself but Paul's later followers and offshoots, whom he calls "Polyanity" and says became Christianity. His main evidence is textual and scholarly. He says Jude was probably written by someone in Jude's circle near the end of the first century, citing Bart Ehrman for that dating and for the view that the letter responds to Pauline offshoots. He also argues that Ephesians, which he says most scholars regard as non-Pauline, radicalized Paul's teaching on grace, and that Paul himself rejected that reading in Romans 3:8.

Scott's wider claim is that the original movement of Jesus was Torah-observant, carried forward by James "the Just," and that Paul's teaching on grace was later pushed toward antinomian conclusions. He also argues that Paul never met Jesus, that the New Testament was assembled much later, and that "Christian" means a follower of Paul rather than of Jesus. He ends by inviting listeners to drop the label "Christian" and adopt "Nazarene."

Several central claims are asserted without citation or with only generic attribution ("most scholars," "most scholars with a scrutinizing eye"), and some are rhetorically charged (for example, Paul described as a "grandiose narcissist" and the early church councils as "white-haired Roman pagan cult leaders"). ## Key Teachings

### Jude 1:4 targets Paul's offshoots, not Paul
Scott reads Jude 1:4 ("For certain intruders have stolen in among you... who pervert the grace of our God into debauchery") and argues that the grace language is the same vocabulary Paul uses, so the target is a misuse of Paul's teaching. He reads "debauchery" as the Greek "lentiousness" and says the danger is the idea that grace makes works irrelevant, so one may do things like eat temple sacrificial flesh. He argues that because Jude was written after Paul's death, the "intruders" must be Paul's followers, a point he attributes to Ehrman's reading.

### Jude's authorship and date
Scott says the letter claims to come from "the brother of James," which he takes to mean James the Just, the brother of Jesus and leader of the Jerusalem church. He reports that Eusebius and Jerome affirmed early circulation of Jude, but that its authorship was debated when the canon was decided, with some doubting it was written by James or Jude at all. Citing Ehrman, he says the letter was probably written at the very end of the first century by someone in Jude's circle, perhaps a descendant or follower, and that this does not change its value as a witness to Jewish Christian ideas. He also argues that most of the early Galilean followers, including Jesus, were poorly educated and that literacy makes the traditional authorship doubtful, though he does not give a source for the "96%" illiteracy figure he cites.

### Faith and works, and the Torah
Scott presents Jude and the letter's group as holding that righteousness comes through both faith and works: "Faith in God and actually doing God's will," including not murdering, keeping the Ten Commandments, and rejecting the blood sacrificial cult of the Jerusalem temple. He describes the letter as "Torah observant" and says it represents the Nazarene perspective. He ties this to the letter of James's claim that "faith without works is dead," which he says some early church figures disliked, and notes that James was debated for canonical inclusion.

### Ephesians 2:8-9 as a radicalization of Paul
Scott says that most scholars with a scrutinizing eye conclude Ephesians was not written by Paul but by a follower who took Paul's ideas further. He reads Ephesians 2:8-9 ("by grace you have been saved through faith... not the result of works") as the logical end of Paul's own message and argues that this is what Jude was condemning. He cites Romans 3:8, where he says Paul defends himself against the charge that grace encourages sin, as evidence that the authentic Paul would have rejected the later reading.

### Paul's letters, his authority, and the "Christian" label
Scott says 13 of the 27 New Testament books are attributed to Paul, but not all of them are authentic. He presents Paul as a self-proclaimed apostle who claimed a vision of Jesus, compares that claim to those of Muhammad and Joseph Smith, and says Paul clashed with Peter and had to answer to James. He argues that "Christian" (Christianos) was coined about a hundred years after Jesus's death and that the honest self-description for followers of Jesus is "disciple of the Way" or "Nazarene." He closes by asking listeners to stop identifying as Christian.

- **Jesus had the New Testament.** Scott says Jesus used the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh: Torah, Prophets, Writings), and that the New Testament was later assembled.
- **"Christian" means a follower of Jesus.** Scott says the term was coined about a century after Jesus's death and really means a follower of Paul.
- **Church of the Nazarene is connected to the Nazarenes.** Scott says his group has "nothing to do with" the Church of the Nazarene, which he describes as a Baptist church from the Wesleyan tradition.
- **Jude was written by Jude, the brother of James.** Scott says authorship is doubtful and that the letter was probably written later by someone in Jude's circle.
