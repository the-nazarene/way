## Summary

Scott asks what the Eucharist was in the earliest followers of Jesus, before it became, in his account, a Roman-influenced rite of eating Christ's flesh and drinking his blood. His answer is that the original practice centered on thanksgiving, shared bread, and unity, with no priesthood and no sacrificial or magical element. He builds this on two texts. The first is the Didache, which he describes as a Jewish Christian text dated to about 80 CE (some scholars, he says, place it nearer 100 CE). He reads its prayers over the cup and the broken bread closely. The second is the letters of Ignatius of Antioch, which he dates to about 100 to 110 CE, and especially a passage in which Ignatius says some people "abstain from the Eucharist" because they do not confess it to be the flesh of Jesus Christ. Scott reads the roughly thirty-year gap as the point where the meal changed from a symbol of gathering into a literal sacrament.

Scott's argument rests mainly on his reading of the Didache's wording: it speaks of "the holy vine of David" and of Jesus as "your servant" rather than "your God," and it says "cup" rather than "wine." He links these to James's supposed Nazarite abstinence from wine and to a claim that the Nazarenes and Ebionites were vegetarian. He also argues that Ignatius was a Pauline-leaning figure who "never walked with Jesus." He presents these as his own interpretation and draws the conclusion that the real "heretics" were the later Christians who rejected the Nazarene practice.

The talk ends as a practical sermon. Scott invites viewers to hold a simple Sabbath meal of real bread and optional wine, with a blessing over the cup and bread, and to focus on "awareness over ritual." He closes with a benediction, "as the grains once scattered became one bread," and asks viewers to like, subscribe, and comment. He also mentions a group of other Nazarene-oriented channels and a planned "Nazarene trident" of phases for his ministry.

## Key Teachings

### The Eucharist was originally thanksgiving, not sacrifice
Scott's central claim is that the early Eucharist centered on giving thanks. He says the Didache's word for the rite is "give thanks" and that "the original followers of Jesus" had "evidence of this" in the text. He argues that the meal concerns "gratitude, around knowledge, around unity, not as not animal sacrifice or not sacrifice of a human flesh or blood or superstition." His summary line is that "The Eucharist essentially means thanksgiving not sacrifice."

### Close reading of the Didache's prayers
Scott reads the Didache's prayers in sequence. Over the cup he quotes "first concerning the cup, we give you thanks, our father, for the holy vine of David, your servant." Over the broken bread he quotes "concerning the broken bread, we give you thanks, our father, for the life and knowledge which you made known to us through Jesus, your servant." He draws three points from this: Jesus is called "servant," not "God" or "Son of God" (which he attributes to Tertullian about 200 years later); the cup is not called wine; and the image of bread gathered from scattered grain stands for a unified church. He quotes the closing line as "let your church be gathered together from the ends of the earth into your kingdom." He reads the scattered-then-gathered bread as a call to unity among the Jewish sects of the period, which he lists as Pharisees, Sadducees, Zealots, Essenes, and Nazarenes.

### Wine, James, and the Nazarite vow
Scott argues the Didache's wording "cup" rather than "wine" reflects a practice of abstinence. He says "James the just... took the Nazarite vow" and "never drank wine," and that "it's probably approximately correct to say Jesus also did not drink wine." He acknowledges that the gospels describe Jesus turning water into wine and suggests this may have been a different matter. These are his inferences from the text and from James's reported practice, not documented facts he cites with sources in this talk.

### Diet and bread as the staple
Scott says the Nazarene diet was simple, "about 70% of the diet of Jesus's people... consisted of bread," with figs and jams, and that the first followers "did not drink blood or eat flesh." He says that "the Ebianites and the Nazarenes and James the just was vegetarian," and that John the Baptist and Jesus were too. He contrasts this with "a big slab of freaking protein and lamb shank."

### Ignatius and the shift from symbol to sacrament
Scott reads Ignatius as the first witness to a literal understanding of the Eucharist, and as the one who labels abstainers heretics. He quotes Ignatius: "They abstain from the Eucharist and from prayer because they confessed not the Eucharist to be the flesh of our savior Jesus Christ." He argues that Ignatius was "not part of the... Nazarene group" but a follower of Paul, that he "never walked with Jesus," and that his letters show a desire for martyrdom shaped by the Senecan "noble death" culture. Scott describes Ignatius as "clearly mentally ill," a characterization he offers without further support in this talk.

### Unity, not hierarchy
Scott ties the Eucharist to his movement's organization. He says "there is no institution. There is no hierarchy," and that the early practice "revolves around... unity, not hierarchy." He presents the Nazarene restoration as a network of like-minded teachers rather than a formal body, and says that hierarchical institutions "got you know Christianity into a perverse place in the first place."

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Christians consume Christ's body and blood, which the earliest followers also did.** Scott argues the first disciples did not eat flesh or drink blood and that the literal reading began with Ignatius.
- **Paul's gospel represents the true teaching of Jesus.** Scott says Paul "did not know Jesus," "taught his own gospel," and was "an apostate of the law."
- **Ignatius was a faithful saint and the orthodox authority.** Scott calls him a "forefather" of Christianity who shaped it toward Pauline ideas, not a reliable witness to the earliest practice.
- **Jesus drank wine.** Scott argues Jesus and James likely did not drink wine, and that the Didache's word "cup" rather than "wine" supports this.
- **The Nazarenes and Christians are the same group.** Scott says he is "not a Christian," but a Nazarene and "a follower of Jesus, not a follower of Paul."
