## Summary

Scott asks whether Jesus ever claimed to be God, and answers no. He argues that Jesus taught belief in one God, the Father, and that Jesus was a human "son of man" who was anointed (Messiah) by God. He frames this as the original religion of Jesus, which he says was later replaced by Pauline and Roman-influenced Christianity. Much of the opening is commentary on recent social-media posts about a Vatican doctrinal note on Mary's role in salvation, which Scott treats as an example of "idol worshiping" and a "slippery slope" toward devotion to Mary and to saints and objects. He also mocks a Catholic blessing of an ice cube, though the transcript gives no context for that claim.

The core evidence is textual. Scott cites a passage he attributes to the Clementine Homilies saying that the Lord "neither asserted that he was God nor taught his disciples to do so." He cites Mark 10:18 ("Why do you call me good?") and John 14:28 ("the father is greater than I"). He reads Epiphanius, whom he describes as a fourth-century heresy-hunter, as reporting that the Nazarenes and Ebionites held Jesus to be a man "chosen and anointed by God's spirit." His main logical argument is that if Jesus is the Messiah, meaning "anointed one," then someone must have anointed him, and God cannot anoint himself. He also says the virgin birth rests on a mistranslation of Isaiah 7:14 and that Matthew 6:9 shows Jesus teaching prayer to the Father alone.

The talk ends with a prayer for deliverance from idolatry, a call to like and subscribe, and a list of other channels and a book (Keith Akers's *The Lost Religion of Jesus*) for further study.

## Key Teachings

### Jesus as the anointed human, not God
Scott's central claim is that Jesus called himself "son of man" and was a human "sent by God." He says "Jesus never said, 'I am God.' He said, 'I am sent by God.'" He presents the Messiah title as the key: Messiah means "anointed one," so Jesus must have been anointed by someone, and "if Jesus were God then Messiah would be[ ] less for God cannot anoint himself." He reads the Ebionite and Nazarene position through Epiphanius, who he says describes them as holding that Christ "was a man born of Mary and Joseph and that he was chosen and anointed by God's spirit."

### Textual evidence he cites against Jesus' divinity
- **Clementine Homilies 6:15** (as Scott cites it): "our Lord neither asserted that he was God nor taught his disciples to do so." He calls these pseudonymous texts, comparing them to the Gospels, which he also says were pseudonymously written. He argues they likely draw on the same source material as the New Testament.
- **Mark 10:18**: Jesus separates himself from God. Scott says Mark is probably the earliest written Gospel and argues it predates the Gospel of the Hebrews, which he describes as an Aramaic text.
- **John 14:28** and **John 5:30**: Jesus speaks of the Father as greater and says he does nothing on his own.

### Prayer directed to the Father alone
Scott argues from Matthew 6:9 ("Our Father in heaven, hallowed be your name") that Jesus taught prayer to the Father, not to himself. He contrasts this with Paul, whom he calls "a narcissistic psychopath," saying Paul taught that one must pray to and believe in Jesus to be resurrected. He concludes "We worship the same God Jesus worshiped."

### Against the Trinity and the virgin birth
Scott says the Trinity was "invented by Tertullian in the second third century" (his dating). He argues the virgin birth in Matthew's opening rests on a misreading of Isaiah 7:14, where "young maiden" was rendered "virgin." He says Mary's perpetual virginity comes from a non-canonical text, which he names as the Protevangelium (he also says "ascension of James," which may be a transcription slip), and that Catholic dogma adopted it. He says the Council of Nicaea, which the transcript garbles as "Maca," was driven by the divide over whether Jesus was adopted at baptism or born of a virgin.

### Idolatry and the "slippery slope"
Scott applies the Second Commandment (he gives no verse) and Psalm 115:5 ("they have mouths but do not speak") to Catholic and Eastern Orthodox practice, including devotion to Mary, saints, statues and images. He cites John 4:23 ("true worshippers will worship the father in spirit and truth") and calls on listeners to "pull yourself away" from such practice. He contrasts church building costs (he mentions a reported $300 million Orthodox building) with serving the poor, which he says James the Just also focused on.

### Jesus' followers as the "Way" and the Nazarenes
Scott says Jesus's disciples called themselves followers of "the Way," and that externally they were known as Nazarenes. He identifies the later Nazarenes and Ebionites with the poor of Jerusalem and presents Epiphanius's heresy-hunting as accidentally preserving their views. He says the texts these groups used, such as the Gospel of the Hebrews, are lost and survive only in fragments.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus claimed to be God.** Scott says he did not, and cites Mark 10:18, John 14:28, John 5:30 and the Clementine Homilies in support.
- **The Trinity is original to Jesus' teaching.** Scott says it was invented by Tertullian in the second or third century.
- **The virgin birth is prophesied in Isaiah 7:14.** Scott says the verse was mistranslated as "virgin" and that the birth narrative was added to Matthew.
- **Mary's perpetual virginity is Scripture.** Scott says it comes from the non-canonical Protevangelium of James.
- **Praying to Mary and saints is acceptable devotion.** Scott says this is idolatry that violates the Second Commandment and redirects devotion from God.
- **The Ebionites and Nazarenes were heretics.** Scott says Epiphanius's label was a Roman-era judgment, and that these groups preserved Jesus' actual teaching.

## Connection to the Repo's Questions

- **What Jesus taught vs. what Paul taught:** Scott's talk is built on the contrast. He says "not Paul of Tarsus" and calls Paul's teaching about praying to and believing in Jesus for resurrection a corruption of Jesus' own instruction.
- **James and the Jerusalem community:** Scott names James the Just as Jesus' brother who focused on serving the poor, and presents the Jerusalem poor as the Ebionites' forebears. He gives no textual detail on James.
- **The Nazarenes/Ebionites:** This is the main evidentiary thread. Scott relies on Epiphanius and the Clementine Homilies as witnesses to their Christology, and he names the lost Gospel of the Hebrews.
- **Who Jesus thought he was:** Scott's answer is "son of man," a human servant anointed by God, not divine. The talk gives no detailed argument about the phrase "son of man" beyond saying scholars are confused about it.
- **How the gospels and canon were formed:** Scott says the Gospels were pseudonymous, that Mark's priority is probable, and that the virgin birth was added later. These claims are asserted, not argued.
- **Resurrection and afterlife:** Scott says Paul taught that belief in Jesus is required for resurrection, and that Jesus did not say this.
