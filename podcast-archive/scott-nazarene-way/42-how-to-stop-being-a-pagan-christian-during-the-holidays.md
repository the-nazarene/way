## Summary

The sermon asks which holidays Jesus of Nazareth actually observed, and how a modern believer can avoid "pagan" holiday practices while following his way. Scott, who presents the Nazarene Way Church channel, argues that Jesus and his earliest followers kept the feasts of the Torah (Passover, Unleavened Bread, First Fruits, Shavuot, Trumpets, Yom Kippur, Sukkot, and the weekly Sabbath) and nothing else. ## Key Teachings

### Paul as the source of the "distortion"
Scott says Paul "never walked with Jesus" and "really only essentially invented his own gospel." He claims about four of Paul's thirteen letters are pseudonymous, and that Paul's accounts "are contradicted across different texts." The talk offers no specific passages for these contradictions. He presents the restoration of "the original message of Yeshua" as the goal, in contrast to Paul's teaching.

### The Gospel of the Hebrews and the Ebionites
Scott says the Nazarenes and Jesus's earliest descendants read the Gospel of the Hebrews, which survives only in fragments "preserved by some church fathers and Epiphanius." He describes it as "essentially the Matthew minus the first two chapters of Matthew." He says the Ebionites (whom he glosses as "the poor ones") shunned wealth and were vegetarian. He takes their version to be "probably the original gospel of the Hebrews," and he attributes to them a correct account of John the Baptist as a Nazirite who ate honey and "mana" bread, not locusts. He argues that the Hebrew/Aramaic word for manna is nearly identical to the word for locusts, and that Greek translators probably confused the two. He says "Hugh Schonfield" (garbled in captions as "Hugh Shafield") and other 20th-century scholars began restoring "Jewish Christianity" studies, and that the internet now lets amateurs connect the research.

### Christmas as a re-labeled winter festival
Scott says Jesus and his disciples never celebrated Christmas. He argues that December 25 "overlaps the winter solstice rituals," so it was chosen for that reason, and not because it is Jesus's birthday. He connects the holiday to Saturnalia, Sol Invictus, and Germanic Yule, and to pagan symbols such as evergreens, mistletoe, and the Yule log. He draws a link between Satan or Lucifer and the symbol of Saturn, and says the Lord's Prayer's "deliver us from the evil one" points to this. He presents this as "the polar opposite" of Christ's religion and advises believers to keep Christmas, if they like it, in a purely secular sense.

### Easter as a re-labeled spring festival
Scott says Easter was "named after Eoster, which is an Anglo-Saxon fertility goddess," with eggs and rabbits as her symbols. He adds that Easter "origins also come from sunrise services that echo ancient dawn rituals," and that "the Romans merged spring fertility festivals with the resurrection observance." He offers a challenge of $1,000 (or $100,000) to anyone who can find Jesus endorsing eggs, rabbits, or Easter in the New Testament. The talk gives no source for the Romans' merger or for the sunrise-service origin.

### The virgin birth as a later invention
Scott says the Nazarenes and the disciples never believed in the virgin birth. He argues that "someone misread a prophecy in Isaiah 7:14" and that "the word for virgin meant young maiden." He dates the invention to between about 60 and 120 CE in one passage and "about 70 CE and 120 CE" in another. He says Paul's letters (which he dates to about 50 CE) never mention it, that it first appears "about like 80 CE" in the Greek Matthew and Luke, and that the Gospel of the Hebrews "do[es] not, surprise surprise, do not even have the whole virgin birth." He concludes that the story was "tacked on and concocted later on."

### The Torah feasts Jesus observed, and the Hanukkah distinction
Scott gives a list of holidays that he says Jesus observed: Passover, Unleavened Bread, First Fruits, Shavuot, Trumpets (Yom Terua), Atonement (Yom Kippur), Tabernacles (Sukkot), and the weekly Sabbath. He gives 2025 dates for each, without showing a calendar source, and says the Torah feasts are "Keyword, observe." On Hanukkah he says it is "not a Torah commanded feast" and "began in the 2n century BCE after the Mabian reve[lt]." He cites John 10:22-23 as showing that Jesus was in Jerusalem during the Feast of Dedication and "acknowledged it," then argues that "presence does not equal mandate." He says the nine-branched menorah for Hanukkah is much later than Jesus.

### Rabbinic Judaism and the "613 commandments"
Scott says the Mishnah and Talmud "developed 200 years after Jesus," and that rabbinic Judaism was a "rebranding of the Pharisees and the Sadducees" after Roman persecution and the loss of the Temple. He says "Moses gave us ten commandments" and that these "blew up into 613 commandments unknown to Jesus." His conclusion is that a modern synagogue would not be recognized by Jesus, so visiting one does not recover his practice. He also reports having attended a Messianic Jewish congregation, which he judges to be "really just Pauline Christianity in disguise," although he says "the people are amazing."

### Diet, animals, and nonviolence
Scott says Jesus, John the Baptist, and James the Just were all vegetarian, and that "thou shalt not kill extended to animals as well." He says Jesus "freed the animals when he turned over the tables in the temple," and that the Jerusalem temple system is comparable to "factory farming" today. He also cites the early martyrs and "put your sword down to Peter" as evidence of Jesus's pacifism. He says that the Church's shift to accepting violence came with a fifth-century council that "said the ends justify the means." He gives no source for the council or for the Jesus-freed-the-animals claim.

### The Star of David, the fish, and a rebrand
Scott explains that he is changing his church's logo because he found that the Star of David "was actually invented in the 3rd to 7th century," and that it was not a primary Jewish symbol. He also says the Star of Refon (or Rephan) in Acts is a source of confusion. The talk gives no sources for the symbol's date. The replacement image is the River Jordan, tied to John's baptism of Jesus. He calls Jesus "Ben Adam," "son of man," and says Jesus "never referred to himself" as the Son of God in the mythological sense.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **John the Baptist ate locusts.** Scott says the Ebionite account has him eating honey and manna, and that "locusts" is a translation error for the word *mana*.
- **The virgin birth was part of the earliest gospel.** He argues it was a later addition derived from a misreading of Isaiah 7:14.
- **Christmas and Easter are Jesus's holidays.** He says they came from winter solstice and spring fertility festivals, and that Jesus observed neither.
- **Attending Hanukkah or a synagogue recovers Jesus's practice.** He says Hanukkah is not a Torah feast, that presence does not equal mandate, and that rabbinic Judaism came about 200 years after Jesus.
- **The Star of David is an ancient symbol of Jesus's people.** He says it was invented in the third to seventh century.
- **The Trinity is compatible with the first commandment.** He argues that a triune God violates the commandment to have "one God."
- **"Jew" is the proper name for Jesus's people.** He says Jesus "was not a Jew," and that the term was invented later.
