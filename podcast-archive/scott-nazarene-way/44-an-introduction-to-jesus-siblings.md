## Key Teachings

### Jesus had four brothers and at least two sisters

Scott's starting text is Mark 6:3, which he calls "the very beginning of Mark, the shortest gospel" and "the one that scholars think is the... first written gospel." He reads from it a list of four brothers: James, Joseph (also called Joses), Judas, and Simon. He adds that at least two sisters are mentioned, and says the same roster appears in John and Matthew "hidden in plain sight." He reports that Simon may be Simon the Zealot, a view he attributes to James Tabor.

He describes James as "the central... person of the three pillars of Jerusalem," with Cephas/Peter and John as the other two. He identifies Judas as the author of the Epistle of Jude, which he says was renamed to avoid confusion with Judas Iscariot. He claims Jude quotes the Book of Enoch, which he says was excluded from the canon at a council of bishops in the fourth century.

### Witnesses to the brothers: Paul, Josephus, and Hegesippus

Scott cites several early sources. He says Paul's letters, written around the 50s CE, refer to "brothers of the Lord" (no letter or verse is given). He cites Josephus, whom he describes as a historian "completely independent, completely unbiased," who "describes James as the brother of Jesus." He then turns to Hegesippus, a "Jewish Christian" writer whose works Scott says are lost. He says Eusebius, the church historian, quotes and preserves some of Hegesippus's writings, and that Hegesippus "refers to Jude as Jesus's brother." Scott says there are "multiple independent sources confirming this," but he names only these.

### The Catholic reading of "brothers" and the Catechism

Scott quotes the Catechism of the Catholic Church, paragraphs 499 and 500. He says paragraph 499 declares Mary "ever virgin," meaning virgin before, during, and after the birth of Jesus, with her hymen intact. He says paragraph 500 teaches that the Bible's "brothers" are not children of Mary, with James and Joseph described as sons of "another Mary." He calls this a strained "straw man" reading that contradicts Mark 6:3 and the rest of the New Testament.

### The Nazarenes, Ebionites, and the virgin birth

Scott relies on Epiphanius, whom he calls a church father who listed about 80 heresies, for the claim that the Nazarenes and Ebionites used a Hebrew Gospel of Matthew that lacked the first two chapters. Those chapters contain the virgin birth narrative, which Scott says was added later. He says Jerome misread the Nazarenes as accepting the virgin birth and that this error reached Wikipedia. He also says Isaiah 7:14 was mistranslated: the Hebrew says "young maiden," but a Greek translator wrote "virgin."

### The Protoevangelium of James and the making of Mary

Scott argues that the idea of Mary's perpetual virginity comes from the Protoevangelium of James, which he calls apocryphal and outside the canon. He says the Catholic Church "cherrypicked" this text while excluding what he calls the canonical evidence of Jesus's siblings. He reads the Marian elevation as a product of Roman religious culture, linking it to a preference for divine figures and to festivals he says were absorbed into Christianity. He says the dogma was fixed in the fourth century, citing Nicaea and the Nicene Creed. He recommends Tabor's *The Lost Mary* as a corrective.

### Paul as founder, and the Nazarene "Way"

In his opening, Scott says Paul "invented Christianity," describing him as a man who never met Jesus and who claimed a vision seven years after Jesus's death. He says "we do have evidence that he was clearly a grandiose narcissist," while adding that he doesn't think Paul "meant evil." He compares Paul's claim to visionary claims made by Muhammad and Joseph Smith. He contrasts this with a "Nazarene" movement he says Jesus began and that was carried on by James. He says the group called itself "disciples of the way," citing Acts of the Apostles, and was called Nazarenes externally, a term he derives from *netzer*, "branch."

### Jesus's social background

Near the end, Scott introduces Meyer's *A Marginal Jew* as a description of Jesus's family as poor Galileans. He says Joseph was a *tekton* (a day laborer or builder, which he distinguishes from "carpenter"), and that the region was poor. He says he got the book from references in Aslan's *Zealot*, and he is still reading it at the cutoff.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Mary remained a virgin her whole life (perpetual virginity).** Scott says this doctrine comes from the apocryphal Protoevangelium of James and was made dogma later. He argues that Mark 6:3 and the rest of the New Testament show Jesus had brothers and sisters.
- **"Brothers" in the Bible means cousins.** Scott says the Catholic "another Mary" explanation is a strained reading that the text does not support.
- **The virgin birth was part of the original Jesus movement.** Scott says the Nazarenes and Ebionites did not accept it and used a Gospel of Matthew without the first two chapters.
- **The Nazarenes are named for the town of Nazareth.** Scott says the name comes from *netzer* ("branch") and that it is debated whether Nazareth existed at the time.
- **Paul faithfully carried on Jesus's teaching.** Scott says Paul "invented Christianity," and he treats Paul's visionary claim as unreliable.
- **Christian festivals were sacred to Jesus.** Scott says Christmas and Easter were absorbed from Roman pagan festivals, and he urges listeners to celebrate the holidays Jesus observed.
