## Key Teachings

### 1. Jesus had brothers and sisters, and the plain text says so

Scott cites Mark 6:3, which names four brothers (James, Joseph, Judas, Simon) and mentions sisters. He notes that the name Judas was later changed to Jude in the 4th century, and jokes about a Catholic who named a daughter Jude thinking it was a woman's name. He also says the sibling claim appears in John, and that James Tabor thinks Jesus had at least eight siblings (Tabor's basis is not given in the talk).

### 2. Paul and Josephus as early witnesses

Scott says the earliest evidence comes from Paul, who calls James and others "the brothers of the Lord." He contrasts Paul with Josephus, whom he calls non-affiliated and "if anything anti-Christian," and who also refers to James as Jesus's brother. Scott adds that Paul "never met Jesus," a claim he compares to the visionary claims of Joseph Smith and Muhammad. He says Hegesippus, preserved by Eusebius, was an early Jewish Christian from a line that had known Jesus's disciples.

### 3. The Catholic doctrine of perpetual virginity and its sources

Scott reads Catechism of the Catholic Church paragraphs 499 and 500 as teaching that Mary was a virgin before, during, and after Jesus's birth, and that the "brothers" are sons of another Mary or cousins. He argues the perpetual virginity idea does not come from any canonical text, but from the apocryphal Protoevangelium of James. He attributes the doctrine to Greco-Roman tastes for divine virgin figures, to a desire to elevate Mary above ordinary human life, and to dogma "fixed by the late 4th century councils." He says these claims are his own reading of history, not proven.

### 4. Meier's linguistic argument: "brother" means brother

This is the core of the talk. Scott quotes Meier that "there is not a single clear case where quote brother means cousin or even stepbrother" in the New Testament (p. 331). He notes the Greek word *adelphos* is used in its natural sense, and that Paul uses the separate word for cousin in Colossians 4:10 but not for Jesus's brothers. Scott reads Meier's verdict (p. 332) that from "a purely philological and historical point of view" the siblings reading is most probable, and that some church writers kept it alive until the late 4th century.

### 5. The Nazarenes, Ebionites, and the early gospel

Scott says the Ebionites ("the poor ones," which he derives from Hebrew) had a gospel lacking Matthew 1-2, so it had no magi or nativity story. He says that gospel reports that Jesus first appeared to James after the resurrection and ate bread with him, "not flesh, not lamb." He treats this as evidence that James led the movement after Jesus's death. He does not cite the gospel's text or a source for this account in the talk.

### 6. Holidays, diet, and the Nazarite vow

Scott recommends celebrating the holidays Jesus would keep, such as the Feast of Unleavened Bread, and suggests ritual washing in running water at the new year, following John the Baptist and Jesus at the Jordan. He criticizes a megachurch Christmas event with live camels and animals. He recommends vegetarianism, which he says the Ebionites and Nazarenes practiced, and says the optional Nazarite vow was taken by James the Just and John the Baptist.

### 7. Jesus as "son of man," and critical reading of scripture

Scott says Jesus called himself *bar 'enash* ("son of man") in Aramaic and never called himself "son of God." He argues that scripture contains "scribal errors" and insertions, such as the sacrificial temple system Jeremiah criticized as the "pens of the lying scribes," and that Jesus read the Torah, Prophets, and Writings critically to find the "probably approximately correct" version.
