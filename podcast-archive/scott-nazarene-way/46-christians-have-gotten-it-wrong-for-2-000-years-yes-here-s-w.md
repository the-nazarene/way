## Summary

The talk asks whether mainstream Christianity has misunderstood Jesus for about 2,000 years, and whether the original practices of Jesus and his first followers, whom Scott calls the Nazarenes (later the Ebionites), were replaced by a religion built on Paul of Tarsus and shaped by Constantine. Scott opens by explaining the Christmas tree in his background (his wife loves Christmas, and he says it is a secular family choice), then moves to the main sermon.

His argument rests on a few claims. He says the Nazarenes' Gospel according to the Hebrews resembled Matthew but lacked its first two chapters, so it had no nativity or virgin birth. He attributes this knowledge to Epiphanius, Eusebius and Hegesippus. He says a mistranslation of Isaiah 7:14 in the Septuagint produced the virgin-birth idea. He argues that the victors wrote history and destroyed rival texts, which he uses to explain why the Nazarene view is poorly documented. ## Key Teachings

### 1. Restoring the Nazarene/Ebionite way against Paul and the later church
Scott says the church he leads is "restoring the first century sect" of the Nazarenes, the group Jesus belonged to and that the people who walked with Jesus belonged to. He distinguishes this from Paul, whom he says never knew Jesus and whose followers "essentially concocted" Christianity. He says the later name Ebionites means "the poor ones." He describes his own position as "Jesus maximalist," not "Paul of Tarsus maximalist," "Pope maximalist," or "Catholic catechism maximalist." The aim, in his words, is to follow "the actual words and practices of Jesus the Nazarene and his disciples." He also says Paul's teaching that one only needs to profess faith in the death and resurrection to be "saved forever" was not Jesus's teaching and was not held by Jesus's disciples.

### 2. The nativity story and the Gospel according to the Hebrews
This is the talk's central example. Scott says the Nazarenes and Ebionites used the Gospel according to the Hebrews, which he says is known through Epiphanius, Eusebius and Hegesippus. He says it was "very similar to the Gospel of Matthew" except that it "was missing the first two chapters of Matthew," which contain the genealogy and the nativity. He says the virgin-birth idea came from a mistranslation of Isaiah 7:14 when the Hebrew word for "young woman" was rendered as "virgin" in the Septuagint. He adds that Mark, which he calls the first and oldest complete Greek gospel, contains no virgin birth. Scott does not give chapter-and-verse references for the Hebrews gospel fragments in this talk, so the claim depends on the patristic sources he names.

### 3. Non-violence, Torah and vegetarianism
Scott presents Jesus as non-violent, faithful to the Torah, and as someone who "taught mercy, not sacrifice." He says Jesus and his earliest followers were vegetarian, and he argues that the Nazarenes' vegetarianism is documented in Epiphanius, Eusebius and Hegesippus but was rarely written about because adherents were persecuted. On the Torah, he says it must be read "with a critical eye," separating what is true from later additions. He cites Jeremiah 8:8 for the "pen of the lying scribes" and credits Cameron Waters for drawing his attention to it. He also argues that the commandment "thou shalt not kill" should guide Christmas practice, which is the basis of his challenge to viewers.

### 4. Who Jesus claimed to be
Scott says Jesus was the Messiah, and he glosses "Messiah" as "anointed one," not "God man" or "son of God." He says Jesus called himself "the son of man, Ben Adam in Aramaic," and that the Nazarenes and Ebionites, along with Jesus's brother, did not believe Jesus was God's son in the later sense. He says the divinity of Jesus was added after Jesus's message "was merged into the Roman pagan Greek culture." He points viewers to Aaron Abke's video on what he calls the "five properties of God."

### 5. Constantine, Nicaea and the suppression of rival views
Scott's main historical claim is that Constantine and the Council of Nicaea enforced a state-sponsored creed and suppressed dissenters. He reads from Pines's paper (which he says is at page 31) that "318 men gathered in Nika and formulated a symbol of faith which was accepted and made obligatory by Constantine." He reads a further passage saying that those who dissented were killed, that Constantine "continued for 50 years to put to death people who did not worship the cross," and that those who did not eat pork were killed. He links this to the Nazarenes' dispersal after the destruction of the temple in 70 CE and the persecution after the Bar Kokhba revolt, which he dates to about 130 CE. He says Constantine chose a single religion over "truth" to end civil conflict among rival cults, which he identifies as Mithraism and Christianity.

### 6. Why the Nazarene view is poorly documented
Scott argues from the principle that "history is written by the victors." He says the Nazarene texts were destroyed when the movement was scattered and persecuted, so their survival in fragments, quotations and "lost gospels" was never a sign of their falsity. He points to Eusebius's preservation of Hegesippus, and says Pines's paper is an accidental survival of a Jewish Christian text inside an Islamic text. He also rejects the popularity argument: "there was at one point in time 100% of the homo sapiens on the planet" who believed the world was flat, so majority belief proves nothing. Scott's claim that the Nazarene view was "about 20%" wrong in mainstream Christianity is stated without support.
