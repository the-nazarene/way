## Summary

Scott, preaching at Nazarene Way Church, asks what joy Jesus actually taught. He answers that Jesus does not forbid joy but limits its source: joy is relational and "love oriented," found through love for one's brother and for one's enemies. His main evidence is a single quotation. Scott says Jerome, in a commentary on Ephesians 5:4, cites a saying of Jesus from the Gospel of the Nazarenes (which he equates with the Gospel according to the Hebrews): "Never be joyful except when you look upon your brother with love." He argues that Jerome chose this unfamiliar saying over the canonical Gospels, and that the original Nazarene writings were suppressed, which he blames on Epiphanius in the fourth century. Several of his wider claims, including that Jesus spoke Aramaic and not Greek, are asserted without sources.

Scott then develops the idea through the cross, the Hebrew Bible, and a contrast between "true" and "false" joy. He says joy must pass through love, that loving an enemy means praying for and forgiving them rather than spending time with them, and that the question is "whom do I love?" rather than "am I happy?" The talk ends with a challenge to pray tonight for a person who has wronged the listener, and the sermon closes with "That is where joy comes from."

## Key Teachings

### Joy as love-oriented, not self-generated
Scott's central claim is that Jesus "does not forbid joy" but "limits its source." He says joy "is relational," "not private," and "not something that you can even attain by your own accomplishments." He contrasts this with the "hedonic treadmill," illustrated by athletes who move on after winning. In his words, "joy must pass through love" and "The kingdom of God, the kingdom is communal. It's not emotional."

### The Jerome citation (Ephesians 5:4) and the Gospel of the Nazarenes
This is the basis of the whole sermon. Scott says Jerome, in a commentary on Ephesians 5:4 ("Never be joyful"), quotes Jesus from the Gospel of the Nazarenes / Gospel according to the Hebrews. Scott's reasoning is that Jerome knew Matthew, Mark and Luke but chose to quote this saying instead, so the saying must have been important. He also says Jerome "is said to have translated the gospel of the Hebrews." The talk offers no manuscript, edition, or scholarly discussion of this quotation. Scott's translation is the only wording given.

### Loving enemies and praying for them
Scott takes "your brother" to include enemies: "those people that when you think of, you want to stop thinking of them." He says Jesus commands prayer for enemies across "many of Jesus's teachings, canonical or non-cononical, in the dedicay or even in a lost verse." He draws on a passage he attributes to "the apostles" in the "dedicay" (apparently the Didache; the captions garble the name) that contrasts ordinary love with loving enemies. He says loving an enemy means forgiving, praying, and "think[ing] the best of people," not being around them. He uses Jesus' criticism of the Pharisees and Sadducees as "a brood of vipers" as an example: Jesus condemned them but prayed for them and did not spend time with them.

### The cross as the ultimate other-oriented love
Scott says the cross is central because it is "the ultimate act of other oriented love." He presents Jesus' death as showing commitment to "the restoration of the original message, the true message of God." He says the message was later corrupted by "lying scribes," who "created this temple sacrificial cult" and inserted "misguided scripture" into the Hebrew Bible. The argument is that joy "is rooted in faithfulness not comfort" and that "There is no joy apart from self-giving love."

### The Hebrew Bible as the canon Jesus used
Scott describes the Hebrew Bible as having three parts (Torah, Prophets, Writings) and says this "is the same cannon and Bible Jesus used" and that Nazarenes use. He says Jesus told his hearers to read it and understand it through Jesus' words, and that Jesus "came to fulfill it" rather than abolish it. He says the New Testament was created in the fourth century and that the label "Old Testament" is one Jesus "would never call it."

### True joy versus false joy
Scott lists the joys he rejects: joy from ignoring one's neighbor, joy that requires "superiority," joy "that depends on someone else's loss," and joy from material things. He says, "We are not anti-joy. We are anti- false joy." He identifies the practical question as "what am I joyful about that has nothing to do with love?" and concludes that "If joy excludes others, it will not last."

### Historical claims about Jesus' language and the suppressed gospels
Scott makes two historical claims without supporting evidence: that Jesus spoke Aramaic and that "every single legitimate scholar" rejects the idea that he preached in Greek. He says the original gospels of Jesus' disciples were lost because Epiphanius "declared it heretical" in the fourth century, and that the surviving traces are "tidbits" preserved in patristic commentaries. These are his assertions, and the talk does not cite the scholars or texts involved.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus preached in Greek.** Scott says this is "absolutely rubbish" and that Jesus spoke Aramaic. He attributes this to apologists with an agenda and makes the claim without citing evidence.
- **Joy is forbidden or depends on circumstances.** Scott says Jesus does not forbid joy or tie it to achievement or possessions. He says it is tied to love for others.
- **Loving enemies means spending time with them.** Scott says loving an enemy means praying for them, forgiving them, and moving on, not being "sucked into their world."
- **Jesus came to abolish the Hebrew Bible.** Scott says Jesus "came to fulfill it," and that the Hebrew Bible, Torah included, is the canon Jesus used.
- **The New Testament and Christianity are Jesus' original teaching.** Scott says the church created the New Testament in the fourth century and that a "Roman pagan cult" developed from that point, which he says distorted the original movement.
