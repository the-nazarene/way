## Summary

Scott asks what Jesus actually taught, and whether his first followers, the Nazarenes or Ebionites, preserved teaching that later Christianity softened. His focus is a verse from the lost Gospel of the Hebrews, which he says survives only in quotations by Jerome. The verse is a warning: "even if you are resting on my breast" but not doing the Father's will, the speaker says Jesus will cast you away. Scott argues that this Nazarene wording is harsher than the Matthew 7:21-23 version in the canonical Gospel, which he says was softened to fit Paul's teaching that faith alone brings salvation. He explains "resting on the breast" as a reclining-meal custom signifying covenantal closeness, not sexual intimacy, and concludes that intimacy does not cancel obedience.

Scott then gives practical steps for "walking the Nazarene way": refusing intoxication, not eating flesh (citing James the Just and John the Baptist), refusing to join gossip, praying for enemies rather than for oneself, daily immersion in running water, and honoring agreements or renegotiating them openly. He ends by saying his reading comes from many sources rather than the 27-book canon, which he claims was fixed 367 years after Jesus's death. The talk offers no manuscript evidence for the Nazarene variant beyond Jerome's report, and many of its historical claims (about Paul's appearance, scholarly consensus, James's diet) are asserted without sources.

## Key Teachings

### The Nazarene variant of Matthew 7:21-23
Scott reads the canonical Matthew 7:21-23 ("not everyone who says to me Lord Lord will enter the kingdom of heaven but only the one who does the will of my father in heaven") and then gives what he says Jerome recorded from the Gospel of the Hebrews. He says Jerome, who read Matthew in Hebrew, noticed a different wording. In that version, Jesus warns that even someone resting on his breast will be cast away unless they do the Father's will. Scott says the canonical version "gives a little wiggle room," while the Nazarene wording makes the warning "even more explicit." He does not give a page reference for Jerome's text.

### "Resting on the breast" as a meal custom
Scott asks what "resting on the breast" means and answers from a description of ancient dining. He says that in the first and second centuries, people reclined on their left side on couches around a shared table. He presents this as the setting for Jesus and his disciples, and says the image signaled "physical intimacy and belonging," covenantal family, and inner-circle friendship. He explicitly says "It was not sexual at all." He links this to the beloved disciple who rests on Jesus's chest in the canonical Gospel of John, and says that this image was "mentioned and is a core part of what Jesus said" only in the recovered Nazarene verse.

### Closeness is not obedience: against Paul
The central argument is that proximity to Jesus, including inner-circle discipleship and faith, does not guarantee salvation. Scott says "intimacy does not cancel obedience" and that "the kingdom of heaven comes by taking action." He contrasts this with a faith-alone doctrine he attributes to Paul of Tarsus and to Paul's later followers, whom he says built a religion on that basis. He says Paul's personal flaws are not the main issue, and that the later institutional development ("Eastern Orthodox," "Catholicism") was the real departure. These are his characterizations; he gives no sources for them.

### One God, Torah, and who Jesus was
Scott says Jesus "believed in one God," "did not believe in the trinity," and "did not believe he himself was God." He says Jesus believed himself to be the Messiah, and that John the Baptist was the forerunner foretold in scripture. He says Jesus came "to fulfill the Torah," and that the original followers "lived Torah faithfulness." He does not cite specific verses for these claims in the talk.

### Practical steps: intoxication, diet, gossip, prayer
Scott lists four practices, each tied to Jesus's movement:
- **Refusing intoxication.** He says James the Just, John the Baptist, and the Nazirite vow point this way, and calls for stopping the daily drinking habit, even one drink a night.
- **Not eating flesh.** He says James the Just was a vegetarian, that Jesus "probably" did not eat flesh, and that John the Baptist did not either. He reads "thou shalt not kill" as originally meaning all living beings, and recommends the documentary *Christ Spiracy* for more.
- **Refusing gossip.** He does not forbid humor, but calls for committing to step away from gossip, including by saying so to others.
- **Praying for enemies.** He says not to "rest in prayer alone" and to pray not for oneself or one's family but for those who wronged you.

He also describes daily cleansing in running water, a practice he attributes to Jesus's movement and John the Baptist, and advises keeping agreements. If an agreement no longer works, he says, one should renegotiate it openly rather than work around the other person.

### The canon and the lost Gospel of the Hebrews
Scott says the original Gospel of the Hebrews, of the Nazarenes, and of the Ebionites no longer survives in full. He says "various accounts of scholars" describe it as similar to Matthew minus its first two chapters, which he says he uses as a working model. He lists possible causes of loss: a Roman siege, a later Muslim siege at Caesarea (he says "Caes Cesaria or Cesaria"), or other means. He says he draws his teaching from "many different sources" instead of "a canonical list of texts of 27 texts" that he says were fixed "367 years after the death of Jesus." He gives no source for the date.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Salvation is by faith alone.** Scott says that Paul taught this, and that Jesus taught that action (doing the Father's will) is required, so intimacy or belief without obedience does not save.
- **"Resting on his breast" implies a sexual or effeminate relationship.** Scott says the image was a reclining-meal custom signifying covenantal friendship, and "was not sexual at all."
- **Daily prayer is enough to be a holy, faithful person.** Scott says Jesus rejected "rest[ing] in prayer alone" as a basis for security before God.
- **The Christian canon is the original teaching of Jesus.** Scott argues the 27-book canon was fixed centuries later and that the Nazarene sources preserve different wording.
