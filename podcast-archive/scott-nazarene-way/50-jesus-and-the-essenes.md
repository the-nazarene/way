## Summary

Pastor Scott opens this sermon, a continuation of the prior week's talk, by asking how Jesus should be understood in his "Hebraic" setting rather than through later Christianity. He argues that the religion named after Jesus is in fact the religion of Paul, and that Jesus's own movement, the "Nazarenes" or "followers of the Way," was overshadowed by a Roman-influenced church. He presents James the Just, Jesus's brother, as leader of the Jerusalem church, with Paul answerable to it.

The central question is whether Jesus was an Essene. Scott draws on the Dead Sea Scrolls, Josephus, and James Tabor's list of Essene characteristics (apocalyptic expectation, purity rituals, water immersion, opposition to the Jerusalem temple, and concern with the Zadokite priesthood). He reports the scholarly consensus, attributing it to Bart Ehrman, that Jesus was not an Essene. He then argues that a close relationship is "probably approximately correct" and "possible," that John the Baptist may have been influenced by an Essene offshoot, and that Jesus differed from the Essenes by focusing on love, mercy, and action rather than ritual purity and legal detail.

The talk ends with a call to action: the listener should pray for an enemy, which Scott presents as the core of Jesus's teaching and the way to embody his "true nature."

## Key Teachings

### Hebraic context versus later Christianity and rabbinic Judaism
Scott argues that Christianity, as it later formed, was a "Roman pagan cult" that merged pagan deities and mythos into a new religion roughly a century or more after Jesus. He says Jesus would not recognize rabbinic Judaism either, with its 613 commandments, and he prefers "Hebrew" to "Jew" for the world Jesus lived in. He locates Jesus in Galilee, contrasted with the temple city of Jerusalem and its priestly, commercial sacrificial system.

### Paul, James, and the Jerusalem church
Scott presents Paul (Saul of Tarsus) as the outspoken figure whom Romans and Gentiles mistook for the leader of the Nazarenes. He says Paul was called back to Jerusalem to answer to the "three pillars": James the Just (listed first, described as Jesus's biological brother, "largely written out of history"), Peter, and John. He cites Mark 6:3 for the brothers' names and Acts for the "Way" terminology and for Stephen. He also says that Acts may draw on an early source shared with the pseudo-Clementine literature. He frames Paul's "faith" as a professed belief that allows one to "step over a poor person," which he contrasts with Jesus's emphasis on action.

### The Essenes and the Dead Sea Scrolls
Scott says the scholarly consensus links the Qumran scrolls to the Essenes, as described by Josephus and Pliny the Elder. He lists features he associates with them: apocalyptic expectation of God's judgment on Jerusalem, Rome, and the Gentiles; withdrawal from Jerusalem; rejection of the temple priesthood; purity obsessions; water immersion; calendar disputes; and a focus on the Zadokite line. He explains the Zadokite issue through the Maccabean revolt and the Hasmonean takeover of the high priesthood, which he says the Essenes did not recognize. He credits Tabor with listing these nine properties and notes that early readers of the scrolls sometimes assumed they were Christian.

### Was Jesus an Essene? Probable, possible, and the scholarly weakness
Scott says that scholars such as Bart Ehrman conclude Jesus was not an Essene. Ehrman's point, as Scott relays it, is that Jesus shows no concern for the Zadokite priesthood, calendar disputes, or ritual purity boundaries, and that Jesus moved toward outsiders rather than withdrawing from them. Scott nonetheless argues that "probably approximately correct" is the right stance, that an Essene offshoot may have influenced John the Baptist, and that the name "Essenes" may derive from "Jesseans," linked to Jesse's lineage in Matthew and Luke. He also cites Robert Eisenman's speculation that James could have been the "Righteous Teacher" of the scrolls, with Paul possibly cast as the "Wicked Priest." He insists that it is "possible" Jesus was an Essene, and he says that calling it impossible is a scholarly "blind spot."

### Shared and divergent features: the ethics of Jesus
Scott lists overlaps: apocalyptic kingdom expectation, critique of the temple, water immersion, discomfort with animal sacrifice. He claims that Nazarenes "hated" animal sacrifice, that James the Just was a vegetarian, and that Jesus probably was too. Where Jesus goes further, he says, is in refusing to "major in the minor": he centers love of God and neighbor, defines neighbor as everyone, and prioritizes mercy over purity and separation. He points to the Matthew 5 teaching about walking the extra mile, which he reads as Jesus choosing moral high ground over retaliation against Roman soldiers.

### The canon and critical reading
Scott says Jesus had no fixed canon, only the Hebrew scriptures, which he read critically to separate God's intent from what "lying scribes" may have written. He says the Nazarenes adopt the same approach, drawing on the Old Testament, the New Testament, Apocrypha, the pseudo-Clementine writings, and fragments of the Gospel of the Hebrews. He describes the Catholic canon as a 4th-century bishops' vote, which he gives as "51 to 49." He also says the Nazarenes and Ebionites were declared heretical by Epiphanius, and he treats that label as a badge.
