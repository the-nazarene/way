## Summary

Scott, presenting a sermon for Nazarene Way Church, asks what Jesus meant by "pray for your enemies" and argues that the command was a practical challenge to cycles of hatred and retaliation, not sentimental niceness. He says the teaching sat "at the center of the way," a phrase he uses for the movement's internal identity, and that it was carried forward by the Nazarenes, whom he links to Jesus's brother James and to the Jerusalem community.

His main textual evidence is the Didache, which he calls "the dedicay." He quotes its opening line, "pray for your enemies and fast for those who persecute you," and says it predates parts of the New Testament. He pairs this with Matthew 5:44 ("love your enemies and pray for your persecutors") as a synoptic parallel. He also makes historical claims about the Didache's discovery, about Eusebius, and about Marcion's role in the term "New Testament." These are offered as the speaker's assertions, with no sources given in the talk.

The talk ends as a practical exercise. Scott asks viewers to name a person, system, or political figure they resent, to pray for that person's actual healing rather than a surface-level wish, and to fast for them when prayer feels insufficient. He presents this as the "Nazarene practice," closes with a Shabbat greeting, and promises weekly teachings.

## Key Teachings

### Stopping the cycle of hate at its root
Scott's central claim is that the core of Jesus's teaching was "stopping the cycle of hate" so that hatred "cannot reproduce inside us." He says ordinary human instincts toward an enemy are retaliation or withdrawal, and that both are driven by replaying the wrong done to us. Prayer, in his account, is more dangerous to that cycle than retaliation because it refuses the enemy's terms. He summarizes it as "prayer dismantles power not people."

### The Didache's command to pray and fast for persecutors
Scott cites the Didache (which he calls the teaching of "the original apostles") as the opening of the text, at "dedicay 1 verse three": pray for enemies, fast for persecutors, love those who hate you. He contrasts this with a Gentile or Roman pattern of praying only for loved ones. He claims the text was written "even before some of the texts that we have in the canonical New Testament." He also says the Didache was found by accident, in digging related to a graveyard or tomb. The talk gives no date, manuscript, or scholarly source for either claim.

### Matthew 5:44 and the enemy within
Scott turns to Matthew 5:44, which he describes as a synoptic witness to the same command. His further argument is psychological and spiritual: an enemy is a "test, not a target," and the real danger is becoming like the enemy internally. He says unforgiveness makes a person their own enemy. He also quotes Jesus as saying "My kingdom is not of this world," and reads that kingdom as "within you and without you." He gives no verse for that quotation.

### Fasting as the next step
Scott says Jesus commanded fasting for enemies too, and that this practice is "actually removed from Matthew" but preserved in the Nazarene tradition. He says James "adjusted" it and that the Didache commands it. He presents fasting as the step when prayer and words fail, and gives a personal example: a friend named Cameron Waters who fasted on water for 21 days.

### Practical exercise: name, pray, fast
The closing section gives a concrete method. Name the enemy honestly and specifically. Pray for that person's actual healing and for the thing driving their harm. If prayer resists, set a fast, even a short one ("until let's say 2:00 p.m."). Scott says the purpose is to care for the other person, since an unprayed-for enemy "live[s] internally and control[s] you."

### Nazarene identity and lineage
Scott frames the talk within a larger claim: that the Nazarenes were Jesus's original movement, descended through James "the just" and "the brother of the Lord," and that Eusebius shows what early Christianity looked like before the name "Christianity" was used at Antioch. He also says Paul was mistaken for the ringleader of the Nazarenes by Gentiles and Romans, and calls him "the kind of psychopathic apostle." These are asserted without citation in this talk.
