## Summary

Scott asks why Christians worship on Sunday when, he argues, Jesus and his earliest followers kept the Sabbath on Saturday. He argues that the Nazarenes, the Jewish-Christian movement he identifies with Jesus's original community and with James the Just, kept Saturday as the Sabbath, fasted on Wednesdays and Fridays, and observed the Torah. His evidence is a patristic witness, Epiphanius's *Panarion* (which he calls the "29th heresy"), the *Gospel of Thomas* (saying 27), the *Didache* (chapter 8, which he dates "around the turn of the first century"), and a contrast with Ignatius's letter *To the Magnesians* 9. He presents Ignatius as the figure who moved Christian worship to Sunday and "Rome-fitted" the message for Roman audiences. He also rejects the idea that Jesus's death was a blood sacrifice, and argues that Jesus was a messiah and prophet rather than a "Godman."

The talk is almost entirely Scott speaking, with no guest or host. It is also a personal and devotional piece: it closes with a description of how his congregation keeps the Sabbath (unplugging, prayer, journaling, fasting for enemies) and with an announcement that the congregation is moving to a larger space. Scott uses heavy, insulting rhetoric toward Epiphanius, Ignatius, and Paul ("Saul of Tarsus"), and several of his historical claims are stated more confidently than the cited sources, as quoted in the talk, can carry. ## Key Teachings

### The Sabbath on Saturday as the original practice
Scott's central claim is that Jesus and his disciples, and their disciples for "many hundreds of years," kept the Sabbath on Saturday and not on Sunday. He describes the Sabbath as running from Friday sundown to Saturday sundown, and he lists practices such as unplugging, prayer, shared meals with unleavened bread, and journaling. He calls Sunday observance a borrowing from a "pagan holiday" tied to the sun god. He offers no direct New Testament text for the claim that Jesus kept Saturday in a distinctive way beyond the general assertion.

### Epiphanius and the Nazarenes
Scott cites Epiphanius's *Panarion*, which he describes as a "medicine cabinet" against heresies, and says it lists the Nazarenes as its 29th heresy. He quotes Epiphanius: the Nazarenes "disagree with the Jews because of their belief in Christ, but they are not in accord with Christians because they are still fettered, meaning restrained by the law, meaning by the Torah." Scott reads "fettered by the law" as meaning that Jesus himself was bound by the Torah, and he says that Nazarenes practiced circumcision and Sabbath. He dates Epiphanius's writing to the 300s and says the Nazarenes were called heretics for Sabbath observance. He also says a Catholic monk wrote about the Nazarenes in the 8th century, and that the Nazarenes later "branched out" under names such as Ebionites, Elkasites, and Cerinthians. Scott treats these as one lineage ("At the core they were the Nazarenes"). He does not separately argue for the historical relationship among these groups in this talk.

### The Gospel of Thomas and fasting from the world
Scott answers the common objection that the *Gospel of Thomas* is a Gnostic text because it was found at Nag Hammadi. He says that "some scholars" find an Aramaic or Syriac base in it and that it may contain early sayings, possibly related to "Q." He quotes saying 27: "If you do not fast from the world, you will not find the kingdom. If you do not observe the Sabbath as a Sabbath, you will not see the Father." He reads this as an endorsement of Sabbath practice and of fasting, and he extends the idea to a 21-day water fast undertaken by Cameron Waters of Christ Conspiracy.

### The Didache and fasting on Wednesday and Friday
Scott says the *Didache* (which he calls the "Dedicay") is a handbook for early Christians, written "around the turn of the first century," with Jewish-Christian, or Nazarene, elements. He cites chapter 8, which instructs believers to fast on Wednesday and Friday, "the Friday before the Sabbath," to set themselves apart from the Monday and Thursday fasts of other Jewish groups. He takes this as evidence that Sabbath observance on Saturday continued in these communities.

### Ignatius, Sunday, and "Rome-fitting" the message
Scott's account of the move to Sunday centers on Ignatius of Antioch. He says that scholars "definitively" agree Ignatius wrote *To the Magnesians* 9, which speaks of people "no longer observing the Sabbath, but living in observance of the Lord's day." Scott says Ignatius was trying to proselytize Romans, and that he adapted, or "Rome-fitted," the message so that the Romans could keep their own sun-day. He says Ignatius recommended "moderation" in drinking wine and eating flesh. He also links the idea of the "noble death" and martyrdom to Seneca's time, and to Ignatius's own eagerness to die. He names "Saul of Tarsus" (Paul) and "Saul's followers" as the likely source of the shift, but he does not quote a Pauline text in this talk.

### Jesus as messiah and prophet, not a sacrificial God-man
Scott rejects the idea that Jesus's death was a "blood sacrificial" atonement, which he calls a "Roman pagan cultic idea." He says the Nazarenes "believed that Jesus was not some Godman deity figure like Caesar" and that he was a messiah and prophet who communicated what God was about, and who was killed because of the attention he drew from the Pharisees and Sadducees. He sets this against Ignatius's teaching about Christ's blood. He also says that worship of Jesus as God "breaks the second commandment," without giving the reference.

### Diet and the vegetarian claim
Scott says John the Baptist and "James the Just's brother" were vegetarians and asks whether Jesus was. He acknowledges that many people find a vegetarian Jesus "unlikely," and he answers that Jesus ate meat, based on the Roman mythic accounts of his life. He then argues that the Christian fish symbol (ichthus) and the "son of God" title were borrowed from Roman sources, and that Caesar's coin inscription was the original. This claim is only partly developed in the talk.

### Contemporary Nazarene practice
Scott concludes by describing what he and his congregation do on the Sabbath: they "unplug," pray, journal, light a candle, and meditate. They also fast for enemies, including political figures and Jeffrey Epstein, and he presents this as what sets the Nazarenes apart.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus and his first followers worshipped on Sunday.** Scott says the earliest followers kept Saturday, and that Sunday observance came later through Ignatius and Pauline influence.
- **The *Gospel of Thomas* is simply a Gnostic text.** Scott argues that its Nag Hammadi discovery led to a "guilt by association" judgment and that it may contain earlier Aramaic or Syriac material.
- **Jesus's death was a blood sacrifice for sin.** Scott calls this a "Roman pagan cultic idea" and says the Nazarenes did not hold it.
- **Jesus was a God-man.** Scott says the Nazarenes saw Jesus as a messiah and prophet, not a deity like Caesar.
- **Jesus was a vegetarian, or at least the Christian tradition has always been vegetarian-friendly.** Scott says the vegetarian view of Jesus is "unlikely" and argues that Jesus ate meat.
- **The fish symbol and "Son of God" title are distinctly Christian.** Scott says both were borrowed from Roman sources, with the "Son of God" title derived from Caesar's coinage.
