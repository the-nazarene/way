## Summary

Scott asks what the first Sabbath observance of Jesus' movement looked like and how a modern person might practice it. He argues that the Nazarenes kept the Sabbath on Saturday, from sundown Friday to sundown Saturday, and that the Sunday observance of mainstream Christianity came later. His main evidence is a passage from Epiphanius's *Panarion* (chapter 29), which he reads as saying the Nazarenes were "still fettered by the law, circumcision, the Sabbath, and the rest." Scott treats Epiphanius's hostility as a sign that the Nazarenes kept Jewish practice. He also points to the Gospel of Thomas, saying 27, which links the Sabbath to seeing the Father. He says he "believe[s]" Constantine moved the observance to Sunday to merge with a pagan holiday, a claim he offers without a source.

The second half turns to practice. Scott recommends treating Saturday as a day of rest and reflection (lighting a candle, prayer, handwritten journaling, unplugging from devices). He warns against turning this into a list of rules, which he links to Orthodox Judaism and Catholicism. He then argues that the Sabbath should include praying for enemies and fasting for them, which he presents as the teaching of Jesus and an "extremist" love that he contrasts with the failed militant messiahs of the period. He closes with a challenge to observe the Sabbath on Saturday and pray and fast for enemies, and he announces the group's new broadcast location. The talk is a single presenter's monologue. ## Key Teachings

### Saturday as the Nazarene Sabbath
Scott's central claim is that the Nazarenes ("the disciples of Jesus's disciples") kept the Sabbath on the same day Jews do, from Friday sundown to Saturday sundown. He says this is known "thanks to the writings of a man named Epiphanius." He presents this as a continuation of Jesus' own practice, which he says is now "lost" to the Sunday observance he attributes to Paul's movement.

### Epiphanius as a witness against his own target
Scott reads a line from Epiphanius's *Panarion* 29 in which the Nazarenes are faulted for still keeping the law, circumcision, and the Sabbath. Scott treats the complaint as evidence that these practices were characteristic of the group. He reads Epiphanius's condemnation as odd, since the practices were those "of Jesus and his followers." The reference is given roughly ("chapter 29 9 in uh section 7"), and the transcript is garbled at that point, so the exact section should be checked.

### Sunday as a later development
Scott says Sunday observance came from "the emperor Constantine," who "declared that Sunday because it was already a celebrated pagan Greek holiday," in order to merge the new religion with the people. He qualifies this with "I believe." He gives no source for the Constantine claim in this talk.

### The Gospel of Thomas and the Sabbath
Scott cites Gospel of Thomas saying 27: "If you do not observe the Sabbath as a Sabbath, you will not see the Father." He pairs this with the preceding line about fasting from the world. He presents Thomas as a sayings collection that may be older than the narrative Gospels, and he credits the Syriac and Aramaic character of the sayings to April DeConick (transcribed as "April Donic"). He argues against dismissing Thomas as "Gnostic" by reflex, which he calls "gnostic bucketing."

### Practical Sabbath observance and avoiding legalism
Scott's practical advice is simple: keep the day on Saturday, set it apart, pray, light a candle, journal by hand, and unplug from digital devices. He contrasts this with the detailed rules of Orthodox Jewish practice he describes from a friend, and with the "5,000 catechisms" of the Catholics and the "613 commandments" of Orthodox Judaism. He tells listeners not to "turn your life into a list of commandments," and says "Jesus taught you to not major in the minor."

### Praying and fasting for enemies
Scott links the Sabbath to Jesus' teaching to pray for enemies, which he attributes to "the dedicay" (the transcript's rendering, presumably the Didache). He adds that the Gospel of Thomas also says to fast for enemies. He proposes spending a morning fasting (water or tea only) for someone who wronged you, and he contrasts this with self-focused fasting for health or appearance, which he calls a "hedonic treadmill."

### Nonviolent love versus militant messiahs
Scott contrasts Jesus with militant figures of the period: "Barca" (the Bar Kokhba revolt), and "Judas the Galilean," from the same region as Jesus. He argues that those movements failed because they used "force," "war," and "pain," while Jesus used love. He reads Jesus' teaching about the extra mile as a refusal of the Roman demand, and he frames love for enemies as the key to Jesus' lasting success.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Sunday has always been the Christian day of rest.** Scott says the Nazarenes kept Saturday, and that Sunday observance was later, which he attributes to Constantine merging the faith with a pagan holiday (a claim he gives without a source).
- **Gospel of Thomas is simply a Gnostic text that can be ignored.** Scott says this is "gnostic bucketing," a reflexive dismissal, and he argues the sayings may reflect Aramaic or Syriac origins and be older than the Gospels.
- **Sabbath observance is about a detailed list of rules.** Scott says Jesus simplified the practice, and he warns against the rule-based approaches of Orthodox Judaism and Catholicism.
- **Fasting is for self-improvement.** Scott says fasting should be for an enemy, not for health or appearance.
