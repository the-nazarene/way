## Summary

Scott asks what Jesus meant in the parable of the pearl of great price (Matthew 13:45-46), in which a merchant sells everything he owns to buy one valuable pearl. He reads the parable as a contrast between accumulating many things and committing wholly to one thing. For Scott, the pearl stands for the kingdom of heaven, which he presents as a way of life to be pursued now, not something you add to a busy schedule or reach only after death. He sets this against what he calls a "Hellenized" Jesus, a Greek-influenced figure he attributes to Paul's followers, and argues that the original teaching survives in the practices of the early Nazarenes.

His evidence is mostly a set of claims about early sources and practice. He says the early Nazarenes used a Hebrew gospel, the "Gospel according to the Hebrews," which lacked the first two chapters and therefore the virgin birth narrative. He cites the Gospel of Thomas for the idea that "the kingdom of heaven is within you," and Epiphanius's writings on the Nazarenes and Ebionites. He also says that Jesus, John the Baptist, James the Just, and other relatives did not eat flesh, and he gives three practices as ways to "follow the way": abstaining from flesh (and optionally alcohol, under a Nazarite vow), giving clothes to the poor, and keeping the Sabbath.

The talk ends with an appeal to reorder one's priorities around "the way" as Jesus taught it. Scott gives no citations for several central claims, such as the diet of the named relatives and the content of the Hebrew gospel, so the group should check those against primary and scholarly sources before relying on them.

## Key Teachings

### The pearl as an exclusive commitment
Scott reads Matthew 13:45-46 as teaching that the kingdom is not one more addition to a life already full of pursuits. He contrasts "many treasures" with "one" and says the parable's point is that everything else becomes negotiable once one finds it. He also stresses that the merchant is a specialist "in search of fine pearls," so the kingdom is found by people who keep searching for what is genuinely valuable.

### The kingdom is present, not only future
Scott pushes back on the idea that the kingdom of heaven is something reached only after death through good deeds. He attributes to Jesus the teaching that "the kingdom of heaven is within you," citing the Gospel of Thomas, and adds that it is "right here right now if you follow the way." He does not discuss the Thomas passage itself or its text.

### The Hebrew gospel and the rejection of the virgin birth
Scott claims that the earliest Nazarenes followed one gospel, "the gospel according to the Hebrews," written in Hebrew. He says it resembled Matthew but lacked the first two chapters, which he identifies as containing the virgin birth narrative. He argues that the virgin birth story came from a misreading of Isaiah 7:14 and a later "mythologized" Greek story. He presents this as the basis for a Hellenized/Pauline Jesus he rejects. No manuscript evidence or scholarly source for the Hebrew gospel's contents is given in the talk.

### Three practices: diet, charity, Sabbath
Scott gives three concrete actions for following "the way":
- **Abstain from flesh.** He says Jesus, John the Baptist, James the Just, Simeon (called Simon the Zealot), and Jude/Judas Thomas did not eat flesh, and that many also took the Nazarite vow, which he says includes abstaining from alcohol. He reads "Give us this day our daily bread" as evidence. He points to Epiphanius on the Nazarenes and Ebionites as a source.
- **Give clothes to the poor.** He tells listeners to donate clothing, including unworn items, to the next poor person they meet.
- **Observe the Sabbath.** He says Jesus kept the seventh day and recommends shutting off devices, lighting a candle, praying, and reading the Hebrew Bible, which he says was later labeled the "Old Testament."

### Attribution of the Jude and James letters
Scott says the letters of Jude and James were written by Jesus's brothers and that Jude has been "basically written out and hidden." He offers this as part of the case that the family's teaching was suppressed. No argument for authorship is given.
