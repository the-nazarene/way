## Summary

Scott opens with a passage he read in Isidore of Seville, a 6th-7th century Catholic archbishop and historian, who says that Paul attacked the Ebionites in his letter to the Galatians. Scott asks what that implies. His answer starts from Galatians 2:11, where Paul writes that he opposed Cephas (Peter) to his face in Antioch. Scott argues that this shows Paul in direct conflict with a leader from the inner circle of Jesus' movement, and that Paul's opponents were therefore the original followers of Jesus rather than heretics. He says Paul's authority rested on a private vision, reported to the three pillars in Jerusalem (James, Peter and John), and that the Ebionites, whom he calls "the poor ones," rejected Paul as an apostate from the Law.

The talk's second half argues that the heresiologists Epiphanius and others split one movement into separate sects, the Nazarenes and the Ebionites, and that Epiphanius invented a founder named Ebion. Scott says "scholars" reach this conclusion but names none. He then lists what he takes to be Jesus' practices: Torah observance, water immersion, care for the poor, abstention from animal flesh, and possibly a Nazirite vow. He contrasts these with what he calls Pauline doctrine (blood atonement, the God-man, the Trinity).

The talk ends as a sermon exhortation. Scott calls listeners to "peel back the layers," follow the way, care for the poor, avoid eating animal flesh, and keep the Sabbath. Most of the argument is Scott's own reading of the sources, with few citations, and he presents the Ebion-invention and the Nazarene/Ebionite identity as consensus without naming the scholars who hold them.

## Key Teachings

### Paul versus Peter as evidence of an internal conflict
Scott reads Galatians 2:11 ("I opposed Peter to his face because he stood self-condemned") as proof that Paul confronted a man from Jesus' inner leadership. He notes that Peter was close to James the Just, who became leader of the Jerusalem assembly after Jesus' death. From this he concludes that Paul was in conflict with the original movement rather than with outsiders.

### Paul's apostolic claim rested on a private vision
Scott argues that Paul never walked with Jesus and was not taught by him for the two years of ministry that Scott attributes to Mark. In his view this means Paul's message was not the one Jesus gave his disciples. He compares Paul's "private vision" to the claims of cult founders, citing Joseph Smith as an example. He presents this as his own interpretive argument, not as a historical finding.

### The Ebionites and Nazarenes as one movement
Scott says Isidore of Seville, Epiphanius and earlier heresiologists divided what was really one group. He says the Ebionites ("the poor ones") were descended from Jesus' original followers and are the group Epiphanius describes in Panarion 30. He also says Epiphanius's Panarion 29 Nazarenes were probably a Jewish Christian group that later accepted the Virgin Birth. He attributes the claim that Epiphanius confused "Nazareans" and "Nazoreans" to unnamed "universal scholarly consensus."

### Ebion as an invented founder
Scott says Epiphanius "invented a character, or at least perpetuated a complete falsity," by naming the Ebionites after a man named Ebion. He says "there is no man out there named Ebion" and that scholars are unanimous on this. He adds that it is a mistake Wikipedia repeats. He presents this as established scholarship but does not name any scholar, source or text that makes the argument.

### Jesus' practices according to Scott
Scott lists what he believes Jesus practiced: Torah observance, water immersion/baptism, care for the poor, and not eating animal flesh. He says Jesus "likely took the Nazarite vow," in parallel with John the Baptist and James. He cites Hegesippus as saying that James did not eat animal flesh and treats Hegesippus as an early Nazarene source. He also uses this as a standard for criticizing modern prosperity preaching, naming Joel Osteen.

### Christology and the Trinity
Scott argues that Jesus was not understood as the Son of God and God himself. He says the Ebionites considered Jesus the Messiah and "Ben Adam" (son of man), which Scott says Jesus called himself. He says the Virgin Birth is a misreading of Isaiah 7:14 and that the Trinity is not something Jesus taught. He identifies "blood atonement" and the God-man doctrine as the core of Pauline Christianity.
