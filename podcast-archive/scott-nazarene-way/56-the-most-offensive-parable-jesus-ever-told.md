## Summary

Scott asks what Jesus meant in the Parable of the Workers in the Vineyard (Matthew 20:1-16), a passage that most listeners read as a story about unfair wages. He argues the parable is about the Kingdom of heaven and about how people relate to God. In his reading, the landowner pays late-hired and early-hired workers alike, and the point is that God's reward is not a transactional payment for early or extra service. He says that "comparison is the enemy" and that the workers who grumble have treated their faithfulness as a bargain.

Scott's evidence is mainly his own close reading of the Matthew 20 text as he reads it aloud, along with appeals to the Gospel of the Hebrews, which he says was lost, and to the church writers Epiphanius and pseudo-Tertullian, whom he says invented an Ebion figure. He also draws a contrast between Jesus, whom he describes as calling himself "son of man" rather than God, and Paul of Tarsus, whose doctrines he says modern Christianity follows. He frames the teaching as a call to "follow the way of the Nazarenes," including compassion, avoiding flesh, and observing Passover with unleavened bread.

The talk ends with a short exhortation to follow Jesus' way "without comparison," followed by a Shabbat Shalom greeting. Scott gives no cited scholarly sources for most of his historical claims, so those claims rest on his own assertion in this talk.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The reward is not transactional
Scott reads Matthew 20:1-16 and says the workers hired at dawn agreed to a daily wage and the later workers were promised "whatever is right." When the early workers received the same pay as the last-hired, he says they grumbled. His reading is that the landowner's reply ("Friend, I am doing you no wrong") shows that the Kingdom operates on generosity rather than on a contract. He concludes that everyone who "follows the way" receives "the same exact reward for following God."

### 2. Comparison and grumbling
Scott argues the grumbling workers represent people who "negotiate" with God, asking what they will get before they commit. He lists a hypothetical Christian who starts praying and attending church early to earn a "better payoff" than a person who converts late in life. He says both will receive the same reward, and that comparison with others "is the road to endless suffering." He tells listeners to "Go" without first asking what they will gain.

### 3. Jesus as "son of man," not God
Scott says "Jesus never called himself God" and "never called himself son of God," and that he called himself "the son of man, ben adam in Aramaic." He presents this as the original teaching, in contrast to what he calls "the doctrine of Saul of Tarsus," which he says promises salvation and eternal reward through faith. He says this is why the parable's reward is attached to following Jesus' instructions rather than to a creed.

### 4. The Nazarenes and Ebionim
Scott says Jesus' first movement called itself "people of the Way" internally and was known externally as the Nazarenes, later called Ebionim, "the poor ones." He says the patristic writers (he names Epiphanius and pseudo-Tertullian) invented a founder named Ebion, and that "biblical scholars know that the figure of Ebion never existed." He describes the Ebionim as the group that practices care for the poor and compassion for animals. He also says modern Judaism was "concocted by the rabbinic movement in 300 CE," which he calls a rebranding of the Pharisees.

### 5. The Gospel of the Hebrews and Matthew's text
Scott says the earliest followers used the "Gospel of the Hebrews," which scholars also call the Hebrew Gospel of Matthew. He says it was missing the first two chapters of the Greek Matthew and that it was probably destroyed by the "Roman pagan cult." He argues that the Greek Gospel of Matthew, with the first two chapters removed, still preserves the teaching he wants to explain. He offers no manuscript evidence in the talk for these claims.

### 6. Diet and Passover practice
Scott says the Nazarene way includes "care for the poor," compassion toward animals, and "Do not eat flesh," along with observing the Sabbath and the festivals Jesus observed. He instructs listeners to celebrate Passover by eating only unleavened bread with family. He explains this as a reminder of the Exodus departure, when the Israelites "didn't have time for their bread... to rise," and says it is "better to eat as a poor person with complete and utter freedom" than to feast while enslaved.
