## Summary

Scott, presenting a Nazarene Way Church sermon, asks what Jesus meant in the parable of the wheat and the weeds (Matthew 13:24-30). In his reading, the householder forbids the servants to pull out the weeds before harvest, so the "good seed" and the "bad seed" grow together until God judges at the end. Scott takes the parable as a warning against human judgment inside a community: the people of the Way should not try to remove those they consider false teachers or hypocrites. Judgment belongs to God and the reapers at harvest time. He supports this with the explanation given in Matthew 13:36-43, which he ties to the "Son of Man" imagery of Daniel 7.

Scott presents the sermon as a teaching from "Yeshua's eyes" rather than from Paul or the later church. He says his congregation teaches from the Gospel of Matthew minus its first two chapters, because it stands in for the lost *Gospel of the Hebrews*, which he says the Nazarenes used. He also cites the Didache and the Gospel of Thomas as other sources. He acknowledges that the Gospel of the Hebrews survives only in fragments and that the Matthew text is a substitute, so the choice is a working position rather than an established fact.

The talk ends with an application: follow Jesus' words, do not "rip out" the weeds yourself, and keep loving God, one's neighbor, the poor and the Ebionim. The first part of the video is largely church announcements (services, a website, a planned home meeting place, a podcast connection), so the exegetical content is concentrated in the middle of the transcript, roughly 06:00 to 18:00.

## Key Teachings

### The parable: do not pull the weeds early

Scott reads Matthew 13:24-30 in full. The servants ask whether they should gather the weeds now, and the householder refuses because uprooting the weeds would also uproot the wheat. Scott glosses this as "if you go out right now amongst yourselves and rip out the weeds, you will also rip out the good seed." The lesson he draws is that the community must "live together, intertwined with the weeds and the good seeds, and let God do that at the end of days."

### The explanation: sower, field, weeds, and the Son of Man

Scott reads the private explanation in Matthew 13:36-43 and identifies its roles: the sower of good seed is "the son of man," the field is "the world," the good seed are "the children of the kingdom," the weeds are "the children of the evil one," and the enemy is "the devil." He interprets the sowing as Jesus planting good teaching, and the enemy's weeds as later interpreters who would "enter into our own synagogue and plant weeds," corrupting the movement from within. He says this is what happened to Christianity, though he does not argue the point beyond asserting it.

### Daniel 7 as the framework for the harvest judgment

Scott says that the harvest imagery "echoes" Daniel 7 and that Jesus "deciphered it in a amazing way." He reads the angels in Matthew 13:41 as "messengers" that the Son of Man sends to gather "all causes of sin and all evildoers" and cast them into a furnace, with the righteous shining "like the sun in the kingdom of their father." Scott's point is that the judgment is the Son of Man's act at the end of the age, so present judgment by humans is out of place. He does not discuss Daniel 7 verse by verse in this transcript.

### "Way" discipleship: action over ritual and legal minutiae

Scott explains that Jesus' followers called themselves "disciples of the way" internally. He defines this as loving God with heart, soul and mind, loving one's neighbor, caring for the poor and the Ebionim, and prioritizing "righteous action" over public display or "majoring in the minor" legal details. He names Orthodox Judaism and Catholicism as examples of such legalism, which is a contemporary polemic and is not supported by textual argument in the talk.

### Source base: Matthew as a stand-in for the Gospel of the Hebrews

Scott's method is to teach from Matthew (minus chapters 1-2) because the Gospel of the Hebrews survives only in fragments. He says he avoids "techno babble" about the Nazarenes' rejection of Paul, which he calls "the great contradictions" (he tells viewers to look it up). He also says the Gospel of Thomas is "based on a Syriac root of Jesus's sayings," a claim he states without citing a scholar. This is the most methodologically relevant section, since it explains why the sermon relies on Matthew's wording while claiming to represent the Nazarene tradition.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The parable is about identifying and removing bad people from the church.** Scott says the opposite: the servants' impulse to pull out the weeds is the error, and the community should live with those it considers wrong while leaving judgment to God at the harvest.
- **The parable is about God's judgment of the world only.** Scott says the "field" is the world but argues that the weeds also appear inside Jesus' movement, through later teachers who corrupt his message; the danger is internal, not only external.
- **Paul and the later church are part of the same message as Jesus.** Scott says his teaching comes from "Yeshua, no one else," not Paul, Saul of Tarsus, or the author of Revelation, and he says the Nazarenes regarded Paul as an apostate from the law. This is his position; the talk does not offer the evidence in detail.
