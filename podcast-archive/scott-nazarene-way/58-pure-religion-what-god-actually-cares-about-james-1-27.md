## Key Teachings

### Religion is measured by care for the vulnerable, not by ritual or attendance
Scott's central claim is that James 1:27 gives the definition of "pure religion," and that God is "measuring alignment," not attendance. He describes the first-century situation of orphans and widows, who had "no welfare system... no social services system... no safety net," and argues that a faith that does not "move towards the vulnerable" is not the faith of Yeshua. He asks listeners to identify the figurative "widow" in their own lives: "Who is the overlooked? Who is the struggling? Who is the invisible?"

### "Unstained by the world" means integrity when no one is watching
Scott reads "unstained by the world" as "remaining uncorrupted when no one is watching." He gives his own example of giving a plant-based pizza to a homeless man with no camera present, and urges listeners to examine habits and find where they "speak one way publicly, but live another way privately."

### James against Paul: "Faith without works is dead"
Scott says the line "Faith without works is dead" in the book of James is a direct correction to Paul ("Sorry, Paul. Your faith doesn't matter unless you actually back up your faith"). He contrasts the Pauline summary of the gospel (professing that Christ is Lord, that he died for sins, and that he was raised on the third day) with what he says James and Jesus taught: caring for the poor and acting on one's faith. The talk does not quote Paul's letters directly.

### Authorship and the history of James's letter
Scott says the letter was "hidden" because the people who set the New Testament "argued over" it, and he credits Marcion, whom he calls a "heretic," with coining the term "New Testament" and favoring Paul. He says that "a lot of scholars" doubt James wrote the letter, possibly because he "might have been illiterate," but that disciples of James wrote it to record what he taught. He also gives a translation chain (Yakov, Hebrew, Greek, Latin, Old English, English) to explain the name "James."

### James as a Nazirite, and the Nazarene identity
Scott describes James as "James the Just," a lifelong Nazirite who never cut his hair, wore linen, avoided wine and strong drink, and attended no funerals, citing the book of Numbers for the Nazirite rules. He presents this as part of the "Nazarene" identity that the church is seeking to restore, and he calls the community "Jesus maximalists" who want to "act as Jesus acted" rather than follow "Saul of Tarsus or some other doctrine."

### The Didache and the Eucharist
Scott describes the Didache as "the teaching of the 12 apostles," says it was "found in a grave" (giving "13th or 16th century" as the date of finding, a claim he presents with some hesitation), and dates it to "around 80 C.E. 70 to 80 C.E." He presents its Eucharist instructions as opposed to a Roman-influenced idea of drinking Jesus's flesh and blood, which he says developed through bishops and popes. He reads a Eucharist prayer that includes the phrase "the Nazarenes, and the Ebionites, and the Essenes" and that asks God to gather the church "from the ends of the earth into thy kingdom." The wording as read includes Scott's own additions, such as the name of his church, so it should not be treated as a verbatim Didache text.

### Background claim: the title "tekton"
Scott calls Yeshua a "tekton," which he glosses as a day laborer or carpenter who worked for his livelihood, and he uses this to frame the church's own self-support. The talk gives no source for this gloss.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Religious commitment is shown by attendance, ritual, and correct words.** Scott says God measures "alignment," and that "major[ing] in the minor" (rosaries, suits, Easter clothes, creeds) misses what counts: care for the vulnerable and integrity in private.
- **Professing Christ and belief in the resurrection is the core of the gospel.** Scott says this Pauline formula is "invented by Paul" and that James and Jesus instead required caring for the poor and backing faith with action.
- **The Eucharist is literal drinking of Jesus's flesh and blood, as taught by the early church.** Scott says the Didache's instructions are free of this idea, and that it came from "Roman pagan ideology" as bishops and popes developed it. (This is Scott's claim; the talk offers no sources for the historical development.)
