## Summary

Scott, presenting a pre-recorded Saturday assembly service for Nazarene Way Church, works through Matthew 13:18–23, Jesus's explanation of the parable of the sower. He reads the passage in full, then argues that the four soils describe four conditions of the human heart. The question he sets is why people who hear the same message respond so differently. His answer is that the sower and the seed are the same for everyone, so the difference lies in the receiver. He says "as scholars have said" the passage should be called the parable of the four soils, but names no scholar.

Most of the talk is application. Scott ties each soil to a type of listener: the closed, defensive person; the emotionally excited convert with no roots, drawing on a personal story about a Harvest Festival altar call that he links to "Paul's message"; the person whose life is choked by wealth, popularity and other priorities; and the good soil, which he illustrates with a recent "boots on the ground" food giveaway. He then makes his most contested claims. He says Jesus, James the Just and John the Baptist did not eat flesh, that the early Nazarenes and Ebionites were vegetarian, and that God desires "mercy and not sacrifice," which he sets against the temple cult. He identifies the early Nazarenes, Ebionites and the Gospel of the Hebrews as one group. He presents these as established without citing evidence.

The talk ends with three self-reflection questions for the week (hardened heart, shallow enthusiasm, competing thorns, plus a fruit question) and a closing prayer. Scott asks listeners to choose one small practical act, such as giving away clothes or feeding someone, and to read Scripture "the way Yeshua would parse it." No guest speaks.

## Key Teachings

### The sower is not the problem; the soil is the listener's responsibility
Scott says the seed is "the same exact seed" for everyone and that the sower's success is not in question. He argues the passage "should be called the parable of the four different types of soil, cuz it's not about the sower at all." He describes receiving the word as "your radical responsibility."

### Path: the closed, defensive heart
He reads the path as people whose attitude is "completely defensive, that is closed... and committed to being right." The evil one, in his reading, makes them reject good teaching. He uses the image of "earwax" to describe words that never register. His example is someone who would reject the claim that the early Nazarenes, Ebionites and James the Just were vegetarian.

### Rocky ground: emotional conversion without roots
Scott tells a personal story from around age 19 or 20, when he was in Hawaii at a Greg Laurie Harvest Festival. He says the event was "really, in retrospect, just sharing Paul's message," raised his hand, and went forward for baptism. Two weeks later, he says, it had "zero effect" because he had "no deep root" and his habits had not changed. He contrasts this with "a Pauline version of salvation" in which one profession at the front of a stage is enough, which he calls "modern-day junk food Christianity."

### Thorny ground: wealth, popularity and approval
He reads the thorns as "wealth," "other priorities," "popularity," and the search for "approval, control, and security from everyone else besides God." He says such people may profess Christianity while their consumption, time use and table fellowship contradict the teaching of Yeshua.

### Good soil: fruit that spreads the message
For Scott, good soil hears and understands the word and then produces fruit by passing it on and acting. He cites the Santa Ana outreach in which Nazarene Way members handed out vegan pizza to workers and people at bus stops, and he says "Action matters over faith." He invokes James the Just: "Faith without works is dead."

### Diet and sacrifice: no flesh, mercy over sacrifice
Scott says God "desires mercy and not sacrifice," and he describes the temple cult, the Sadducees and the Pharisees as having turned the Abrahamic God into a "blood cultic sacrificial system." He links this to diet, asserting that Yeshua, James the Just and John the Baptist "did not eat flesh" and that the Nazarene and Ebionite movement was "vegetarian, plant-based." He gives no source for these dietary claims in the talk. He also says the commandment "thou shall not kill" covers all animals with a heartbeat.

### The Gospel of the Hebrews and the Nazarene–Ebionite identity
Scott says the early Nazarenes used a gospel scholars call the Gospel of the Hebrews. He says some speak of three gospels (Hebrews, Nazarenes, Ebionites), but "with my fellow Nazarenes, we have conferred on this" and concluded they were probably one group, "the Hebrew Gospel of Matthew," which lacked the first two chapters. He says it was "probably destroyed by uh the Roman pagan proto-orthodox church or lost." His practice is to use the Greek Matthew minus chapters 1–2, reading the Gospel of the Hebrews where fragments survive. He presents this as his group's conclusion, not as an outside finding.

- **Hearing and emotionally responding to a message is enough.** He argues that rocky-ground hearers who respond with joy but have no roots are "shallow in practice," and that faith must produce changed habits and fruit.
- **Salvation is a one-time profession of belief.** He says the altar-call model, which he ties to "Paul's message," is a "Pauline version of salvation" that is insufficient and replaces the call to walk the way.
- **Faith is more important than works.** Scott says "Action matters over faith," and that agreeing with his teaching is "not enough." He gives no scholarly basis for opposing Paul's view of faith and works in the talk.
- **God requires sacrifice.** He says God desires "mercy and not sacrifice" and that the temple cult transformed the God of Abraham into a blood sacrifice system.
- **Eating animals is normal for Yeshua's followers.** He asserts that Yeshua, James the Just, John the Baptist and the early Nazarenes and Ebionites were vegetarian. The talk gives no citation for these claims.
