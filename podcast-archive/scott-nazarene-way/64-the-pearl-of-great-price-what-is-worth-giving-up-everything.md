## Summary

Scheper asks what is worth giving up everything for, using the parable of the merchant who sells all he has to buy one pearl of great value (Matthew 13:45-46). He argues that the "kingdom of heaven" in the parable is not a reward after death but something that "can be achieved right here and now," and that the "one true pearl" is the priority of the soul and its connection to God. He treats the parable as a test of priorities: whatever has become one's master is the "pearl" one must be ready to sell. Most of his evidence is interpretive and personal rather than textual. He reads the parable aloud, reasons from the properties of pearls (he calls them "indivisible" and "from nature") and from a contrast between gold and pearls, which he says were valued differently "back in Jesus's time" without citing a source. He then describes his own move to "the Nazarene way" about two years earlier, which included a week-long test of vegetarianism, avoiding alcohol and funerals, and not cutting his hair, following what he reads in Numbers 6 and in the practice of James, John the Baptist, and Samson. He also cites Epiphanius, Reza Aslan, and Schonfield as sources for his understanding of the Nazarenes and Ebionites.

The talk ends with a reflective question for the congregation ("What pearls are you currently worshipping...?"), a closing prayer, and an appeal for donations to the church's food and animal-welfare outreach. The description promises a comparison with the rich young ruler (Matthew 19:16-22) and a reading of Matthew 6:19-24, but the captioned transcript does not discuss either passage.

## Key Teachings

### 1. The kingdom of heaven is present, not a postmortem reward
Scheper says Christianity has lost the core meaning of "kingdom of heaven," which people read as meaning heaven after death. He says: "What Jesus meant is not something that you get as a reward after you die. The kingdom of heaven can be achieved right here and now." In his reading, this happens by following the Father and by listening to the inner voice "deep down inside your heart and soul." He ties this to the parable's opening word "again" (a reference back to Matthew 13:44) and to the idea that once one "get[s] a taste of it you will never go back."

### 2. The pearl as a single, indivisible priority
Scheper reads the merchant's pearl as a symbol for a single priority. He contrasts gold, which is "malleable" and can be "transformed," with pearls, which are "from nature" and "indivisible," comparing them to a human being who cannot be cut in half. He says the merchant's decision involved "no deliberation" and "no negotiation," and that the kingdom works the same way: "You stop deliberating over it." He says the person who is "hungry and actively searching" is the model, not the one who sits back.

### 3. Mind, body, heart, soul: sorting one's priorities
Scheper offers a four-part scheme: mind (intellectual interests and work), heart (relationships), body (nutrition, fitness, sleep), and soul (connection with God and reading scripture). He warns that people often neglect the fourth area. He says the soul must sit "at the top, even above your family," and that "You cannot have two masters," with body, hobbies, or sport in the place of God as examples of false masters.

### 4. Religion as giving, and the rejection of Paul and the Trinity
Scheper defines religion as "giving to those in need," which he attributes to James, "Jesus' brother," and which he says is "hidden in the back of the New Testament." He says the Nazarene community rejects "the Gospel of Saul of Tarsus," quoting an unnamed Ebionite saying that "Paul was an apostate of the law." He also insists "Jesus never said that he is God," and that Jesus is "the son of man" (Ben Adam), not a figure of the Trinity. He says the virgin birth was "kind of invented and concocted in the 2nd century," though he states he does not want to "go down that rabbit hole right now."

### 5. The Nazarene way as practice: diet and the Nazirite vow
Scheper says the Nazarenes, Ebionites, James, John the Baptist, and Jesus "did not eat flesh." He describes the Nazirite vow from Numbers 6 as three practices: not cutting one's hair, avoiding contact with dead bodies (so no funerals), and avoiding wine and strong drink. He presents his own practice as an experiment: a week of vegetarianism and the other practices, expanded over about two years through "baby steps." He calls this "the Nazarene way," and says it has made his life "exponentially better." He presents his testimony as evidence, not as a textual argument.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **The kingdom of heaven is heaven after death.** Scheper says this reading has been lost in Christianity and that Jesus meant a present reality attainable now by following God.
- **Jesus is God, and the Trinity is Jesus' teaching.** Scheper says Jesus never claimed to be God and taught "one true God, our Father in heaven."
- **The virgin birth is a core teaching of Paul.** Scheper says Paul "never even brought up" the virgin birth and that it was "kind of invented and concocted in the 2nd century." He leaves the claim undeveloped.
- **Religion means ceremony, large churches, idols, or the Pope.** Scheper says religion is simply "giving to those in need," which he takes from James.
- **Following the Christian gospel of Paul is the path to God.** Scheper says the Nazarene community rejects the Pauline gospel and that "there is a pure path... just following the words of Jesus."
