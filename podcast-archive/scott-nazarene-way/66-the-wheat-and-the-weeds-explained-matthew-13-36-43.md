Scott takes Matthew 13:36–43, the explanation of the parable of the wheat and the weeds, and asks what Jesus means by the field, the weeds, the furnace, "weeping and gnashing of teeth," and the righteous who "shine like the sun." The speaker's main answer is practical: the passage tells listeners not to spend their lives deciding who the "weeds" are, because judgment belongs to God, and their own task is to "become the wheat" by growing in love, patience, mercy and faithfulness. ## Key Teachings

### The weeds, the reapers, and judgment belonging to God
Scott reads Matthew 13:36–43 and identifies the sower as "the Son of Man," meaning Jesus, the field as the world, the good seed as "children of the kingdom," and the weeds as "children of the evil one," sown by the devil. When the servants offer to pull the weeds, the master says to leave them until the harvest. Scott reads this as a warning against self-appointed judging. His application is that the listener's only task is to grow: "Your job is to grow." He tells listeners to ask whether they are becoming more loving, patient, merciful, truthful and generous.

### *Scandala*: judgment reaches causes of sin, not only people
Scott says the Greek *scandala* gives English "scandal," and that the word originally meant "something like a trap or a stumbling block." From this he argues that the reapers gather "all causes of sin," which he takes to mean "any noun, person, place, or sin" that causes temptation, not just the people who commit evil. He gives the example of a red-light district as an "entire area of scandal." He also describes the "hedonistic treadmill" of chasing success, fame and "more," which he calls a trap God will remove.

### "Angels" means messengers
Scott says the popular picture of angels as "a pagan idol that you see at the top of a Christmas tree" misses the word's meaning. He says it "really translates to one thing": messenger. He admits he does not know whether the messengers in the parable take angelic form, and he says the Greek does not settle that question.

### "Weeping and gnashing of teeth": an echo of the Psalms and the anguish of realization
Scott says Jesus was not the first to use the phrase, which "actually comes from Psalms," and he reads it as a phrase Jesus knew from Scripture. He reads the Greek aloud from what he calls "the Greek manuscript," giving the transliteration "buggos" for the key word, which the captions render oddly and which should be checked against the text. He interprets the phrase as "utter anguish," the realization at the end of life that God was real and that one's inner sense of calling to goodness should have been followed. He says the people in view will not be merely sad but will suffer literal gnashing of teeth.

### "Shine like the sun": reward and reunion
Scott says the phrase is "an ode" to Jeremiah and Daniel, which "use similar language," though he gives no verses. He reads it as a promise that followers of the way will "shine like the sun in the kingdom of their father." He ties this to personal grief: he mentions deceased relatives and a congregant who recently lost his mother, and says that according to Jesus the faithful will see their loved ones again.

### The opening digression: Torah, Hosea and diet as a basis for outreach
In the introduction, Scott describes a campaign in which a team went to a site where he says a ritual is practiced and showed Jewish children texts such as Genesis (which he says sets an ideal of not eating flesh) and Hosea 6:6 ("I desire mercy, not sacrifice"). He claims these people "don't even read the Hebrew Bible," says the rabbis they follow created rules "700 years after" the last Hebrew Bible book, and presents the campaign as loving outreach. This section is not an exegesis of Matthew 13, and the claims about the group's practice and knowledge are not supported in the transcript.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus taught no real devil or evil forces.** Scott describes a view associated with "Eastern philosophy or new age philosophy" that Jesus "didn't believe in the devil." His correction: the parable shows evil forces, and Satan does exist.
- **Angels are pagan figures, like Christmas-tree ornaments.** Scott says the word simply means "messenger," and the Greek does not tell us whether the messengers are angelic figures.
- **"Causes of sin" means only sinful people.** Scott argues from *scandala* that the term covers traps, places and ideas, not just the people who commit evil.
- **"Weeping and gnashing of teeth" means only sadness.** Scott reads it as "utter anguish," physical and bitter, arising from the realization that judgment has come.
- **The parable tells us to identify the weeds.** Scott says the opposite: the listener should not judge but grow.
