## Summary

Scheper asks what Jesus meant in Matthew 13:33, the short parable in which "the kingdom of heaven is like" leaven that a woman hid in three measures of flour until all of it was leavened. He treats the parable as a sermon on the hidden, slow work of the Kingdom and as a call to "focus on the fundamentals" of Jesus' teaching: love God, love neighbor, care for the poor, and show mercy rather than sacrifice. He reads the verse against the Passover prohibition on leaven, arguing that Jesus deliberately used a symbol that his audience considered bad. His other main claims are that the Greek word for "hid" (enkrypto) is the root of "encrypted," that three measures of flour is about 60 pounds, and that Jesus was not an "apocalyptic Jew" as Bart Ehrman has argued, because the Kingdom "can actually be attained right here, right now."

The evidence is mostly the speaker's own reasoning, illustrated with a kitchen-style analogy (dough that seems inert before it rises) and an anecdote about learning a skateboard trick from a coach who insisted on basics. He cites no scholarship on the parable itself beyond the Ehrman disagreement and an unnamed Schonfield, and the 60-pound figure and the etymology are asserted without sources in the transcript. He also links the parable to his church's commitments: plant-based food, opposition to animal sacrifice, and a community that "lets the weeds grow" rather than policing its members.

The talk ends with a closing prayer that invites viewers to recommit to God as their "number one priority," an invitation to join the church through its website, and a sung song over the final minute. The talk is a devotional sermon, not a historical argument, and most of its claims are stated rather than documented.

## Key Teachings

### 1. "Chametz," not "yeast," and why the crowd would have been shocked
Scheper says the Greek should be read through the Hebrew word *chametz*, which he says Jesus would have used, and that English Bibles render it "yeast." In his account, leaven was regarded in Hebrew tradition as the enemy, tied to the Passover and the flat unleavened bread of the desert. Jesus, he argues, chose the forbidden item on purpose to shock a crowd of Pharisees and others who had walked a long way to hear him. He reads the shock as a rebuke: the listeners were "majoring in the minor" while the Jerusalem temple cult was "letting blood rain down." Note that the claim about how leaven was viewed is the speaker's; the transcript gives no text for it.

### 2. The "hid" word and the "three measures" of flour
Scheper says the Greek for "hid" is *encrypto*, which he says appears in early Greek manuscripts, and he links it to modern "encryption" to argue that Jesus meant something concealed and protected. He says three measures is "about like 60 lb of flour," enough to "feed an entire village," so the Kingdom is not a small amount but a large, transforming quantity. He gives no source for the measure and does not discuss the manuscript evidence beyond the single word.

### 3. Hidden action and patience
The central lesson is that leaven does nothing visible for a long time after it is mixed into flour. Scheper compares this to people who give up on a goal too early. He illustrates it with his own struggle to land a "rock and roll" skateboard trick, which he says he mastered only after a coach spent several sessions on balance and basics. The Kingdom, on this reading, works "beneath the surface" and needs time, not complex effort.

### 4. The Kingdom is present, not a future or heavenly event
Scheper rejects the reading that the "kingdom of heaven" is a future earthly kingdom or a place after death. He quotes the phrase "which is within you," says the Kingdom "can actually be attained right here, right now," and disputes Bart Ehrman's view that Jesus was an apocalyptic preacher of a coming Kingdom. He does not engage Ehrman's arguments; he only states the disagreement.

### 5. The fundamentals: love God, love neighbor, mercy over sacrifice
Scheper summarizes Jesus' teaching as "everything hangs on two things": love God with all your heart, soul and mind, and love your neighbor as yourself. He ties this to James "the Just, Jesus' brother," whom he quotes as saying true religion is caring for the poor, and to the saying "I desire mercy, not sacrifice," which he reads as a rejection of animal sacrifice rather than of ordinary sacrifice or effort. He cites the Nazarite vow (with John the Baptist, James and Samson as examples) as an optional model of abstinence from alcohol and flesh and of consecration to God.

### 6. Parable context and the community's ethic
Scheper places Matthew 13:33 within the chapter: the parable of the four soils, the mustard seed, the weeds that should be left to grow until judgment, and the statement that those who do God's will are Jesus' "mother, brothers, and sisters." He presents these as a pattern in which God, not the community, does the judging, and he applies this to his own church's refusal to police members. This is his reading of the chapter and is not argued from the Greek text in the transcript.
