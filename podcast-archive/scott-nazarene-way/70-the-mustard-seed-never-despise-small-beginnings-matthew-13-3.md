His central question is what Jesus meant by "the kingdom of heaven" and why he chose a mustard seed to describe it. ## Key Teachings

### The kingdom is not a place after death
Scott says many people think "the kingdom of heaven" means going to heaven after death, and states that "that is not at all what Jesus ever taught." He reads it as something attainable now: "if we all live the way that God and Jesus taught us, we can actually find peace in our heart and our soul right now." He grounds this in Gospel of Thomas saying 3, which he describes as drawing on an Aramaic source and a list of sayings that he thinks Jesus's disciples used.

### The mustard seed as a picture of small beginnings
Scott stresses that the mustard seed is "the smallest of all the seeds" (he gives its size as about 1 to 2 mm) and that the image shows how the smallest movement can grow large. He points to the spread of Christianity as proof that "What he said came true." He also notes that mustard was considered a common, pesky plant in Galilee and Judea, which he links to Jesus's movement of "commoners" and "day laborers," whom he calls the Ebionim, "the poor ones."

### The tree and the birds: a Hebrew Bible allusion
Scott argues that Jesus is exaggerating when he says the mustard plant "becomes a tree," since (as Scott puts it) a mustard seed grows into a shrub. He reads the word "tree" as an allusion to Ezekiel 17:23, where a great tree shelters every kind of bird, and to Daniel, where a great tree's branches shelter the birds of the air. His point is that the growth is "not about you": the seed is the true word of God, and others benefit from it. He says the Hebrew Bible was later renamed "Old Testament" by Romans about 300 years after Jesus and that the scriptures have been altered by scribes and Pharisees.

### Seed as message: love, mercy, and no sacrifice
Scott defines the "seed" in the parables as the words of Jesus: loving God with all one's heart, mind, and soul, loving the neighbor, caring for the poor, and not sacrificing animals. He quotes Jesus as saying "I do not desire sacrifice. I desire mercy," and concludes that Jesus "was not a big fan of sacrificing and hurting and killing animals and eating flesh." He does not give a verse for this quotation in the transcript.

### Christianity, Paul, and James the Just
Scott argues that the movement of Jesus went to James the Just, who "took over the Nazarene movement," and then passed to Simeon, while Saul of Tarsus "went off to Rome" and founded a different movement that became the Catholic lineage. He says Christian apologists are "finally addressing the whole Paul issue," and that Paul's teachings contradict Jesus's. He cites Gospel of Thomas saying 12 for the claim that Jesus named James as the disciples' future leader, and he says James was Jesus's biological brother, listed in Mark 6:3. He offers a guideline for his congregation: "Where did Jesus say that?"

### Daily practice: planting one seed
Scott asks listeners what tiny seed they are refusing to plant. He suggests helping one person, starting one routine or prayer, or forgiving one enemy, and says the Didache instructs fasting for a person who has wronged you (he treats fasting for one enemy as the concrete step past "lip service").

### Abstaining from flesh as "saving one"
Using the starfish story, Scott argues that choosing a plant-based diet makes a difference "to that one." He cites a calculation he ran using an AI tool, claiming that he will spare about 4,500 cows over his lifetime (his own projection). An unnamed congregant then says that the greatest difference is to "your flesh," that God "is against mixture" according to the Torah, and that eating animal DNA "is a defilement actually to your body."
