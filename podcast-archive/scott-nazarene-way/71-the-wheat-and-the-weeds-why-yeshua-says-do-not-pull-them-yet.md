## Summary

This is a Saturday service at Nazarene Way Church, opened by Tessa with announcements (an outreach mission on September 12, a community Zoom in October, a prayer chain, and a membership form). Scott then teaches on Matthew 13:24-30, the parable of the wheat and the weeds, reading from what he calls the Hebrew Gospel of Matthew, which he says the community follows "as best we can." He frames the question as why the householder tells his servants not to pull up the weeds yet, and why the weeds should be left to grow alongside the wheat.

Scott's answer is that the weeds stand for people inside Jesus's movement who oppose its message, specifically Pharisees in the crowd who favored the temple's animal sacrifice system. He argues the householder does not want the weeds uprooted now because judgment belongs to God at the harvest, not to the disciples. He links this to darnel, which he says "looked like wheat" while young, and to a Roman law he says penalized sowing weeds on a neighbor's land. He gives no source for the law. The talk then turns practical: discernment (naming one's values and beliefs) is the believer's responsibility, while judgment is God's, and he suggests a 48-hour waiting rule before acting in conflict and a month-long practice of meditating on a single text.

The talk closes with a prayer that includes a call to avoid eating flesh and to care for all creatures, and it ends with singing. Scott carries the teaching; Tessa only frames the opening.

## Key Teachings

### The weeds are darnel, and the danger lies in their resemblance to wheat
Scott says the Greek word for weeds is "darnel" and that the plant "doesn't even look like a weed" but was "disguised" as wheat and "destructive." He applies this to people who look like they belong to Jesus's movement but are working against it. He presents this as the reason the householder's servants could not easily tell the difference. The description of the video makes the same botanical point, saying darnel "can closely resemble wheat while it is young." Scott does not cite a botanical or lexical source for the identification.

### Judgment belongs to God at the harvest, not to the disciples
This is the central argument. Scott reads the householder's refusal ("No, for in gathering the weeds, you would uproot the wheat along with them") as a rejection of expelling opponents. He says that in the movement's setting, some people were Pharisees sitting in the crowd who were trying to "subvert" the message, and that the disciples expected Jesus to say "root out" the Pharisees. Instead, Scott says, Jesus said to let them grow, and God would "set them apart" at the end of time. He adds that Jesus "didn't say to back down" and that discernment remained required.

### The temple sacrifice system as the target of the parable
Scott claims the Pharisees and Sadducees "loved the blood sacrificial cult system of the temple," and that the animal offerings were "one big barbecue" that the officials ate from, which made the system a main source of wealth. He describes Jesus's act of "freeing the animals in the temple" as part of the rebellion against that system, which he says was one reason Jesus was killed. This historical reading is Scott's own. He gives no textual or scholarly reference for the claim about who ate the sacrificial meat or about the reason for Jesus's death.

### Discernment versus judgment
Scott separates two responsibilities. Believers should clarify their values, beliefs, and goals, and state them plainly ("Here is what we believe. Here's why we believe it"), but judgment of a person's final destiny is reserved for God. He illustrates this with the parable of the sower, which he says is really a parable of four soils, where Jesus casts seed on every soil and does not sort the hearers in advance. He says that Jesus welcomed tax collectors and prostitutes, which "ruffled" Pharisees' feathers.

### Practical application: a 48-hour rule and self-examination
Scott gives four questions for the week: where one spends more energy "identifying weeds than growing wheat"; whether someone has been mentally appointed for judgment; whether one is forcing a situation to resolve; and whether one's zeal to fix something is "damaging something good." He recommends waiting 48 hours (not 24) before acting, and says this does not mean giving up principles or accepting the opponents' views. He adds the observation that "whenever you try to force something, the least likely it is to actually happen."

### The Nazarenes, Ebionites, and the Hebrew Gospel of Matthew
Scott describes the early movement as calling itself "disciples of the way" internally, known as Nazarenes externally, and later as the Ebionites ("the poor ones"). He claims these groups did not eat flesh, and that James the brother of Jesus was a Nazirite who abstained from wine and cut no hair, and that "all the church fathers" knew this, though no church father is named. He says the community reads the Hebrew Gospel of Matthew, which survives only in fragments, so it reads the Greek Matthew "minus the first two chapters" instead. He says the group follows "the words of Jesus" over "the words of Paul of Tarsus."

### A suggested reading practice
Scott recommends picking a text (he names Jeremiah, Isaiah, Matthew, Jude, James, and the Gospel of Thomas) and reading it passage by passage, meditating on one verse a week for about six months. He calls the Gospel of Thomas a list of sayings of Jesus with "Syriac root," and says it is wrongly labeled "Gnostic."
