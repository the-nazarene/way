## Summary

Scott preaches on Matthew 12:43-45, the saying about an unclean spirit that leaves a person, wanders through waterless places, and returns to find its house "empty, swept and put in order," then comes back with seven worse spirits. He asks what Jesus meant by "this evil generation" and argues that the warning is aimed at the scribes and Pharisees. His reading is that a person who is freed from a spirit but does not repent "from within" is left vulnerable to something worse. He illustrates this with the television series *Breaking Bad*, comparing Walt's wealth and his empty home to the house in the parable. He says the cure is inward: love of God with heart, soul and mind, and love of neighbor. He supports the reading with the saying's appearance in Luke and with his view that it comes from a shared source (the "Q" source), though he does not show the text of that source.

About a third of the talk is a digression, not part of the sermon. Scott reports nightly reading of Eusebius's church history and describes a figure named Symmachus, a Hebrew-speaking translator into Greek whom Eusebius links to the Ebionites. Scott reads a passage (which he dates to "page 277" of his edition) saying that the "heresy of the Ebonites" holds that Christ was the son of Joseph and Mary and insists on keeping the law in a Jewish manner. Scott says Symmachus is said to have criticized the Gospel of Matthew and to have read the Gospel of the Hebrews, which he describes as Matthew without its first two chapters. The talk ends with prayer and announcements about an outreach event in San Clemente and a Zoom meeting.

Scott presents all of this as his own reading and interpretation. The transcript does not contain the Greek or English text of Eusebius, so the quotations and the page number should be checked against the edition he is reading.

## Key Teachings

### Deliverance is not repentance

Scott's central point is that casting out an unclean spirit is not the same as curing the person. He says the parable's man has been freed from the spirit "but has not been cured," and that "It is not it's not like the person has repented and cured himself from within." He ties this to the idea of an exorcist. The house is "clean" but has no inner life in it. He makes this a general warning: "Deliverance is not the same as discipleship" is the question his description poses, and the talk argues that a swept house is not a restored one.

### "This evil generation" means the scribes and Pharisees

Scott takes "this evil generation" (Matthew 12:45) to refer to the scribes and Pharisees. He says they were "obsessed with all of these practices and doctrinary legalistic rules, but they don't actually know God." He brings in John the Baptist's phrase "brood of vipers" and a remark about the Pharisees wanting fine robes and public prayer. In the talk, this is the target of the warning, and the scribes and Pharisees are the people who have been asking Jesus for "another sign" in the preceding passage (Scott summarizes this from the previous week's study).

### The Breaking Bad illustration

Scott uses Walt White from *Breaking Bad* as a modern analogy. He describes Walt as leaving his family and getting rich through illegal work in "this evil world, in the desert." When Walt returns to a clean, fully furnished condo, he finds no family, "no love." Scott says Walt "tried to basically use his mind and not his heart and his soul to heal from within," and so the parable's pattern repeats: an outward fix leaves an inner emptiness that grows worse. This is Scott's interpretation of the parable, not a claim from any historical source.

### Inner healing as the answer

Scott's practical conclusion is that the way forward is not logic or outward signs but inward change: "Solve it from within. Solve it by praying to God. Solve it by repenting." He links this to the greatest commandments, loving God with all heart, soul and mind, and loving one's neighbor as oneself. He presents this as Yeshua's own teaching.

### Parallel in Luke and the Q source

Scott says the saying "is also present in Luke" and is one of the passages that "a lot of scholars think" Luke and Matthew drew on from a source they share, which he calls the Q source. He says this makes it "probably a saying that Jesus said at some point." He does not show the parallel text or name the scholars he has in mind.

### Symmachus and the Ebionites

In the opening segment Scott introduces Symmachus as a Hebrew-speaking Ebionite and a respected translator of Hebrew into Greek. He says Origen (whom he calls a church father who "formed the Roman Orthodox Church") praised him, and that Eusebius describes him as an Ebionite. Scott explains that the name "Ebionite" comes from the Hebrew word for "the poor ones," and says the Galilean followers "internally called themselves" Nazarenes. He reads Eusebius's claim that the Ebionites keep the law "in a Jewish manner," and he presents this as a positive picture of early Jewish-Christian practice, though Eusebius's text as he reads it calls it heresy.

### Matthew's gospel and the Gospel of the Hebrews

Scott says the community of Jesus's disciples used the Gospel of the Hebrews, which he describes as "basically Matthew without the first two chapters." He says that only remnants of it survive, preserved through early church writers such as Eusebius. According to Scott, Symmachus is reported to have criticized Matthew's Greek version, which included the virgin birth narrative, and to have preferred a Hebrew-based text. This is part of his argument that the community's original gospel differed from the Greek canonical text.

### The law and Paul (as Scott frames it)

Scott says Matthew records Jesus as saying "I did not come to abolish the law. I came to fulfill it." He then contrasts this with what he describes as Paul's teaching that followers "don't need to follow the law or follow the Torah." He encourages his audience to keep the Ten Commandments, keep reading the Hebrew Bible, and "parse it in a way that Jesus, Yeshua, parsed it better than the heres the Pharisees." This is a contrast Scott draws himself, and the talk does not cite Pauline texts to support it.
