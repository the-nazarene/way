## Summary

Scott, speaking to his congregation at Nazarene Way church in Southern California, preaches on Matthew 12:38–42: the scribes and Pharisees ask Jesus for "a sign," and he answers with "the sign of the prophet Jonah," then points to the Ninevites and the Queen of the South as examples of people who responded to one sign. Scott reads the Pharisees' request as derision rather than sincere inquiry, and he applies the passage to daily life: when your intuition gives you a sign, trust it, pray, and act, rather than asking God for another one.

Before the sermon, Scott gives church announcements (a monthly outreach to distribute pizza, a prayer team, a clothing drive for adopted twins) and reads from Eusebius's *Church History* about Pantaenus, an Alexandrian teacher whom Scott describes as a former Stoic. According to Eusebius as Scott reports him, Pantaenus found a Hebrew Gospel of Matthew among believers in India that Bartholomew had left there. Scott uses this to argue that the first Nazarenes and Jesus's disciples used a Hebrew Matthew, the "Gospel according to the Hebrews," minus its first two chapters, and that this is why the congregation preaches from Matthew rather than from quotations mostly drawn from Paul. He closes by naming the translations the church uses (NRSV and the Tree of Life Version).

## Key Teachings

### The sign of Jonah and the three days
Scott reads Jonah's three days "in the belly of the sea monster" as a figure for Jesus being "in the heart of the earth" for three days, which he takes to mean the time in the tomb. He reads "the sign of the prophet Jonah" as the answer to the request for a sign, and he treats the saying as a rebuke to the Pharisees rather than a promise of a future miracle.

### Nineveh and the Queen of the South
Scott argues that the Ninevites repented after the first sign they saw (Jonah's preaching) and that the Queen of Sheba traveled to hear Solomon after hearing of his wisdom. In his reading, both were Gentiles who acted on one sign, and Jesus holds them up against Pharisees who had already seen signs of healing and still asked for more. He adds that "something greater than Jonah is here" and "something greater than Solomon is here" refer to Jesus.

### Trust the sign you have
Scott applies the passage to everyday life: when something seems questionable, do not wait passively for another sign. Trust your gut, pray, and "decisively" act. This is his pastoral reading of the text, not a claim about its original sense.

### Matthew as the Nazarene text
Scott says the disciples used the Gospel of the Hebrews, which he identifies as Matthew in Hebrew without its first two chapters. He reports that Bartholomew took it to India according to Eusebius's account of Pantaenus, and he says the church reads Jesus's words in Matthew rather than "a church outline" built from Paul's letters "taken out of context."

### Temple sacrifice as a business
Scott describes the Second Temple sacrifice system as a "mass factory farm slaughterhouse" and a money-maker, with a share of each animal going to the priests. He gives a percentage he says he is not sure of. No source is given for the figure in the talk.

### James the Just
Scott says that Eusebius describes James the brother of the Lord as "vegetarian" and as having taken the Nazarite vow from birth.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Misconception (implied by the Pharisees' request):** The request for "a sign" is a sincere request for proof. **Scott's correction:** He says many scholars read the phrase as derision, and that the Pharisees had already seen healings.
- **Misconception (implied by the usual reading of Jonah):** The sign of Jonah is a promise of a future miracle to come. **Scott's correction:** He reads it as a rebuke: the signs already given should be trusted, and asking for more is the mistake.
- **Misconception (implied by church outlines built from Paul):** Paul's letters are the natural core of a church teaching. **Scott's correction:** He argues the church should read Jesus's words in Matthew, and that Paul's letters are often quoted out of context.
