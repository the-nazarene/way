## Key Teachings

### Mercy as the interpretive key to Torah
Scott argues that the disciples' hunger is both physical and "metaphysical": their souls were hungry. He says the Pharisees saw only an infraction, while Jesus "sees people." He concludes that Torah "when interpreted without mercy, it becomes a cage instead of a path of life." He contrasts "religious accountants" with the purpose of Torah, which he says was "given to form a holy people." The argument is pastoral and interpretive. Scott does not engage Sabbath halakhah or texts on the Sabbath beyond Matthew 12.

### Jesus restores the Sabbath rather than abolishing it
Scott's central claim is that "Yeshua does not abolish the Sabbath." He reads Matthew 12:5 (priests break the Sabbath in the temple and are innocent) and 12:6 ("something greater than the temple is here") as showing Jesus arguing from Scripture against the Pharisees. He says Jesus "is not teaching lawlessness, he is teaching the true interpretation of the Torah." He also says Jesus argues "from scripture" and "knows the Hebrew Bible better than the Pharisees."

### Hosea 6:6 and the Hosea story
Scott says Jesus is quoting Hosea 6:6 and that the point is "knowing God," which he equates with mercy. He then retells the story of Hosea and Gomer as a parable of God's mercy toward Israel: Hosea buys back his unfaithful wife from bondage and remarries her, and Scott reads this as the true nature of God. He uses the story to connect "God does not desire sacrifice" with an critique of the second-temple sacrificial system, which he calls "a bloodthirsty killing ring."

Note that the Hosea retelling is Scott's own narration. The sermon does not cite a verse for the story beyond Hosea 6:6.

### Early textual and canonical claims
Scott makes several claims about the earliest Jesus tradition:
- **Gospel of the Hebrews.** He says it was "basically a Hebrew version of Matthew minus the first two chapters," and that Jesus's disciples and "his brother James the Just" would have used it. He says "you can look this up" but gives no citation. He says that "we do not have the Gospel of the Hebrews."
- **Matthew 1-2.** He says the nativity and genealogy were "pre-pended" later and that the virgin-birth narrative "is a misreading of Isaiah 7:14."
- **Mark.** He calls it "the earliest written Gospel that we have," dated "around 60 CE," and says it begins at John's baptism.
- **Marcion.** He presents Marcion as the first to assemble a "proto-New Testament" and as the originator of the two-gods view, which Scott rejects.
- **Paul.** He says Saul of Tarsus makes up "the majority of the books of the New Testament" with "13 letters," and that "many of them were um you know, forgeries." He says these can be cut out.

These claims are offered as background and are not developed or sourced in the talk.

### Jesus's literacy
Scott discusses whether Jesus could read. He cites Reza Aslan's *Zealot* and reports Aslan's view that "it's probably approximately correct to say that Jesus could not read." He says scholars are "split" on this and that he has "no strong opinion." He argues that the way Jesus cites David implies he knew the text well.

### Name and titles
Scott prefers "Yeshua" and says the name was altered through Greek "Lesous" and Old English into "Jesus." He glosses "Ben Adam" as the Aramaic for "son of man" and says Jesus called himself that. He refers to Jesus as "Lord of Shabbat" in Matthew 12:8 but does not develop christological claims.
