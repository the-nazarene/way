## Summary

Scott asks what Jesus meant in Matthew 11:25-29 when he calls his yoke "easy" and his burden "light," given that Jesus also speaks of carrying a yoke at all. Scott's answer is that Jesus does not offer a life without a yoke. Everyone carries something, and the question is whether the burden crushes or sustains. He contrasts the "yoke of religion," which he ties to the Pharisees, scribes, and an oral law of 613 commandments, with the simple "yoke of Yeshua," which he summarizes as love God, love your neighbor, stop eating flesh, be kind to every creature, and give to the poor. His main evidence is the text of Matthew 11 itself, his reading of "the wise" and "infants" as metaphors for proud elites and humble disciples, and an agricultural image of the wooden yoke for oxen. He also draws on a claim that the Gospel of the Ebionites and Gospel of the Nazarenes contained a Hebrew Matthew without the first two chapters, and on a book by an unnamed friend, *Breaking Apart Matthew*.

The talk runs about 28 minutes and is almost entirely Scott speaking. It is a sermon delivered to a live congregation, opening with church announcements (an outreach pizza drive in Santa Ana, a clothing drive, and the search for a permanent meeting place) and a prayer. Scott's applied points are that modern people are weighed down by outrage, politics, and phone use, and that Christian ritual and Catholic practice, including saints and a statue of Donald Trump, would not be recognized by Jesus. He ends with a closing question for listeners ("What yoke am I carrying?"), a prayer, and worship music. ## Key Teachings

### The yoke is not the absence of burden
Scott's central claim is that Jesus does not offer release from every yoke. He says "Yeshua is saying, you will still need to bear something, bear something on your back," and that the choice is between "the right type of yoke" and the wrong one. His illustration is two hikers, one carrying bricks and one carrying food and water. The bricks stand for the religious load he attributes to the Pharisees and scribes; the food and water stand for Jesus's teaching.

### "The wise" and "infants" as metaphors
Scott reads Matthew 11:25's "hidden from the wise and discerning and revealed them to infants" as figurative. He identifies "the wise" as "the proud religious elites," including the Pharisees and Sadducees who ran the temple, and "the little children" as "the humble, teachable, ordinary disciples." He stresses that the text says the kingdom is hidden from proud people, not from intelligent ones, so "the doorway into the kingdom is humility." He ties this to the claim that Jesus reveals "the heart of God," which he describes as "mercy, compassion, healing, forgiveness," in contrast to a "crushing taskmaster."

### The yoke image and the oxen
Scott corrects the reading of "yoke" as an egg yolk. He explains that in the first century farmers used a large wooden yoke across two oxen's shoulders, a heavy steering device that he links to modern "yoke" steering wheels on cars and aircraft. He presents this as the background for Jesus's image of a yoke that is "easy."

### Torah without the burdensome interpretations
Scott argues that Jesus is "not abolishing the Torah" but "rescuing people from suffocating interpretations of it." He says Jesus teaches that "the Father desires mercy over sacrifice," and he cites an earlier Matthew passage (presumably Matthew 5:17, which he paraphrases) where Jesus says he came to fulfill the Torah. He claims the oral tradition produced "613 laws" about 300 years after Jesus's death, including a rule about putting on the left shoe first. This is his own assertion, with no source given.

### Simple ethics against Paul's doctrine of salvation
Scott sets Jesus's teaching against what he calls the Paul-centered message. He says Jesus's way is to "love God, love other people, love other beings, all creatures, stop eating the flesh like James the Just did," and "care for the Ebionim, the poor ones." He says that following this "is probably approximately correct that you will attain eternal salvation," and he rejects anyone who claims 100% certainty, calling that "dogmatic thinking." He contrasts this with "Paul, a man who has never Paul Saul of Tarsus, who had never walked with Jesus." He does not develop the argument beyond this point.

### Modern yokes and the critique of institutions
Scott applies the passage to present-day burdens: outrage, social media, politics, comparison, consumerism, and AI-driven "attention recession." He also criticizes Christianity and Catholicism, citing saints, rosaries, and a Trump statue. He appeals to the second commandment and says Jesus "would not understand what the heck is going on." He closes with the line "Do not major in the minor," which he treats as the central lesson.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **"Yoke" means egg yolk.** Scott says some Christian books have misread the word this way, and he explains the first-century oxen yoke.
- **Jesus offers an easy life with no burdens.** Scott argues that Jesus's yoke still requires carrying something. The easiness lies in the kind of burden, not in its absence.
- **The wise are intelligent people, and God hides truth from them.** Scott says the "wise" are proud religious elites, and the issue is humility, not intelligence.
- **Jesus abolished the Torah.** Scott says Jesus came to fulfill Torah and to rescue people from burdensome interpretations of it.
- **Salvation depends on the blood sacrifice atonement and Paul's teaching.** Scott says Jesus's message was simpler: love God, love others, stop eating flesh, and care for the poor. He rejects claims of guaranteed salvation.
