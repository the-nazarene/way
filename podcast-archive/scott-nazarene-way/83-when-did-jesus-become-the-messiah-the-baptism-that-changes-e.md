## Summary

Scott asks when Jesus became the Messiah, and argues that it happened at his baptism in the Jordan, not at his birth. He says "Messiah" means "anointed one," and that the anointing marks the moment God declared Jesus his chosen one. He frames the question against later Christian doctrines he thinks came after Jesus: the Trinity, which he attributes to Tertullian, and the virgin birth, which he says arose from a misreading of Isaiah 7:14 in the Septuagint. He presents three witnesses. The first is a passage he reads from the Gospel of the Ebionites, which he says was used by Jesus' first followers. The second is Acts 10:38, which he says shows God anointing Yeshua "with the Ruach Ha Kodesh and power." The third is a passage from Epiphanius' *Panarion*, which Scott reads as evidence that the Ebionites and Nazarenes held that Jesus was a physical man on whom the Messiah descended. He concludes that Messiahship is shown by obedience to "the way" rather than by faith alone, and he ties this to care for the poor and to Jesus' message as he understands it.

Most of the talk is a devotional sermon rather than a historical argument, and Scott gives few dates or page references. His claims about when Mark, Acts and the canon were written, and about the content of the Ebionite and Nazarene writings, are his own and are not supported in the transcript. The talk ends with the Lord's Prayer, read aloud by Scott, and a sung song. Scott also recounts giving pizza and a sweatshirt to people in downtown San Diego. Tessa's opening segment covers the service schedule, a planned outreach, and a prayer-request page.

## Key Teachings

### The baptism as the moment of anointing
Scott reads from what he calls the Gospel of the Ebionites: when Jesus came up out of the water, "the heavens were opened," the Holy Spirit came down "in the form of a dove," and a voice said, "You are my beloved son. In you I am well pleased." He says the words "Today I have begotten you" follow. From this he concludes that "Jesus became the Messiah, the anointed one, the son of man at that moment that he was baptized by John the Baptist." He sets this against the nativity narratives, which he says are found only in Matthew and Luke and contradict each other (for example, on whether the family goes to Egypt).

### Acts 10:38 and the source of Jesus' authority
Scott reads Acts 10:38 from the Tree of Life Version and says the verse shows that Jesus' authority "flows from God's anointing." His reasoning is that if Jesus were God, God would not need to anoint him. He dates Acts to roughly 100 to 130 CE, which he presents as his own estimate, and argues that the verse predates the later doctrine of the virgin birth and the divinity of Jesus, which he says the writer of Acts had not yet absorbed.

### Epiphanius and the "offspring of a man's seed"
Scott reads a passage from Epiphanius' *Panarion* that he says criticizes the Nazarenes and Ebionites as heretics. In the passage, the Messiah "descended in the form of a dove" and "entered" Jesus, and Jesus "is the offspring of a man's seed and a woman." Scott takes this as evidence that Jesus' first followers believed in Jesus as a physical human being, the son of Joseph and Mary, anointed by God. He notes that the critics' accounts survive while the Nazarene and Ebionite books were burned, so only fragments remain.

### Jesus did not call himself God
Scott argues that Jesus "never called himself God" and "always called himself the Son of Man," which he links to the Aramaic *Ben Adam*. He says the Trinity was "coined" by Tertullian in the second or third century as a Latin term, *Trinitas*. He presents this as a later development that Jesus would not have recognized.

### Messiahship by obedience, not by faith alone
Scott argues that Messiahship is "defined by obedience, by actually following the way, by practicing the way," and that "action matters over faith." He cites the book of James ("Faith without works is dead") and contrasts it with what he calls "Paul's doctrine" that one need only profess Jesus' death as a sacrifice to be saved. He presents the anointing as a reward for living the way, which he lists as caring for others, not killing (including animals), loving one's neighbor, giving to the poor (the "Ebionim"), keeping the Sabbath, and water baptism.

### Restoring the Nazarene movement and its symbols
Scott argues that the early Nazarenes called their gatherings "assemblies" (Greek *synagoge*) and met in homes, and that they would not have recognized Catholicism or Orthodoxy. He proposes the dove and running water as symbols for the movement, not the cross, and says the Nazarene Way's own logo has water in it for that reason. He also says the Nazarenes and Essenes used the dove as a symbol.

## Misconceptions Addressed

- **Jesus was God from birth.** Scott says Jesus was anointed at the baptism, and the virgin birth came later.
- **The virgin birth is original.** Scott says it came from a mistranslation of Isaiah 7:14 in the Septuagint, and that Mark and John have no such narrative.
- **The Trinity is what Jesus taught.** Scott says Jesus never called himself God and that the Trinity was coined by Tertullian in the second or third century.
- **Jesus was Jewish in the modern sense.** Scott says "No, he wasn't" and calls him a "Hebraic man from Galilee," distinguishing him from modern Judaism, which he says stems from rabbinic Judaism in about the 4th century.
- **Salvation is by faith alone (Paul's doctrine).** Scott says Messiahship is defined by obedience, citing James, and that Jesus' message is not "hold your hand up" and profess belief.
- **The Nazarenes and Ebionites were heretics.** Scott argues they were the actual movement of Jesus and were labeled heretics by the later Roman-linked church.
