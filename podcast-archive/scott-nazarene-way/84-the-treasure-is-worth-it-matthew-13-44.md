## Key Teachings

### The treasure is hidden in ordinary sight
Scott reads Matthew 13:44 ("The kingdom of heaven is like treasure hidden in a field which someone found and hid") and stresses that the field is ordinary, walked over by everyone who "thinks nothing of it." His reading is that the kingdom is "hidden in everyday sight" and that it is recognized only once it is "seen" and "experienced." He says he is reading the parable through the lens of first-century Nazarenes and Jesus' hearers rather than modern Christian readings, though he gives no historical citations for that lens.

### Treasure equals the Way: Torah, monotheism, and the human "Ben Adam"
Scott identifies the treasure with Jesus' teaching as lived practice: "Treasure equals the way that Jesus lived. It's obedience to the Torah, faithfulness to God, the one and only God." He asserts that Jesus never called himself God or "the son of God," and that he called himself "Ben Adam in Aramaic, meaning the son of man." He also calls Jesus the Messiah who was "anointed by John the Baptist" and who taught the one God better than anyone. These christological claims are stated as Scott's conclusions; no specific passages are read to support them in the talk.

### Sacrifice, decision, and declaration
Scott argues that "sacrifice feels like gain" once the Way is experienced, and that nothing changes without a deliberate decision. He lays out three steps: decide in the heart, declare it to others, and practice it. The practices he names are giving up flesh, keeping the Sabbath on Saturday, and "giving to the poor." He calls the result being a "Jesus maximalist," in contrast to following Paul. He adds water immersion as a further declaration, linking it to John the Baptist ("John the immerser").

### Diet, compassion, and the Nazarite vow
Scott describes giving up flesh, wine, and alcohol, and his personal Nazarite vow (no cutting hair, no strong drink, wine, or grapes, and no contact with dead bodies). He reads "Thou shalt not kill" as covering "all creatures," not only humans, and says a plant-based life is what Jesus would have practiced. He also describes the post-vow clarity he felt while watching people at a Starbucks, which he uses to contrast the "hedonic treadmill" with the Way.

### Against Paul, faith alone, and Nicaean doctrine
Scott repeatedly contrasts Jesus' teaching with "the later doctrine of faith without works," "Paul of Tarsus's doctrine of just having faith," the Roman Catholic Church, and the Council of Nicaea in the fourth century. He claims the Roman pagan notion of eating a god's flesh and drinking his blood entered the movement, and says this language is absent from the Didache's Eucharist.

### The Didache: prayer, fasting, and the Eucharist
Scott reads a prayer from Didache chapter 8 about fasting for enemies, and the Lord's Prayer in its Didache form ("give us today our daily needful bread"), which he says is in Matthew. He reads the Didache's broken-bread prayer and instructs the congregation to use unleavened bread, explaining it as representing the scattered grains gathered into one loaf (and the church gathered from the ends of the earth), not Jesus' body. He also describes the Didache as discovered in a grave; the captioned date for that discovery is garbled, and he tells listeners to research it themselves.

### The kingdom within and without
Scott says the kingdom "is within you and without you as we know from the Gospel of Thomas," and that you must "trade" for it through action in the present, "right here, right now." This is the sermon's most direct link between the kingdom and present practice.
