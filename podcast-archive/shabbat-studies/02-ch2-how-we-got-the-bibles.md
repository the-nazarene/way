The thirteen named are real traditions and match the columns of Wikipedia's "Biblical canon" comparison table; the count depends on how traditions are split (Lutheran, Anglican, and generic Protestant are counted separately). Say "about a dozen living canon traditions" and name them, rather than treating thirteen as a formal figure.
3. **The Protestant canon was "most recently edited in the late 1800s"; the 1611 KJV had "16 extra books"** [21:42] [3:44:35]. The 1611 KJV printed the Apocrypha between the testaments: fourteen books (fifteen if Baruch and the Letter of Jeremiah are split). The British and Foreign Bible Society stopped printing them in 1826-27, and the 1885 Revised Version omitted them. "Late 1800s" fits the Revised Version; "sixteen" fits no count.
4. **Codex Vaticanus is "the oldest complete Bible"; "all Bibles come from" Vaticanus, Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus, and Bezae** [41:49] [1:15:20] [3:11:22]. Vaticanus (4th century) is incomplete (it lacks Genesis 1-46, part of Psalms, Hebrews 9:14 onward, the Pastorals, and Revelation); Sinaiticus has the oldest complete New Testament. Alexandrinus and Bezae are 5th century. Modern critical texts rest on these plus papyri and thousands of later manuscripts. And the KJV does not come from these codices: its New Testament follows the Textus Receptus (Erasmus 1516-1535, Stephanus, Beza), built from late Byzantine minuscules; Vaticanus was not accessible to the translators and Sinaiticus was not found until 1844. This matters for Claim 26.
5. **"Alexandrinus is what Luke went to Alexandria for"; Luke's gospel was "probably the Hebrew Matthew kept at the library of Alexandria"** [3:04:48] [3:11:22]. Codex Alexandrinus is a 5th-century manuscript, four centuries after Luke. Luke 1:1-4 says he used earlier accounts; nothing in the sources connects him to Alexandria or to a Hebrew Matthew. Treat the Alexandria idea as Chris's speculation, which is how he offers it.
6. **King James's biography** [45:06] to [53:22]. Right: born at Edinburgh Castle, 19 June 1566, to Mary, Queen of Scots; raised by tutors (George Buchanan); James VI of Scotland and, from 1603, James I of England; the Geneva and Bishops' Bibles were the predecessors; the KJV took decades to displace the Geneva Bible. Wrong or garbled: his father was Henry Stuart, Lord Darnley, murdered in 1567 (James V was Mary's father); the suspect in Darnley's death was Bothwell, whom Mary then married; the cousin who imprisoned Mary was Elizabeth I of England; the translators numbered about 47 to 54 in six companies, not 200; the KJV was commissioned in 1604 and published in 1611 (1566 is James's birth year, not a first edition).
7. **"James" replaced "Jacob" on the king's whim** [48:23] [1:10:15]. "James" is the English form of Latin *Iacomus* (a variant of *Iacobus*) and was already the standard rendering in Wycliffe (1380s), Tyndale (1526), the Geneva Bible (1560), and the Bishops' Bible (1568). The KJV inherited it. The underlying point stands (the brother's name is Ya'akov); the attribution to King James does not. [1:11:56]
8. **The KJV is Crown copyright** [38:24]. True in the United Kingdom, where the text is under perpetual Crown copyright administered by letters patent (Cambridge, Oxford, Collins). It is public domain elsewhere.
9. **"Lord" is the English rendering of Baal (Catholic Encyclopedia); "Lord" did not enter the Bible until the King James era** [1:32:07] [1:33:54]. Baal does mean owner, master, lord, so reference works gloss it that way; that is a translation of meaning, not a derivation. English "lord" comes from Old English *hlāford* ("loaf-ward"), unrelated to any Semitic word. And "Lord" for YHWH long predates 1611: it is the practice of Wycliffe, Tyndale, Coverdale, and Geneva, following the Vulgate's *Dominus*, the Septuagint's *Kyrios*, and the Jewish custom of reading Adonai for the Name. This argument should not be repeated as stated.
10. **The Name was replaced "over 7,000 times"** [1:38:57]. YHWH occurs about 6,800 times in the Hebrew Bible; the KJV renders it LORD in small capitals (which do mark the Name) roughly 6,500 times, GOD about 300 times, and "Jehovah" seven times (four alone, three in compounds). The order of magnitude is right; the small-caps convention cuts against "hidden."
11. **Exodus 20:7 means "do not bring the Name to naught"** [55:07] [2:35:35]. The Hebrew is *lashav*, "for emptiness, falsehood, vanity"; the standard sense is false or empty invocation (perjury, misuse). "Render it non-existent" is the HalleluYah Scriptures / ISR rendering and an interpretation, not the plain lexical meaning. Both readings can be stated; the application to the Name's disappearance is the hosts'.
12. **The pictographic alphabet carries word meanings; first and last letters give the nutshell** [36:43] [1:05:11] [1:20:25]. The letter names and glyph origins are standard (aleph as ox, shin as tooth, ayin as eye, tav as mark). Reading a word's meaning from its letter pictures is a popular method (Jeff Benner, Frank Seekins) that Semitic philologists do not accept; Hebrew words derive from consonantal roots, not from sums of letter meanings. Present it as the hosts' devotional method, not as linguistics.
13. **"Do not call Abraham your father... God can raise up stones"** (Jen) [1:06:52]. That is John the Baptist (Matthew 3:9; Luke 3:8), not Jesus; Jen flags the uncertainty herself. Matthew 21:13 as Isaiah 56:7 plus Jeremiah 7:11 is correct.
14. **2 Timothy 3:16: the italicized "is"; *graphē* means "writings," even a receipt** [58:33] to [1:05:11] [1:28:45]. The KJV does italicize the first "is," and the Greek has no verb there, which is normal Greek. "All scripture is God-breathed and profitable" and "Every God-breathed scripture is also profitable" are both grammatical, and the second is how the American Standard Version (1901) rendered it, so the hosts' reading has a mainstream pedigree. But *graphē* in the New Testament (about fifty uses) always refers to sacred writings, never to receipts, and it is not a technical term for the Ketuvim; the three-tier argument is an inference, not something the verse says. "Inserted by some guy in the 1500s" overstates: the italic marks a supplied copula, which every English version supplies in some form.
15. ***Pisteuō* means "obey"** [1:30:24]. It means believe, trust, have faith; "obey" is *hypakouō* or *peithomai*. The hosts' point about faith as faithfulness has support in the Hebrew *emunah* and in James 2, not in the Greek verb's lexical meaning.
16. **The two tests for a prophet; Balaam** [1:23:43] [1:25:26]. Deuteronomy 18:21-22 (the word comes to pass) and 13:1-5 (does not lead away from God's commands) are the two tests; accurate in substance. Balaam's star oracle (Numbers 24:17), the stumbling block (Numbers 31:16), and Revelation 2:14 are correctly cited; that his oracle "came true" is interpretation.
17. **The Maccabees liberated Israel from "Babylonian captivity" and built the Second Temple; the Maccabean settlement imposed the overlord's king, taxes, and high priests** [1:57:20] [1:58:56] [2:00:36]. Two events are merged. The return from Babylon was under Persia (Cyrus, 538 BCE) and the Second Temple was finished about 516 BCE. The Maccabees (167-160 BCE) fought the Seleucid Greeks and rededicated that temple (Hanukkah). Judas's treaty with Rome (1 Maccabees 8) was an alliance, not submission; Roman control came with Pompey (63 BCE) and Herod (37 BCE). The Zadokite high priesthood was displaced by the Seleucids and then by the Hasmoneans themselves (Jonathan, 152 BCE), which is the grievance behind the Dead Sea Scrolls (`christianity/dead-sea-scrolls/00-overview.md`).
18. **Ezekiel names only Zadok's sons; "Sadducee" derives from Zadok** [2:00:36] [2:02:19]. Ezekiel 44:15-16 (also 40:46, 43:19, 48:11): accurate. The Zadok etymology for "Sadducee" is the usual one and is debated.
19. **The Herodians were Edomites; the priesthood was "fully Edomite"** [2:02:19] [2:04:02] [2:23:57]. Herod's father Antipater was Idumean; Idumea had been forcibly converted under John Hyrcanus (c. 125 BCE), so Herod was Jewish by religion and Idumean by descent (`FIX-LIST.md` already corrects a repo file on this). The high priests Herod and the Romans appointed came from Jewish priestly families (Boethus, Annas), not from Edom; Herod's first appointee, Ananel, was a Babylonian Jew (Josephus, *Antiquities* 15.2.4), which is the kernel of truth in "Babylonian."
20. **A pay-to-play high priesthood, "chief priest for a month"** [2:10:35]. The substance is attested: Josephus counts 28 high priests in the roughly 107 years from Herod to the war (*Antiquities* 20.10), and rabbinic sources complain of purchased appointments (Yoma 8b; Pesachim 57a). "For a month" is hyperbole.
21. **Houses of Shammai (scribes) and Hillel (Pharisees); Hillel changed the calendar to suit Greek markets** [2:08:57]. Both Houses were Pharisaic schools of the first centuries BCE and CE. The calculated calendar is traditionally attributed to Hillel II, a different Hillel, about 359 CE, and the traditional motive is the loss of the Sanhedrin's ability to proclaim months under Roman restriction, not market alignment. That the Zadokite/Qumran calendar (364 days) differed is correct.
22. **Dead Sea Scrolls details** [2:14:00] to [2:17:17]. Discovered 1946-47 (Chris hedges 1930s/40s). The Great Isaiah Scroll (c. 125 BCE) is about a thousand years older than the previous oldest complete Hebrew Isaiah (the 10th- and 11th-century Masoretic codices), not "a couple hundred years older than the 400s" (that figure belongs to the Greek codices). The Teacher of Righteousness, the Wicked Priest, and the Spouter of Lies are in the Habakkuk Pesher and the Damascus Document; mainstream dating puts them in the second to first centuries BCE, and reading them as first-century-CE figures is Eisenman's thesis. The "temple archive hidden during the siege" theory is Norman Golb's minority view; the standard account is the community's own library deposited around 68 CE (`christianity/dead-sea-scrolls/00-overview.md`, `## The Discovery`).
23. **The Nazarenes fled to Pella, Qumran, and Beirut** [2:49:40]. Pella: Eusebius, *Church History* 3.5.3; Epiphanius, *Panarion* 29.7 (both in the repo). Qumran was destroyed by the Romans in 68 CE and was not a refuge. "Beirut" is probably a slip or mis-hearing for Beroea in Coele-Syria (Aleppo), where Epiphanius places Nazarenes (`christianity/the-nazarenes.md`, `### Beroea in Coele Syria...`).
24. **Essene means "doers"; the Nazarenes took the Nazirite vow** [1:28:45] [2:28:57] [2:40:35]. The etymology of *Essenoi* is unsettled; "doers of the law" (*'osim*) is one proposal (Goranson), alongside Aramaic "pious" (*hasayya*) and "healers" (*'asya*). Nazarene (*Nazōraios*) and Nazirite (*nazir*) are different words; James's Nazirite-like practice comes from Hegesippus, but the sect's name is not from the vow. Ebionim as "the poor" is standard.

Annas (Ananus ben Seth) was high priest 6-15 CE and appears at Jesus's hearing (John 18:13, 24), though his son-in-law Caiaphas held the office; his son Ananus ben Ananus had James stoned in 62 CE (Josephus, *Antiquities* 20.9.1). The camel's knees are Hegesippus in Eusebius 2.23. All in `christianity/james-the-just-key-to-understanding-jesus.md`.

The textual facts are right and are in the repo: no Greek manuscript before the 14th century, and verses 7-8 form one sentence in the Greek. The chronology is the problem: the clause first appears in Latin writers in the late fourth century (after Nicaea), enters Vulgate manuscripts centuries later, and reached the printed Greek text only when Erasmus added it to his third edition (1522) after being shown a manuscript containing it; from Erasmus it passed through Stephanus into the KJV. No one at Nicaea cited it, which is Newton's argument and the repo's. "Inserted to enforce Nicaea" should become "a Latin gloss that migrated into the text and into the KJV by way of Erasmus." Chris also twice misnames the verse ("John 1:5" at [3:38:00]; "John 1:7" at [4:09:35], where he means John 1:1).

Convened by Constantine in 325: yes. Not the canon: correct, and a useful corrective. Easter: yes, Nicaea ruled on the date. Arius was a presbyter of Alexandria, not a pope. The council's sanction was anathema and exile, not death; Constantine's edict ordering Arius's writings burned threatened death for concealing them (Socrates, *Church History* 1.9), which is the nearest thing to the hosts' claim. Two bishops (Theonas and Secundus) refused to sign and were exiled with Arius; Eusebius of Nicomedia and Theognis followed shortly. "Smaller councils before Nicaea established a bi-deity" [4:17:57] needs a source; the pre-Nicene synods did not define Christology.
