# Does God Need Blood to Forgive? And What Actually Saves?

**Position:** God does not need blood, and Jesus never taught that he did. Every time Jesus is asked directly how a person is saved — how to inherit eternal life, how sins are forgiven — he answers with repentance, mercy, commandment-keeping, and forgiving others. Never once with his own death. Penal substitutionary atonement is a Levitical framework imported onto a Roman execution: it was argued by Paul, formalized by later theologians, and read back into the gospels by scribes and redactors whose work we can still see in the manuscripts. The Hebrew prophets had already rejected the premise centuries earlier — God desires mercy, not sacrifice — and the earliest Christian liturgical document we possess, the Didache, celebrates communion with no blood theology in it at all. We are not minimizing the cross. We are asking who put the price tag on it.

---

## Strongest Evidence

### 1. Asked point-blank how to be saved, Jesus names behavior
> "If you would enter life, keep the commandments." — Matthew 19:17

The rich young man asks the single most important question in Christianity, and Jesus answers with the commandments. Luke 10:25-28 is a separate exchange with the same shape: "What must I do to inherit eternal life?" → love God, love your neighbor → "Do this and you will live." Two distinct scenes, two direct answers, zero mention of his coming death.

Note the attestation before anyone challenges it: "keep the commandments" is not Matthew's invention — it is in Mark 10:19 and Luke 18:20 as well. (See *"Multiple attestation when it suits you"* below, and the objection on the rest of that pericope — know verses 21-31 before you quote verse 17.)

### 2. Forgiveness is reciprocal, not purchased
> "For if you forgive others their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you. But if you do not forgive others, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses." — Matthew 6:14-15

This is the mechanism Jesus actually describes, and he describes it constantly (Luke 6:37; Mark 11:25; the Lord's Prayer at Matthew 6:12). It is conditional, ongoing, and participatory — and the Parable of the Unforgiving Servant (Matthew 18:21-35) has the king *revoke* forgiveness already granted. A finished transaction cannot be revoked. These are two incompatible models of how forgiveness works.

### 3. Jesus quotes the anti-sacrifice prophets — twice
> "Go and learn what this means: 'I desire mercy, not sacrifice.'" — Matthew 9:13 (quoting Hosea 6:6); repeated at Matthew 12:7

He is not softening the sacrificial system; he is citing the prophetic tradition that subordinated it to mercy — Jeremiah 7:22-23, Isaiah 1:11-17, Amos 5:21-25, Psalm 51:16-17, Micah 6:6-8.

**Say the Hebrew carefully.** Hosea 6:6a is a flat *lo*: "mercy, and *not* sacrifice." But 6:6b's synonymous parallel uses comparative *min* — "knowledge of God **more than** burnt offerings" (מֵעֹלוֹת). The verse holds both idioms in two lines. **Do not claim "it's not a comparison"** — anyone with a Hebrew Bible open will correct you, and it's the kind of correction that makes the audience discount everything after it. The defensible claim is stronger anyway: this is prophetic idiom that subordinates cult to mercy so sharply that **Jesus deploys it twice as a rebuke to the men running the cult** (Matthew 9:13 to Pharisees objecting to his table, 12:7 to Pharisees objecting to Sabbath grain). He is not citing Hosea to reform sacrifice. He is citing it to overrule them.

### 4. "For the forgiveness of sins" is Matthew's addition, not Jesus's words
> Mark 14:24: "This is my blood of the covenant, poured out for many."
> Matthew 26:28: "...poured out for many **for the forgiveness of sins**."

Matthew adds the atonement clause to his own Markan source. It appears in no other early witness — not Mark, not Luke 22:20, not Paul at 1 Corinthians 11:25, not John (who has no institution narrative at all), not the Didache. Strip the addition and what remains is an Exodus 24:8 covenant-ratification echo. Sinai blood seals an agreement; it does not pay for sins.

**You can watch Matthew move the phrase.** In Mark, "for the remission of sins" belongs to John the Baptist: "John did baptize in the wilderness, and preach the baptism of repentance for the remission of sins" (Mark 1:4, KJV). When Matthew copies that scene he drops it — his John says only "Repent ye: for the kingdom of heaven is at hand" (Matthew 3:2, KJV) — and the phrase turns up once in his whole gospel, at the cup (26:28). So it isn't just absent from the other witnesses; it was taken off John's baptism of repentance and attached to Jesus's blood. Davies and Allison, Luz, and Meier read it as deliberate Matthean editing. The words are secure in Matthew's manuscripts; the claim is about Matthew's pen, not a copyist's. (Florez sheet, B14.) Mark's own cup word is a separate and harder question — Paul's independent version in 1 Corinthians 11:25 shows a cup saying is earlier than Mark — so don't stretch this argument to it.

### 5. The earliest Christian communion liturgy has no blood in it
> "We thank you, our Father, for the holy vine of David, your child, which you have revealed through Jesus, your child. To you be glory forever." — Didache 9 (cup prayer)

The Didache (c. 50-120 CE) is the oldest Christian church manual we have. Its Eucharistic prayers thank God for what Jesus *revealed* — "life and knowledge" — not for what he paid. **The prayers of chapters 9-10 contain no body, no blood, no death, and no remembrance of a death.** Its soteriology is the Two Ways: "There are two ways, one of life and one of death" (Didache 1:1), and the way of life is love of God and neighbor.

**Say it that precisely.** Didache 14 *does* use sacrifice language — "first confessing your sins so that your sacrifice may be pure," quoting Malachi 1:11 — but it applies *thusia* to the assembly's own offering of praise and a reconciled community, not to Jesus's death. That distinction is the whole point: the earliest Christians had a concept of sacrifice, and it was theirs to offer, not his to pay. If you claim "the Didache has no sacrifice," someone reads chapter 14 aloud and you lose the exchange.

## Questions People Raise

### "Multiple attestation when it suits you, single-source Matthew when it doesn't. Pick a criterion."
*The full form of the challenge: "You reject Matthew 26:28 because it's lone Matthean editing of Mark — then you build your whole case on Matthew 25:31-46, Matthew 6:14-15, Matthew 11:30, and Matthew 19:17, none of which Mark has."*

**This is the sharpest challenge to the whole collection. Memorize the answer. We do have a criterion, and it is a standard one.**

**The criterion, stated plainly:** Counting sources is not the test. *Direction of editing* is the test. When we can watch a writer alter his own source in the direction of his own known theology, the alteration is evidence about the writer, not about Jesus. When material has no such motive — or costs the writer something — the fact that one gospel preserves it is not an objection. This is ordinary redaction criticism plus the criterion of embarrassment (contra-tendency). Every historical-Jesus scholar on the other side of the table uses both.

**Run it on the texts, out loud:**

- **Matthew 26:28 — fails.** Matthew has Mark open in front of him and *adds* "for the forgiveness of sins" to Mark's cup saying. Matthew is the evangelist most invested in Jesus as the one who "will save his people from their sins" (Matthew 1:21 — also his own addition, also absent from Mark). The clause moves *toward* the redactor's program and *away* from his source. That is the textbook definition of suspect.
- **Matthew 19:17 — passes, and it isn't single-source.** "Keep the commandments" is in Mark 10:19 and Luke 18:20 too. Matthew *does* edit this pericope: Mark's "Why do you call me good? No one is good but God alone" (Mark 10:18) becomes "Why do you ask me about what is good?" Notice *what* he edited — the Christology, not the soteriology. He fixed the line that embarrassed him and left the commandment answer standing.
- **Matthew 25:31-46 — passes on embarrassment.** No Markan parallel to deviate from, and the scene gives Matthew nothing. He is the gospel of church order, Peter's keys, and "whoever confesses me before men" (10:32) — and he preserves a last-judgment scene where confession is never asked about and the righteous don't recognize Christ at all. Editors do not invent material that undercuts their own program.
- **Matthew 6:14-15 — passes on substance.** Reciprocal forgiveness is independently attested: Mark 11:25 ("forgive... so that your Father also may forgive you") and the Lord's Prayer in Luke 11:4. Matthew 6:14-15 is Matthew's *wording* of a multiply attested teaching, not a Matthean idea.
- **Matthew 11:30 — weakest of the set. Cite it as corroboration, not as a pillar.** "My yoke is easy" has no Synoptic parallel; the shared Q block ends at 11:27 (= Luke 10:22). What it has going for it is contra-tendency: Matthew is the evangelist demanding righteousness "exceeding that of the scribes and Pharisees" (5:20) and "be perfect" (5:48). An easy yoke is not what his program wants. And if someone discounts it entirely, nothing moves — the same point is corroborated outside the gospels by Didache 6:2 ("If you can bear the Lord's full yoke, you will be perfect. But if you cannot, then do what you can.") and inside them by Luke 10:28.

**Concede the limit out loud.** The criterion is directional and probabilistic, not proof. It can be aimed at us, and we accept that — which is exactly why the load-bearing claims on this card are the multiply attested ones (the salvation question, reciprocal forgiveness, the Hosea citations), not the single-source ones.

Then turn it: "We're not counting gospels. We're asking which way the editor's hand is moving. Matthew adds atonement to Mark. Nobody adds a judgment scene that makes their own creed irrelevant."

### "Mark 10:45 — 'the Son of Man came to give his life as a ransom for many.' That's in Mark, not Matthew."
**Expect this first. It is the strongest verse against us and it has no redaction escape hatch.** Answer it straight, in three moves.

First, concede the text: it's there, it's early, and it is ransom language. Don't dispute the verse.

Second, read what the word actually says. *Lytron* is a release-payment — the price to free a captive or manumit a slave. It is **not** sacrificial vocabulary; Leviticus doesn't use it for sin offerings. The money goes to the *captor*, never to God. So even granting the verse fully, it does not describe God requiring blood before he can forgive — which is the doctrine under dispute. Ransom frees you from something; it doesn't pay an angry God.

Third, read the verse in its own paragraph. Mark 10:42-45 is about **status**, not atonement: the disciples are fighting over who ranks highest, and Jesus says the great one serves. "Just as the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve" is the punchline of an argument about power. The ransom clause is the far end of that — a life spent all the way down, not a transaction ledger.

Then turn it: "Does Mark 10:45 tell you to believe something, or to do something? Read verse 43 — 'whoever would be great among you must be your servant.' That's the instruction attached to it."

**Know the authenticity debate, and don't lead with it.** There *is* a redaction argument, it's just not decisive. Luke has what looks like the same saying at the Last Supper with no ransom clause — "I am among you as he that serveth" (Luke 22:27, KJV) — and the ransom wording sounds like the later church's (1 Timothy 2:6; Titus 2:14). Bultmann read the clause as a Hellenistic reworking; Morna Hooker denied the Isaiah 53 background. On the other side, Jeremias, Stuhlmacher, Watts, Yarbro Collins, Allison, and N. T. Wright argue it is early and Semitic ("the many" from Isaiah 53:11-12, the Son of Man from Daniel 7). Florez tiers it CONTESTED, with critical weight slightly against the exact wording (Florez sheet, B8). That's why the three moves above concede the verse: our answer works even if Jesus said every word of it. Mention the Luke 22:27 parallel as a fact about the tradition, never as "the verse is fake."

### "Hebrews 9:22 — 'without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness of sins.'"
Hebrews is not Pauline and not Jesus. Its argument is a sustained reading of Leviticus applied to the crucifixion after the fact — it's a first-century theologian's interpretation, which is exactly what's in dispute. Set it beside Jesus quoting Hosea 6:6 twice, and beside Jeremiah 7:22, and you have to choose which stack you build on. We build on the red text. (Don't argue "it's anonymous" — so are all four gospels, and we build our case on them. That one hands them a criterion that cuts our own throat.)

### "Jesus himself said his blood was shed for the forgiveness of sins."
He says it in exactly one source: Matthew. His source Mark lacks the phrase, Luke lacks it, Paul lacks it writing earlier than all three, John omits the scene entirely, and the Didache's liturgy has none of it. The standard reading requires treating a single evangelist's editorial clause as a verbatim quotation — which is a textual-criticism claim, not a faith claim, and the manuscripts don't support it.

### "Leviticus 17:11 says the life is in the blood — God clearly established blood atonement."
Mainstream scholarship places the bulk of that Priestly sacrificial legislation in the exilic/post-exilic period (Wellhausen onward), later than the 8th-century prophets who condemned sacrifice. That ordering explains the contradiction honestly: Jeremiah isn't attacking an abuse of a divine command, he's denying the command existed. Even granting Leviticus as given, the Temple is gone and Jesus still forgave sins directly, with no altar and no priest (Mark 2:5; Luke 7:48).

### "Jeremiah 33:18 — 'nor shall the Levitical priests ever lack a man to offer burnt offerings.' Your Jeremiah 7:22 argument dies in the same book."
**Pre-build this. Jeremiah 7:22 is load-bearing for us and this is its known counterpunch. Do not improvise it.**

Concede the verse immediately and without hedging: Jeremiah 33:18 promises perpetual Levitical sacrifice, in Jeremiah, in black and white. Then three answers, in order of strength:

**First — read 7:22's own qualifying clause.** "I did not speak to your fathers or command them *in the day that I brought them out of the land of Egypt* concerning burnt offerings and sacrifices." The denial is about **origin**: sacrifice is not what the Sinai covenant was founded on. Verse 23 says what was: "Obey my voice, and I will be your God, and you shall be my people." That is a claim about the *basis* of the covenant, not a claim that no sacrifice was ever permitted or performed. Our argument needs exactly that much and no more — and at that strength, Jeremiah 33:18 doesn't touch it.

**Second — the two passages sit in different layers of the book.** Jeremiah 33:14-26, the unit containing verse 18, is **absent from the Old Greek (Septuagint) Jeremiah entirely** — the longest omission in the book, widely read as a late restorationist, pro-Zadokite supplement. If they want to hold us to redaction criticism on Matthew 26:28, redaction criticism applies here too. The same method that costs us a verse gains us this one. (Note the symmetry out loud — it demonstrates the criterion isn't ad hoc.)

**Third — even taking both at face value, the prophetic case survives.** Jeremiah can promise a functioning restored priesthood and still say God never asked for offerings as the ground of the relationship. Isaiah 1:11-17, Amos 5:21-25, Micah 6:6-8, Psalm 51:16-17, and Hosea 6:6 all make the same move. (Expect Psalm 51:18-19 as the mirror-image objection — "then bulls will be offered on your altar" — and the same answer: that coda is long recognized as a post-exilic addition to the psalm.)

**Say it this way, not the other way.** The bad version, which invites Jeremiah 33:18: *"Jeremiah 7 is the one chapter in the Bible where God denies ever asking for sacrifice."* The good version, which survives it: *"Jeremiah 7:22-23 says the covenant God made coming out of Egypt wasn't founded on sacrifice — it was founded on 'obey my voice.' That's verse 23. Read it."*

### "Isaiah 53 — 'the LORD laid on him the iniquity of us all.' And Jesus applies it to himself: Luke 22:37."
**This belongs on the card. Isaiah 53 is the most-preached atonement text in evangelicalism and Luke 22:37 is red text. Concede what's true first, then be precise.**

Concede three things, because all three are true: (1) Isaiah 52:13-53:12 genuinely uses substitutionary and sacrificial language — verse 10 makes the servant's life an *asham*, a guilt offering. (2) Jesus cites the chapter about himself: "For I tell you that this Scripture must be fulfilled in me: 'And he was numbered with the transgressors'" (Luke 22:37, quoting Isaiah 53:12). (3) Luke 24:26 has the risen Jesus say, "Was it not necessary that the Christ should suffer these things and enter into his glory?" A suffering-messiah expectation is in the gospels, in his mouth. Don't pretend otherwise.

**First, know where the citation actually stands.** Luke 22:37 is the only place in the gospels where Jesus himself explicitly quotes Isaiah 53 (allusions like "the many" in Mark 10:45 are another matter). The KJV's Mark 15:28 — "And the scripture was fulfilled, which saith, And he was numbered with the transgressors" (KJV) — is narration, and it's a copyist's import from Luke: it's missing from Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Alexandrinus, Bezae and the oldest versions, and modern Bibles drop it (Florez sheet, A21). Luke 22:37 itself is textually secure, but it sits in the two-swords passage (22:35-38), found only in Luke, which Conzelmann and Fitzmyer read as Luke's own composition or special material (Florez sheet, B12; ESTABLISHED as Lukan, CONTESTED whether an older source stands behind it). Don't say "Jesus never cited Isaiah 53." Say: one citation, in one gospel, in a passage scholars widely treat as Luke's.

Now the precision, four moves:

1. **Look at which clause he quotes.** Out of a chapter containing "he bore the sin of many," "an offering for guilt," and "the LORD laid on him the iniquity of us all," Jesus quotes the one line about being **counted among criminals** — in a context about swords and an imminent arrest. He uses Isaiah 53 to interpret his *arrest*, not to explain a payment mechanism.
2. **Necessity is not purchase.** Luke 24:26 says the Christ *had to* suffer. It does not say why — and Luke gives his own answer twenty verses later: "that repentance and forgiveness of sins should be proclaimed in his name to all nations" (Luke 24:47). *Proclaimed.* The outcome Luke names is a message of repentance, not a debt settled. He never writes "to pay for."
3. **Luke is the gospel editing *away* from atonement — which makes 22:37 more interesting, not less.** He has Mark's status dispute at Luke 22:24-27 and drops the ransom clause: Mark's "to give his life as a ransom for many" becomes "I am among you as one who serves." And the atonement wording at Luke 22:19b-20 is missing from a significant stream of witnesses (Codex Bezae and Old Latin — the classic Western non-interpolation). By the criterion above: an evangelist who trims payment language and still keeps Luke 22:37 is telling you Isaiah 53 was being used for **vindication of the righteous sufferer**, not penal substitution.
4. **Who the servant is, inside Isaiah.** The same book names the servant as Israel in the surrounding chapters (Isaiah 41:8; 44:1; 44:21; 49:3), and Jewish reading has taken the figure corporately for two millennia. That doesn't forbid the Christian reading — but it does mean "Isaiah 53 obviously teaches penal substitution" is a conclusion, not a datum.

Close honestly: "The suffering servant is in the gospels. The righteous one suffers and is vindicated. What is *not* in the gospels is a God who cannot forgive until the blood arrives. Show me Jesus saying that."

### "You stop reading at Matthew 19:17. Finish the pericope: 'sell everything,' the man walks away, and 'with man this is impossible, but with God all things are possible.'"
**They're right that we usually stop too early, and the arc they're describing — law, exposed inability, divine action — is the standard evangelical reading. Know the whole passage.**

Concede the structure is there and that Mark 10:26-27 is Mark, with no redaction escape hatch. Then take it apart in five moves:

1. **Verse 21 escalates the doing; it doesn't replace it.** "Go, sell what you possess and give to the poor... then come, follow me." The second answer is *harder* than the first and still an instruction to act. The movement is commandments → radical divestment → following. At no point does it become "believe something about my death."
2. **Jesus accepts the man's claim.** "Teacher, all these I have kept from my youth." Mark's next line: "And Jesus, looking at him, loved him" (Mark 10:20-21). He does not say *no you haven't*. The exposed-inability reading needs Jesus to be laying a trap, and the text says he responded with affection.
3. **What is declared impossible is specific.** Read verses 23-25: the impossibility is a **rich** man entering, and the camel-and-needle saying is tied to wealth three times in three verses. "With God all things are possible" answers the wealth problem. There is no sentence here about a universal inability to keep commandments — that premise arrives from Romans 3:20, not from the pericope.
4. **The next four verses close the door on the reading.** Peter says, "We have left everything and followed you" (Mark 10:28). Jesus does not correct him. He promises payout — "there is no one who has left house or family... who will not receive manifold more" (Mark 10:29-30; Matthew 19:28-29; Luke 18:29-30). If the lesson were *nobody can do this*, that is the sentence where Jesus says so. Instead he rewards the men who did.
5. **The pericope states its own conclusion, and it isn't imputed righteousness:** "But many who are first will be last, and the last first" (Mark 10:31).

Then turn it: "You read 'with God all things are possible' as a doctrine of human inability. He says it in a room where Peter has just done the impossible thing and is being promised a hundredfold for it. In this story grace is what makes the obedience possible — not what makes it unnecessary."

### "'Keep the commandments' is works-righteousness — nobody can do it, which is the whole point."
That argument is Paul's (Romans 3:20), not Jesus's. Jesus never says the commandments are unkeepable; he says "my yoke is easy and my burden is light" (Matthew 11:30) and "do this and you will live" (Luke 10:28). And his brother James, who led the Jerusalem church, states the position plainly: "a person is considered righteous by what they do, and not by faith alone" (James 2:24). The faith-alone reading requires Jesus and his brother to both be wrong about Jesus. (James 2 is contested — the prepared version of that fight is the next objection. Don't drop James 2:24 and assume it holds unaided.)

### "James 2 doesn't contradict Paul. 'Even the demons believe' (2:19) — his target is dead faith, not Pauline faith. And 2:23 quotes Genesis 15:6, Paul's own verse, the same way Paul uses it."
**This is the best-prepared objection on the Protestant side, and both halves are partly right. Don't deny them — outflank them.**

Concede: yes, James 2:19 shows his target includes bare notional assent — demonic monotheism, belief that changes nothing. And yes, 2:23 cites Genesis 15:6, Paul's proof text in Romans 4. If our argument were only "James says works, Paul says faith," harmonization wins that exchange.

Three things harmonization cannot do:

- **The verb.** James 2:21: "Was not Abraham our father **justified by works** when he offered up his son Isaac?" Romans 4:2: "If Abraham was **justified by works**, he has something to boast about — but not before God." Same verb (*dikaioō*), same patriarch, same question, opposite verdicts. Harmonizing requires assigning *dikaioō* two different senses in two places — a defensible interpretive move, but neither author announces it, and it is not what the texts say.
- **The reading of Genesis 15:6.** James reads it *through* Genesis 22: the faith "was completed by his works" (2:22), credited when Abraham acted. Paul reads it as credited *before and apart from* what followed — Romans 4:10 insists it was before circumcision. Same sentence, opposite logic about sequence.
- **The slogan.** "Faith alone" (*pistis monon*) occurs exactly once in the New Testament: James 2:24, as the thing being denied. Whoever he is answering, he is answering a *formula*, and he names it in order to reject it.

**On the New Perspective (Sanders, Dunn, Wright) — take the concession. It helps us.** The New Perspective argues Paul's *erga nomou* means Torah boundary markers — circumcision, food laws, calendar, table fellowship — not good deeds in general. We think that reading is substantially right, and our own research already grants the premise: `christianity/dead-sea-scrolls/00-overview.md` builds on **4QMMT**, where *miqsat ma'ase ha-torah* ("some of the works of the Torah") is precisely such a halakhic list — purity rulings, sacrificial procedure, calendar — closing with "it will be reckoned to you as righteousness when you do what is right and good before Him." Paul's phrase has a documented pre-Christian referent, and that referent is Jewish legal observance, not kindness to the poor.

Notice what the concession costs the other side:

1. **It retires the verse they were about to use on us.** If Romans 3:28 is about circumcision and food laws, it is not a statement that commandment-keeping is irrelevant to salvation — so it cannot be laid over Matthew 19:17 at all. The New Perspective disarms the pulpit version of Paul before we have to.
2. **On Wright's own reading, Paul affirms judgment by works.** Romans 2:6-13: God "will render to each one according to his works," and "it is not the hearers of the law who are righteous before God, but the doers of the law who will be justified." That is Paul, and it is Matthew 25's criterion. (Quoting Paul to characterize Paul is fair play — we are not using him as evidence for Jesus.)
3. **The James–Paul dispute relocates; it doesn't dissolve.** If *erga nomou* means Torah observance, then the fight is over Torah observance and Gentile inclusion — which is exactly the fight in Galatians 2:11-14 and Acts 15, James on one side and Paul on the other. Making the conflict historical rather than semantic doesn't make it vanish. It makes it the best-documented conflict in the early church.

One-liner: "The New Perspective says Paul didn't mean what the pulpit says he meant. We say Jesus didn't say what the pulpit says he said. Those two arguments are allies, not opponents."

### "You're reducing the cross to nothing."
No — we're relocating its meaning. The cross is what empire and priesthood do to a man who confronts them, and it is a demonstration that love does not retaliate even under maximal pressure. That reading honors the event without requiring an omnipotent God who cannot forgive until someone bleeds.

## In One Sentence

- Jesus was asked how to be saved. He answered. It wasn't "believe I'm about to die."
- "Forgive and you will be forgiven" and "without blood there is no forgiveness" cannot both be the mechanism.
- One gospel out of four adds "for the forgiveness of sins" — and it's the one copying from the gospel that doesn't.
- The oldest communion prayer in Christianity thanks God for the vine of David. Not for blood.
- He said "I desire mercy, not sacrifice." Twice — both times to the men running the sacrifices.
- We're not counting gospels. We're watching which way the editor's hand moves. Matthew adds atonement to Mark.
- Jeremiah 7:23 says what the covenant was founded on: "Obey my voice, and I will be your God." Not "bring me an animal."
- Isaiah 53 is in the gospels. "God can't forgive until the blood is paid" is not.
- Peter says, "We have left everything." Jesus doesn't say, "No you haven't, nobody can." He promises a hundredfold.
