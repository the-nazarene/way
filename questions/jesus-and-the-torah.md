# What Did Jesus Teach About the Law?

**Position:** Jesus was a Torah-observant Jewish teacher who **intensified the Law's ethical core and subordinated its cultic and purity apparatus to that core.** He never abolished it and never declared it void — he *ranked* it. Where ritual and mercy collide, mercy is what the ritual was for. That is not antinomianism and not abolition; it is a recognizably Jewish prophetic argument with a long pedigree — Hosea, Micah, Isaiah, Jeremiah — conducted inside the covenant, not against it. It matters for everything else on these cards, because it means the "faith versus works" frame was never Jesus's question. His question was never *whether* to keep Torah. It was *which part of it is load-bearing* — and he answered out loud, more than once, with justice, mercy, and faithfulness.

---

## Strongest Evidence

### 1. He says it himself, and then raises the bar
> "Do not think that I have come to abolish the law or the prophets; I have not come to abolish but to fulfill... For truly I tell you, until heaven and earth pass away, not one letter, not one stroke of a letter, will pass from the law until all is accomplished. Therefore, whoever breaks one of the least of these commandments... will be called least in the kingdom of heaven... For I tell you, unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven." — Matthew 5:17-20

Read verse 20 slowly. The problem with the Pharisees is **not enough** righteousness, not too much. And the jot-and-tittle saying at v.18 is not Matthew's invention — Luke has it independently (Luke 16:17), which puts it in **Q**, upstream of both evangelists.

**Expect the obvious comeback, and answer it before it's made.** We say Jesus didn't speak Matthew 28:19 because the critical method treats it as Matthew's. Critics will point out that the same method treats 5:17-19 as Matthew's too: Luz, Davies and Allison, and Meier all read it as the voice of Matthew's Torah-keeping community (Florez sheet, B18). We can't use the method on 28:19 and wave it away on 5:17. The honest difference is evidence, not taste:
- **5:18 has an independent parallel; 28:19 has none.** Luke 16:17, "it is easier for heaven and earth to pass, than one tittle of the law to fail" (KJV), puts the core saying in Q, upstream of Matthew. Nothing outside Matthew has the triadic formula on Jesus's lips.
- **5:17-19 matches what Jesus does; 28:19 contradicts what the apostles did.** In the Synoptic gospels he goes to synagogue on the Sabbath (Luke 4:16), keeps Passover, pays the Temple tax (Matthew 17:24-27), and sends the healed leper to the priest (Mark 1:44). In Acts nobody baptizes in the three names, and Jerusalem is shocked when Gentiles are let in.
So lean on 5:18 and its Luke parallel. Treat 5:17 and 5:19 as Matthew's framing of a saying that is older than Matthew, and say so when asked. That's still enough to carry the argument.

Then the Sermon does exactly what v.20 announces. Murder → anger. Adultery → the look. Oath-keeping → no oaths at all. Eye for eye → no retaliation. Love your neighbor → love your enemy. **Every single move makes the commandment harder.** That is a stricter Torah, not a looser one.

### 2. The ranking criterion, stated out loud, with both halves kept
> "Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you tithe mint, dill, and cummin, and have neglected the weightier matters of the law: justice and mercy and faith. **It is these you ought to have practiced without neglecting the others.**" — Matthew 23:23

This is the thesis of the whole card in one verse, from his mouth. There are *weightier* matters — a comparative, inside the law, not a replacement of it. And the final clause forecloses the antinomian reading: he does not tell them to stop tithing herbs. He tells them they got the weighting wrong.

**Quote the last clause every time.** If you cite 23:23 without it, someone who knows the text will finish the verse for you and it will look like you were hiding it.

### 3. He settles legal disputes by quoting a prophet's hierarchy
> "Go and learn what this means: 'I desire mercy, not sacrifice.'" — Matthew 9:13 (Hosea 6:6)
> "But if you had known what this means, 'I desire mercy, not sacrifice,' you would not have condemned the guiltless." — Matthew 12:7

Notice the second one. Matthew 12 is a **Sabbath halakhic dispute** — his disciples plucked grain — and his ruling argument is a prophetic ranking principle. He is not waving the Law off. He is arguing a case within it, from scripture, the way a Jewish teacher argues.

### 4. The Sabbath controversies: he argues from inside the Law
> "The sabbath was made for humankind, and not humankind for the sabbath; so the Son of Man is lord even of the sabbath." — Mark 2:27-28
> "Is it lawful to do good or to do harm on the sabbath, to save life or to kill?" — Mark 3:4

Look at *how* he defends the grain-plucking in Mark 2:25-26: David and the bread of the Presence (1 Samuel 21). **A Torah precedent.** This is legal reasoning, not dismissal — the position that human need overrides ritual obligation is a position *in* Jewish law, with rabbinic parallels. And he does not abolish the day; he states its purpose.

Thomas keeps the Sabbath as a live category, independently:
> "If you do not observe the Sabbath as a Sabbath, you will not see the father." — Gospel of Thomas 27 (Lambdin)

### 5. The Law summarizes itself — and a Torah scholar agrees out loud
> "'You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart...' The second is this, 'You shall love your neighbor as yourself.' There is no other commandment greater than these." — Mark 12:29-31

Both halves are quotations **from Torah** (Deuteronomy 6:4-5; Leviticus 19:18). Then the scribe answers him:

> "'You are right, Teacher... this is much more important than all whole burnt offerings and sacrifices.' When Jesus saw that he answered wisely, he said to him, **'You are not far from the kingdom of God.'**" — Mark 12:32-34

A scribe states our position on sacrifice, and Jesus commends him for it. That exchange is in Mark — the earliest gospel — and it is the single cleanest proof that this was an argument *among Jews about Torah*, not a repudiation of it.

### 6. Asked about ritual six different ways, he redirects to the interior every time
> Disciples: "Do you want us to fast? How shall we pray? Shall we give alms? What diet shall we observe?" Jesus: "Do not tell lies, and do not do what you hate, for all things are plain in the sight of heaven." — Thomas 6
> "Is circumcision beneficial or not?" — "If it were beneficial, their father would beget them already circumcised from their mother. Rather, the true circumcision in spirit has become completely profitable." — Thomas 53
> "Why do you wash the outside of the cup? Do you not realize that he who made the inside is the same one who made the outside?" — Thomas 89 (cf. Matthew 23:25-26)

Independent attestation, outside the synoptics, of exactly the same move: the ritual question gets answered at the level of the heart. Thomas 53 does not say circumcision is sin — it relocates it ("the true circumcision in spirit"). **Relocation, not abolition.** That is the pattern everywhere.

### 7. The earliest Jewish-Christian reading says the same thing
> "Moses... perceived that the vice of sacrificing to idols had been deeply ingrained into the people from their association with the Egyptians, and that the root of this evil could not be extracted from them; he allowed them indeed to sacrifice, but permitted it to be done only to God, that by any means he might cut off one half of the deeply ingrained evil, leaving the other half to be corrected by another, and at a future time." — *Clementine Recognitions* 1.36

And on Matthew 5:17 directly:

> "And also that He said, 'I am not come to destroy the law,' and yet that He appeared to be destroying it, is the part of one intimating that the things which He destroyed did not belong to the law." — *Clementine Homilies* 3.51

Second-century Jewish Christianity read Jesus exactly as a Torah-ranker: the ethical core is the law, the cultic apparatus was a concession. Whatever else you think of the Clementines, **this reading is not a 21st-century invention.**

## Questions People Raise

### "Matthew 8:4 / Mark 1:44 / Luke 5:14 — he sends the healed leper to the priest with the Mosaic offering. He puts a man *into* the sacrificial system."
**This is the hardest text on the card. Triple-attested, early, and it means what it says. Concede it immediately and completely — then show that it proves our thesis rather than theirs.**

> "Go, show yourself to the priest, and offer the gift that Moses commanded, as a testimony to them." — Matthew 8:4

Three honest things:

1. **It is a civil-status statute, not a sin offering.** Leviticus 13-14 makes the priest the certifying authority for reentry into the community — the ancient equivalent of a public-health clearance and a restored legal personhood. Jesus heals the man, then sends him to get his life back on paper. Nothing in the errand concerns the forgiveness of sins.
2. **"As a testimony to them."** The point of the trip is a witness delivered *to the priesthood* — the certifying authority is being handed evidence.
3. **It wrecks the "Jesus abolished the Law" reading — which is our reading's opponent, not ours.** If you want a Jesus who voided Torah, you have to explain this verse. We don't. He worked inside it.

Then draw the line that actually matters: **he never once sends anyone to the Temple to purchase forgiveness.** He forgives directly, on his own authority, with no altar and no priest (Mark 2:5; Luke 7:48). Civil statute: obeyed. Sin-atonement machinery: bypassed. That distinction is the entire case, and this verse sharpens it.

### "Mark 7:19 — 'thus he declared all foods clean.' That kills your vegetarian Jesus *and* James's Acts 15 food decrees."
Three moves, in order, and don't skip to the third.

1. **The clause is Mark's aside.** The Greek participle *katharizōn* is syntactically detached from Jesus's quoted speech — which is why translations have to supply "thus he declared." Decisive tell: **Matthew copies this whole pericope (15:1-20) and the comment is not there.** Matthew had Mark in front of him and did not treat it as dominical. Underneath that is a textual fact (Florez sheet, B4): the oldest manuscripts have a masculine participle, *katharizōn*, agreeing with "he said," so the clause is the narrator's comment. Later Byzantine copies have a neuter form, which folds it into Jesus's own sentence about digestion. That's why the KJV has Jesus himself say food "goeth out into the draught, purging all meats" (Mark 7:19, KJV), and why several modern Bibles put the clause in parentheses. The scholarship is mainstream, not fringe: Meier, a Catholic priest, concludes in *A Marginal Jew* vol. 4 (2009) that the historical Jesus didn't abolish the food laws and that 7:19b is Mark's; Yarbro Collins and Marcus read it the same way. (A smaller point: the KJV's Mark 7:16, "If any man have ears to hear, let him hear," is itself missing from the oldest manuscripts.) And if Jesus had plainly declared all foods clean, the Jerusalem church in Acts 10-11 and 15 and Galatians 2 wouldn't still be fighting about food purity twenty years later.
2. **But do not rest on that**, because the underlying saying is early and independently attested and **we accept it.** Thomas 14: "For what goes into your mouth will not defile you, but that which issues from your mouth — it is that which will defile you." And the mission instruction "eat what they will set before you" is in both Thomas 14b and Q/Luke 10:8. The principle is real.
3. **So read what the principle is about: ritual defilement, not ethics.** The pericope's actual question is *handwashing* (Mark 7:2, 5) — Pharisaic *paradosis*, not Leviticus. Jesus's answer is that purity is moral, not alimentary. **A thing can be ritually clean and still be wrong to do.** Our case about killing animals was never a kosher case; it is a non-harm case built on Isaiah 66:3, Hosea 6:6, and the Temple action.

On Acts 15: look at what James actually decreed — blood, strangled meat, idol-meat, *porneia*. That is not a kosher code either. It is a restriction on **killing and blood**, which is a different question from clean-and-unclean species. Mark 7 and Acts 15 are answering two different questions and the collision is apparent, not real. (Didache 6:3 preserves the same shape: flexible on food generally, immovable on idol-meat.)

### "Matthew 23:2-3 — 'do whatever they teach you' — but Matthew 15 has him calling their tradition a nullification of God's word. Your Jesus contradicts himself in one gospel."
The tension is real, it is inside one evangelist, and it resolves on a distinction Jesus draws explicitly.

- **23:2-3 affirms the seat.** "The scribes and the Pharisees sit on Moses' seat; therefore do whatever they teach you... but do not do as they do, for they do not practice what they teach." The next thirty verses attack **the occupants**, not the office. The whole chapter's complaint is that *they don't do their own teaching* — which presupposes the teaching is right.
- **15:1-20 contests the superstructure.** The subject is named: *paradosis*, handed-down tradition — korban vows and handwashing. "You have made void the word of God for the sake of your tradition" (15:6). He defends written Torah *against* the tradition.

Written Torah: affirmed. Scribal accretion: contested. Teachers' hypocrisy: flayed. That is an intra-Jewish argument of a kind the Talmud itself conducts, in the same register.

### "'Fulfill' (πληρῶσαι) means 'complete, and therefore bring to an end.'"
Verses 18-19 immediately forbid that: "not one stroke will pass from the law," and "whoever breaks one of the least of these commandments... will be called least." If "fulfill" meant "terminate," v.19 is incoherent — you cannot be downgraded for breaking a commandment that was just retired. And v.20 demands *more* righteousness, which an ending does not produce. In Matthew the word means **fill full** — bring to intended fullness. The Sermon then demonstrates it for five straight paragraphs.

### "If he kept the Law, why do you reject Leviticus's sacrificial system?"
We don't reject it on Jesus's authority — we question whether it was original. Jeremiah 7:21-22 denies God ever commanded burnt offerings; mainstream scholarship dates the bulk of the Priestly legislation later than the prophets who condemned sacrifice; and the Clementine tradition read it as a Mosaic concession (Recognitions 1.36-39). That's a separate argument with its own card. **On this card the narrower point holds regardless:** even granting Leviticus entirely as given, Jesus ranks it beneath mercy, and he says so in a Sabbath courtroom.

### "This is just liberal cherry-picking — keep the ethics, drop the ritual."
It isn't our ranking, it's his, and he states it with the protective clause attached: "without neglecting the others" (Matthew 23:23). We are not dropping anything. We are putting it in the order Jesus put it in, and the order came from Hosea before him. Cherry-picking would be citing 23:23 and stopping before the last clause — which is precisely what we refuse to do.

### "So are Christians obligated to keep the Sabbath and the food laws?"
Honest answer: Jesus never addressed Gentiles on this, so we don't pretend he did. James did (Acts 15), and the oldest Christian church manual gives a graduated answer rather than a binary:

> "If you can bear the Lord's full yoke, you will be perfect. But if you cannot, then do what you can. Now about food: undertake what you can. But keep strictly away from what is offered to idols." — Didache 6:2-3

A floor and a direction of travel. That is what earliest practice actually looked like — not "all of it or none of it."

## In One Sentence

- He didn't lower the bar. Read Matthew 5:20 — the Pharisees weren't righteous *enough*.
- "Weightier matters of the law." That's a comparative, inside the law, in his own mouth.
- Finish the verse: "these you ought to have practiced **without neglecting the others.**" He never said stop tithing.
- He settled a Sabbath case by quoting Hosea. That's not dismissing the Law — that's arguing it.
- A scribe said love matters more than all burnt offerings, and Jesus told him he was close to the kingdom.
- He sends the leper to the priest and never sends anyone to the altar for forgiveness. Both of those are true, and together they're the whole argument.
- Mark 7 is about handwashing. Something can be ritually clean and still be wrong to do.
- Marcion abolished the Law. Paul relativized it. Jesus intensified it. Three different positions, and we're on the third.
