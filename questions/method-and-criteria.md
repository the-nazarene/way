# What Is Your Criterion? How We Decide What Jesus Actually Said

**Position:** We have a stated method, it is public, and it is applied the same way to passages we like and passages we don't. Four tools do the work: multiple independent attestation; the *direction of redaction* (we distrust an editorial change that moves toward the editor's known theology and away from his own written source); the criterion of embarrassment (we trust material preserved against the preserver's interest); and the manuscript record itself, which is physical evidence rather than preference. We are not claiming the Bible is corrupt. We are claiming it has an editorial history that textual critics of every theological persuasion already document, and that the history is visible at specific, nameable seams. This method is also not a modern invention — the Jewish-Christian wing of the early church ran a version of it, and in the Clementine literature an ancient opponent asks Peter the exact question we get asked, and gets a criterion in reply.

---

## Strongest Evidence

### 1. The criterion, in one sentence — and both test cases, so you can check it

**Stated:** *A reading is suspect in proportion to how much it moves toward the known theology of the person who wrote it and away from the source he was copying. A reading is strong in proportion to how little its preserver gained by keeping it.*

Now watch it run in both directions.

**Case A — we prefer Mark.** Mark 14:24: "This is my blood of the covenant, poured out for many." Matthew 26:28 copies Mark and adds **"for the forgiveness of sins."** Matthew had Mark in front of him; the clause is new; it moves toward Matthew's heavy fulfillment theology; and no other early witness has it (Luke 22:20, 1 Corinthians 11:25, John, the Didache). Flag it.

**Case B — we prefer Matthew.** Matthew 25:31-46, the sheep and the goats, has no Markan or Lukan parallel. It survives in one source only — and it is load-bearing for us, because its judgment criterion is mercy performed. Does the criterion let us keep it? Yes, and for a reason we can state: **Matthew is the most Torah-intensifying, most Jewish-framed evangelist, and this passage hands salvation to people who did not know who they were serving** ("Lord, when was it that we saw you hungry?"). It gives nothing to Matthew's program and takes the verdict out of the hands of the religiously credentialed. Material that embarrasses its own preserver is the strongest material there is.

**Same tool, opposite outcomes, for reasons available to inspection.** That is what a criterion is.

### 2. The criterion costs us things — which is how you know it isn't a preference

> "Till heaven and earth pass, one jot or one tittle shall in no wise pass from the law." — Matthew 5:18 / Luke 16:17

This saying is in **Q** — attested independently in Matthew and Luke, upstream of either editor — and it affirms the permanence of the Torah in the strongest possible terms. A Marcion-style reading would want it gone. We keep it, and we build Card 2 on it.

**The same method has to run on our own verse.** The scholars who read Matthew 28:19 as Matthew's composition (Luz, Davies and Allison, Meier) also treat Matthew 5:17-19 as the work of Matthew or his Torah-keeping community. We can't take their verdict on 28:19 and wave it off on 5:17-19 without a reason, and the reason has to be said out loud. It is two things. **5:18 has an independent parallel and 28:19 has none:** "It is easier for heaven and earth to pass, than one tittle of the law to fail" (Luke 16:17, KJV). And **5:17-19 fits what Jesus does in all three Synoptics** (the healed leper sent to the priest with the offering Moses commanded, Mark 1:44), while 28:19's three-name formula fits nothing the apostles do: every baptism in Acts that states a formula is in Jesus's name. What we don't claim is that 5:17 and 5:19 are his verbatim words; our ground is 5:18, the Q saying. Even that is strong ground rather than untouchable ground: Kloppenborg assigns the Q form to Q's latest layer. (Florez sheet, B18)

Same with **Mark 10:45** ("a ransom for many"): earliest source, no redaction escape hatch, and we concede the text and argue the *meaning* instead. If our method were "delete what's inconvenient," these two verses would be the first to go.

### 3. The seams are in the manuscripts, not in our opinions

| Passage | What the manuscript record shows |
|---|---|
| Mark 16:9-20 | Absent from Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, our two oldest complete Greek codices. Multiple competing endings survive. Nearly every modern Bible brackets it. |
| 1 John 5:7-8 (Comma Johanneum) | No Greek manuscript before the 14th century; no Greek Father ever cites it; it originates as a Latin marginal gloss. |
| John 7:53-8:11 | Absent from P66 and P75 (about 200, the earliest copies of John) and from every Greek copy before Codex Bezae in the fifth century; Origen's commentary goes from 7:52 straight to 8:12. It *floats* — some manuscripts place it in Luke. The story is older than its place in John: Eusebius reports that Papias "relates another story of a woman, who was accused of many sins before the Lord, which is contained in the Gospel according to the Hebrews" (*Church History* 3.39.16, McGiffert). (Florez sheet, A2) |
| Matthew 6:13b ("For thine is the kingdom…") | Absent from Sinaiticus, Vaticanus and Bezae. The earliest commentaries on the prayer (Tertullian, Cyprian, Origen) go phrase by phrase and stop at "deliver us from evil"; Luke's prayer has no doxology in any manuscript. Liturgical growth. (Florez sheet, A6) |
| Matthew 17:21, 18:11, 23:14; Mark 7:16, 9:44 and 9:46, 11:26; Luke 17:36; John 5:3b-4 | Whole KJV verses the oldest witnesses lack, with witness lists that differ verse by verse and often include the papyri, Bezae or the Old Syriac, not only the two great codices. Most are copied in from a parallel where the saying is secure (18:11 from Luke 19:10; Mark 11:26 from Matthew 6:15; Luke 17:36 from Matthew 24:40), so nothing Jesus said is lost; the text grew. (Florez sheet, A7-A16) |
| Matthew 26:28 vs. Mark 14:24 | Not a manuscript variant but a redaction visible by direct comparison of a copy with its source. |

**The method overrules the two great codices too.** At Matthew 27:49, Sinaiticus and Vaticanus both carry the spear thrust from John 19:34, placed *before* Jesus dies in verse 50, which would make the spear the cause of death. The critical editions reject them there (NA28 omits; Metzger calls it an early intrusion), and the KJV, following later manuscripts, has no spear: "let us see whether Elias will come to save him" (KJV). So "you just follow two Alexandrian manuscripts" is false. They are weighed like every other witness, and when everything else is against them, they lose. (Florez sheet, A34)

**Some seams are honestly open; don't put them on the list with Mark 16.** Luke 22:43-44, the bloody sweat: the critical text brackets it, and the evangelical Tyndale House edition keeps it. Luke 23:34a, "Father, forgive them": the best manuscripts omit it, but early writers know it, and Ehrman himself argues it is original and was cut. Luke 22:19b-20, the longer cup words: in every Greek manuscript but Bezae; the critical text prints them, and Ehrman argues they import Paul. Call all three CONTESTED. Overclaiming one of these costs us the uncontested ones. (Florez sheet, A3-A5)

None of that is our claim. It is in the apparatus of every critical edition and the footnotes of the Bible in the other person's hand. **We did not find the seams. We are reading the ones textual scholarship already marked.**

### 4. We apply embarrassment against ourselves too

The criterion of embarrassment is the main reason we trust the historicity of things that are awkward for high Christology — Jesus baptized by John for repentance (Mark 1:9), "why do you call me good? No one is good but God alone" (Mark 10:18), Gethsemane, "my God, my God, why have you forsaken me" (Mark 15:34), "about that day or hour no one knows... nor the Son" (Mark 13:32).

But it cuts our way too, and we say so: the Last Supper tradition itself is awkward for a Jewish teacher who opposed blood ritual, and it is early and multiply attested. We do not deny the scene. We dispute one clause in one gospel. **Those are very different claims, and the difference is the whole argument.**

### 5. Ancient precedent: the Clementine "false pericopes" doctrine

> "For the Scriptures have had joined to them many falsehoods against God on this account... after a little the written law had added to it certain falsehoods contrary to the law of God."
> — *Clementine Homilies* 2.38, "Corruption of the Law"

The doctrine is then worked out across Homilies 3.42-51 — and the striking thing is the shape of the exchange. **Simon asks the question we get asked:**

> Simon: "How is it possible to recognise the other things in the law which are from the tradition of Moses, and are true, and are mixed up with these falsehoods?" — *Homilies* 3.48

Peter's answer is a criterion, not a preference: the true Prophet's own teaching is the test (3.49), applied with discernment —

> "And also, inasmuch as He said, 'Be ye prudent money-changers,' it is because there are genuine and spurious words." — *Homilies* 3.50

And he reads Matthew 5:17 exactly as Card 2 does:

> "And also that He said, 'I am not come to destroy the law,' and yet that He appeared to be destroying it, is the part of one intimating that the things which He destroyed did not belong to the law." — *Homilies* 3.51

A second-century Jewish-Christian text, claiming Petrine authority, answering "what's your criterion?" with a criterion. We are not improvising a method. We are recovering one that had an ancient address.

### 6. What we actually hold about scripture, stated positively

Not "the Bible is unreliable." This:

1. **Scripture is a library, not a monolith** — dozens of authors across a millennium, with editors who had identifiable institutional interests. Jeremiah says so from inside the canon: "the false pen of the scribes has made it into a lie" (Jeremiah 8:8).
2. **It is a faithful witness and an uneven one.** Faithful enough to trust; uneven enough to require discernment. Both halves of that sentence matter.
3. **It is most authoritative where it carries Jesus's own voice**, and next where the prophets and Jesus independently agree — which is exactly where they put mercy over sacrifice.
4. **We add no documents.** Thomas and the Nag Hammadi texts are historical witnesses to early Christianity; so are the Didache, the Clementines, and the books the Ethiopian church kept. Witnesses, not a replacement canon.
5. **We hold scripture high enough to read it honestly.** Inerrancy asks a reader to notice less, not more. We want every seam looked at, including the ones that hurt us.

## Questions People Raise

### "That's a canon within a canon. You've just built a Bible out of your preferences."
Everyone has a canon within a canon — the question is whether yours is **stated**. Ask how they reconcile Leviticus 11 with Mark 7, or Jeremiah 7:22 with Numbers 28, or "no one has ascended to heaven" (John 3:13) with Elijah's chariot. He has a method. He will call it "the clear teaching of scripture," which is a canon within a canon with the criterion left unspoken. Ours is written down, in section 1 of this card, with both test cases shown. **Published and falsifiable beats silent and unexaminable.**

### "If the text is corrupted, why trust the parts you like?"
Because we are not claiming the text is corrupted. We are claiming it has a documented editorial history located at specific places, and we identify those places with external evidence: manuscripts that lack a passage, a copy that differs from its surviving source, a reading no early Father quotes. Mark 16:9-20 isn't suspect because we dislike snake-handling; it's suspect because it isn't in Sinaiticus. And the same evidence cuts against Sinaiticus when it carries an insertion (Matthew 27:49, §3). **"Corrupted" is a global claim we don't make. "Edited here, visibly, and here's the witness list" is a local claim, and it's the only kind we make.**

### "You've reinvented Marcion. Same canon surgery, same anti-sacrifice God, same discarded Hebrew cult — you just cut Paul instead of the Jews."
**The twenty-second answer, memorize it:** Marcion threw out the Hebrew Bible, the Hebrew God, and the Jewish Jesus — and kept Paul. We keep the Hebrew prophets, the Hebrew God, and the Jewish Jesus — and we question Paul. He cut the Jews and kept the Gentile apostle; we are the photographic negative of that. And he *wrote a new canon* — an edited Luke plus ten Paulines, with the Old Testament thrown in the fire. We have added nothing and removed nothing. The second-century group that actually held our position were the Ebionites, and Marcion's opponents and ours are the same people.

It's a good line and it will get a laugh. Take the laugh, then land that. One more beat if you have room: Marcion needed two Gods because he could not read the Hebrew scriptures as anything but a monolith. Our whole point is that it *isn't* one — which is why we don't need a second God and he did.

### "This is just the Jesus Seminar / the Jefferson Bible with extra steps."
Jefferson took scissors to a printed Bible and kept what a deist found reasonable. We keep Jesus's apocalyptic sayings, his exorcisms, his judgment language, his Torah affirmations, and Mark 10:45 — all of it awkward for a tidy modern reading. Our criterion is **source-critical** (what moved, from what, toward whose theology), not **taste-critical** (what a modern finds plausible). Those produce different results, and ours keeps more of the strange material, not less.

### "Jeremiah 8:8 isn't about falsifying Torah. It's about scribes who misuse it."
Fair, and worth conceding cleanly. The Hebrew is debated and the referent of "made it into a lie" is contested. What the verse establishes beyond dispute is that **a canonical prophet accused the scribal class of falsifying the written law of the LORD.** That the specific falsification was the sacrificial legislation is *our inference* from its immediate neighborhood — Jeremiah 7:21-22, where he denies God ever commanded burnt offerings. Say "inference" out loud. It's a strong one and it survives being labeled.

### "Your criterion is unfalsifiable. Any text you dislike becomes 'redaction.'"
Then falsify it. The method makes predictions: if Matthew's atonement clause is redactional, it should be missing from his source and from independent early witnesses — and it is missing from all of them. If the longer ending of Mark were original, it should appear in our oldest codices — and it doesn't. Run it the other way: show us a passage where we claim redaction and the manuscript evidence goes against us, and we'll drop the claim. **We already keep Q's jot-and-tittle saying and Mark's ransom saying against our own interest. A method that can lose is a method.**

### "Textual criticism is a Christian discipline. The scholars who catalogued those variants mostly still believe in substitutionary atonement."
Correct, and we say so freely — it's one of the strongest things about our evidence base. **The data is not ours and it isn't partisan.** Metzger was a devout Presbyterian; Nestle-Aland is the standard in evangelical seminaries; the brackets around Mark 16:9-20 were put there by committees of believing Christians. We are not disputing their textual findings at any point. We disagree with them about **theology** — about what the ranking of sources implies for what Jesus taught — and that is a separate argument conducted on top of shared evidence. When someone points out that our textual facts come from believing scholars, the right response is "yes, which is why you can't call them biased against you."

### "Who are you to sit in judgment over scripture? That's pride."
It would be, if the alternative were sitting under it without choosing. But nobody does that. The moment you decide that Leviticus 20:13 doesn't govern your legal code and Matthew 22:39 does, you have made a judgment about scripture. Every tradition has — that's what a hermeneutic *is*. The choice isn't between judging and not judging; it's between **doing it in the open with a stated rule and doing it silently while calling it obedience.** We'd rather be accountable for our rule than pretend we don't have one. And the rule we chose is: whatever Jesus said outranks what anyone said about him. If that's pride, it's an odd kind.

### "Fine — then what would change your mind?"
Answer it, because the willingness is the argument. A pre-Matthean source with the atonement clause in it. A second-century Greek witness to the Comma. Mark 16:9-20 in a 4th-century codex. Evidence that Jesus directed anyone to the Temple for the forgiveness of sins rather than for civil certification. Any of those would cost us a position and we'd pay it. **Then ask the same question back,** gently, and listen to the answer — because that exchange, more than any verse, tells the room which side is doing history.

## In One Sentence

- Everyone reads with a criterion. Ours is written down. Ask him for his.
- We're not saying the Bible is corrupted. We're saying it was edited, in specific places, and the manuscripts are where we found out.
- We didn't discover the seams. We're reading the footnotes already in your Bible.
- Matthew copied Mark and added five words. That's not a theory — you can lay the two columns side by side.
- Marcion kept Paul and burned the Hebrew Bible. We keep the Hebrew Bible and question Paul. We're his photographic negative.
- A method that can't lose isn't a method. Ours loses Mark 10:45 and we keep the verse anyway.
- Our textual evidence comes from believing Christian scholars. You can't call it biased against you.
- Ask what would change his mind. Then answer it about yourself first.
- Simon asked Peter "how do you tell true from false in the law?" in the second century. Peter answered with a rule. So do we.
