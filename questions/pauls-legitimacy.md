# Is Paul a Reliable Source for What Jesus Taught?

**Position:** Paul never met the living Jesus, and he grounds the authority of his gospel in private revelation rather than in anything handed to him by the men who did. He says so emphatically and repeatedly, in his own undisputed letter: not from any man, not taught, received by revelation; and of James, Peter, and John — "they added nothing to my preaching." He also wrote the only surviving body of Christian theology that almost never quotes Jesus. He contradicted the binding ruling of James — Jesus's brother and head of the Jerusalem church — once he was out of the room, and the earliest Jewish-Christian communities, the ones with direct lineage back to that church, named him as an opponent rather than an authority.

This is not a claim about Paul's character. He was a brilliant, sincere man working from a Pharisaic framework and a vision he genuinely trusted; nothing here requires him to be a fraud, and we will not argue that he was. The claim is structural: **Paul's gospel has a different source and a different content from the teaching of Jesus.** He is a primary source for Paul's theology, and he is not a source for what Jesus taught. Thirteen of twenty-seven New Testament books are his, which is why the distinction matters so much.

**How we weight Acts.** Acts is late (c. 80–110 CE) and openly irenic — its project is to show Paul and Jerusalem in harmony. So we weight it most heavily where it reports something *embarrassing to its own agenda*: James ruling against the Gentile-freedom position (15:19-20), Jerusalem still anxious about Paul a decade later (21:20-25), the Paul–Barnabas split (15:39). We weight it least where it smooths things over. Where Acts and Paul's own letters conflict, the letters win — they are earlier and first-hand. That principle is stated up front so nobody has to guess at it.

## Strongest Evidence

### 1. Paul locates his gospel's authority in revelation, not in transmission from the eyewitnesses
> "The gospel I preached is not of human origin. I did not receive it from any man, nor was I taught it; rather, I received it by revelation from Jesus Christ." — Galatians 1:11-12

He presses the point: "I did not go up to Jerusalem to see those who were apostles before me" (Gal 1:16-17), and of the Jerusalem pillars — "whatever they were makes no difference to me... they added nothing to my preaching" (Gal 2:6).

**And three verses after that first quote, he says he did go to Jerusalem.** Finish the passage yourself, because a careful reader will: "Then after three years I went up to Jerusalem to get acquainted with Cephas and stayed with him fifteen days. I saw none of the other apostles — only James, the Lord's brother" (Gal 1:18-19). The verb is ἱστορῆσαι — to make inquiry of, to interview. So yes, Paul met Peter and James and asked them things.

Notice what the whole paragraph is *for*. Every clause minimizes dependence: not immediately, not to the apostles, three years later, fifteen days, only James, and then "added nothing." Paul brings the visit up in order to argue that it did not make him anyone's student. That is his claim, not ours. He is not hiding the contact; he is denying that the contact is where his gospel came from.

### 2. The letters almost never quote Jesus
Across roughly two thousand verses in the undisputed letters, Paul cites a saying of Jesus about twice — on divorce (1 Cor 7:10-11) and on supporting preachers (1 Cor 9:14) — plus the words at the Supper (1 Cor 11:23-25). No parables. No Sermon on the Mount. No "the kingdom of God is within you," no good Samaritan, no Matthew 25, no "keep the commandments." Almost no narrative of a life he is writing about within thirty years of it.

He does use formal tradition-language twice, and it's worth knowing exactly where: *παρέλαβον / παρέδωκα*, "I received / I passed on," at 1 Corinthians 15:3 and 11:23. Look at the content. 15:3-7 is a creed — died, buried, raised, appeared — and a list of appearances, not teaching. And at 11:23 Paul says he received it "from the Lord" (*ἀπὸ τοῦ κυρίου*), crediting the vision again rather than the Twelve. The one place he unambiguously transmits received material is a resurrection-appearance list. On the question "what did Jesus teach?" the transmission channel is essentially empty.

*A note we make ourselves:* Acts 1:21-22 — present from John's baptism, witness to the resurrection — is sometimes run as a definition of apostleship Paul fails. **Don't use it that way.** It is the criterion for filling Judas's seat among the Twelve, and the New Testament calls plenty of non-Twelve figures apostles: Barnabas (Acts 14:14), James the Lord's brother (Gal 1:19), Andronicus and Junia (Rom 16:7). We are not arguing Paul wasn't an apostle. We are arguing he isn't an eyewitness source for the teaching — and that argument stands on the silence above, which no one can list their way out of.

### 3. James ruled, and Paul reversed it in writing
> "It is my judgment that we should not make it difficult for the Gentiles who are turning to God. Instead we should write to them, telling them to abstain from food polluted by idols, from sexual immorality, from the meat of strangled animals and from blood." — Acts 15:19-20

James presides and decides; Paul does not overrule him in the room. Then in his letters he reverses the first decree outright: "Eat anything sold in the meat market without raising questions of conscience" (1 Cor 10:25). This is exactly the kind of Acts detail our stated principle weights heavily — Luke has no motive to invent Jerusalem constraining Paul. And the Jerusalem church's authority is not a modern inference: Paul himself lists James first among the pillars (Gal 2:9), and Thomas records Jesus appointing him directly — "Wherever you are, you are to go to James the righteous, for whose sake heaven and earth came into being" (Gospel of Thomas 12, Lambdin). On Thomas, cite it as an independent sayings tradition, not as a 50s-CE text; the mainstream date for the collection as received is c. 140 CE, and James's primacy stands on Acts 15, Galatians 2, Hegesippus, and Josephus without it.

### 4. James's letter answers the same question the other way, and the church knew it
> "You see that a person is considered righteous by what they do, and not by faith alone." — James 2:24
> "A person is justified by faith apart from the works of the law." — Romans 3:28

Luther understood the problem perfectly well. His resolution was to downgrade James as "an epistle of straw" rather than question Paul. He chose Paul over Jesus's brother, and Protestantism inherited that choice.

### 5. The Jewish-Christian tradition remembers him as the opponent
> "For some from among the Gentiles have rejected my legal preaching, attaching themselves to certain lawless and trifling preaching of the man who is my enemy." — Epistle of Peter to James 2 (prefixed to the Clementine Homilies)

The Clementine Homilies stage Peter against an opponent whose credentials are a vision: "If, then, our Jesus appeared to you in a vision, made Himself known to you, and spoke to you, it was as one who is enraged with an adversary... if you were seen and taught by Him, and became His apostle for a single hour, proclaim His utterances, interpret His sayings, love His apostles, contend not with me who companied with Him" (Homilies 17.19). The named target is "Simon," but the argument is about a man who claims apostleship from a vision, and Peter answers him: "if you say that I am condemned, you bring an accusation against God, who revealed the Christ to me" (Homilies 17.19, Donaldson, ANF 8). "Condemned" is Paul's word for Peter at Galatians 2:11 (Greek *kategnōsmenos*; the KJV has "to be blamed"), turned back on him. Epiphanius (*Panarion* 30) records the Ebionites and Nazarenes rejecting Paul as an apostate from the law.

An older layer of the same literature drops the mask. In *Recognitions* 1.70, as James has the people ready to be baptized, "some one of our enemies" starts a riot in the Temple, and "that enemy attacked James, and threw him headlong from the top of the steps." In 1.71, "that enemy had received a commission from Caiaphas, the chief priest, that he should arrest all who believed in Jesus, and should go to Damascus with his letters" (Smith, ANF 8). The text never names him; the Damascus commission is Saul's in Acts 9:1-2. Specialists date this source to about 150-200 (Van Voorst; Jones), so it is a second-century counter-history to Acts from the James side, not eyewitness testimony.

## Questions People Raise

### "Galatians 1:18-19 destroys your whole premise. He spent fifteen days with Peter and met James."
Correct, and we raise it ourselves above rather than wait for it. Three things. First, those verses are Paul's, in a paragraph whose every clause is designed to minimize what he owed that visit — he is the one insisting it didn't make him a disciple of the Twelve. Second, fifteen days with Peter three years after his call is not an apprenticeship in a teaching corpus, and the proof is the letters: whatever he inquired about, Jesus's sayings are not what came back. Third, and decisively, Paul tells you what the authority of his gospel rests on, and it isn't that visit: "not from any man, nor was I taught it" (1:12), "added nothing" (2:6). Our claim is about the source of the gospel's authority and its content. Verse 18 adjusts the biography; it doesn't touch either.

### "The Jerusalem church accepted him — they gave him the right hand of fellowship (Galatians 2:9)."
That's Paul's account of a meeting whose outcome he then overrode in practice. Acts 21:20-25 shows the Jerusalem leadership still anxious about reports of Paul teaching Jews to abandon Moses, and asking him to publicly prove otherwise — a full decade later. Acceptance of a Gentile mission is not endorsement of a theology, and Paul himself describes opposing Peter to his face and losing Barnabas, his own sponsor, to the other side (Gal 2:11-13).

### "Peter personally endorses Paul — 2 Peter 3:15-16 calls him 'our beloved brother Paul.'"
2 Peter is the most widely doubted book in the New Testament. Critical scholars generally treat it as pseudonymous, with proposed dates ranging from c. 80 CE into the second century; conservative scholars defend Petrine authorship. We are not going to pretend their own experts concede the late date — they don't. The point survives the dispute: a letter whose authorship is contested across the field is thin ground for settling a first-century question, and the passage itself concedes that Paul's letters contain "some things hard to understand, which the ignorant and unstable twist." That concession is the useful part, and it's there on anyone's dating.

### "James 2 isn't aimed at Paul. 2:19 targets *dead* faith — bare assent, the kind demons have. And 2:23 quotes Genesis 15:6 as faith, exactly like Paul does."
The best version of the objection, and both halves are true. James 2:19 does target bare assent, and James 2:23 does cite Genesis 15:6. Concede that cleanly, then look at what each man does with that verse.

Paul: Genesis 15:6 proves Abraham was reckoned righteous *apart from* works — "if Abraham was justified by works, he has something to boast about" (Rom 4:2-5). James: Genesis 15:6 was *fulfilled* when Abraham offered Isaac — the verse is completed by the deed (Jas 2:21-23). Same proof text, opposite function, both arguing about justification. That is hard to explain as two unrelated conversations.

Second, James 2:24 negates a specific phrase — *πίστει μόνον*, "by faith alone." No one else in Second Temple Judaism is on record advocating it. A position is only worth refuting by name if somebody is teaching it.

Third, grant the harmonization entirely and the historical fact is unchanged: within a generation, a letter circulating under the name of Jesus's brother found "faith alone" worth refuting by name. Something in the air needed answering.

And we'll concede one more thing, because it's true and because conceding it helps: Paul can sound like James. "God will repay each person according to what they have done... it is the doers of the law who will be justified" (Rom 2:6-13). The tension isn't only between the two men; some of it is inside Paul. Which is the point — his system needs harmonizing, and Jesus's answer doesn't. Asked directly how to inherit eternal life, Jesus said "keep the commandments" (Matt 19:17); his judgment scene sorts by what people did (Matt 25:31-46); "not everyone who says to me, 'Lord, Lord'" (Matt 7:21). James agrees with Jesus without needing a framework.

### "'Works of the law' doesn't mean good deeds — Dunn and Wright showed it means Jewish boundary markers. The New Perspective dissolves your contradiction."
This is the strongest scholarly objection on the card, and the evidence for it is partly *ours*. 4QMMT uses the Hebrew *ma'ase ha-torah* for a list of specific halakhic rulings — calendar, the *nitzok* purity ruling, red-heifer procedure, Gentile grain offerings. Our own primary-text file says so in as many words: these were "the entrance requirements and boundary markers of a particular Jewish community — not a general statement about human morality" (`christianity/dead-sea-scrolls/Incoming/dead-sea-scrolls-selected-texts.md`). We're not going to argue against our own file.

Three responses, in order of strength.

**It's a reading of Paul, not a source for Paul.** The New Perspective narrows what Paul was attacking. It supplies no transmission line from Jesus to Paul, and it doesn't put a single parable into his letters. Argument #2 above is untouched.

**It makes the break with Jerusalem sharper, not softer.** If "works of the law" means the practices that defined who belonged to the covenant community, then what Paul dismantled was precisely the identity of the movement James led — the Torah-observant Jewish community in Jerusalem. The New Perspective makes Paul less of a proto-Lutheran and *more* of a departure from James, which is our thesis, not theirs.

**It doesn't reach Jesus's own words.** Jesus isn't arguing about boundary markers. "Not the smallest letter" (Matt 5:17-19). "Keep the commandments" (Matt 19:17). Woe to those who neglect "justice, mercy and faithfulness" (Matt 23:23). He answers the salvation question with commandments, and no reading of *erga nomou* changes what he said.

### "You cite Acts when it suits you and dismiss it when it doesn't."
Fair challenge, and the reason we state the principle up front rather than mid-argument. We weight Acts where it is embarrassing to its own harmonizing project and discount it where it serves that project — the same way historians read any apologetic source. Note what we are *not* doing: we are not running the three conflicting conversion narratives (9:3-8; 22:6-11; 26:12-18, including whether the companions heard the voice at 9:7 vs. 22:9) as evidence that Paul lied. Those are evidence about Luke's compositional method, not about Paul's honesty. And where Acts and the letters conflict, we take the letters.

### "The Clementines are 3rd-4th century anti-Pauline fiction — you can't use them as history."
Agreed that they are not first-century documents, and we don't cite them as eyewitness testimony. We cite them for something narrower and uncontested: Jewish-Christian communities claiming descent from the Jerusalem church preserved a tradition in which Peter's authority and a visionary apostle's authority were in direct conflict. That such a tradition existed at all is the point. And "3rd-4th century" dates the books, not everything in them. The Homilies and Recognitions as we have them are fourth-century, built on a lost early-third-century novel; most specialists hold that *Recognitions* 1.27-71 is an older source inside it, dated about 150-200 (Van Voorst, *The Ascents of James*, 1989; F. Stanley Jones, 1995; neither held). Around 375 Epiphanius saw Ebionite books he calls the *Ascents of James*, "as though he were declaring against the temple and the sacrifices, and against the fire upon the altar" (*Panarion* 30,16,7, Wescoe). Whether that is the same book as Recognitions 1 is disputed; Jones says no. What is settled and what isn't: Christian Florez's Clementine kernels sheet (`extended-library/clementine-kernel-texts-florez.md`).

### "Paul's prophecies about the return didn't fail — he spoke of a general future hope."
He used the first-person plural and framed it as revelation: "we who are still alive, who are left until the coming of the Lord" (1 Thess 4:15-17); "we will not all sleep, but we will all be changed" (1 Cor 15:51); "the night is nearly over" (Rom 13:12). Ehrman, Allison, and Schweitzer all read this as genuine imminent expectation. We are not running Deuteronomy 18:22 as a verdict on the man — that's a character argument and we don't need one. The structural point is enough: imminence was load-bearing for Paul's ethics (stay as you are, 1 Cor 7:29-31) and for his soteriology, and it didn't happen. A framework built on a timetable has to be re-engineered when the timetable fails, and the church spent the next two centuries doing exactly that.

## In One Sentence

- Paul told us himself what his gospel rested on: "not from any man, nor was I taught it." That's the quote, and it's in his own letter.
- The men who ate with Jesus for three years "added nothing" to his message — his words, not ours.
- Thirteen books, two thousand verses, and he quotes Jesus about twice. If you want the teaching, you're reading the wrong author.
- He met Peter for fifteen days and came back with a creed, not a Sermon on the Mount.
- Luther couldn't harmonize Paul and James either. He just decided James had to go.
