# What Does the Resurrection Evidence Actually Support?

**Position:** Something happened after the crucifixion that convinced a shattered group of Jewish followers their teacher was alive and vindicated. We take that seriously, and we don't claim to know what it was — the honest historical verdict is that the sources describe *appearances*, not an examined body, and they describe them inconsistently and with increasing detail the later they were written. Our actual claim is narrower and harder to dodge: salvation in Jesus's own recorded teaching never depends on believing a historical event. He was asked how to obtain eternal life and answered "keep the commandments." In the forty days the texts give him after the resurrection, he commissions repentance, forgiveness, service, and the kingdom of God — and never once tells anyone to believe his death was a payment for sin. The requirement to affirm the event as the condition of salvation is Pauline and then Nicene. It is not his.

---

## Strongest Evidence

### 1. The risen Jesus commissions commandments, forgiveness, and the kingdom — and never belief in a mechanism
> "...teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you." — Matthew 28:20

> "If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them." — John 20:23

> "He presented himself alive to them after his suffering by many proofs, appearing to them during forty days and speaking about the kingdom of God." — Acts 1:3

Post-resurrection scenes across three books, and the content of the commission is the same every time: the commandments, forgiveness delegated directly to human beings with no blood mechanism attached, and the kingdom of God. If believing in the atoning death were the saving message, this was the moment and this was the audience.

**Luke 24:46-47 — own it, don't lead with it.** A well-read Christian will raise this verse before we do: *"Thus it is written, that the Christ should suffer and on the third day rise from the dead, and that repentance for the forgiveness of sins should be proclaimed in his name to all nations"* — with Luke 24:26 behind it ("Was it not necessary that the Messiah should suffer these things?"). Concede the obvious half: Luke's risen Jesus does read the suffering as scripturally necessary, and Luke is writing in a milieu where that reading is already established. Then read the rest of the sentence. What gets *proclaimed* is **repentance** for the forgiveness of sins. Not belief in a transaction, not assent to a mechanism, not a doctrine of substitution — a change of life. Luke 24:47 is the earliest statement of the commission's content in that gospel, and its content is repentance. "Necessary that he suffer" and "therefore believe his death paid your debt" are two different claims, and Luke only makes the first one.

### 2. He answered the salvation question before the cross existed as a doctrine
> "If you want to enter eternal life, keep the commandments." — Matthew 19:17 (parallels: Mark 10:17–19; Luke 18:18–20)

A condition anyone in any century can meet. No historical event has to be verified first. Whatever happened Easter morning, this answer stands or falls on its own.

### 3. The earliest account is a creed older than Paul's letter — and it is a list of appearances, including a vision
> "...and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve... Last of all, as to one untimely born, he appeared also to me." — 1 Corinthians 15:5, 8

Cited here to characterize Paul's claim, not Jesus's teaching. **State the early date ourselves — it is the strongest thing about this passage and we have no reason to hide it.** The letter is c. 53-54 CE, but vv. 3-5 are not Paul composing; they are Paul transmitting. He flags it himself with the technical handover language of received tradition (παρέλαβον / παρέδωκα, "I received / I passed on"), the formula uses the Aramaic *Cephas* rather than Paul's usual Greek *Peter*, and its diction and parallel structure are not Paul's. Scholars who agree on almost nothing else about this material — Gerd Lüdemann, Martin Hengel, Gary Habermas — converge on a date within roughly two to five years of the crucifixion. So: granted, and granted loudly. A formula from the mid-30s is excellent evidence that Jesus's followers were convinced very early that he was alive and vindicated. **That is the thing we already concede.** The early date establishes the speed and sincerity of the conviction; it does not establish the nature of the event, because of what the formula actually says.

What it says is ὤφθη (*ōphthē*, "he was seen / appeared to") — one verb, used identically for Cephas, the Twelve, the five hundred, and for Paul himself. Paul's own experience is a light and a voice, which Acts has him call exactly that: "I was not disobedient to the heavenly vision" (Acts 26:19). The New Testament's only first-person resurrection witness describes a vision and places it in the same grammatical category as everyone else's — a point James Tabor presses at length in *Paul and Jesus* (2012). **Pushing the creed back to the 30s makes that problem older, not smaller:** the earliest formula we possess reports appearances in language that cannot distinguish a body from a vision. One further honesty: v. 3's "died for our sins in accordance with the scriptures" is inside the received formula too, so the interpretive clause is early as well. Early attestation of an interpretation is not the same as Jesus teaching it — and nothing in the red text does.

The same list says "After that, he was seen of James" (1 Corinthians 15:7, KJV), and no canonical gospel tells that appearance. The gospel the Hebrew believers used did. Jerome quotes the Gospel according to the Hebrews: "the Lord... appeared to James (for James had sworn that he would not eat bread from that hour in which he drank the cup of the Lord until he should see him rising again from among those that sleep)" (*Lives of Illustrious Men* 2, NPNF 2.3). Jerome wrote around 392 about a book he says he translated, so this is the Hebrew believers' memory, not a first-century report. It shows James at the center of the resurrection story in the community that claimed him.

### 4. The earliest gospel *promises* an appearance and then narrates none — and the later gospels can't agree on where it happened
> "And they went out and fled from the tomb, for terror and amazement had seized them; and they said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid." — Mark 16:8

Be precise here, because the sloppy version of this argument is easy to break. **Mark does know an appearance tradition and points at it:** "after I am raised up, I will go before you to Galilee" (14:28), and at the tomb, "he is going before you to Galilee; there you will see him, just as he told you" (16:7). Anyone can look that up in thirty seconds, so we say it first. The claim is not that Mark is ignorant of appearances. The claim is about what Mark *narrates* and what the later gospels do with it.

Mark (c. 70 CE) ends at 16:8 in our oldest complete manuscripts — Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus. The "longer ending" (16:9-20) is absent from those witnesses and is regarded across textual criticism, including by Bart Ehrman, as a later addition. So our earliest gospel gives a forward reference to Galilee and stops: no described encounter, no physical examination, no dialogue, no meal. Then watch the development in two directions at once. **Content:** Matthew gives a mountain encounter at which "some doubted" (28:17); Luke and John give touching, wound inspection, and eating broiled fish (Luke 24:39-43; John 20:27, 21:13). **Location:** Mark and Matthew send the disciples to Galilee; Luke keeps them in Jerusalem and has Jesus tell them to stay there (Luke 24:49; Acts 1:4). The appearances don't get earlier as the sources get earlier — they get more corporeal, more detailed, and less geographically consistent as the sources get later.

### 5. A whole early community preserved his sayings with no resurrection in them
> "That which you have will save you if you bring it forth from yourselves. That which you do not have within you will kill you if you do not have it within you." — Gospel of Thomas 70

> "Whoever finds the interpretation of these sayings will not experience death." — Gospel of Thomas 1

114 sayings, no passion narrative, no empty tomb, no appearances, no atonement. Thomas's answer to death is recognition and interpretation — bringing forth what is already within. Whatever you conclude about Thomas's date, it is evidence that an early Jesus community built its entire soteriology without the event.

Thomas isn't alone. The sayings Matthew and Luke share (Q, the material the method card relies on) also have no passion narrative, no empty tomb and no appearance. Their one cross is the follower's: "whosoever doth not bear his cross, and come after me, cannot be my disciple" (Luke 14:27, KJV). Q does know he faced death: it sets him among the prophets Jerusalem killed (Luke 11:49-51; "O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, which killest the prophets," 13:34). A murdered prophet, not a sacrifice. Its compilers lived after the crucifixion and knew of it; what they didn't do was build their book on it (Christian Florez's Q sheet, C2 and C6).

## Questions People Raise

### "If Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile — 1 Corinthians 15:14. The whole thing collapses without it."
That is Paul's criterion, stated inside Paul's system, and it is the same system that makes confession and belief the mechanism of salvation (Romans 10:9). Jesus, asked the identical question, pointed to the commandments (Matthew 19:17). You are entitled to prefer Paul's standard — but then say so plainly, because the two standards are not the same and only one of them comes from Jesus.

### "Luke 24:26 — 'Was it not necessary that the Messiah should suffer?' Your own gospel says the cross was required."
Granted, and we put that verse up ourselves rather than waiting for it. Luke's risen Jesus does present the suffering as foretold and necessary. Now notice the gap between that and what's being argued from it. "Necessary" in Luke is *scriptural fulfillment* — the Messiah's suffering was written about, therefore it happened as written. That is not penal substitution, which requires a further claim Luke never makes: that the death functioned as a payment transferring guilt. And when Luke's Jesus states what is to be *proclaimed* three verses later, it is repentance (24:47) — not belief in a mechanism. A necessary death and a transactional death are different doctrines; the second one is Paul's, systematized by Anselm in 1098 and made penal by the Reformers.

### "The minimal-facts case is strong: the empty tomb, the appearances, five hundred witnesses, the conversion of skeptics."
Appearance claims are good evidence that people had experiences they could not explain, and we grant that fully. What they cannot do is discriminate between a physical body and a visionary encounter, because the sources use the same word for both — and the one witness who speaks in the first person describes light and a voice (Acts 26:19). The five hundred (1 Corinthians 15:6) are never named, located, or interviewed; they appear once, in a list, in a letter. That is testimony about conviction, not a forensic finding.

### "The disciples died for this. People don't martyr themselves for something they know is a lie."
Correct, and irrelevant to the question. Sincerity establishes belief, not accuracy — it tells us they were convinced, which is exactly what we concede. Sincere conviction is also what the tomb narratives themselves show straining: Mark has three women and a young man; Matthew an earthquake and one angel; Luke two men; John Mary alone, then two angels. And the earliest of them ends by pointing the reader to Galilee and then stopping mid-sentence (Mark 16:7-8) — a promise of an appearance rather than an account of one.

### "Denying the bodily resurrection means you aren't a Christian."
We are not denying it. We are declining to make an unverifiable event the gate, because the earliest Christians didn't. The Didache — the pre-baptismal catechism of "the Twelve Apostles" — has no creed, no resurrection requirement, and no blood language in its Eucharistic prayers (Didache 1–6, 9–10; chapter 14's sacrifice language is the assembly's own offering, not Jesus's death). The "unless you affirm X" boundary line enters with Paul and hardens at Nicaea in 325 CE. If the first converts could be baptized without it, the word "Christian" is doing work here that history won't support.

## In One Sentence

- "We're not saying it didn't happen. We're saying nothing in his own words makes your standing with God depend on your opinion about it."
- "If believing in the resurrection were the saving message, forty days was the time to say it. He talked about the kingdom."
- "The first gospel ends by telling you to go to Galilee and see him — and then stops without showing you. The appearances get described, and get physical, as the decades pass."
- "Mark and Matthew send them to Galilee. Luke tells them to stay in Jerusalem. One of those gospels is wrong about where the most important event in history happened."
- "The creed in 1 Corinthians 15 is probably from within five years of the crucifixion, and we'll say that for you. It proves they were convinced fast. It uses the same word for Peter's experience and Paul's — and Paul's was a light and a voice."
- "Our only first-person witness describes a light and a voice — not a body he examined. And he says 'he appeared to me' in the same breath as 'he appeared to Peter.'"
- "The risen Jesus handed the power to forgive sins to his friends. No blood in that sentence."
