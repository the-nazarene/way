# How Reliable Is the Gospel of Thomas?

**Position:** Thomas is a real, ancient, independently circulating collection of 114 Jesus sayings with no narrative, no passion, no atonement, and no apocalypse — and the honest claim about it is narrower than the one our own notes have sometimes made. The defensible position is layered: a *kernel* of early sayings tradition inside a collection whose received form is late and accretive. The mainstream dating of the collection is c. 140 CE; the 50-70 CE date is a real position held by real specialists (Koester, Patterson, Crossan, Davies) but it is a **minority** one, and anyone who tells you "many scholars date it to 50-70" is overstating the field. April DeConick's rolling-corpus model is the most defensible frame: an early kernel with accretions running to roughly 120 CE. So we use Thomas as **corroboration** where it parallels Synoptic material, as **evidence about a documented early community** where it stands alone, and never as a standalone proof. It also contains sayings we do not defend and would not read aloud, and we name them below rather than be caught off guard by them. If Thomas were subtracted entirely, every argument in this collection would still stand on the red text — Thomas strengthens the case; it does not carry it.

## Strongest Evidence

### 1. What we actually physically have — state this before anyone else does
| Witness | Date | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| Nag Hammadi Codex II, tractate 2 (Coptic, Sahidic) | manuscript c. 340 CE | the complete 114 sayings — our only full text |
| P.Oxy. 1 (Greek) | c. 200 CE | prologue, Saying 1, Sayings 26-33 (partial) |
| P.Oxy. 654 (Greek) | c. 250 CE | prologue, Sayings 1-7 |
| P.Oxy. 655 (Greek) | c. 130-250 CE | Sayings 36-39 (partial) |

Two things follow, and we concede the first before it's used on us. **One:** our earliest physical fragment is around 200 CE, and the only complete text is a fourth-century Coptic translation. **Two:** the Greek fragments prove Thomas circulated in Greek, in Egypt, well before the Coptic translation — it is not a Coptic or Nag Hammadi composition — and they differ from the Coptic in minor ways, which is direct manuscript evidence that the collection moved and grew in transmission. That second point is what makes the layered model more than a convenience: the text we can compare is already a text that changed.

### 2. The dating debate, stated accurately
**Majority / mainstream:** c. 120-140 CE, dependent on the Synoptics. Principal advocates Mark Goodacre (*Thomas and the Gospels*, 2012) and Simon Gathercole (*The Gospel of Thomas: Introduction and Commentary*, 2014). This is where the weight of current specialist opinion sits.

**Minority early:** core sayings 50-70 CE, independent of the Synoptics. Helmut Koester (*Ancient Christian Gospels*, 1990), Stephen Patterson (*The Gospel of Thomas and Jesus*, 1993), Stevan Davies, John Dominic Crossan (who placed Thomas in his earliest stratum, 30-60 CE).

**The layered middle:** April DeConick (*Recovering the Original Gospel of Thomas*, 2005) — a kernel of roughly 40 logia from c. 30-50 CE, with accretions c. 60-120 CE. On this model the dating question is malformed: Thomas is early *and* late, and the real work is sorting which saying belongs to which layer.

We argue the layered model, and we say out loud that **the collection as received is later than its earliest material.** That is a weaker claim than "Thomas may predate Mark," and it is the one we can actually defend in a room with someone who has read Goodacre.

### 3. The independence evidence that actually holds up
Four arguments survive contact with the critics, and they are the reason this is still a live debate rather than a settled one:

- **Thomas's parallels often lack the Synoptic editorial layer.** Thomas 9 gives the Sower with no allegorical interpretation attached; Mark 4:13-20 appends one. Patterson catalogued this pattern across the shared material. If Thomas were copying finished gospels, the consistent *stripping* of their editorial work needs an explanation.
- **No passion narrative, at all.** No crucifixion, no empty tomb, no appearances, no atonement. The passion is the organizing spine of all four canonical gospels. A second-century author who had read all three Synoptics and omitted it entirely is a strange hypothesis.
- **No developed soteriology and no institution.** No priesthood, no church, no Peter-gets-the-keys — and the authority goes to James: *"Wherever you are, you are to go to James the righteous"* (Thomas 12, Lambdin). That appointment is against the interest of every later orthodoxy, Roman and Protestant alike. Nobody invents it in 140 CE.
- **The Q overlap.** Roughly 37 of the 114 have Q parallels, and Thomas proved the *genre* of a sayings gospel existed — which before 1945 was the standard objection to Q itself. Whether Thomas and Q share a source or not, Thomas retired the "nobody wrote sayings-only collections" argument permanently. For why Q is thought to exist at all, one verse is enough: Luke 16:13 in Westcott-Hort reads "ουδεις οικετης δυναται δυσιν κυριοις δουλευειν...", and Matthew 6:24 has the same 27 Greek words in the same order, lacking only οικετης ("servant"). Agreement that close between two writers who don't otherwise share this material is what the hypothesis explains.

DeConick also argues for Aramaisms in sayings whose Synoptic parallels lack them, pointing to a Palestinian rather than a Greek-Gnostic origin for that layer. Present that as *argued*, not as established.

### 4. The use that's actually bulletproof: multiple attestation
Where Thomas and a Synoptic carry the same teaching, the teaching is better attested than it would be from either source alone — and that is all our central argument needs.

> "Rather, the kingdom is inside of you, and it is outside of you. When you come to know yourselves, then you will become known, and you will realize that it is you who are the sons of the living father." — Thomas 3 (Lambdin)

> "It will not come by waiting for it. It will not be a matter of saying 'here it is' or 'there it is.' Rather, the kingdom of the father is spread out upon the earth, and men do not see it." — Thomas 113 (Lambdin)

Set against Luke 17:20-21 — "the kingdom of God is not coming with things that can be observed... the kingdom of God is among you" — two source streams agree on both location and timing. That holds even on a 140 CE dating, because the point is attestation across streams, not Thomas's priority.

One saying has a second non-canonical witness. Thomas 2: "Let him who seeks continue seeking until he finds. When he finds, he will become troubled. When he becomes troubled, he will be astonished, and he will rule over the All" (Lambdin). Clement of Alexandria, about 200, quotes it from another book by name: "So also in the Gospel to the Hebrews it is written, 'He that wonders shall reign, and he that has reigned shall rest'" (*Stromata* 2.9). So the saying circulated outside Thomas. Two cautions: the Coptic Thomas has no "rest" (that step is in the Greek fragment P.Oxy. 654, not held), and the longer form at *Stromata* 5.14 names no source.

### 5. The one claim about Thomas that is completely date-proof
Whatever its date, Thomas documents a Christian community that transmitted Jesus as a wisdom teacher whose answer to death was recognition — with no cross-as-payment, no blood, no judgment day, no rapture, and no bishop.

> "That which you have will save you if you bring it forth from yourselves. That which you do not have within you will kill you if you do not have it within you." — Thomas 70 (Lambdin)

> "Whoever finds the interpretation of these sayings will not experience death." — Thomas 1 (Lambdin)

If Thomas is early, this is a window onto pre-Pauline tradition. If Thomas is late, it is proof that a substantial Christian community in the second century still read Jesus this way and the canonical process excluded them. **Both conclusions are useful to us, and one of them is certainly true.** Never let the conversation become "prove Thomas is early" — that's a bet we don't need to take.

## Questions People Raise

### "Goodacre settled this in 2012. Thomas shows Synoptic redactional fingerprints — it read the finished gospels."
Goodacre's argument is serious and partially correct, and pretending otherwise is how you lose a room. He identifies verbal agreements between Thomas and material that is demonstrably Matthean or Lukan *editing* of Mark, plus a diffuse pattern in which Thomas agrees now with Matthew against Mark, now with Luke against Mark, now with material unique to one of them — a pattern most simply explained by access to all three. Concede that.

Then the response, in three parts.

**First, his evidence is saying-by-saying, and his conclusion is collection-wide.** Demonstrating Synoptic knowledge in some sayings establishes Synoptic knowledge in *those sayings*. That is precisely what the rolling-corpus model predicts: later accretions, added in a Synoptic-saturated environment, will carry Synoptic wording while the kernel does not. Goodacre's data and DeConick's model are substantially compatible; the disagreement is about how much of the collection the data covers.

**Second, "secondary orality."** By 100 CE the Synoptics were being read aloud in communities. Sayings re-entered oral circulation carrying Matthean and Lukan phrasing without anyone copying a scroll. James Dunn's work on oral transmission makes this a real mechanism, not a dodge — literary dependence and oral contamination produce similar verbal data and are hard to separate.

**Third, the hole in the late-dependence account stays open.** If Thomas read Matthew, Mark, and Luke, the total absence of the passion narrative is unexplained — and so is the appointment of James over Peter, and so is the absence of any Synoptic *narrative* material whatsoever. Dependence is a hypothesis with a large unpaid bill.

**Bottom line to say out loud:** we cannot prove Thomas is independent, and we don't claim to. We claim it is genuinely contested by named specialists on both sides, which is enough for corroborative use and not enough for proof-text use.

### "Thomas 114. Read it out loud and you're done."
Read it out loud yourself, first, before they do:

> Simon Peter said to him, "Let Mary leave us, for women are not worthy of life." Jesus said, "I myself shall lead her in order to make her male, so that she too may become a living spirit resembling you males. For every woman who will make herself male will enter the kingdom of heaven." — Thomas 114 (Lambdin)

**We don't defend it, we don't need it, and it is ugly.** Now here is the honest analysis, which is better than a defense.

**It is the strongest candidate in the collection for a late accretion.** It is the last saying, appended after Saying 113 — which reads like a closing — and its "make her male" transformation theology sits with the collection's latest, most ascetic material rather than with its wisdom kernel. On DeConick's layering, it belongs to the accretions, not the kernel. The layered model doesn't make 114 go away; it tells you where it came from.

**Thomas itself states the opposite goal, in Saying 22:** *"when you make the male and the female one and the same, so that the male not be male nor the female"* (Lambdin). That is the dissolution of gender as a category. Saying 114 is a cruder, later restatement of 22's "make the two one" in the hierarchical idiom of its own culture — where "male" is the unmarked, default, complete human. On that reading 114 is bad second-century anthropology applied to a first-century non-dual idea. That is an explanation, not an excuse, and don't oversell it.

**Note who says what.** "Women are not worthy of life" is **Peter's** line. Jesus's reply overrules Peter's exclusion — Mary stays. By modern standards the terms are still indefensible; structurally it is a reversal of an exclusion, not an endorsement of one. And Mary is a disciple in good standing who asks Jesus a question at Thomas 21 without comment or objection.

**The consistency point, used carefully.** Canonical texts carry the same problem: 1 Timothy 2:11-15 ("I permit no woman to teach") and 1 Corinthians 14:34-35 (widely argued to be a later insertion on manuscript grounds). We are not saying "yours is bad too, so mine is fine." We are saying the tool that handles 1 Corinthians 14:34-35 — identify the layer, date it, don't attribute it to the center — is the same tool that handles Thomas 114, and we apply it to both or to neither.

### "Thomas 56 and 80 call the world a corpse. 29, 87, and 112 despise the body. 7 is incoherent. This is Gnostic world-hatred, not Jesus."
The quotes, verbatim, so nobody gets to spring them:

> "Whoever has come to understand the world has found (only) a corpse, and whoever has found a corpse is superior to the world." — Thomas 56
> "He who has recognized the world has found the body, but he who has found the body is superior to the world." — Thomas 80
> "Wretched is the body that is dependant upon a body, and wretched is the soul that is dependent on these two." — Thomas 87
> "Woe to the flesh that depends on the soul; woe to the soul that depends on the flesh." — Thomas 112
> "If the flesh came into being because of spirit, it is a wonder. But if spirit came into being because of the body, it is a wonder of wonders. Indeed, I am amazed at how this great wealth has made its home in this poverty." — Thomas 29
> "Blessed is the lion which becomes man when consumed by man; and cursed is the man whom the lion consumes, and the lion becomes man." — Thomas 7

**Concede the cluster.** This is world-renouncing wisdom, and Gathercole's reading — that it reflects a second-century Syrian ascetic milieu rather than first-century Galilee — is a reasonable account of where this material comes from. On the layered model it is accretion, and we should say so.

**But read what they actually say.** Thomas 56 and 80 are near-identical and both end the same way: having *understood* or *recognized* the world, you are "superior to" it. That is a knowledge claim about seeing through materiality, not a command to hate creation — and the same collection says the kingdom is "spread out upon the earth" (113) and inside you and outside you (3). A text that locates the kingdom on the earth is not teaching that the earth is evil. Thomas 29's "this great wealth has made its home in this poverty" is the divine-spark-in-matter image, which is also Paul's "treasure in clay jars" (2 Corinthians 4:7).

**And it isn't unique to Thomas.** "Whoever loves their life loses it" (John 12:25); "whoever does not hate father and mother... cannot be my disciple" (Luke 14:26); "deny themselves and take up their cross" (Mark 8:34-35); "do not love the world" (1 John 2:15). Paul's flesh/spirit dualism in Romans 7-8 and Galatians 5 is harsher than anything in Thomas and it's canonical. If world-renunciation language disqualifies a text, it disqualifies most of the New Testament.

**On Thomas 7, say the true thing:** nobody knows what it means. There is no scholarly consensus on the lion saying. Do not improvise an interpretation under pressure — "that one's genuinely obscure and I'm not going to pretend otherwise" is a stronger answer than a guess, and it buys credibility for everything else on the card.

### "Thomas has no love-of-enemies command and no love-of-neighbor command. Your whole case is that love is the law."
Verified, and it's the most substantive criticism on this page. Across all 114 sayings the closest thing to a love command is:

> "Love your brother like your soul, guard him like the pupil of your eye." — Thomas 25 (Lambdin)

That's in-group love. Saying 95 gives an economic ethic ("If you have money, do not lend it at interest, but give it to one from whom you will not get it back"), and 101 actually requires *hating* father and mother. There is no "love your enemies" and no "love your neighbor as yourself" anywhere in the collection.

**So the division of labor has to be stated honestly: Thomas supplies the epistemology, the Synoptics supply the ethics.** Thomas tells you where the kingdom is and how it's perceived — within, outside, already spread out, found by self-knowledge. It does not tell you what to do once you see it. Using Thomas as the basis for the love-is-the-law argument would be a category error, and we don't.

The love case stands entirely on the red text and the earliest community documents: Mark 12:29-31 (the two greatest commandments), Matthew 5:43-48 ("love your enemies... be perfect as your heavenly Father"), Luke 6:27-36, Matthew 7:12, Matthew 25:31-46, John 13:34-35, Leviticus 19:18 behind all of it, and Didache 1-2, where the Two Ways opens with love of God and neighbor as the whole of the Way of Life. Not one of those needs Thomas. **This is why Thomas is a corroborating witness rather than the foundation — the foundation is the red text, and it always was.**

### "Thomas is a Gnostic gospel. The church excluded it for cause."
"Gnostic" is doing work as a slur here rather than as a classification. Thomas has none of the defining machinery of classical Gnosticism: no demiurge, no archons, no Sophia myth, no aeons, no evil creator god, no cosmogony at all. Stevan Davies read it as Jewish wisdom literature; it exalts James the righteous (12), blesses the poor (54), and debates circumcision in Jewish terms (53). It is also the one Nag Hammadi text that turns out to have been circulating in Greek at Oxyrhynchus — it's not a sectarian curiosity from a single jar.

Concede what's real: Thomas 28 ("I took my place in the midst of the world, and I appeared to them in flesh... I found all of them intoxicated") is a revealer-descent saying, and the *monachos* / "solitary one" material (16, 49, 75) has genuine proto-Gnostic affinities. Those belong to the later layer. The accurate description is a wisdom collection with proto-Gnostic accretions, not a Gnostic treatise.

### "Thomas 55 mentions the cross, so your 'no cross in Thomas' line is false."
Correct, and we should say it first. Thomas 55: *"whoever does not hate his brothers and sisters and take up his cross in my way will not be worthy of me."* That's one occurrence, and it's the discipleship saying with Synoptic parallels at Matthew 10:38 and Luke 14:27 — cross-bearing as the cost of following, not the crucifixion as atonement. The precise claim is: **no passion narrative, no crucifixion account, no atonement theology, no blood.** Make it that way and it's airtight. Say "no cross in Thomas" flatly and you hand someone a correction.

### "If you won't stand behind all 114 sayings, you can't cite any of them."
Nobody applies that standard to the canon, and the person making the objection doesn't either — they already distinguish the Comma Johanneum from 1 John, and Mark 16:9-20 from Mark. Layer analysis is the ordinary tool of the field. We use it on Thomas 114, on John's discourses, and on Matthew 26:28's "for the forgiveness of sins" alike. Applying it consistently is the position; applying it only to the other side's texts is what we're objecting to.

- "Mainstream dating is around 140. A real minority of specialists argue 50 to 70. We'll argue the layered model, and we'll tell you which layer we're standing on."
