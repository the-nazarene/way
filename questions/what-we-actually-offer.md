# If Salvation Is by Works, What Do You Offer Someone Who Has Failed?

**Position:** Everything — and it arrives faster than the transaction model allows. This is the best question anyone asks us, and it deserves a straight answer rather than another argument. Here it is: Jesus forgave people on the spot, repeatedly, before any restitution, without an altar, without a priest, and without a drop of blood — and he did it for the worst-positioned people in his society. Our position has never been that you earn God's welcome by accumulating good deeds. It is that **the welcome is free and the life that follows is real.** Repentance is *metanoia* — a turn, a change of mind and direction — and it is available in a second to anyone still breathing. The threshold is not a completed résumé; Jesus proved that with a man dying on a cross beside him who had no time and nothing left to give. What we do not offer is a mechanism that makes the turning optional. In the story, the father runs the whole distance — and the son still gets up and walks home.

---

## Strongest Evidence

### 1. Luke 23:43 — the verse that settles the performance question
> "Truly I tell you, today you will be with me in Paradise." — Luke 23:43

A condemned criminal. Hours to live. No works remaining and no possibility of any. No creed recited, no sacrifice offered, no baptism, no restitution to his victims. He says one thing — "remember me" — and he gets a promise with *today* in it.

**If anyone claims our position demands performance, this is the verse we hand them.** Whatever the threshold is, that man cleared it from a cross with nails through his hands. We do not need to argue around it. It is the floor of our position, stated by Jesus, at the worst moment of his life, to the least qualified person present.

### 2. The prodigal son — the father runs before the apology finishes
> "But while he was still far off, his father saw him and was filled with compassion; he ran and put his arms around him and kissed him." — Luke 15:20

Read the order. The father sees him *far off*. The father runs. The embrace lands **before** the prepared speech, which the son starts and never gets to finish — the father interrupts him with instructions for a party.

And notice what is absent from the most famous forgiveness story Jesus ever told: no ledger. No penalty. No third party absorbing the debt so the father can legally receive the son. **The father simply eats the loss.** That is expensive — he is out an entire inheritance — and it costs the son nothing but the walk home.

### 3. The tax collector — seven words, and Jesus uses the word "justified"
> "'God, be merciful to me, a sinner!' I tell you, this man went down to his home justified rather than the other." — Luke 18:13-14

*Dedikaiōmenos.* The justification word, in Jesus's mouth, attached to a short prayer from a man in a collaborator's profession with nothing to show. And **not** to the man with the compliance record, who is in the story specifically because he had one.

### 4. He forgave directly, with no altar and no priest — before the crucifixion
> "Son, your sins are forgiven." — Mark 2:5
> "Your sins are forgiven." — Luke 7:48

Both before Golgotha. Both with no sacrifice, no Temple, no intermediary, no waiting period. If blood were the mechanism, these scenes are impossible. They show that forgiveness was always God's to give and always being given. **The cross did not open a door that Mark 2 shows already open.**

### 5. Zacchaeus — the order of operations, which is the whole thing
> "Hurry and come down; for I must stay at your house today." ... "Look, half of my possessions, Lord, I will give to the poor..." ... "Today salvation has come to this house." — Luke 19:5, 8, 9

Jesus invites himself to dinner **first**. Zacchaeus's restitution is his *response*, not his admission fee. Nobody negotiated. Nobody audited.

That sequence — welcome, then transformation, then the verdict "salvation has come" — is our entire soteriology acted out in nine verses. Works are what gratitude looks like when it has hands. They are never the ticket.

### 6. How forgiveness is received: you participate in it
> "For if you forgive others their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you." — Matthew 6:14
> "Should you not have had mercy on your fellow slave, as I had mercy on you?" — Matthew 18:33

Here is why that is good news rather than another burden: **forgiving someone is a thing you can do today, from anywhere, owning nothing.** From a hospital bed. From a cell. On the last day of your life. It requires no money, no health, no time, and no priest.

And look at what the unforgiving servant is actually condemned for. Not failing a quota — for refusing to pass on what he had already been given for free. The debt in that parable is forgiven *first*, in full, unprompted (18:27). The failure comes after.

### 7. Matthew 25 — the currency is a cup of water, and nobody was keeping score
> "Lord, when was it that we saw you hungry and gave you food?" — Matthew 25:37

Both groups ask the same stunned question. **Neither side had a tally.** The sheep did not know they were accruing anything; the goats did not know they were missing anything. That is the opposite of a performance quota — the criterion is not what you logged, it is who you became.

And the units are deliberately tiny: food, water, a coat, a visit. The smallest available denominations of mercy. Jesus set the currency low enough that nobody is priced out.

### 8. He stayed in long conversations with people whose lives were wrecked
The woman at the well (John 4) — wrong ethnicity, wrong hill, five husbands — gets the longest theological conversation in the gospel and the first "I am he." He does not ask her to fix anything before the conversation happens.

And the one everybody quotes:
> "Neither do I condemn you. Go your way, and from now on do not sin again." — John 8:11

**Say the textual status out loud before someone else does:** the *pericope adulterae* (John 7:53-8:11) is absent from the earliest copies of John (P66 and P75, about 200, and the great fourth-century codices), Origen's commentary goes from 7:52 straight to 8:12, and in later manuscripts it floats between locations — some put it in Luke. But the story is older than its place in John: Eusebius reports that Papias "relates another story of a woman, who was accused of many sins before the Lord, which is contained in the Gospel according to the Hebrews" (*Church History* 3.39.16, McGiffert). We cite it as early and treasured Christian tradition, not as John's text and not as textual bedrock. (Florez sheet, A2) We flag it because flagging it costs nothing and buys credibility, and because **the case does not need it.** Luke 23:43 and Luke 15:20 are not in doubt by anyone.

## The Hard Questions

### "I've already ruined it. There's no repairing what I did. What do you give me?"
Nothing in Jesus asks you to repair it. Restitution shows up in the gospels where it happens to be possible — Zacchaeus had money — and it is never the price of entry. The father ran. The tax collector went home justified from seven words. The man on the cross had nothing and was told *today*.

What we don't hand you is the feeling of having paid, and we think that is the honest part. The debt in Jesus's parables is **dropped**, not transferred to someone else. Nobody bled to make the father's embrace legal. If it helps to hear it plainly: your account is not what's standing between you and God, and it never was.

And here is the part that is actually available to you right now, today, before anything is fixed — **the mercy you cannot give yourself, you can give to someone else.** That is exactly where Jesus locates the mechanism (Matthew 6:14). It is the one move nobody can be too far gone to make.

### "What about someone who physically can't do the works — the dying, the disabled, the imprisoned, the exhausted?"
Luke 23:43 answers this and it is not a close call. A man with no limbs free and no hours left was told he'd be in Paradise that day.

Then the oldest Christian church manual we possess answers it pastorally, in writing, around the same era as the gospels:

> "If you can bear the Lord's full yoke, you will be perfect. But if you cannot, **then do what you can.**" — Didache 6:2

That is the earliest surviving pastoral instruction in Christianity, and it is not "meet the standard." It is "do what you can."

Jesus did the same arithmetic out loud:
> "Truly I tell you, this poor widow has put in more than all of them; for all of them have contributed out of their abundance, but she out of her poverty has put in all she had to live on." — Mark 12:43-44

**Two coins outscored the large gifts.** The measure is proportional, not absolute. There is no floor below which a person has nothing to offer — in Matthew 25's economy a kind word from a hospital bed is legal tender. A sick person is not a disqualified person. They are usually the one Jesus is standing next to in the story.

### "Walk me through it. How is forgiveness actually received?"
Three things, and all three are free and available in the next sixty seconds.

1. **Turn.** *Metanoia* is a change of mind that becomes a change of direction (Mark 1:15). It is not "feel bad" — remorse is a feeling and Jesus never asks for it as payment. It is facing the other way.
2. **Ask.** "Ask, and it will be given you... how much more will your Father in heaven give good things to those who ask him" (Matthew 7:7-11). The tax collector's prayer is seven words long and it worked.
3. **Pass it on.** "Forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors" (Matthew 6:12). Note that the Lord's Prayer puts "forgive us" in the *daily* list, alongside bread. Not a one-time transaction — a relationship you live inside. Which is exactly why it is both free and ongoing.

No altar, no priest, no intermediary, no qualifying period. We know that because he did it face to face for people who had done nothing yet.

### "Then what is Jesus to you? You've emptied him out."
We'd say the opposite — a mechanism is a smaller thing than what he actually is.

He is the one who shows what a human being fully aligned with God looks like, under maximum pressure, all the way to the end without flinching and without retaliating. And he said that was the point: "the one who believes in me will also do the works that I do" (John 14:12). "I have set you an example, that you also should do as I have done to you" (John 13:15).

The Eastern church kept this and never lost it. Athanasius, Irenaeus, Maximus — *theosis*: God became human so that humans could become what he is. Not a legal status imputed from outside while you stay the same person. **An actual transformation, participated in from the inside.** Early Christian language for it is "partakers of the divine nature" (2 Peter 1:4) — and Jesus's own version is "that they may all be one, as you, Father, are in me and I am in you" (John 17:21).

So: not a payment we bank. A pattern we join. He went first.

### "'Be perfect as your Father is perfect.' That's an impossible standard, which is exactly why you need substitution."
This is where the transaction model wins arguments, so take it seriously rather than swatting it.

*Teleios* does not mean flawless. It means **complete** — brought to its intended end. The same word describes a full-grown adult and a finished building. And the context fixes the meaning precisely: Matthew 5:43-48 is about loving your enemies because God "makes his sun rise on the evil and on the good." "Be perfect" means *love without excluding anyone, the way the sun doesn't*. It is naming a direction so you know which way you're walking.

It is a horizon, not a bar. Nobody fails a horizon. And the earliest Christians read exactly this idea and immediately added the line we already quoted: *if you cannot bear the full yoke, do what you can* (Didache 6:2). They did not hear a crushing demand. They heard a direction of travel, with permission to be where you actually are.

### "What do you say at a funeral?"
Short, and without dodging.

We say the person is held. We say what Jesus said: that God "is God not of the dead, but of the living" (Mark 12:27), that the kingdom is not somewhere else (Luke 17:21), and that the measure their life was taken by is mercy — which most people, including plenty who never went near a church, have far more of than they were ever given credit for.

What we don't do is run the calculation. We don't say "they made a decision in time" or "they didn't." We don't know, and when someone asked Jesus for the headcount — "Lord, will only a few be saved?" — he refused to give one and told the asker to mind his own door (Luke 13:23-24). We're not going to pretend to knowledge he declined to supply.

On suffering itself: he never explained it. Asked whose sin caused a man's blindness, he rejected the question outright (John 9:3) and then did something about the blindness. **That is our whole theodicy and it's his.** Grief does not need certainty about the next room. It needs somebody to stay in this one. That is what we offer, because that is what he did — and it is more than a doctrine, because somebody has to actually show up.

## In One Sentence

- "What do you offer the man who failed?" The same thing Jesus offered the man dying next to him. Everything, immediately, for nothing.
- The father ran. Before the apology finished, before a cent was repaid, before anybody bled.
- Seven words from a tax collector and he "went home justified." That's Jesus's word, not ours.
- Repentance isn't feeling bad. It's turning around. It takes a second and it's free.
- "Do what you can." Oldest pastoral instruction in Christianity. Not "meet the standard."
- Zacchaeus got dinner first and paid it back after. That order is the gospel.
- Two coins beat the big gifts. The measure was never absolute.
- Nobody in Matthew 25 was keeping score. Neither group knew. That's the point.
- We don't say your debt was transferred. We say it was dropped.
- Jesus isn't a payment we bank. He's a pattern we join — and he went first.
